This is a quick response I first made on my Instagram stories to ["What hundreds of American public libraries owe to Carnegie’s disdain for inherited wealth"][Carnegie], written by Arlene Weismantel for *The Conversation*. 

>Carnegie argued that handing large fortunes to the next generation wasted money, as it was unlikely that descendants would match the exceptional abilities that had created the wealth into which they were born. He also surmised that dynasties harm heirs by robbing their lives of purpose and meaning.

>He practiced what he preached and was still actively giving in 1911 after he had already given away 90 percent of his wealth to causes he cared passionately about, especially libraries.

In essence, Carnegie believed that inherited wealth made people lazy, so instead of causing a perceived poor work ethic in his heirs, Carnegie decided to pass that wealth onto institutions. Much of that wealth was used to establish public libraries across the United States. Sounds okay so far.

>In 1850, Carnegie, by then working as a messenger, learned that iron manufacturer Colonel James Anderson let working boys visit his 400-volume library on Saturdays. Among those books, “the windows were opened in the walls of my dungeon through which the light of knowledge streamed in,” Carnegie wrote, explaining how the experience both thrilled him and changed his life.

>Books kept him and other boys “clear of low fellowship and bad habits,” Carnegie said later. He called that library the source of his largely informal education.

Here we start to see the idea of public libraries as the "great equalizers" of American society - spaces where anything is possible, where everyone is welcome to learn (though, as I recently talked about, many public libraries exclude the people who most need them, such as when they implement body odor rules to exclude homeless patrons).

Carnegie valued libraries because he didn't receive much in the way of a formal education, and access to a private library during his adolescence allowed him to learn more than he could have otherwise. This is the vision we have of public libraries today, even now that we have compulsory schooling. In much of the past, many people did not have access to education, and others were expected to leave schooling once they reached adolescence to enter the workforce. Today, adults and adolescents alike suffer from the effects of under-funded and under-equipped schools, and pressure is exerted on public libraries to fill in the educational gaps. Leaders and users expect libraries to provide access to resources, as well as instruction on how to best use these resources, even while many public libraries exclude the patrons who most need access to free resources, and while public libraries nationwide are facing budget cuts.

>Carnegie believed in exercising discretion and care with charitable largess. People who became too dependent on handouts were unwilling to improve their lot in life and didn’t deserve them, in his opinion. Instead, he sought to “use wealth so as to be really beneficial to the community.”

>For the industrial titan, that meant supporting the institutions that empower people to pull themselves up by their bootstraps like universities, hospitals and, above all, libraries.

>In Carnegie’s view, “the main consideration should be to help those who will help themselves.” Free libraries were, in Carnegie’s opinion, among the best ways to lend a hand to anyone who deserved it.

Carnegie refused to directly provide financial assistance to people for, essentially, the same reason he didn't support inherited wealth: he feared creating lazy, dependent people as opposed to productive workers.

Here we see one of the major visions of public libraries, that by providing information to all people, we give them the power to change their lives and socioeconomic status. This relies on the belief that books are tangible sources of power and that there aren't larger, systemic obstacles that prevent people from leaving poverty.

It's telling that this myth - a myth that pervades the history of public libraries in the U.S. - was perpetuated by a capitalist. Public libraries were partially established, per American library lore, in order to create a more educated workforce, the idea being that a learned laborer is better for employers than an illiterate laborer. (Note: this isn't a comprehensive explanation of all the myths and visions that influenced public libraries as we know them today in the U.S.)

However, if we look at this myth from the flipside, this means that literate workers cannot escape their working-class status by utilizing the library's resources. If public libraries actually empowered workers to become bosses and capitalists, then the ruling class wouldn't have given laborers free and absolute access to that freedom, the way that Carnegie established public libraries across the country. Instead, libraries are designed to make workers even more profitable for their employers, not to empower members of the working class to leave their jobs and change their lot in life.

So, in that way, we ended up with dual competing visions of libraries: that they help bosses by creating literate workers, and that they help workers by providing them the opportunity to lift themselves up by their bootstraps and become bosses themselves.

The idea that self-directed learning and access to books enables socioeconomic mobility is a myth that carries danger, potentially making laborers complacent. It tells laborers that their key to liberation is through independent studying, working hard for the boss and then pursuing knowledge in their leisure time. This focus on independent self-education as the path toward class mobility undermines the tangible power of unionizing and class solidarity - even as unions gave laborers leisure time in the first place. The gains of unions are replaced with the potential of self-education in working-class visions of liberation.

>Carnegie built 2,509 libraries in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, 1,679 of them across the U.S. in nearly every state. All told, he spent US\$55 million of his wealth on libraries. Adjusted for inflation, that would top \$1.3 billion today.

>Some were grand but about 70 percent of these libraries served towns of less than 10,000 and cost less than \$25,000 (at that time) to build.

So, in establishing libraries across the United States, Carnegie may have also contributed to the idea that independent intellectual work, not unionization, is the key to class mobility. It's key to note that public libraries were established as a necessary part of American education and society by a capitalist, which begs the question: how can we expect libraries to be sites of class liberation when they were shaped by the ruling class?
