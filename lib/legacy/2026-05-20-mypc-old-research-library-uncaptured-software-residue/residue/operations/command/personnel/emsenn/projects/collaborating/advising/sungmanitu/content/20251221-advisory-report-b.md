Decolonial Marxist formations add a further constraint: justified suspicion toward Western institutional forms makes formalization feel like betrayal or regression. That pushes groups to seek “organization” without the mechanisms that make organization distinct from a network: membership boundaries, decision rights, enforcement, exit rules, and bounded scopes. Those functions do not disappear; they relocate into informal power: charisma, founder control of channels, gatekeeping by social capital, and donor leverage. The politics produced can therefore converge with mainstream capitalist patterns even when content is radical: personality-centered authority, competitive signaling, marketplace differentiation, scarcity anxieties, and reputational warfare.

This is the heart of the point: media-as-politics networks train people into a capitalist political economy regardless of content. They reproduce the same politics because their operating logic is not “what we believe,” but “how value is produced and allocated.” Value becomes attention. Allocation becomes algorithms plus patronage. Competition is permanent because attention is scarce. Coordination becomes costly because conflict is engagement but also corrosive. Outcomes skew toward what is legible and repeatable as content, not what is durable or institution-building. Even “anti-capitalist” media networks can end up functioning like small firms competing in a niche market—differentiating brands, defending audiences, and optimizing outputs—because that is the substrate they are built on.

Once you see that history, the contemporary confusion stops being a mystery. A group starting in 2020 often lacks the felt distinction between media network and political organization because the organizational half of political tradition was not transmitted as lived practice; it was replaced by networks and platforms that feel like organization but behave like markets. That mismatch creates burnout and strategic stagnation: constant motion without accumulated capacity, constant talk without binding decisions, constant identity labor without insulation.

What follows from this, for CLN specifically, is not a scolding about “memes.” It is a sober recognition: if you accept the media-network identity, you can work with the substrate and reduce burnout by adding role boundaries and production discipline. If you reject it, you are forced either to abandon most existing assets or to fight the substrate continuously—burning people out faster while reproducing the same attention-market politics you think you’re escaping.
