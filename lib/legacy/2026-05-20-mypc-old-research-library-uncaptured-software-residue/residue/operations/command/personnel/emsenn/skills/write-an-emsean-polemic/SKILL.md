# Write an emsean polemic


## Purpose

Produce polemical analytical writing in the style used by emsenn: conceptually rigorous, grounded in defined objects, explicitly theoretical, and willing to take positions without rhetorical inflation. The goal is not persuasion-through-affect but *intelligibility-through-constraint*: the reader should be able to see how the claim follows from the object and the grammar applied to it.

This skill exists to prevent “AI slop” failure modes: unearned claims, abstract padding, tone smoothing, and conclusions that feel true without having been demonstrated.

## Core Commitments

### 1. Objects Before Interpretation

Every essay begins by defining the object **as it exists in the world**.

- If the object is a law: name it, date it, describe what it legally requires.
- If the object is an institution: explain what it does operationally.
- If the object is a concept: trace where it comes from and what work it performs.

No interpretation, analogy, or theoretical framing may appear before the reader can independently answer: *what is this thing, concretely?*

Motivation: interpretation without object-construction is ideological free play.

### 2. Grammar Is Treated as Material

Language is not a neutral carrier of meaning. Sentence structure, voice, agency, and abstraction are treated as *governing technologies*.

Writing must therefore:

- Attend to active vs passive voice
- Track where agency appears or disappears
- Treat abstraction as a political move, not a stylistic one

Claims about “grammar” must be supported by sentence-level analysis or explicit examples.

Motivation: following Klemperer, grammar is where political drift becomes durable.

### 3. Theory Is Used, Not Displayed

Theorists are cited only when they are doing work on the page.

Acceptable uses:
- Clarifying a mechanism (Klemperer on syntax and ideology)
- Naming an already-shown pattern (Schmitt on friend/enemy)
- Extending an argument already built from the object

Unacceptable uses:
- Name-checking for authority
- Citations that merely “align” the essay with a tradition
- Smooth reinforcement of a point that already stands

Motivation: theory is a tool, not a credential.

### 4. Polemic Without Performance

The tone is polemical but plain.

This means:
- Claims are stated directly, without hedging
- No moral panic, no urgency theater
- No inspirational language, no rhetorical crescendos
- No “stakes signaling” (e.g., “this matters because…”)

Condemnation is allowed, but only when it follows demonstration.

Motivation: persuasion should arise from recognition, not affect.

### 5. No Defensive Writing

The essay does not preemptively reassure the reader.

Avoid:
- “This does not mean…”
- “To be clear…”
- “Not X, but Y…” unless analytically necessary

Assume the reader is capable and attentive. Let misreadings fail.

Motivation: defensive writing recenters imagined critics instead of the object.

### 6. Asymmetry Is Preferred Over Balance

Arguments do not need to “feel complete.”

Allowed:
- Uneven paragraph lengths
- Claims that tilt without closure
- Endings that foreclose rather than summarize

Avoid:
- Symmetrical phrasing for its own sake
- Neatly paired oppositions unless materially justified
- “Pattern recognition” conclusions that stop short of consequence

Motivation: reality is lopsided; symmetry is often cosmetic.

### 7. Endings Close Possibilities

Conclusions do not open new vistas or call for dialogue.

A proper ending:
- Names what is now harder or impossible
- Identifies a constraint that has been withdrawn or imposed
- Leaves the reader with fewer illusions than they started with

Motivation: polemic clarifies limits, not hope.

## Common Failure Modes (Explicitly Avoid)

- Abstract noun density without re-instantiation
- Wikipedia-style factual resets as openings
- Over-smooth paragraph rhythm
- Generic “insight” phrasing
- Pattern recognition without escalation
- Neutrality leakage due to imagined safety constraints

## Success Criteria

A piece written with this skill should allow a critical reader to say:

- “I can see how this claim follows.”
- “I could point to the sentence where responsibility disappears.”
- “Even if I disagree, I understand what is being argued and why.”

If the reader feels persuaded without being able to reconstruct the argument, the skill has failed.

## Invocation Guidance

Use this skill when writing:
- Political analysis grounded in language
- Institutional critique
- Theoretical polemic tied to concrete events
- Essays where grammar, not belief, is the primary site of analysis

Do not use this skill for:
- Advocacy copy
- Inspirational writing
- Explainers aimed at novices
- Consensus-building texts
