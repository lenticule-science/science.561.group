## Summary
Ana Maria has recently found freedom from the home of her villainous stepmother, but--as she enters Society--she finds Society's rules and limits constraining--but she finds immense joy and a sense of freedom in wearing beautiful dresses and re-decorating the home of her cousin, Thaddeus, Duke of Hasford. Thaddeus has given her a huge dowry to ensure she can find a suitable husband, though she cannot find a prospective husband who respects and appreciates her as a person, not a dowry.

Nash, Duke of Malvern, is uninterested in finding a wife, afraid that marriage will turn him into his abusive father. When his alienated paternal grandmother insists he marry to prevent his philandering cousin from inheriting the dukedom, Nash officially enters the marriage market. He is tall and muscular--and finds fights for recreation--and scares off most of his potential brides; the others are off-put by his seeming inability to speak in words rather than grunts. The only woman who interests him and understands his grunts--but whom he cannot marry to protect her from his potential tyranny--is his childhood friend, Ana Maria.

After Nash twice finds Ana Maria under attack, he insists that he train her in self-defense. When these trainings turn into lessons in kissing--and Ana Maria expresses interest in other sexual activities--Nash struggles to keep distance between him and the woman he cares for.

## Review

#### Erasure of systemic issues
I didn't mention in the summary that Ana Maria is 28-years-old and is of Spanish descent--and I left these unmentioned because these characteristics are entirely incidental. As far as I noticed, Ana Maria's age isn't mentioned until more than 50 pages in, and it only gets brought up a few times over the novel's course. In any other sub-genre, being 28 _would_ be entirely incidental, but this is generally significant information in a historical romance, where a 28-year-old unmarried woman is often an undesirable spinster in terms of social status. However, no character ever brings up Ana Maria's age in the book, and she has an overabundance of suitors, apparently none of whom are deterred by her age. I can see why this would be a positive for many readers: the sub-genre is overrun by young heroines under the age of 20, and a 28-year-old heroine is some much-needed representation that helps this title stand out. It is pleasant to read a historical where a 28-year-old woman is a desirable, sought-after woman without any fuss over her age--it's a change from the typical historical heroine that normalizes women, rather than teens, finding love.

Ana Maria is also of "Spanish descent," per Nash's grandmother, and her golden skin sets her apart from the pale-skinned women who make up English Society. Like her age, this never gets brought up as a negative about Ana Maria--her skintone doesn't ever seem to deter suitors, despite not looking like the expected ivory-skinned woman in a historical romance set in England.

However, although I can see why this would work for many readers, the fact that her age and skin tone are entirely incidental did not sit right with me. These are characteristics that absolutely would have caused issues for her, historically speaking: her age would have kept her from having every available man pursuing her, and her skin tone, because it deviated from the skin tone that the English nobility bred for, would have also made her less desirable as a wife for many nobles. By refusing to even address the ways that Ana Maria's age and skin could have posed problems for her, Frampton has also stripped these characteristics of their weight and power.

Ana Maria's age and skin tone become meaningless, superficial nods to diversification of the romance heroine: she deviates from the genre's norm, but not in ways that make space for conversations about privilege and systemic oppression. It's as though Frampton and Avon heard the calls for romance books featuring protagonists of color--such as The Ripped Bodice's [State of Racial Diversity in Romance Publishing Report](https://www.therippedbodicela.com/state-racial-diversity-romance-publishing-report)--and decided to include the most meaningless diversity they could. Ageism and white supremacy are real forces that have had power in English and white western society for centuries, creating sites and systems of oppression that would have worked against Ana Maria as a Spanish-English 28-year-old woman entering Society after a life of poverty.

Additionally, by erasing the systems of oppression that problematize Ana Maria's age and skin tone, Frampton--a white author--ignores these real systems of oppression. When white authors write historical fiction that that romanticizes the past _without_ reconciling our nostalgia with the oppressive systems, they allow us to shed these from our collective memory. It underplays the historic role of racism as a driving force in imperialism and settler colonialism, as well as its continued existence as the foundation of the white mono-culture. White authors cannot ignore systems of racism to provide a cheery setting for a historical romance and quirky traits for a heroine, solely to create a heroine who looks distinct from other heroines while diversity-washing romance as a genre. This is a problem the romance industry has ignored for too long, and it cannot continue to slide by without note.

#### Choice
Moving beyond commentary on the presence of sneaky racism in romance, I did enjoy this novel--I just cannot, in good faith, start a review of this book in a positive light, then add commentary to the end as though it were an afterthought.

Ana Maria and Nash both struggle with choice throughout the novel. Nash has chosen, from before the novel starts, not to marry or have children because he fears turning into his abusive father. However, early in the novel, Nash accepts his grandmother's argument that he should marry, so that he can prevent his cousin from inheriting the dukedom. Nash's choice isn't based in a fear of becoming a husband and father, it's based in a fear of losing self-control. He expresses worry that marrying a woman he actually loves will turn him into a monster:
> What would it be like to want to spend time with a wife? The future he was planning for himself--that he knew was the only future he could allow--was the opposite of that. He wanted to find someone not to care for. (166)

After finally emotionally opening up to Ana Maria and to his mother and grandmother, however, he is assured that love will not cause him to lose self-control and intentionally hurt women. No matter what his father said, he does not have to take after him in that way. Nash can choose to be a faithful and kind husband and father, rather than emulating the relationship he knows so well. His father evidently said that Nash takes after him, but Nash realizes he does not have to believe this:
>A tiny glimmer of hope flickered inside him.
_You take after me. In every way._
_No, I don't. I choose not to. (309)

Nash is ultimately able to decide for himself who he will be. He can choose to continue to be good and kind, just as he has the entire book--and in doing so, he doesn't have to give up love.

Ana Maria also struggles with choice. She lived much of her life as an unpaid servant for her wicked stepmother, and now that she has entered Society, she finds that she does not actually have more choices available to her. She cannot continue to do what she knows, because it is beneath her as a lady, and yet she cannot choose another route except for marriage. Her brother and cousin speak for her, trying to buy her a husband; but Ana Maria knows that she cannot possibly find marriage to a man who is attracted to her dowry, because he would not respect that she lived beneath her current station:
> I do want those things [marriage and children]. But I can't see how, not in my current situation. I don't want a gentleman who wouldn't be proud of who I was before, and none of the gentlemen I have met, or will meet, would be anything but horrified at what I used to do. (183-184)

Ana Maria may have a choice in being allowed to pick from her suitors, but her choice will not bring her happiness. Instead, Ana Maria's delight in choice comes from her new ability to wear surround herself with sumptuous and vibrant fabrics. She wears loud and over-the-top gowns because she could not before; she re-decorates rooms to leave an imprint of herself, whereas before she was invisible in the household as a servant. Her ability eventually promises to bring her money as a decorator, providing her with a purpose that she selects for herself, rather than being forced into marriage.

For Ana Maria, pursuing Nash is a choice, too, and she revels in knowing that she got to decide the parameters of their relationship. He is a long-time friend, and she chooses to pursue his sexually:
> "Now," she said, tugging at his hand. "Now I want to kiss you." It was deliberate, not asking him to kiss her but telling him she wanted to kiss him. It was powerful to put it that way, to make it her choice rather than something he had put upon her, no matter how much she wanted it herself. (208)

She delights in her ability to make her intentions known and to decide whether she wants to kiss a man. Nash, in turn, delights in her ability--and willingness--to choose, especially to choose him:
>He wanted to hear her tell him what to do some more. He wanted it very much. Because it was her choice. (212)

At every step, Ana Maria decides for herself what she is comfortable with and explicitly communicates her boundaries and expectations. Nash loves that she has made her choice so clear, knowing that he has never over-stepped or forced himself upon her. Her desire to choose for herself balances Nash's desire to never choose for someone else, to deprive women of consent.

#### Side Remarks
This is absolutely just me being nitpicky. Toward the end, Nash and Ana Maria have sex for the first time on a bench in a garden at a party. Now, I enjoy romance with sex scenes--that isn't my issue here. The sex itself was written fine, but the clean-up description bothered me. Nash pulls out and ejaculates onto the bench--alright, fine, I'm with you. But then Nash offers to clean up, "gesturing to the bench. He picked up his cravat from the floor, then began to wipe up, stroking her thighs to clean of his spill" (322). So, does he clean up the bench or not? He specifically comes on the bench, not on her, and yet he cleans her up? Who's going to clean the bench? Right after that, someone approaches so Ana Maria takes the now come-covered cravat and holds it behind her back--what's she going to do with her hands now? She's going to need to wash up after holding a sticky piece of cloth in her hands, but she's fine to just go about when the scene ends. I need answers! Who cleans the bench? Why didn't Ana Maria need to wash her hands before going back to the party?

#### Final Thoughts
All in all, I did really enjoy this novel--this is, in my opinion, on the good side of typical historical romance getting published now. Tropes abound (the inarticulate grunting duke, rags-to-riches heroine, wicked stepmother, there's a sex deal, childhood friends-to-lovers, outsiders to Society, and so on) which I always enjoy, but the tropes here didn't just feel recycled. The tropes didn't feel like a crutch for mediocre writing and or an insubstantial story, but instead felt different enough from other romance novels to feel relatively fresh. The writing was good: not too many euphemisms for sexual organs, steamy sex scenes, well-written tension between the hero and heroine.

The backstories, for me, are where the characters fell a little flat. It felt like tragic backgrounds kept getting referenced, but only Nash actually talks about what happened to him; Ana Maria, by contrast, talks just a little bit about her background right at the start of the novel, but then we don't get more information. That may be because she doesn't have to tell Nash her story, since he already knows about it, whereas Nash _did_ have to tell Ana Maria about his father. However, as a reader, it made it feel like Ana Maria's backstory was more about situating her as an outsider to Society rather than actually crafting a well-rounded and well-developed character.

Even with its flaws, I do think this historical is a worthwhile read if you're looking for something lighthearted and fun, something with a heroine who's older than 18, or if any of the tropes are super up your alley. It's not a standout for the historical sub-genre, but it's a pleasant read.
