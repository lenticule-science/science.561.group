#+name: 2021-10-09-straight-el-dependencies
It's important to note that Git needs to be installed on whatever system is running Emacs; that's how straight.el bootstraps itself.

#+name: 2021-10-09-straight-el-bootstrapping-elisp
#+caption: The Elisp declarations that bootstrap straight.el, the Emacs package manager.
#+begin_src elisp
  (defvar straight-install-script-path
    "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/raxod502/straight.el/develop/install.el")
  
  (defvar bootstrap-version)
  (let ((bootstrap-file
	 (expand-file-name "straight/repos/straight.el/bootstrap.el" user-emacs-directory))
	(bootstrap-version 5))
    (unless (file-exists-p bootstrap-file)
      (with-current-buffer
      (url-retrieve-synchronously straight-install-script-path
       'silent 'inhibit-cookies)
	(goto-char (point-max))
	(eval-print-last-sexp)))
    (load bootstrap-file nil 'nomessage))
  
  (straight-use-package 'use-package)
#+end_src

So it sounds like I need to come up with a question-list about whether or not to track something as a log entry, starting with those questions:

#+name: 2021-10-11-log-entry-qualification-question-list

- How long does the entry take to make?
  - Does it require text-entry, or just selecting the entry and (when ready to clock-out), closing it?
- If the entry tracks hard data, how big is the margin of error on that data?
  - Will tracking it actually be useful, or is the MoE too big for seeing trends or whatever?

It's the day after our Winter Solstice here, and I'm starting the day (after a slow cup of coffee by sitting down to talk to my journal. Last night, I was thinking about my fantasy world. I've been wondering how to get back to it, to use it as a vehicle for crafting parables out of what I'm experiencing in this world. And I think... I want to "start over," in some ways.

There are a few things that define my fantasy world, but perhaps the most central is that it was a world that had magic, but does not. In a sudden event, magic, which had become the backbone for the human civilization, vanished, leaving behind monsters and terror that humanity had to unite against. That's a fun, but juvenile, perspective on how civilizations collapse.[fn:: Fair enough, I started creating Teraum when I was 7 to 11 years old.] As I've learned more from the past, and present, about how civilizations move toward collapse, I've come to appreciate the details of the thing as being /the thing/. It is /important/ to the story of collapse, that collapse is not a sudden universal switch from "progressive" to "collapsed." Even the representation of the concepts in English there, kind of hints at the missing step: how do we get from a future-state ("progressive") that's at one end of a dichotomy, to a future-state that's at the totally other end ("collapsed"), without going through any sort of collapsING present?

John Michael Greer wrote, in 2005, the essay, "How Civilizations Fall: A Theory of Catabolic Collapse," which has grown into one of the central texts of appreciating the almost-certainly-contemporary collapse of the dominant civilization. To sum up the ideas in the essay far too briefly, some human societies create such a mass of "infrastructure" for their society that the cost of maintaining the infrastructure becomes greater than the output of the society, so trends of growth reverse and instead, the society becomes focused on maintaining itself. But, they have fewer resources than are necessary, so they have to make choices about which parts of their society they let decline. This reduction increases capacity in other parts of society, temporarily, but the overall reduction in capacity means that there are fewer resources to perform maintenance with, and so the collapse continues, in an un-even lurch as the general availability of resources trends downward.

This catabolic collapse, this lurching collapse, is what I want to represent through a fictional narrative, so I can explore, more disconnected from the limits of my material reality, different facets of those circumstances and the ideas they force. But in order to do that, I have to change a key event in my fiction's history, and I'm not sure... if that's feasible, or if I should start over with a new world, a new story, and go from there.

I lean toward the latter, in part because I can use tools available like fantasy map generators to create more geographically possible environments, which seems important to be able to do if resource use is going to be such a central part of things. In the world I have, I'll admit, some of the watersheds only make sense if you suspend your disbelief for the sake of the plot. But in the fiction I want, the watersheds almost need to be realistic, because that's the only way to get the perspective I want to explore.
