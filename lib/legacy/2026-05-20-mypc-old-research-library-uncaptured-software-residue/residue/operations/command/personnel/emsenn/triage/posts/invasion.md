I participated in National Novel Writing Month in 2006, and this is an excerpt from it. I'd post all of it, but I don't have anything else beside these few pages. It is uneditted, since I don't feel like revising one part of a story from 3 years ago. Before this part of the story, you meet the characters, read about them all impersonating Franciscan monks. Some time after this, the war ends and they're left to fight off zombies. Hey, it was over 2000 words a day, I had to think of something to keep it interesting.

Afterward, life in the camp continued as it had for a while before the move. The only real change was the fact that when they wanted to go fishing in the river, they had to put their monk outfits back on. This, in turn, led to their being a lot fewer fish fries, which made PFC Smith, who was a pseudo-vegetarian, very grumpy. Not just because he no longer had any source of protein, but because he was finding it much harder to stay a pseudo-vegetarian and not a full vegetarian. (He hated vegetarians almost as much as he hated everything.) So he decided to eat meat, but not fish, as a way of balancing things out. Unfortunately, when PFC Smith finally seemed at piece with the world, someone pointed to him that he was no longer even a pseudo-vegetarian, and he shot the someone in the foot. Unfortunately, the someone was the captain, who immediately returned the favor, and had the now-limping Private (Not PFC) Smith sent back home. Johnson ran with his cot into the tent that belonged to Private (Not PFC) Smith, and danced a little happy dance. That night, the entire camp had a small fish fry as a celebration, and the captain danced a sort of foot and crutch jig, hobbling around with the beat of the music.

A few weeks later, the camp was not as relaxed nor as happy when it was when Private (Not PFC) Smith left. They had received orders that they were to invade the town, and install themselves as the new government. They would have been fine with the orders, only they didn't see any point in invading a town that had no defenses, especially when there was nothing that they were going to do in the town. Eventually, the Major decided on a battle plan. The next day, the troops walked into the town, not even carrying their guns. Most of them went straight to the bar, but the Major and the captain went to the town hall directly across the street from the bar. They asked if they could see the mayor, and were informed he was out to tea.

"Oh, I see. Well, when he comes back can you tell him that he's been invaded and thrown out of power?" the Major asked.

"Certainly, sirs," the secretary said without even blinking. She, as well as the rest of the town, had just been patiently waiting for the invasion to finally happen.

"Thank you," the Major said, and went into the mayor's office and sat down at the mayor's desk, while the captain scampered off to join his comrades. The Major took the portrait of the mayor's family that was sitting on the desk and placed it face down on the desk. After a few minutes of leaving it like that, he put it in the bottom drawer. he opened another drawer, and took out of a stack of paperwork and began work on it. (There is a universal rule that anyone whose main job is paper pushing can push papers anywhere in the universe, regardless of the type of papers.) A few minutes later, the secretary came in.

"Sir?" she asked, knocking lightly at the doorjamb.

"Yes?" the Major replied, looking up from his paperwork.

"Is this your office now?"

"Oh, you're right. Can you get a thing, uhm, a plaque for the door that says 'Major Levitz'?"

"Right away sir. But I meant, where is the mayor going to be?" the secretary said, making a note on her pad about the plaque.

"Does it matter?" the Major asked suggestively.

"Well, no sir, it wouldn't, except I need to know where he'll be in order to tell him there's an invasion."

"Don't you think he'll notice?"

The mayor came in at around 12:32 in the afternoon. He smiled at his secretary the same way he had for twenty years, and tried to enter his office.

"Miss Rienne?" the mayor asked, turning and frowning.

"Yes sir?" she said, looking up from a book.

"Is there a reason my office door is locked?"

"Oh yes, I forgot to mention it to you. That's Major, oh, hold on," she said, and looked at her notebook. That's Major Levtiz's office now. I was supposed to tell you there was an invasion while you were out.," Miss Rienne said, and went back to reading.

"I thought the soldiers were a little strange. Well, no, the soldiers were normal. The fact that they weren't dressed like monks was a little strange," the mayor commented. "Can you tell the Major that the mayor is here to see him. Or the former mayor. I'm not quite sure," the mayor (or former mayor) admitted.

"Yes sir, or former sir," the secretary said, and went into Major Levitz's office. After a few moments, she returned, telling the mayor (or former mayor) that the Major would see him now.

"Hello there, Mayor," the Major said, indicating the chair in front of his desk.

"I'd prefer to stand, Major," the mayor said.

"I see. I assume you know why I'm here?" the Major asked.

"I thought it was because it was your office?"

"Well, yes. I'm glad to see the transition will not be much of a
problem," the Major said, sounding immensely relieved.

"Sir, do you know how many times this village has been invaded?" the
mayor asked.

"Yes, it was in my report. Twelve times in the last fifty years," the
Major said, almost sadly.

"yes, that's right. I've been mayor of this town for twenty years. Not
very many of those, however, have been consecutive. Now, if you don't
mind answering, I have a question."

"What is it?" the Major asked, genuinely curious.

"Why has your country invaded my town? It has been proven, a dozen
times in half a century, that this town does not tolerate invaders.
You will be forced out, you see. It is just the way of the people.
And, after you leave, I will be mayor again. Provided you don't shoot
me first," the mayor stated, and turned to leave.

"Excuse me, sir, but why are you leaving? You asked me a question, and
now you are going before I even answer it? Well, that's fine, because
I cannot answer it. I don't know why I was told to invade this town. I
asked myself the same question this morning. I will wait for my
commanders to tell me. You, you will wait for your commanders to tell
you. That can either be your country's capital, which will have fallen
by the end of the week, or us. The choice is yours," the Major said,
looking at the mayor for a few seconds. "You can leave now."

At dusk, a dusty jeep with cracking paint rolled into the town. A fat
boy with a small helmet perched on his head went into the Major's
office, after asking around for the commanding officer.

"Sir, has this town been successfully invaded?" the boy asked
immediately upon entering the Major's office.

"Yes, earlier this morning. Why?"

"How many casualties were there?" the boy asked, writing on his
clipboard. The Major frowned at him; he didn't appreciate having his
question ignored by a private.

"None on either side."

"Do you- what? There were no casualties?" the lad asked, finally
looking up from his clipboard.

"That's right. Mind if I ask what you're here for? the major asked,
glad to have broken the private's line of questioning.

"How did you manage to invade without any casualties?"

"How can you manage to ignore the questions of someone higher in rank
than you?" the Major replied, getting annoyed.

"I'm sorry, sir. We're going around the country, seeing which towns
were not invaded."

"What do you do with the towns that weren't?" the Major asked.

"Firebomb them, of course," the boy said, turning to leave.

"Oh, of course," the Major commented to himself, and went back to
doing the mayor's paperwork while he waited for his own file cabinet
to be brought to him. After a while he asked his secretary to set up
an appointment with the mayor.

The people of the town were in a state of shock. They had known an
invasion would happen at any time, but they had expected to have to at
least fake putting up a fight. When a mass of soldiers walked into the
bar and ordered a round of beers for everyone, however, they saw that
conventional warfare was going to be useless. They were wondering how
to deal with this new, friendly enemy. They were also wondering why
they were invaded, yet again. Every invasion, there were only a few
villagers who were involved with the invading troops. The rest were
left to wonder why they had to put up with these people with guns.
Although there was usually some bloodshed, their biggest concern was
making sure that the children didn't get any of the guns that the
soldiers left lying around. This enemy was different though. They
hadn't even brought guns. The only thing that they seemed to be here
for was beer. Then the soldier with the too-small helmet came, and
they saw him go into the mayor's office. It was then that someone
noticed, looking through the window, that the mayor wasn't in his
office, it was some other fellow. They told a soldier, and were
informed that it was just the Major. That seemed good enough, so life
went on as it had been before.

The only difference in the town, at least for the first few weeks, was
that there were soldiers standing around with guns. They slept in
their own camp, and didn't bother the townsfolk much. The biggest
hassle they had was that they had to keep their homes heated during
the winter nights, which were becoming increasingly long. In fact,
most of the townspeople had less of a hassle dealing with the soldiers
now than when they had before they were officially invaded, because
they didn't have to make so much of an effort pretending that there
weren't soldiers among them.

However, another dirty jeep arrived in December. It had orders that
made life a lot harder for everyone in that town. The Major called a
town meeting, which took place in the freezing town square.

"Welcome, comrades," he began, putting unnecessary emphasis on the
second word. "As I am sure you are all aware, I received orders today
as to what the fate of this town is. It seems, unfortunately, that all
able bodied men and women are to begin work immediately on installing
a railway that connects the two nearby towns to each other." As soon
as the Major finished speaking, he returned to his office.

"Alright, get up. We march to Trenei, to begin work connecting the
rails," the captain shouted. "Move!" he shouted, and the civilians
looked at each other. They all understood what was happening, and what
had happened. They had been conned, hard and heavy, by an enemy that
was everywhere. They got up, and began the hike, surrounded on all
sides by soldiers. When they got there, Corporal Johnson tried to be
optimistic.

"Look at it this way. The harder you work now, the less you'll have to
work tomorrow." No one found it funny.

Back at the base, in their once again luxurious tent (more luxurious
than before, thanks to their close proximity to the town) Balzary and
Naylor were playing chess. They were not affected very much by the
now-official war.

"King me!" Naylor said triumphantly.

"We're playing chess, not checkers, Naylor. Didn't you hear the
narrator?" Balzary asked, and then looked very confused, wondering why
he had just said what he said.

"Oh... queen me!" Naylor said, smiling and flipping the board, sending
pieces flying everywhere. "What do you figure's going on in town?" he
said, looking at a shocked Balzary.

"I don't know. Major's probably told them by now. So if I had to
guess... one of them used a rail spike to stab Corporal Johnson, and
the stabber got shot by the stabee, and now they're all fighting with
each other, and the captain probably went to get the Major, and...
yeah that sounds about right. What do you think is happening?"

"Oh, I don't know. Anything, I guess," Naylor said, putting his feet
up on the table where the chess board once was.

Later that evening the captain came into Naylor and Balzary's tent.
"Corporal Johnson is dead," he stated solemnly, hoping that neither of
them asked questions.

"Rail spike?" Balzary asked. The captain was taken back.

"Well, yes, it was. How did you know?" he asked.

"Lucky guess. Pay up, Naylor. Say, did you shoot the poor sap that did
it?"

"Well, no, you see, it was me. It was an accident, I swear," the
captain swore, and Naylor hesitated.

"Say, captain. Let's say there was a bet that one of the townsfolk
stabbed someone with a specific tool, but the someone was stabbed with
the tool, but not by one of the townsfolk. Did the better still win?"
Naylor asked.

"Umm... I'd say pay him half the winnings, not the full amount," the
captain said. The captain, along with being a paranoid schizophrenic,
did not appreciate hypothetical situations, especially when they dealt
with his accidental homicide.

The Major did not like the accidental homicide either. It created
paperwork for him. He was upset, because he much preferred the mayor's
paperwork to his own. He contemplated hiring the mayor to do his
paperwork, but the poor fellow was working on the railroad. The Major
felt bad, but he knew that's what happens in war: people get screwed
over.

A few weeks passed in relative peace. The railway was delayed while
the Major waited for an answer to whether he should cut down a path in
the woods or go around. The villagers spent all day inside, staring
angrily out at the patrols who were tramping through the crisp January
snow. Christmas had been put on hiatus, because the village was behind
on its construction. The Major wondered how the rest of the war was
going. He had heard that the rest of the world was pretty much
conquered, that there was no real opposition left. And yet, the Major
had to take his gun everywhere he went.

It became clear why when the Major's tent was set on fire. He was in
his office, and the only thing valuable thing that was lost was the
pirate kidnapping paperwork. But it was then that the real trouble
started. Soldiers were being killed in the alleyways. Graffiti had
been painted on the walls of buildings. This was not entirely new;
there had been graffiti always, but not normally threatening "leave or
perish" messages. Bricks were thrown through windows. Civilians, in
turn, were tried and shot for treason almost daily. Work on the
railroad virtually ceased, due to the increasingly cold weather, and
the fact that the work force was being rapidly executed. But it was
hard to fight against an enemy who you needed to work for you. Several
of the soldier's murders went unaccounted for. Everyone was depressed.

"King me," Naylor said.

"Its chess, not checkers," Balzary said.

"Oh," Naylor said. It was quiet, in the sort of way that only happens
when you've just returned from burying your friends.

"Did you hear the news?" Balzary asked.
