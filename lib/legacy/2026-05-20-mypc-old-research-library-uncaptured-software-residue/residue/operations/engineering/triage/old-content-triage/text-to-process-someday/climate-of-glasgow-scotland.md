Past, Present, and Future Climate of Glasgow, Scotland:

An Observational and Modelling Synthesis

Abstract

Glasgow, a lowland city on the River Clyde in western Scotland, sits within a temperate oceanic (Köppen Cfb) climate regime, characterised by cool summers, mild winters, and frequent rainfall. Recent decades have seen Glasgow’s climate shift measurably in response to anthropogenic global warming. This paper synthesises observational records from nearby Met Office stations (Paisley, Abbotsinch, Bishopton), regional climate summaries, and UK Climate Projections 2018 (UKCP18) to examine Glasgow’s historic, current, and projected future climate.

Relative to the 1961–1990 baseline, Scotland as a whole warmed by about 1.0 °C and became ~10 % wetter in 2014–2023, with winters ~29 % wetter. In the West Scotland region (which includes the Glasgow conurbation), 1991–2020 climate normals show annual mean maximum and minimum temperatures about 0.8 °C and 0.6 °C higher than 1961–1990, a reduction of roughly 11 frost days per year, and ~10 % higher annual rainfall. Local station data for Glasgow Airport (Abbotsinch) indicate an annual mean temperature around 9.2 °C, ~1,370 mm of precipitation, and 181 wet days per year in 1991–2020, with record temperatures ranging from −19.9 °C to 31.9 °C.

UKCP18-based analyses and Scotland-wide climate summaries project continued warming, wetter winters, and drier summers. Under low-emission pathways, Scotland’s summer mean temperatures are projected to be ~1.1–1.5 °C warmer than 1981–2000 by the 2050s; under high emissions this rises to ~2.5–3.2 °C by mid‑century and ~3–5.7 °C by the 2080s. Summer rainfall is projected to decline by ~8–11 % under low emissions and by ~21–40 % under high emissions by 2080, while winter rainfall increases by ~7–11 % (low) and ~19–57 % (high) nationally. Glasgow‑specific UKCP18 “City Pack” statistics show similar patterns but with particularly strong increases in winter rainfall and marked summer drying: winter precipitation rising by up to ~50 % and summer precipitation falling by up to ~45 % by the 2080s relative to 1981–2000, depending on emissions and percentile.

High‑resolution UKCP18 analyses for the Glasgow City Region indicate that a 1‑in‑10‑year hottest summer day, about 28 °C in 1980–2010, could reach ~32 °C by the 2050s and ~34 °C by the 2070s, with the number of heatwave days per decade increasing from ~0 historically to 5–10 by the 2050s and 10–50 by the 2070s under high emissions. Together, these changes imply substantial shifts in thermal comfort, hydrological regimes, and climate‑related risks (heat stress, flooding, drought, and coastal impacts) for Glasgow’s people, infrastructure, and ecosystems.

1. Introduction

Glasgow (population roughly 600–700k in the city proper, >1 million in the wider conurbation) lies near sea level on the River Clyde in western Scotland. Its climate is strongly influenced by the North Atlantic and the North Atlantic Drift branch of the Gulf Stream, which delivers relatively mild maritime air masses year‑round. Despite a latitude comparable to Moscow, winters are much milder and summers cooler than in continental locations at similar latitudes.

Glasgow’s climate matters for several reasons:

It shapes energy demand (particularly heating vs cooling loads).

It influences the performance and maintenance of transport, drainage, and housing infrastructure.

It affects public health via cold and heat stress, air quality, and damp / mould conditions.

It governs hydrological extremes and flood risk in a city built along tidal and fluvial reaches of the Clyde.

Climate change is already altering these boundary conditions. Scotland’s most recent climate projections and risk assessments emphasise that the Glasgow City Region will face increasing extreme weather, heavier winter rainfall and reduced summer rainfall, higher average temperatures and more frequent and severe heatwaves, rising sea levels, and increased flood risk. These changes underpin Glasgow’s Climate Adaptation Strategy and Action Plan and the wider Climate Ready Clyde programme.

This paper focuses on three temporal slices:

Historic climate – especially the 1961–1990 baseline and earlier context.

Current climate – roughly the 1991–2020 norm and recent decades.

Modelled future climate – mid‑century and late‑century changes under UKCP18.

1. Data and methods 2.1 Observational datasets

The analysis relies primarily on:

Met Office station climate normals for Paisley, Abbotsinch (Glasgow Airport, ~11 km west of the city centre), and Bishopton, providing 1991–2020 mean monthly and annual temperature, precipitation, sunshine, and related variables.

Met Office areal climate statistics for the West Scotland district, which aggregates grid‑based observations to provide regional averages for different climate periods, notably 1961–1990 and 1991–2020.

Scotland‑wide time series and decadal statistics, as summarised in Scotland’s environment “Changing climate” and Adaptation Scotland’s Climate Projections for Scotland summary.

Descriptive material and climate charts from the Geography of Glasgow article, and independent climatologies (e.g., climate‑data.org, WeatherSpark) for cross‑checking the seasonal cycle.

Where station‑based and gridded values differ slightly (for example, annual rainfall totals), the discrepancy is noted as representative of spatial variability and methodological differences rather than treated as an error.

2.2 Climate projections

For future climate:

UKCP18 national probabilistic projections are taken from the Adaptation Scotland Climate Projections for Scotland summary (2018 UKCP, updated with observations to 2023), which presents changes in mean temperature and precipitation for winter and summer under low‑ and high‑emissions pathways (RCP2.6 and RCP8.5) for 2050 and 2080.

Glasgow City Pack (August 2022) provides downscaled UKCP18 25 km projections for Glasgow, summarising projected changes in seasonal temperatures, seasonal precipitation, and sea level for the 2030s, 2050s and 2080s relative to 1981–2000.

Climate Ready Clyde heatwave analysis (O’Neill & Tett 2019) uses 12 km regional UKCP18 ensembles under RCP8.5 and extreme value statistics to estimate future heatwave return levels and frequencies for the Glasgow City Region.

Together, these sources allow a multi‑scale view: from Glasgow‑nearest stations through the West Scotland region to national projections, with credible pathways from present to future.

1. Historic climate 3.1 Climatic setting and classification

Glasgow’s climate is classified as temperate oceanic (Köppen Cfb): warm‑temperate with no dry season and mild summers. Key features of this regime include:

Moderate annual temperature range – typically from low single digits in winter to high teens in summer.

Year‑round precipitation, with somewhat wetter winters and somewhat drier springs, but no true dry season.

High cloudiness and relatively low sunshine totals compared to continental interiors.

Long‑term station records from Paisley and Abbotsinch, in operation since the mid‑20th century, show that Glasgow’s winters are considerably milder than those of continental Europe at the same latitude, thanks to maritime air masses and the North Atlantic Drift. Severe cold spells do occur (e.g., late December 1995), but they are intermittent and usually short‑lived.

3.2 Historic seasonal cycle (pre‑1990 baseline)

The 1961–1990 period is widely used as a “historic baseline” in UK climate analysis. For West Scotland (which includes the Glasgow basin), Met Office gridded climate averages for 1961–1990 show:

Annual mean maximum temperature: ~10.9 °C

Annual mean minimum temperature: ~4.4 °C

Annual frost days: ~68 days

Annual rainfall: ~1,646 mm

Annual hours of sunshine: ~1,231 h

In this baseline climate:

Winters (Dec–Feb) typically had mean daily maxima around 5–6 °C and minima just below 1 °C, with frequent frost and occasional snow, particularly inland and at higher elevations.

Summers (Jun–Aug) saw mean maxima in the mid‑ to high‑teens (15–17 °C), with relatively few days exceeding 25 °C.

Rainfall was substantial and frequent, especially in autumn and winter, with moist westerly air encountering upland terrain to the north and west of the city.

These patterns are consistent with independent climatologies that estimate Glasgow’s average annual temperature around 8–9 °C and annual rainfall around 1,200–1,300 mm, depending on exact station, period, and method.

3.3 Observed trends since the late 19th century

Scotland’s climate records, extending back to 1884 for temperature and to the early 20th century for rainfall, show clear long‑term warming and changes in precipitation patterns:

Temperature: Scotland’s ten warmest years on record have all occurred since 1997. The decade 2014–2023 was ~1.02 °C warmer than the 1961–1990 average, with 2022 the warmest year on record.

Rainfall: Over the same period, annual rainfall was about 10 % higher than 1961–1990, with winters ~29 % wetter. More rainfall is now delivered in heavy downpours.

Sea level: Mean sea level around the UK has risen by ~18–19.5 cm since 1901, with an accelerated rate over the past 30 years.

UK‑wide warming: Since the 1980s, the UK as a whole has warmed at roughly 0.25 °C per decade, with hot extremes warming faster than seasonal means.

For West Scotland specifically, comparison of 1961–1990 and 1991–2020 Met Office regional averages shows:

Annual mean maximum temperature increased from 10.87 °C to 11.65 °C (+0.78 °C).

Annual mean minimum temperature increased from 4.38 °C to 5.02 °C (+0.64 °C).

Days of air frost dropped from 68.5 to 57.6 (~11 fewer frost days per year).

Annual rainfall increased from 1,645.8 mm to 1,817.6 mm (+171.8 mm, ~+10.4 %).

Days with ≥1 mm of rain increased by about 9 days per year.

Thus, the Glasgow region’s historic climate has already shifted to a warmer, somewhat wetter state with fewer frost days and more rainfall events, especially in winter.

3.4 Historic extremes

Extremes provide another lens on historic climate:

Cold extremes: At Abbotsinch, the record low in the 1951–present instrumental series is −19.9 °C, recorded in December 1995. Such values are rare and associated with northerly or easterly cold spells (e.g., “Beast from the East” type events).

Warm extremes: The highest observed temperature in the Glasgow area is around 31.9 °C, recorded both at Abbotsinch and at Bishopton (the latter in June 2018).

Recent hot summers: The warmest month on record in the Glasgow data series is July 2006, with an average high of 22.7 °C at Paisley – comparable to a “normal” summer month in many continental European cities at similar latitude, highlighting how unusual such warmth still is for Glasgow.

The State of the UK Climate reports show that, across the UK, hot extremes have increased more rapidly than mean temperatures and that frost days have declined by roughly a quarter since the 1980s. Glasgow’s record highs and the recent 2018 and 2022 heatwaves are consistent with this pattern of shifting extremes.

1. Current climate (1991–2020) 4.1 Mean conditions at Glasgow Airport (Abbotsinch)

The Met Office climate normals for Abbotsinch (1991–2020) provide a good representation of Glasgow’s present‑day climate. Key annual statistics:

Mean daily maximum temperature: 12.7 °C

Mean daily minimum temperature: 5.7 °C

Mean daily temperature: 9.2 °C

Annual precipitation: 1,370 mm

Wet days (≥1 mm): 181 days per year

Sunshine: ~1,344 hours per year (~3.7 h/day on average)

These figures align reasonably well with independent climatologies that estimate ~8.1 °C annual mean and ~1,228 mm rainfall using different periods and datasets.

4.2 Seasonal characteristics

Using the Abbotsinch 1991–2020 normals:

Winter (Dec–Feb):

Mean daily maxima around 6.7–7.4 °C, minima ~1.4–1.6 °C.

Monthly rainfall ~145–160 mm, with ~18 wet days per month.

Occasional snow and ice, but lying snow is usually short‑lived at low elevations.

Spring (Mar–May):

Gradual warming from mean maxima ~9.2 °C in March to ~15.4 °C in May.

Spring is relatively bright, with increasing sunshine (up to ~197 h in May) and somewhat lower rainfall (~72–112 mm per month).

Summer (Jun–Aug):

Mean daily maxima ~17.8 °C (June), 19.3 °C (July), 18.9 °C (August).

Mean minima ~9.4–11.2 °C.

Rainfall remains substantial (~81–107 mm per month, 12–15 wet days), often delivered in showers and frontal systems.

Sunshine peaks in late spring and early summer, with ~150–200 h per month.

Autumn (Sep–Nov):

Temperatures decline from maxima ~16.5 °C in September to ~9.3 °C in November.

Autumn is typically wet, with September–November rainfall ~110–160 mm per month, and increasing storminess.

From a lived‑experience standpoint, this climate can feel:

Often cloudy, especially in winter, with WeatherSpark analyses suggesting January is overcast or mostly cloudy about 72 % of the time.

Changeable: mild, damp “dreich” conditions are common, but spells of bright, clear weather occur in all seasons.

4.3 Current extremes and variability

Recent years have highlighted Glasgow’s exposure to emerging climate extremes:

Heatwaves: The June–July 2018 heatwave saw temperatures exceed 30 °C in parts of the Glasgow region, with Bishopton reaching 31.9 °C.

Wet winters: UK‑wide, multiple recent winters rank among the wettest on record. The 2023–24 winter half‑year (Oct–Mar) was the wettest on record for England and Wales and indicative of a general trend toward wetter winters; Scotland has also seen a greater frequency of extreme rainfall events.

Storms: Since storm naming began in 2015, Scotland has been impacted by at least 60 named storms, contributing to flooding and infrastructure disruption.

In Glasgow, these patterns translate to increased surface‑water flooding, more frequent fluvial flood alerts on the Clyde and tributaries, and growing concern about heat stress in a housing stock largely designed for cool, damp conditions.

1. Modelled future climate 5.1 Scotland‑wide projections (UKCP18 via Adaptation Scotland)

The Climate Projections for Scotland summary provides a Scotland‑wide view of future climate based on UKCP18 probabilistic projections for low (RCP2.6) and high (RCP8.5) emissions scenarios.

Temperature:

By the 2050s (2040–2059) relative to 1981–2000:

Winter mean temperatures: central estimate +0.7 °C (low) to +2.2 °C (high).

Summer mean temperatures: +1.1 °C (low) to +2.5 °C (high).

By the 2080s (2061–2080):

Winter mean: +1.2 °C (low) to +2.9 °C (high).

Summer mean: +1.5 °C (low) to +3.2 °C (high), with likely ranges under high emissions reaching up toward ~5.7 °C in the upper percentiles.

Precipitation:

Winter rainfall:

2050s: central +8 % (low) to +11 % (high), with a wide likely range (from modest decreases to ~40 % increases depending on percentile).

2080s: central +7 % (low) to +19 % (high), with upper bounds approaching +57 % under high emissions.

Summer rainfall:

2050s: central −8 % (both low and high), but with high‑emissions likely ranges reaching −35 to −37 % at the dry end.

2080s: central −11 % (low) and −21 % (high), with likely ranges under high emissions extending to −51 %.

These projections imply warmer, wetter winters and hotter, drier summers for Scotland, with increased intensity of heavy rainfall events and heightened drought risk. The summary notes that extreme droughts could become more frequent, potentially shifting from roughly once every 20 years to once every 3 years, with longer durations.

Sea level:

For central Scottish coasts (including the Clyde estuary), median projections suggest ~12–21 cm of sea level rise by 2050 and ~19–37 cm by 2080 under various emission pathways, relative to 1981–2000, with higher‑risk H++ scenarios reaching ~0.93–1.9 m by 2100.

5.3 Future heatwaves in the Glasgow City Region

The Climate Ready Clyde / University of Edinburgh study explicitly examines future heatwave extremes for the Glasgow City Region using UKCP18 RCP8.5 simulations at 12 km resolution. Heatwaves are defined as at least three consecutive days with maximum temperature above 28 °C and minimum above 15 °C.

Key findings:

The 1‑in‑10‑year hottest summer day:

Baseline (1980–2010): ~28 °C.

2050s (2046–2055): ~32 °C.

2070s (2066–2075): ~34 °C.

Heatwave day frequency (per decade) in the Glasgow City Region:

Baseline (1981–2010): roughly 0 heatwave days per decade.

2050s: 5–10 heatwave days per decade.

2070s: 10–50 heatwave days per decade.

Warm nights warm faster than daytime maxima; night‑time minima above 15 °C become more frequent and prolonged, with implications for human health and indoor overheating.

These projections underline that while Glasgow may remain cooler than southern UK cities, heatwaves that are currently rare can become relatively common by mid‑century, especially under high‑emissions trajectories.

5.4 Implications for drought and flooding

Combining Scotland‑wide projections and Glasgow‑specific statistics:

Drought risk:

Summer rainfall declines and higher temperatures increase evapotranspiration.

Climate projections suggest extreme drought events could become several times more frequent and longer‑lasting over coming decades, raising concerns for water supply, river ecology, and upland peatlands that affect the Clyde catchment.

Pluvial and fluvial flooding:

Heavier winter rainfall and more intense downpours in both winter and summer increase both riverine and surface‑water flood risk.

Glasgow city‑region risk assessments highlight elevated risks to infrastructure, housing, and the NHS estate from river, surface‑water, and coastal flooding, particularly when combined with sea‑level rise.

Coastal / estuarine flooding:

Rising mean sea level in the Clyde estuary amplifies storm surge impacts, reducing freeboard for existing defences and increasing overtopping risk, particularly for low‑lying infrastructure and regeneration areas along the river.

1. Discussion 6.1 From “cool and wet” to “warmer, wetter‑winter, drier‑summer”

Historically, Glasgow’s identity as a cool, wet, and cloudy city has been well‑earned, with around 170 days of rain per year and frequent “dreich” conditions. Observations and projections indicate that:

The city is warmer now than the 1961–1990 baseline by on the order of 0.7–1 °C.

It is wetter overall, especially in winter, despite possible local nuances in annual totals at specific stations.

Frost and snowfall are decreasing, while heavy‑rainfall events and warm extremes are increasing.

By mid‑century, under plausible emissions trajectories, Glasgow is likely to experience:

Regular summers where daytime highs frequently reach the low‑to‑mid‑20s °C.

Occasional hot spells with 30+ °C, with 1‑in‑10‑year extremes approaching or exceeding the mid‑30s °C under high emissions.

Winters with fewer frost days, more rain, and more frequent intense rainfall events.

The overall Köppen climate classification (Cfb) is unlikely to change this century, but the internal character of that oceanic climate – especially regarding extremes and seasonality – will.

6.2 Urban systems and adaptation

The Glasgow City Region Climate Risks and Adaptation evidence summary highlights priority risks, including:

Flood risks to infrastructure (energy, transport, ICT) from river, surface‑water, and coastal processes.

Overheating risks to buildings, including hospitals and housing, during heatwaves.

Risks to soils, crops, and freshwater ecosystems from temperature and water‑regime extremes.

Socio‑spatial vulnerability, with some communities more exposed and less able to adapt (e.g., deprived areas with limited access to greenspace and poorly insulated housing).

Glasgow’s building and infrastructure stock is largely optimised for keeping heat in, not out. Increased frequency of warm nights above 15 °C and days above 28–30 °C is likely to drive:

Higher indoor overheating, particularly in tenement flats, top‑floor dwellings, and modern glazed buildings without passive cooling.

Increased demand for active cooling, potentially shifting electricity demand profiles.

Heightened health risks for vulnerable populations during heatwaves.

Meanwhile, heavier rainfall and rising sea levels demand:

Upgraded drainage and sewer capacity to cope with more frequent intense downpours.

Strategic floodplain management and nature‑based solutions (e.g., wetlands, green infrastructure) to attenuate flows.

Long‑term planning for riverfront and low‑lying regeneration areas in light of future flood and erosion risk.

These concerns are central to the Glasgow City Region Climate Adaptation Strategy, which explicitly calls for a “revolution in planning” and a focus on just, equitable resilience.

6.3 Uncertainties and limits

Key uncertainties include:

Emissions trajectory: The difference between low and high emission scenarios becomes very large by the 2080s, especially for summer temperatures and rainfall. Scotland currently aims for net‑zero by 2045, but global progress will determine how close reality is to the low‑emissions pathway.

Regional circulation changes: Potential changes in North Atlantic circulation, jet‑stream behaviour, and blocking patterns could modulate the balance of warm, dry vs cool, wet episodes over western Scotland in ways that global models only partially capture.

Urban microclimates: UKCP18 grid cells are at 12–25 km resolution; they cannot fully resolve intra‑urban heat islands, local cold‑air drainage, or small‑scale convective storms. Local monitoring and urban climate modelling will remain important.

Despite these uncertainties, the direction of change is robust: warming across all seasons, wetter winters, drier summers, more intense rainfall, and rising sea levels.

1. Conclusions

Historic climate: Glasgow has long experienced a cool, cloudy, and wet maritime climate, with annual temperatures around 8–9 °C, rainfall of roughly 1,200–1,400 mm, and frequent rain days but relatively few very hot or very cold days.

Observed changes: Since the mid‑20th century, the Glasgow region has warmed by ~0.7–1 °C, with fewer frost days, more heavy rainfall events, and a modest increase in annual and winter rainfall, especially across West Scotland. Sea level around the UK has risen by nearly 20 cm since 1901.

Current climate (1991–2020): Present‑day Glasgow, as represented by Abbotsinch and nearby stations, has an annual mean temperature near 9 °C, ~1,370 mm of rainfall and ~181 wet days per year, with mild, wet winters and cool summers. Record extremes range from about −19.9 °C to 31.9 °C.

Future climate (mid‑century): Under plausible emissions, Glasgow can expect annual mean temperatures roughly 1–2.5 °C higher than 1981–2000 by the 2050s, summer temperatures increasing the most, winter rainfall ~10 % wetter, and summer rainfall ~5–20 % drier. The 1‑in‑10‑year hottest day may reach ~32 °C, and heatwave days become common enough to matter for health and infrastructure.

Future climate (late century): Under high emissions, by the 2080s Glasgow’s summers could be ~3–6 °C warmer than the 1981–2000 baseline, with summer rainfall reductions approaching 40–50 % and winter rainfall increases up to ~50 %. Sea levels along the Clyde estuary could rise by ~25–55 cm relative to late‑20th‑century levels, with much larger rises possible under high‑end ice‑sheet scenarios.

Risk and adaptation: These shifts will increase the frequency and severity of heat stress, pluvial and fluvial flooding, drought, and coastal flooding. Glasgow’s ongoing adaptation agenda—through Climate Ready Clyde, Glasgow’s Climate Adaptation Plan, and national planning frameworks—will need to integrate updated climate information, address socio‑spatial vulnerability, and reshape urban form and infrastructure for a warmer, wetter‑winter, drier‑summer future.
