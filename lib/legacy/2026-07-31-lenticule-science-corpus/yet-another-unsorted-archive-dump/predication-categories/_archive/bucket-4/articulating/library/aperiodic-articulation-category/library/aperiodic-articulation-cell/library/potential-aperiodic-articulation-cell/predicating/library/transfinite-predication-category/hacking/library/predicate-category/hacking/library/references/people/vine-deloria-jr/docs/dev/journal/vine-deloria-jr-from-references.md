Vine Deloria Jr. (1933–2005) was a Standing Rock Sioux author, theologian, attorney, and professor whose work, across more than twenty books and four decades, set the terms in which contemporary Indigenous political theory in North America argues with the settler state, with Christianity, with Western science, and with the academy. He served as Executive Director of the National Congress of American Indians (1964–1967), trained as a theologian and as a lawyer, and held faculty positions in political science, law, history, and religious studies at the University of Arizona and the University of Colorado.

## Core ideas

- **Sovereignty is treaty-grounded and unceded.** Tribes are nations whose relationship to the United States is governed by treaties; the settler state's gradual administrative reduction of those treaties to a "trust" or "ward" relation is a violation, not a redefinition. Deloria's legal-historical work in *Behind the Trail of Broken Treaties* and elsewhere is the foundational restatement, in modern argumentative form, of the nation-to-nation premise.
- **Critique of Western universalism.** *God Is Red* argues that Christianity (and the secular liberalism that inherits its universalism) is a religion of time — of historical revelation, linear progress, salvation history — while Indigenous religious traditions are religions of place — of specific land, specific kin with the nonhuman, specific obligations to specific territories. The conflict between them is not a competition between worldviews but the colonization of placed knowledge by time-knowledge.
- **The tribal standpoint against social science.** Deloria's polemics against anthropology (especially in *Custer Died for Your Sins*) and against the cosmological assumptions of evolutionary biology and geology (in *Red Earth, White Lies*) advance a methodological argument: when the discipline's premises encode the colonizer's worldview, taking the discipline's findings as neutral reproduces that worldview. The tribal standpoint is not a folk alternative; it is a theoretical position with its own evidentiary commitments.
- **Treaty as constitutional document.** For Deloria, the body of US-tribal treaties is itself a constitutional corpus that the United States is obligated to honor; legal struggle, in this view, is not assimilation into US law but the demand that US law honor what it has signed.

## Key works

- *Custer Died for Your Sins: An Indian Manifesto* (1969)
- *We Talk, You Listen: New Tribes, New Turf* (1970)
- *God Is Red: A Native View of Religion* (1973)
- *Behind the Trail of Broken Treaties: An Indian Declaration of Independence* (1974)
- *The Metaphysics of Modern Existence* (1979)
- *Red Earth, White Lies: Native Americans and the Myth of Scientific Fact* (1995)
- *Power and Place: Indian Education in America* (with Daniel Wildcat, 2001)
