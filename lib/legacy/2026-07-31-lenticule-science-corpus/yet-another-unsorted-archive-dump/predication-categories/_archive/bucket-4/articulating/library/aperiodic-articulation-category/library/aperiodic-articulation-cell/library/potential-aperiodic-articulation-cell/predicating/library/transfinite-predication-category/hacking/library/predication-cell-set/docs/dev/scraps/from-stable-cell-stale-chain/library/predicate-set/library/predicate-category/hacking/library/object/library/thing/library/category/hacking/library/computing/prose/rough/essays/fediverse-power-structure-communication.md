The Fediverse Does Not Escape Power. It Moves the Problem Somewhere More Honest.

The Fediverse is usually described as a technical alternative to corporate social media. Instead of one company owning the whole social graph, many independently run servers communicate through shared protocols. People can choose instances, instances can choose policies, and communities can refuse one another through moderation or defederation.

That description is true, but it undersells the political promise.

The deeper promise of the Fediverse is not just “social media without billionaires.” It is that a different technical structure might make oppression, marginalization, harassment, inequality, and extraction less structurally baked into online life. If power is centralized in corporate platforms, then decentralization seems to offer a way out. If opaque algorithms create domination, then open protocols seem to offer accountability. If one company controls speech, identity, and visibility, then federation seems to return control to communities.

This is a serious promise. It should not be dismissed.

But it needs to be made more precise, because there is a version of the promise that cannot be true.

The Fediverse can make power more local, more contestable, and more visible in particular places. It can give communities tools of refusal that corporate platforms often deny them. It can let people leave one server for another. It can let servers block each other. It can distribute authority across many sites rather than concentrating it in one company.

What it cannot do is make power structurally irrelevant.

To see why, we have to talk about recognition.

A social system has to decide what kinds of things it can notice. On a Fediverse server, the system can notice posts, accounts, boosts, replies, follows, blocks, reports, moderation actions, domains, server rules, and federation relationships. These are not just neutral pieces of data. They are the forms in which the system can recognize social reality.

That matters because a system can only act on what it can recognize.

If harassment is recognized as “a reportable post,” then the moderation system can act on posts. If harassment works through ambient reputation damage, whisper networks, dogwhistles, selective reply patterns, or exhausting someone over months, then the system may not have a clean object to act on. The harm may be real while also being hard to enter into the system’s available categories.

This is not unique to the Fediverse. It is a general fact about recognition systems.

Here is where cybernetics helps.

W. Ross Ashby’s law of requisite variety says that a controller can only successfully regulate a situation if it has enough variety to respond to the variety of what it is trying to regulate. A thermostat can regulate temperature because, for its purposes, the world has been simplified into a very small set of relevant differences: too cold, warm enough, too hot. But a social world has vastly more variety than that. People act for different reasons, in different contexts, through different conventions, across different histories of power.

A recognition system is a kind of controller. It does not merely observe the world; it sorts the world so that action becomes possible. A moderation system sorts events into categories such as allowed, disallowed, reported, escalated, ignored, blocked, suspended, or defederated. A platform metric sorts activity into categories such as engagement, reach, spam, abuse, popularity, or trust. A governance process sorts speech into categories such as proposal, objection, consent, violation, appeal, or complaint.

But to sort reality, the system has to compress it. It reduces an open-ended social situation into a finite set of categories.

That compression is useful. Without it, no system could act at all. But compression always leaves something out. The world is more varied than the system’s categories. So some labor, some harm, some context, and some responsibility will fail to appear inside the system as the kind of thing the system knows how to handle.

This is the cybernetic side of the argument: invisibility is not only a mistake. It is structurally produced by the act of recognition itself.

Feminist theory and feminist CSCW arrive at the same point from the other direction.

The study of “invisible work” asks why some work does not count as work, even though institutions depend on it. This includes coordination, maintenance, care, emotional labor, repair, remembering, translating, smoothing conflict, managing context, and making formal systems function in practice. These activities often disappear because they are treated as background conditions rather than as work in their own right.

Feminist labor theory then adds the political point: this disappearance is not random. The work that disappears is often the work already associated with women, racialized people, disabled people, colonized people, service workers, caregivers, moderators, maintainers, and people who are expected to absorb social friction for everyone else.

So cybernetics and feminist CSCW are making two halves of the same argument.

Cybernetics says: no recognition system can fully match the variety of the world it regulates.

Feminist CSCW says: the part that falls out of recognition is politically patterned.

Put together, the conclusion is stronger than either one alone:

every system of visibility produces invisibility, and the distribution of that invisibility is a political question.

This is the point the Fediverse has to take seriously.

A federated system does not escape the problem of invisible work. It changes where the invisible work happens.

On a corporate platform, much of the hidden work is absorbed by content moderators, policy teams, contractors, recommender systems, public relations departments, and users who must adapt themselves to opaque rules. The company hides the conditions under which speech becomes visible.

On the Fediverse, some of that work becomes more local and more socially exposed. Admins maintain servers. Moderators interpret rules. Users warn each other. Communities build shared norms. People maintain blocklists, explain histories, contextualize conflicts, and decide whether another server is safe to federate with.

That can be better. It can be much better.

But it is still work. And if the system depends on that work while treating it as informal, natural, voluntary, or merely interpersonal, then the system has not solved invisibility. It has relocated it.

This matters especially because the Fediverse often frames exit as a structural answer to domination. If your server is bad, you can move. If another server is hostile, your server can defederate. If a moderation regime fails, a community can build or join another one.

Exit is important. Refusal is important. Defederation is important. These are real powers.

But exit is not equally available to everyone. Moving servers can mean losing social context, technical confidence, archives, followers, stability, trust, and a sense of home. Running a server requires time, money, skill, and ongoing care. Participating in governance requires confidence, literacy, and enough safety to speak. The people who most need alternatives are often the people with the fewest resources to use them.

So the political question is not simply whether the protocol permits freedom.

The question is what it costs to exercise that freedom, and who pays that cost.

This is why “structurally irrelevant” is too strong a promise. Oppression does not only operate through central ownership. It also operates through defaults, categories, burdens of proof, unequal credibility, unequal technical capacity, unequal tolerance for conflict, and unequal exposure to risk.

A decentralized network can still reproduce those things. Not because decentralization is fake, but because decentralization is not the same thing as justice.

The better claim for the Fediverse is not that it makes oppression irrelevant. The better claim is that it can make the politics of recognition harder to hide.

A corporate platform often says, in effect: trust us, the system is neutral, the algorithm is proprietary, the policies are universal, the enforcement is internal, and your recourse is limited.

A healthy Fediverse community can say something else: these are our rules, these are our boundaries, these are our federation choices, these are the tradeoffs we are making, these are the people doing the work, and these are the places where our system fails.

That is not purity. It is accountability.

And accountability begins by admitting that no system can see everything.

This also changes how we should think about protocol design. A protocol is not just a way for information to move. It is a way of deciding which social acts can be performed.

A post is not just text. It can be an assertion, a joke, a threat, a promise, a refusal, an invitation, an accusation, a testimony, or an act of care. A block is not just a database event. It can be a boundary. A report is not just metadata. It can be a request for recognition. A content warning is not just a label. It can be an attempt to make reading safer. Defederation is not just a network operation. It is a collective speech-act: “we will not be in this relation.”

This is where computer science and speech-act theory meet.

In programming, a Hoare triple describes an action in terms of what must be true before it runs and what should be true after it runs. Precondition, action, postcondition.

Something similar happens in communication systems. Before a moderation report, some situation exists. The report is made. Afterward, something is supposed to be different: a harm is recognized, a moderator is obligated to consider it, a record exists, a possible action has been opened.

But the middle term is not just computation. It is an act of speech. It has force because people and systems treat it as doing something.

So web communication can be understood as a Hoare triple with an illocutionary body.

That sounds abstract, but the practical meaning is simple: every communicative action depends on conditions before it, creates conditions after it, and carries a social force while it happens.

This gives us a better way to ask design questions.

Not: can the system make all harm visible?

It cannot.

Instead: when someone performs an act inside this system, what has to be true for that act to work?

For a report to work, the harmed person may need evidence, shared language, moderator trust, time, safety, and a category the system recognizes.

For a boundary to work, the system may need blocking tools, federation controls, shared norms, and administrators willing to enforce them.

For a community rule to work, people may need to know it exists, understand how it is interpreted, and believe it will be applied without favoritism.

For a refusal to work, the person refusing may need somewhere else to go.

This way of thinking shows us the missing work. It shows us the labor required to make the system’s actions actually become true.

And that, I think, is where the Fediverse’s best promise lives.

Not in the fantasy that a better protocol can make oppression disappear.

Not in the fantasy that decentralization automatically produces justice.

Not in the fantasy that community control frees us from the politics of recognition.

The promise is more modest and more demanding: the Fediverse can give us places where the hidden work of social life becomes more discussable, more local, more revisable, and more accountable to the people affected by it.

But only if we stop treating invisible work as an implementation detail.

Moderation is not an afterthought to federation. It is part of federation.

Care is not outside the protocol. It is one of the things the protocol either supports or makes costly.

Governance is not drama. It is the work of deciding what a community can recognize.

Safety is not a feature that can be added after the network exists. It is a condition for participation in the network.

A better web is not a web where every form of power has been designed away. That is not possible. Every system that recognizes some things will fail to recognize others.

A better web is one where the failure of recognition is itself recognized.

The Fediverse does not make oppression structurally irrelevant.

It can, at its best, make the structure visible enough to argue with.
