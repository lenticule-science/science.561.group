title: "Photometry"
type: term
component-of: astronomy
---

**Photometry** is the measurement of the brightness of celestial objects, integrated over a defined wavelength range. It is the most common astronomical observation: counting photons, in a specified filter, from a specified region of the celestial sphere, over a specified integration time.

Astronomical brightness is reported as a **magnitude**: a logarithmic scale running counterintuitively (brighter = smaller number) inherited from Hipparchus's six-class naked-eye scheme and formalized by Pogson (1856) as $m_1 - m_2 = -2.5 \log_{10}(F_1/F_2)$, where $F$ is flux. The **apparent magnitude** is what a photometer at Earth measures; the **absolute magnitude** is the apparent magnitude an object would have at a standard distance of 10 parsecs and is the right quantity for comparing intrinsic luminosities.

Photometry is performed in **filters** — bandpasses with specified wavelength response — defining standardized **photometric systems** (Johnson UBV, SDSS ugriz, Gaia G, JWST F-filters). Color is the difference between magnitudes in two filters; it is the primary photometric proxy for stellar temperature and is the y-axis of the [HR diagram](theories/stellar/terms/hr-diagram.md). Variability, the time-series version of photometry, is the foundation of time-domain astronomy: variable stars, transits, microlensing events, and supernovae are all photometric discoveries.
