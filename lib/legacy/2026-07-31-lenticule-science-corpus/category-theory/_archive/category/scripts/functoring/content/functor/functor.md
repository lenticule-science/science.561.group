A functor $F : C \to D$ is a map from one [category](./category.md) to another that preserves the structure. It sends [objects](./object.md) to objects and [morphisms](./morphism.md) to morphisms. It does not break [composition](./composition.md) or identities: if $f$ and $g$ compose to $h$ in C, then $F(f)$ and $F(g)$ compose to $F(h)$ in D. If $\mathrm{id}_A$ is an [identity morphism](./identity-morphism.md), then $F(\mathrm{id}_A) = \mathrm{id}_{F(A)}$.

That is the whole definition. A functor maps things and arrows between things, and it respects how arrows chain together. A functor is to categories what a homomorphism is to groups or a continuous map is to topological spaces — a structure-preserving translation between structured worlds.

A covariant functor preserves the direction of morphisms. A contravariant functor reverses direction: if $f$ goes from A to B, its image $F(f)$ goes from $F(B)$ to $F(A)$. Contravariant functors from C to D are the same thing as covariant functors from the opposite category $C^{\mathrm{op}}$ to D. The opposite category has the same objects but every morphism is reversed.

A forgetful functor strips structure away. The functor from groups to [sets](../../sets/terms/set.md) sends each group to its underlying set and forgets the group operation. A free functor adds structure. The functor from sets to groups sends each set to the free group generated by it. These two functors are related by an adjunction — the free functor is left adjoint to the forgetful functor. Adjunctions formalize the idea that two constructions are complementary: one adds structure, the other forgets it, and they are linked by a universal property.

A presheaf on a category C is a contravariant functor from C to Set. It assigns a [set](../../sets/terms/set.md) of data to each object and a [function](../../../terms/function.md) (restriction map) to each morphism. Presheaves are the basic building blocks of sheaf theory.

Functors between two categories form their own category, called the functor category $[C, D]$. The objects are the functors. The morphisms are [natural transformations](./natural-transformation.md). This is the sense in which category theory is self-referential — categories of functors are themselves categories, and the same definitions apply at every level.
