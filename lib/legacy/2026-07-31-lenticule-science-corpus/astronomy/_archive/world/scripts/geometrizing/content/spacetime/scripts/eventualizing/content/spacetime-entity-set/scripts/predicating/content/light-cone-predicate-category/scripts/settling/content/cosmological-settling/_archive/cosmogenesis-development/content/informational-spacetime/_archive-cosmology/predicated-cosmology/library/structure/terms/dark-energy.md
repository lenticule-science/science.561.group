Dark energy is the unknown component comprising approximately 68% of the universe's total energy content, responsible for the observed [accelerating expansion](../../evolution/terms/accelerating-expansion.md) of space.

The simplest model of dark energy is the cosmological constant $\Lambda$, a term in the [Einstein field equations](../../../terms/general-relativity.md) representing a constant energy density pervading all of space.
Unlike [dark matter](./dark-matter.md), which clumps gravitationally and slows expansion, dark energy has a uniform density that does not dilute as space expands and exerts a negative pressure that drives accelerating expansion.

Einstein originally introduced $\Lambda$ in 1917 to obtain a static universe, then reportedly abandoned it after Hubble's discovery of expansion.
It was resurrected in 1998 when two independent teams (the Supernova Cosmology Project and the High-z Supernova Search Team) found that distant [Type Ia supernovae](../../observation/terms/type-ia-supernova.md) were fainter than expected -- meaning farther away -- implying that the expansion of the universe is accelerating, not decelerating.

The physical nature of dark energy is unknown.
The cosmological constant can be interpreted as the energy of the vacuum, but quantum field theory predicts a vacuum energy roughly $10^{120}$ times larger than observed -- the "cosmological constant problem," sometimes called the worst prediction in physics.
Alternative models include quintessence (a dynamical scalar field) and modifications to [general relativity](../../../terms/general-relativity.md).
Distinguishing between these possibilities is a central goal of current and planned cosmological surveys.
