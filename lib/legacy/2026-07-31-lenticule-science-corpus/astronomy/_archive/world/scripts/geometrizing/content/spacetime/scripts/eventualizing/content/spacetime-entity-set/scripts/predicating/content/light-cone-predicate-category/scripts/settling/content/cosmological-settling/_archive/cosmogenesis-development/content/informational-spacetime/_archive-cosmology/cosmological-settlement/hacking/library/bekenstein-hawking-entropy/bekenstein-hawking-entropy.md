The Bekenstein-Hawking entropy is the entropy of a [black hole](./black-hole.md), proportional to the area of its [event horizon](./event-horizon.md):

$$S_{BH} = \frac{k_B c^3 A}{4 G \hbar} = \frac{k_B A}{4 l_P^2}$$

where $A$ is the horizon area, $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $G$ is Newton's gravitational constant, $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant, $c$ is the speed of light, and $l_P = \sqrt{G\hbar/c^3}$ is the Planck length.

Jacob Bekenstein proposed in 1972-1973 that black holes must carry entropy, reasoning as follows: if a black hole had zero entropy, you could reduce the entropy of the universe by throwing high-entropy matter into it, violating the second law of thermodynamics.
Bekenstein conjectured that the entropy is proportional to the horizon area.
Hawking's calculation of [Hawking radiation](./hawking-radiation.md) in 1974 confirmed the proportionality and fixed the constant.

Two features of this formula are remarkable:

**Area, not volume.** Ordinary systems have entropy proportional to their volume.
Black hole entropy is proportional to the area of the boundary.
This observation is the origin of the [holographic principle](./holographic-principle.md).

**Enormous magnitude.** A solar-mass black hole has an entropy of roughly $10^{77} k_B$ -- vastly larger than the entropy of the star that collapsed to form it.
Black holes are the highest-entropy objects of a given size that physics allows.

The Bekenstein-Hawking formula sits at the intersection of gravity ($G$), quantum mechanics ($\hbar$), relativity ($c$), and thermodynamics ($k_B$).
Any future theory of quantum gravity must reproduce it.
