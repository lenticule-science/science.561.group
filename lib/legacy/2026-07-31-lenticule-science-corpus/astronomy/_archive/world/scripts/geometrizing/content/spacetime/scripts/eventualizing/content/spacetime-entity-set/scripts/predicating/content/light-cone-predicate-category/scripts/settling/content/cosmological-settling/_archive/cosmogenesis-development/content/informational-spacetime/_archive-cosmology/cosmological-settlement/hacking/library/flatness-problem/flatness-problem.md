The flatness problem is the puzzle that the spatial geometry of the universe is observed to be very nearly flat (within current measurement precision, $|\Omega_k| < 0.005$), even though flatness in the [Friedmann equations](./friedmann-equations.md) is an unstable equilibrium that should have driven the universe far from flatness over its 13.8-billion-year history.

The Friedmann equations describe the curvature parameter $\Omega_k = 1 - \Omega_{\mathrm{total}}$ as evolving away from zero.
If the universe at the [Big Bang](./big-bang.md) was even slightly non-flat, the deviation should have grown over time — the universe should be either dramatically open ($\Omega_k > 0$) or dramatically closed ($\Omega_k < 0$) by now, not exquisitely flat.

To get the observed flatness today, the universe at the Planck epoch must have been flat to a precision of one part in $10^{60}$.
This is not a calculation; it is a back-extrapolation from the observed near-flatness today to the required initial condition.
No principle of standard cosmology explains why the initial flatness was so finely tuned.

[Cosmic inflation](./cosmic-inflation.md) resolves the flatness problem by stretching whatever spatial curvature existed before inflation by the same factor of $\sim 10^{26}$ that resolves the [horizon problem](./horizon-problem.md).
After inflation, any pre-existing curvature is reduced to a level far below current detectability, regardless of the initial condition.
The observed near-flatness becomes a generic consequence of inflation, not a fine-tuning.

The flatness problem and the horizon problem are independent observations with independent explanations in standard pre-inflationary cosmology — both requiring fine-tuned initial conditions — but the *same* explanation in inflation.
This is the second reason inflation is taken seriously: it dissolves two distinct fine-tuning problems with one mechanism.

The flatness problem is sometimes stated as: why does the universe sit so close to the boundary between recollapse and indefinite expansion, when the Friedmann dynamics would push it strongly to one side or the other?
Inflation answers: because whatever the initial condition was, inflation flattened it.
