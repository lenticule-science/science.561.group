The Hubble parameter $H(t)$ is the rate of [cosmic expansion](./cosmic-expansion.md) at a given time, defined as the ratio of the rate of change of the [scale factor](./scale-factor.md) to the scale factor itself:

$$H(t) = \frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)}$$

Its present-day value, $H_0$, is called the Hubble constant, though it is constant only in the sense that it is evaluated at the current epoch -- the Hubble parameter changes over cosmic time.

$H_0$ sets the relationship between a galaxy's distance and its recession velocity: $v = H_0 d$.
This is Hubble's law.
The current best measurements place $H_0$ at approximately 67-73 km/s/Mpc (kilometers per second per megaparsec), meaning that for every megaparsec (about 3.26 million light-years) of distance, the recession velocity increases by 67-73 km/s.

The range in the quoted value reflects the "Hubble tension" -- a persistent disagreement between the value measured from the [cosmic microwave background](../../structure/terms/cosmic-microwave-background.md) (Planck, ~67.4 km/s/Mpc) and the value measured from local [distance ladder](../../observation/terms/cosmic-distance-ladder.md) methods using supernovae and Cepheid variables (~73 km/s/Mpc).
Whether this tension reflects systematic measurement errors or new physics is an active area of research.

The inverse of the Hubble constant, $1/H_0$, gives a rough estimate of the age of the universe (approximately 14 billion years), though the actual age depends on the full expansion history.
