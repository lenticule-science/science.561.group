Across roughly the last fifteen years, a substantial literature has developed analyzing the ways in which *algorithmic systems* and *quantitative indicators* have become *governance instruments* — instruments that classify, rank, score, allocate, exclude, and discipline at scale, often without the procedural protections that prior governance instruments carried.
This literature draws on regulatory theory, sociology of quantification, science and technology studies, critical legal studies, and the political-economic analysis of platforms.

## Algorithmic regulation: the regulatory-theory frame

Karen Yeung's *Hypernudge* paper [yeung-2017] supplied the most influential conceptual framing of algorithmic governance from regulatory theory.
Her central concept: *hypernudging* is the use of big-data analytics to *dynamically and continuously personalize choice architectures* to influence behavior at scale, in ways that go far beyond the static "nudges" of Thaler and Sunstein's behavioral-economics framework.
The hypernudge operates *through design* — it is built into the digital environment the user navigates, with the regulatory effect achieved before any explicit rule or sanction is invoked.

Her later [yeung-2018] *Algorithmic Regulation* paper supplied the systematic taxonomy: algorithmic regulatory systems differ along three axes — the *type of decision* they automate (allocational, evaluative, behavioral), the *mode of operation* (reactive vs. preemptive), and the *transparency* (open vs. opaque) — and each combination raises distinguishable normative questions about due process, accountability, and contestability.

Mireille Hildebrandt's *Smart Technologies and the End(s) of Law* [hildebrandt-2015] developed the parallel jurisprudential analysis.
Her argument: classical legal regulation operates through *general rules* applied *after the fact* to *individual cases* under conditions that permit *contestation* through reasoned argument.
Algorithmic regulation operates through *individual predictions* applied *in advance* to *categorized populations* under conditions that often *preclude* contestation because the prediction is opaque or the contestation arrives too late.
This is not regulation by other means; it is a structurally different regulatory form whose interaction with constitutional and human-rights protections is deeply unsettled.

## Governance by indicators

Sally Engle Merry, Kevin Davis, and Benedict Kingsbury's edited volume *The Quiet Power of Indicators* [merry-davis-kingsbury-2015] inaugurated the field of indicator studies for global governance.
Indicators (the Doing Business rank, the Human Development Index, the Corruption Perceptions Index, the Transparency Index, the Rule of Law Index) have become central tools of contemporary international governance — used by the World Bank, IMF, OECD, donor agencies, ratings firms, and NGOs to allocate resources, structure conditionality, and direct policy reform.

The volume's central analytical claims:

- Indicators *appear* technical but are *constructed* — every indicator embeds choices about what to measure, how to measure it, how to aggregate the measurements, and what counts as a good or bad score.
- Indicators *exert governance effects* even without formal authority — by making certain entities *visible* and *comparable*, they reorient the activity of the governed toward optimizing the indicator.
- Indicators *displace* other modes of governance — democratic deliberation, expert judgment, contextual evaluation — by offering a numerical apparently-objective alternative that sidesteps political contestation.

Merry's later *The Seductions of Quantification* [merry-2016] extended the framework into the substantive domains of human rights, gender violence, and sex trafficking, documenting how quantification both *enables* policy traction (the unmeasured is unaddressed) and *distorts* what it measures (the measurable comes to substitute for the substantive).

Wendy Espeland and Mitchell Stevens's [espeland-stevens-2008] foundational *Sociology of Quantification* paper had earlier supplied the synthetic theoretical framework: quantification is a *social process* with characteristic effects (commensuration, simplification, objectification) that the sociology of measurement should study as such.

## Platforms as governance regimes

Tarleton Gillespie's *Custodians of the Internet* [gillespie-2018] reframed platform content moderation as a governance question.
His argument: platforms (Facebook, YouTube, Twitter, Reddit) make consequential decisions every day about what speech is permissible, what users to suspend, what content to demote, what categories to police — decisions that affect billions of users without the procedural protections of public regulation, and decisions that platforms have systematically tried to obscure as merely technical or merely commercial.
The platform-as-custodian metaphor names the governance role platforms play whether or not they acknowledge it; the book's argument is that the role must be made visible and accountable.

Nicolas Suzor's *Lawless* [suzor-2019] developed the legal-theoretic complement.
His argument: digital platforms operate as *private regulators* governing major spheres of social life under terms of service that function as *constitutional documents* but lack the legitimacy and accountability mechanisms public constitutions are subject to.
The result is a *lawless* digital sphere — not in the sense of having no rules, but in the sense of having rules unaccountable to the constitutional traditions of liberal democracy.

The literature connects to the broader analysis of *platform governance* that has developed across communication studies, internet law, and political economy.

## Surveillance capitalism

Shoshana Zuboff's *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* [zuboff-2019] supplied the most influential macro-theoretic synthesis.
Her central thesis: *surveillance capitalism* is a new accumulation logic — distinct from industrial capitalism — that extracts behavioral data as raw material, computes prediction products from that data, and sells the predictions in *behavioral futures markets*.
The logic originated at Google in the early 2000s, was generalized across the major platforms, and now operates across consumer products, education, healthcare, finance, and government.
The novelty: the *commodification of human experience* itself, with the platform's most consequential output being not the service it ostensibly provides but the prediction product it sells in markets the user does not participate in.

Zuboff's analysis has been criticized as too monolithic (treating diverse platform business models as a single logic) and too pessimistic (underestimating worker, consumer, and regulatory resistance), but it has set the macro-theoretic frame that subsequent analysis works within or against.

Frank Pasquale's earlier *The Black Box Society* [pasquale-2015] had supplied the parallel analysis focused specifically on *opacity* — the ways in which algorithmic systems in finance, search, and reputation operate as black boxes whose internal decisions are unintelligible to those they affect — and proposed concrete legal interventions (qualified transparency, intermediary disclosure) for restoring accountability.

## The ordinal society

Marion Fourcade and Kieran Healy's *Seeing Like a Market* [fourcade-healy-2017] paper, and their book-length elaboration *The Ordinal Society* [fourcade-healy-2024], developed the most ambitious recent theoretical synthesis.
Their argument: contemporary digital infrastructures are reorganizing social life through *ordinal classification* — the systematic ranking of individuals on consumer credit scores, FICO scores, search rankings, social ratings, employment-screening scores, healthcare risk scores, criminal-recidivism scores.
Each score is locally rationalized as a measurement; collectively they constitute a *new social order* in which one's *position in the ordinal hierarchy* increasingly *re-stratifies* life chances — interacting with rather than displacing class, race, and other inherited statuses, and frequently amplifying their effects through ostensibly individualized scoring that imports historical patterns of disadvantage.

The *ordinal society* is not a future to be feared but a present to be analyzed: it is already operating, with substantial empirical effects on credit access, housing, employment, policing, and healthcare.
The book situates the ordinal in the longer history of statistical thinking about populations (Foucault, Hacking, Desrosières) while attending to what is genuinely novel about the contemporary scale, granularity, and consequentiality of ordinal scoring.

## Inequality, race, and the politics of algorithms

Virginia Eubanks's *Automating Inequality* [eubanks-2018] documented in ethnographic detail how algorithmic systems used in U.S. social services (Indiana welfare eligibility, Los Angeles homeless services coordinated entry, Pittsburgh child protective services) reproduce and amplify existing inequalities — disproportionately denying, surveilling, and punishing poor people.
The book's argument is not that algorithms are uniquely punitive but that they automate and scale existing punitive logics while obscuring them as technical-neutral.

Ruha Benjamin's *Race After Technology* [benjamin-2019] developed the corresponding analysis for the racialization of contemporary algorithmic systems.
Her concept of the *New Jim Code* — drawing on Michelle Alexander's *New Jim Crow* — names the assemblage of algorithmic systems (predictive policing, hiring algorithms, facial recognition, healthcare risk scoring) that systematically disadvantage Black and brown populations under the cover of technical neutrality.
The book proposes *abolitionist tools* — interventions that aim not just to debias the existing systems but to dismantle and replace them with infrastructures aligned with racial-justice commitments.

## Key conceptual distinctions

Four distinctions structure the field:

1. **Algorithmic regulation vs. classical regulation.** Predictive vs. retrospective; population-categorical vs. case-individual; opaque vs. publicly-rule-bound; pre-emptive vs. contestable.
2. **Indicators vs. judgments.** Numerical-comparable scores vs. contextual-evaluative assessments; the indicator's apparent objectivity vs. its constructed selectivity.
3. **Platform private regulation vs. public regulation.** Terms of service as private constitutional documents vs. publicly-enacted law; the legitimacy gap between the two.
4. **Ordinal vs. categorical classification.** Ranking on a continuous score vs. assignment to a class; the ordinal logic's distinctive social effects.

## What the tradition supplies

Three contributions:

1. **A vocabulary for governance-by-software.** The field has developed a substantial conceptual apparatus — hypernudge, algorithmic regulation, the 6 R's of algorithmic management, the ordinal society, surveillance capitalism — that names the distinctive features of contemporary algorithmic governance without reducing them to either pre-digital regulation or technological inevitability.
2. **An empirical record of contemporary effects.** Across welfare administration, criminal justice, finance, healthcare, employment, and platform moderation, the literature documents in case-detail what algorithmic governance actually does — not its design specifications but its lived effects on the governed.
3. **A bridge between sociology of quantification and law.** The field has connected the older sociology-of-quantification tradition (Porter, Desrosières, Espeland, Stevens) with contemporary critical legal studies and regulatory theory, generating a unified framework for analyzing how measurement and rule-making increasingly co-constitute one another in digital infrastructures.
