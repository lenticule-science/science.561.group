An acceptable gap is one the reader can fill — and is *expected* to fill — without external help. Line Edslev Andersen, in "Acceptable gaps in mathematical proofs" (*Synthese*, 2020), introduces the distinction to challenge the common assumption that any unstated step in a mathematical proof is a flaw. Her observation, drawn from interviews with practicing mathematicians, is that gaps are everywhere in published proofs and that mathematicians have detailed, shared, often tacit norms about which ones are fine.

A gap is acceptable when:

- The audience the proof is addressed to can perform the omitted step from their training.
- The step would be uninstructive to spell out — closing it would clutter the argument without illuminating anything.
- The practice's conventions license the omission (e.g., "by induction," "by a routine diagram chase," "WLOG").

A gap is not acceptable when it conceals a step the audience cannot perform, or when it conceals a step the writer themselves never checked. The latter is the dangerous case: an unacceptable gap that the writer believes to be acceptable because they have not noticed they are leaning on something they have not verified.

## Why the term travels

The same structure appears in any communicative practice with framed units. A lecturer may safely skip the algebra in front of an advanced audience; the same skip is malpractice in front of beginners. A tutorial author may safely skip "install Python" for one audience and not another. Acceptability is *audience-relative*. There is no audience-free criterion of acceptable gap. This is the principal way the framework refuses to make rigor a single absolute standard: the gap discipline of a practice is calibrated to the practice's audience.

## Distinguishing acceptable gaps from disciplinary gaps

Within mathematics, Andersen further distinguishes [disciplinary-gaps](disciplinary-gap.md) — gaps closed by appeal to specific shared apparatus that the discipline maintains — from broader acceptable gaps closed by general competence. Both are acceptable; the disciplinary case is more interesting because it points at the visible scaffolding the practice installs to license its omissions.
