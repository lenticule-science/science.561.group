Legibility debt is the cost a hard-to-read artifact transfers to everyone who later has to engage with it. The artifact's writer paid less than they could have to make the artifact readable. The unpaid cost does not disappear: it accumulates with every reader and every secondary writer (reviewer, summarizer, citing author, teacher) who has to recover what the artifact was supposed to do.

Like financial debt, legibility debt has interest. The first reader pays for working through opaque exposition. The second reader pays for working through opaque exposition *and* for failing to find a clear summary because the first reader did not write one. The third reader pays both costs *and* the cost of the field's gradual misciting of the original work. By the time the artifact is ten years old, the debt has been paid many times over, and the cost includes the false beliefs propagated by readers who paid only partial attention to a text they could not really read.

## Where the debt comes from

The debt accumulates whenever an artifact:

- Overdraws the [closure-budget](closure-budget.md) for its actual audience.
- Leaves [unaccountable closures](unaccountable-closure.md) that secondary readers must now negotiate.
- Engages in [frame-cross-dressing](frame-cross-dressing.md) that secondary readers must now untangle.
- Cites with apparatus its readers cannot reach.
- Adopts a register whose [transport](transport-loss.md) into the readers' working register has not been done.

The debt is unevenly distributed. Established practitioners pay less of it because their training closes more of the relevant gaps. Newcomers, students, practitioners of adjacent fields, and machine readers pay more. The artifact's choice not to invest in legibility is, in effect, a choice about who will be in a position to engage with it.

## What pays it down

Legibility debt is paid down by exactly the operations the framework names: licensing the artifact's gaps, marking its frames, naming its icons' work, making its sequences traversable, transporting its claims into the registers its readers actually inhabit. None of these is glamorous. All of them are work the writer can do once and many readers can save. The economics of legibility almost always favor the writer doing the work; the writer's incentive to skip it is local to the writing moment and not local to the artifact's lifetime.
