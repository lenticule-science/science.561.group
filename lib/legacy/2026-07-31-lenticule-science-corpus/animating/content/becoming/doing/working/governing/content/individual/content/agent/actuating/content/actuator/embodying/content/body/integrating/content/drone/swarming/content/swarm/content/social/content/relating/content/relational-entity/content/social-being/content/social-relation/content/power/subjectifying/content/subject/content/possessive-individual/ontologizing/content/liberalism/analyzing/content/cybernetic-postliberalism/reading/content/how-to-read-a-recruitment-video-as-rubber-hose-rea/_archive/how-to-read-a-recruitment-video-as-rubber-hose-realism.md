Military, police, and paramilitary recruitment videos are the paradigmatic site of [rubber-hose realism](../teaching/explanations/rubber-hose-realism.md) in contemporary political aesthetics.
They represent violence, danger, force, and the state's power to compel — and they do so through aesthetic choices that systematically foreclose the suffering that this power actually produces.
Reading them as rubber-hose realism is the analytical practice of identifying those choices and the political function they serve.

## What a recruitment video is recruiting for

Before analyzing the aesthetic, establish what the video is recruiting for — specifically.
A military recruitment video is not simply advertising employment; it is recruiting subjects to participate in the state's monopoly on legitimate violence.
A police recruitment video is recruiting subjects to exercise coercive force within the civilian population.
A paramilitary or private military contractor recruitment video is recruiting subjects to extend the state's violence capacity through non-state institutional forms.

Establishing this clearly is essential because rubber-hose realism's function in recruitment aesthetics is specifically to represent this participation — in violence, force, and coercive power — in a form that forecloses the suffering the participation produces.
Without the clear establishment of what is being recruited for, the aesthetic analysis cannot identify what the aesthetic is concealing.

## Step 1: Inventory the visual language

Watch the video through once without analysis, noting your affective response.
Then watch it a second time, inventorying specifically:

**Visual signifiers of capability and technology.** Weapons, vehicles, equipment, and facilities shown — and specifically how they are shot (motion blur, slow-motion, close-up on precision mechanics, wide shot showing scale).
The aesthetic treatment of capability-objects is a primary site of rubber-hose realism: the weapon is beautiful, the vehicle is powerful, the facility is vast and impressive.
Identify each aesthetic choice and the affective register it produces.

**Visual treatment of bodies.** Whose bodies appear, and how are they shown?
The recruiter's bodies (the personnel in the video) are typically shown in motion, in command, in competent performance.
Note whether any bodies in the video are represented as injured, killed, or subject to the force the video is representing.

**Visual representation of the application of force.** Is the force that the institution exercises ever shown being applied to a person?
If training exercises appear, are they shown with any representation of what training does to bodies?

**Visual language of community and belonging.** The camaraderie, the team, the shared purpose, the "family" rhetoric — note where it appears and how it is shot.

## Step 2: Identify the consequence-foreclosure operations

[Rubber-hose realism](../teaching/explanations/rubber-hose-realism.md) is specifically the aesthetic operation of consequence-foreclosure: the violence is visible, but its consequences are not.
In the recruitment video, identify specifically what is visible and what is foreclosed.

**What is visible:** - The weapons and their technical specifications - The force and precision of military or police operation - The bodies of recruits performing successfully - The institutional belonging and camaraderie

**What is foreclosed — what does not appear:** - The bodies of the people the force is applied to - The suffering of the communities where the institution operates - The psychological consequences for recruits of participating in violence - The structural conditions that produce the conflicts or crimes the institution addresses - The institutional failures, abuses, and harms the institution has produced

Map the foreclosures explicitly.
For each category of foreclosed content, identify which specific aesthetic choices are performing the foreclosure.
Is it framing (the shot simply never shows the foreclosed content)?
Is it aestheticization (the content that might produce the recognition-response is shown but in a register that forecloses the response — the explosion is shown in slow-motion beauty)?
Is it abstraction (the content is represented in technical-abstract terms that prevent affective identification)?

## Step 3: Identify the [fascist grammar](../teaching/explanations/fascist-grammar.md) moves

Recruitment videos typically embed [fascist grammar](../teaching/explanations/fascist-grammar.md) moves alongside the rubber-hose aesthetic.
Identify which of the six moves appear:

- **Crisis:** what threat or problem does the video present that the institution addresses?
- **Pure community:** what is the community of recruits defined as, and how is it presented as unified and special?
- **Corrupt enemy:** is there an enemy, and how is the enemy characterized?
  (Some recruitment videos make this explicit; others leave it implicit through the technology and training they show)
- **Suffering as sacrifice:** is the difficulty of military/police service represented as meaningful sacrifice rather than as hardship or harm?
- **Destiny:** is there a sense of the institution's historical destiny or the recruit's calling?
- **Inevitability:** is joining presented as the natural expression of who the target subject already is?

The interaction of rubber-hose aesthetics and fascist grammar is characteristic: the fascist grammar provides the political meaning-structure (why this participation matters), while the rubber-hose aesthetic forecloses the consequence-recognition that might interfere with the meaning-structure's effectiveness.

## Step 4: Identify the targeted [ontogenric](../teaching/explanations/ontogenring.md) genre

Recruitment videos target specific subjects — not all possible recruits but specific subjects whose existing self-genre the video is designed to resonate with.
Identify:

- What kind of person does the video address?
  What does it assume about who is watching?
- What self-genre does the video invite the viewer into?
  (The warrior-self, the protector-self, the professional-self, the adventurer-self)
- What structural conditions of the target demographic is the video leveraging?
  (Economic precarity that makes military pay attractive; desire for belonging that military community promises; sense of purposelessness that the institution's mission addresses)

The rubber-hose aesthetic functions differently for different target demographics: the aesthetic that forecloses consequence for a viewer already oriented toward the warrior-self genre forecloses consequence differently than it does for a viewer who has no prior orientation toward that genre.
Identifying the target and their existing genre-orientation is part of identifying how the rubber-hose operation functions in this specific artifact.

## Step 5: Write the reading

A complete rubber-hose realism reading of a recruitment video states: what is visible, what is foreclosed, how the foreclosure is aesthetically performed, what fascist grammar moves embed in the aesthetic, and what ontogenric genre the video is recruiting into.
The reading should be specific to the artifact — citing specific shots, specific representational choices, specific moments — not generic.

The reading is analytical, not moral.
It says what the aesthetic is doing, not whether the viewer should have a particular response.
The reader's political conclusions from the analysis are separate from the analysis itself.
