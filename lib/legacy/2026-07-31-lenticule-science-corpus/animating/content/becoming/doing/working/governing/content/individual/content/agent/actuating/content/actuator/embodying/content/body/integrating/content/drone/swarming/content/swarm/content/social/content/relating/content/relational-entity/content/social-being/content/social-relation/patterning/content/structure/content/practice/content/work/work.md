# work

Work is an episode of purposive doing with a defined output — activity that produces some nameable result, as distinct from mere expenditure of time or undirected activity.
It is the base concept of the practice cluster: the general category of which more specific kinds of organized activity are specializations.
Generated by: **practice** — work is what practice produces when an instance of doing is individuated by its output, the unit of result that practice generates and method governs.

Sociologically, work is one of the most contested categories in the discipline, because what counts as work is never a neutral description but a social and political achievement.
The classical sociology of work, descending from Marx and Weber and Durkheim, took industrial wage labor as its paradigm and asked how the division of labor binds or estranges, how work is disciplined and rationalized, and how occupations confer identity and status.
A long line of feminist scholarship — from the Wages for Housework movement through social-reproduction theory — challenged the boundary that paradigm drew, showing that the unwaged labor of care, household maintenance, and emotional support is *work* in every meaningful sense, and that its exclusion from the category was itself a mechanism by which that labor was appropriated for free.
To define work, then, is already to take a position on what kinds of doing deserve recognition, compensation, and protection.

It is essential to the concept that work is neutral as to its mode of organization.
The same activity — growing food, caring for a child, writing, building — can be organized extractively, as wage labor subordinated to the production of surplus-value, or reciprocally, as mutual aid, cooperative production, or the tending of a commons.
Work is not intrinsically exploitative; exploitation is a particular *social relation* into which work can be placed.
This is why the labor side and the reciprocal side of social relations share work as a common substrate: the labor-process tradition analyzes work under the wage and under capital's control, while the reciprocal tradition analyzes the same human capacity to produce results when it is pooled among equals rather than commanded from above.
Keeping work distinct from any one mode of its organization is what makes it possible to ask whether a given arrangement of work serves those who do it or those who command it.

Work also has an internal structure that organizes how it is done.
It can be characterized by how much is settled before it begins — whether the *meaning* of the intended output is already fixed and whether the *path* to producing it is already known.
Activity in which both are settled runs as routine; activity in which neither is settled is open inquiry; the intermediate cases are the familiar kinds of skilled and procedural work.
This is the same distinction practitioners draw informally between following a known recipe, exercising trained judgment within known bounds, and venturing into genuinely uncharted problems — and it matters because each kind of work calls for a different relation between the worker and the method that governs it, and a different degree of the discretion that deskilling seeks to remove.

As a node, work is the hinge between the structural and the practical in social life.
It is organized by tasks (which give it goals and boundaries), governed by method (which says how it should be done), and performed as practice (the concrete, situated doing).
It is the thing that scientific management measures, that deskilling degrades, and that the refusal-of-work withholds.
To analyze a society's work — what it counts as work, who does which kinds, under whose direction, and to whose benefit — is to analyze one of the deepest layers of its social structure.
