# co-equal-kinship

Co-equal kinship is the principle that all relations possess inherent worth without imposing identical obligations — equality of standing without symmetry of role.
Elders, children, medicine carriers, land stewards, disabled kin: each stands within the relational field with equal dignity and unequal responsibilities.
The elder is not more valuable than the child, nor the child equivalent to the elder.
Their obligations differ because their positions in the relational structure differ.
Generated by: kinship — co-equal kinship is the form of kinship that refuses hierarchy, holding that reciprocity does not require sameness.

The distinction that the concept turns on is the difference between equality and symmetry.
Liberal and mutualist frameworks tend to treat equality as symmetry: equal standing means equal obligations, equal voice, equal access, equal treatment, and wherever an asymmetry appears it is read as a defect — something to be corrected, compensated, or procedurally managed.
Cooperative reciprocity rests on exactly this assumption, that legitimate exchange runs between participants of comparable standing who give and receive in kind.
Co-equal kinship rejects the equation of the two.
Equality is about worth and standing; symmetry is about the shape of obligation; and a relation can be entirely co-equal while being deeply asymmetrical in what it asks of each party.

Kim TallBear's *Native American DNA* (2013) makes the underlying point about kinship: it is specific, contextual, and often asymmetrical, and this is its strength, not its failure.
A grandmother's relation to her grandchild is not symmetric — yet neither is it hierarchical in the dominating sense.
The grandmother carries knowledge and requires physical care; the grandchild carries futurity and requires instruction.
Obligations flow in opposite directions because the two beings are differently positioned in the relation, and that asymmetry is the structure of the bond rather than a deficiency awaiting repair.
To demand that the grandmother and grandchild owe each other identical things would be to misunderstand what a kinship relation is.

This distinction does real work under conditions of scarcity and pressure.
When resources are finite and time is short, a framework that insists on treating every participant identically can only do one of two things: distribute evenly regardless of who is most vulnerable, or freeze while it tries to construct a universally justifiable ranking of need.
Co-equal kinship permits direct allocation instead — the elder who cannot walk the hill is carried first, not because a committee has adjudicated that she outranks the others, but because the relation itself names that obligation and someone is responsible for honoring it.
Need is met through the specific tie, not through a general rule of equal shares.

Robin Wall Kimmerer's *Braiding Sweetgrass* (2013) and Leanne Betasamosake Simpson's *As We Have Always Done* (2017) both describe kinship systems in which co-equality extends past the human — to plants, waters, animals, and landforms.
Here too the obligations are not symmetric: humans do not owe the strawberries what the strawberries owe humans.
But the relations remain co-equal, each party possessing inherent standing and each generating real obligations the others are bound to honor.
Co-equal kinship is thus the reciprocal counterpart that grounded normativity needs — a way of generating binding obligation from the specific relations between specific beings, rather than from a universal rule that all participants be treated the same.
