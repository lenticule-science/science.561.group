# precarity

Generated by: grievability, precaritizing — precariousness administered along the grievable / ungrievable line.

Precarity names the condition of vulnerability — to economic deprivation, to violence, to abandonment by institutions — that is not randomly distributed but structured by race, class, gender, disability, citizenship, and other axes of power.
Some lives are made precarious by the very systems that secure others.
The word marks the difference between a vulnerability common to all embodied life and the political production of that vulnerability in particular populations: precarity is precariousness rendered unequal and made to do political work.

The concept carries two overlapping lineages.
In labor studies, precarity describes the condition of workers without stable employment, benefits, or legal protection — the growing class of contingent, gig, temporary, and informal laborers whose insecurity is a structural feature of contemporary capitalism rather than a personal failing.
Here precarity names a regime of work in which risk has been shifted off institutions and onto individuals: no contract, no pension, no floor.
In political philosophy, Judith Butler distinguishes precariousness as an ontological condition — all lives are vulnerable and dependent on others — from precarity as its political distribution, the arrangement by which some lives are recognized as grievable and protected while others are not.
The two lineages meet in the observation that economic insecurity and perceptual invisibility tend to fall on the same populations, and reinforce each other.

Butler's contribution is to tie precarity to the question of recognition.
The differential exposure to harm tracks familiar axes of power, but the mechanism is not only who has resources; it is also whose suffering is allowed to appear.
The frames through which lives are seen decide in advance whose vulnerability is addressed by institutions and whose is treated as a fact of nature requiring no response.
This is why precarity is a biopolitical concept and not merely an economic one: the governance of who is exposed to injury is continuous with the governance of who counts as a life worth sustaining.

Precarity sits at the hinge of several neighboring concepts.
It is the distributive form of precarious life — the uneven allocation of a vulnerability that is, in principle, shared by everyone.
It is administered along the line that grievability draws, so that the ungrievable are also, reliably, the precaritized.
And it overlaps in substance with the abandonment concepts — Gilmore's organized abandonment, Povinelli's economies of abandonment — and with Berlant's slow death, which name the administrative withdrawal and the slow wearing-out that produce precarity on the ground.
The political demand the concept supports is not the abolition of vulnerability, which is impossible, but the redistribution of its political form: an insistence that the structures concentrating exposure on some while shielding others are not neutral background but active arrangements that can be named, mapped, and contested.
