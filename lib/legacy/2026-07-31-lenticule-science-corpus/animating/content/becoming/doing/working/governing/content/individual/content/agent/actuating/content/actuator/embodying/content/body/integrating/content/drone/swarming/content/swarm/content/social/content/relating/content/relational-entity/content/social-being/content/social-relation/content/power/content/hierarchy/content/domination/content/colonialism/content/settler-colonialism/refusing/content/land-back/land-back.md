# land-back

Land Back is the demand for the return of Indigenous land to Indigenous peoples and nations.
It is a material demand — about jurisdiction, governance, and physical territory — not a symbolic gesture, a curriculum initiative, or a co-management arrangement in which settler sovereignty remains the default.
The contemporary #LandBack movement, prominent in Canada and the United States, gives a slogan and a campaign to a demand far older than the hashtag: Indigenous peoples have demanded the return of their lands since those lands were taken.
Generated by: refusing settler-colonialism — Land Back is the concrete content of decolonization, the specific thing that "return of land and life" comes down to in practice.

What distinguishes Land Back from the compromises settler institutions typically offer is its refusal to treat land as a commodity that can be paid for.
Land acknowledgments, conservation easements, co-management agreements, and financial settlements all share a logic: they convert the theft of land into a debt that can be discharged in currency or in words, leaving settler sovereignty intact as the framework within which the compensation is offered.
Land Back insists instead that land is a relationship, not a transaction — that the relationship between a people and a place was severed by force and must be restored, not priced.
A check, an apology, or a seat at a co-management table does not restore a severed relationship; it manages its absence.

Land Back follows directly from the structural analysis of settler-colonialism.
If settler-colonialism is an ongoing structure organized around the occupation of Indigenous land, then decolonization requires the return of that land, not merely the reform of how it is administered.
This is why the demand is, by design, incommensurable with settler property law: that law is itself the legal technology of dispossession, the instrument through which collectively held Indigenous territory was converted into privately owned settler parcels.
Land Back does not ask to be incorporated into that system as one more claimant; it asks for the system, as the organizing principle of the territory, to end.
That incommensurability is the point, not an obstacle to be negotiated away.

A point this library holds: Land Back is a demand against settler-colonial domination, but the relationship to land it would restore is not a product of colonization.
Indigenous relationships to specific places — the grounded normativity that arises from living in and with a territory — precede the settler and persist through the colonial structure that tried to break them.
Land Back returns a people to a relation that was already theirs; it does not grant something new.
Within this library it is the most concrete of the three refusals that answer settler-colonialism, giving decolonization its material substance and standing beside recognition-refusal, which rejects the settler's offer to acknowledge Indigenous existence on settler terms rather than restoring the land that existence is bound to.
