# incarceration

Incarceration is the confinement of a person in a controlled facility — prison, jail, detention center — as a legal sanction or while awaiting legal proceedings.
Generated by: incarceration is the carceral form of the sanction, the paradigmatic expression of the state's coercive power over the individual body.
More than any fine or forfeiture, it makes vivid what a sanction ultimately is: the seizure of a human being by the legal order and the subtraction of their liberty for a measured span of time.

Michel Foucault's *Discipline and Punish* (1975) traced the historical shift from punishment as spectacle — the public execution, the torture, the branding that inscribed sovereign power on the condemned body before a crowd — to punishment as discipline, the quiet, continuous regimentation of the prison.
The prison, on Foucault's account, does not merely confine; it *produces*.
It produces docile bodies through the routinized control of time, space, and movement; it produces knowledge through the classification, observation, and record-keeping that surround the inmate; and it produces categories of personhood — the "delinquent," the "offender," the "felon" — that cling to people long after any sentence ends.
The prison is thus continuous with the school, the barracks, the factory, and the clinic: a disciplinary institution that manufactures the very subjects it claims merely to correct.

Mass incarceration names the dramatic expansion of imprisonment in the United States since the 1970s, where the prison population grew from roughly three hundred thousand in 1970 to over two million by the early 2000s — the highest incarceration rate in the world.
Michelle Alexander's *The New Jim Crow* (2010) argues that this system functions as a mechanism of racial control: through the War on Drugs it targets Black men, sweeps them into the carceral system, and then marks them on release with felony records that legally restrict employment, housing, voting, and civic participation — recreating, in a formally colorblind register, the subordinated caste that earlier regimes of slavery and Jim Crow enforced openly.
Ruth Wilson Gilmore's *Golden Gulag* (2007) reframes the same expansion as a geographic and economic project, a way of absorbing surpluses of land, labour, capital, and state capacity, rather than a straightforward response to crime.

The effects of incarceration radiate far beyond the incarcerated person.
Families lose income, children lose parents, and communities lose members; and because incarceration is concentrated in specific neighborhoods — overwhelmingly poor, overwhelmingly Black and brown — it destabilizes those neighborhoods in ways that compound the very conditions that produced the incarceration in the first place.
Incarceration does not simply respond to social disorder; it manufactures it, hollowing out the informal supports and stable relationships that hold a community together and so deepening the disorder that justifies still more policing and still more imprisonment.

Within this cluster incarceration is the most consequential form the sanction takes and the sharpest instrument of social control, running on the disciplinary logic of surveillance and the panopticon.
It is the central target of the abolitionist tradition, which holds that the prison cannot be reformed into justice, and the institution that restorative and transformative approaches to harm propose to replace — making incarceration the pivot on which the deepest contemporary arguments about punishment, race, and the legitimacy of the legal order turn.
