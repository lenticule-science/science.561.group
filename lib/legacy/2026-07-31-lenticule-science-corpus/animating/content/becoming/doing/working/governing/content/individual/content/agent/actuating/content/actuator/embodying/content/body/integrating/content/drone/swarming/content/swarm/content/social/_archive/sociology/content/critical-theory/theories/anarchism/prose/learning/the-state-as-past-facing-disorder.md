Robert Paul Wolff's defense of anarchism is usually best understood as philosophical anarchism.
Its central problem is the compatibility of political authority with moral autonomy.
If a person is morally responsible for acting from their own judgment, then the state's claim to possess legitimate authority over that judgment is always suspect.
Authority asks a person to treat another will as binding because it has been authorized; autonomy requires that the person remain responsible for whether that command should be obeyed.
The contradiction is not between one bad state and one good individual.
It is between authority as such and responsibility as such.

That argument is serious, but it is not the whole of anarchism.
Political anarchism begins from a different problem.
It asks not only whether the state has a right to command, but whether the state is the kind of institution that can actually produce the order it claims to secure.

The popular objection to anarchism usually assumes that there are only two options: state or disorder.
On this reading, anarchism means the removal of the state, and the removal of the state means the absence of social order.
The example offered is often a collapsed state: Somalia, civil war, warlordism, criminality, failed institutions.
But a collapsed state is not an anarchist society.
A region saturated with colonial borders, external intervention, debt, extractive markets, militarized factions, and broken administrative infrastructure is not a clean experiment in non-state order.
It is a historical result of state formation, state violence, and state failure.

Serious anarchism is not a theory of vacancy.
It is a theory of [anarchist order](../terms/anarchist-order.md): non-state order produced through relation, repair, mutual aid, direct coordination, and shared determination.

Peter Kropotkin's *Mutual Aid* matters because it rejects the assumption that coercive competition is the only real ordering principle in nature or society.
Cooperation, reciprocity, and shared survival are not sentimental additions to political life; they are ordering forces.
Errico Malatesta makes the organizational point more directly: anarchism does not mean the absence of organization, but organization without government.
Colin Ward's practical anarchism develops the same claim from below.
Anarchism is present wherever people coordinate directly, solve problems without command, and build forms of collective life without installing a sovereign power above them.
David Graeber's anthropology extends this further: assemblies, consensus, direct action, joking relations, customary obligations, and federated practices are not marginal curiosities.
They are ordinary forms of social ordering that state theory often fails to see.

The question, then, is not whether there will be order.
The question is what kind of order, produced by whom, and responsive to what.

The state is not merely coercive.
It is metaphysically malformed.

Before an event is settled, there is a field of possible continuations.
A conflict can be repaired, escalated, ignored, ritualized, displaced, punished, transformed, or allowed to decay.
Land can be held in common, enclosed as property, treated as kin, extracted from, restored, abandoned, defended, or returned.
A person can be encountered as neighbor, guest, relative, worker, enemy, citizen, criminal, stranger, debtor, threat, or friend.
The situation is not yet exhausted by any one of these determinations.

Actualization selects a continuation.
After that selection, traces remain: memory, injury, precedent, title, obligation, debt, boundary, institution, archive.
These traces are what become available as the past.
In this sense, the future is first and the past comes after.
This is not a mystical claim about chronological time.
It is a claim about the order of determination.
Possibility precedes actuality.
Actuality leaves traces.
The past is the sediment of what has been actualized.

The state reverses this order.
It begins from stabilized traces: law, property title, border, census, office, citizenship, criminal record, contract, precedent, jurisdiction.
It then projects those prior determinations onto situations whose possibilities have not yet been exhausted.
It governs becoming by means of archives.

This is why James C.
Scott's account of legibility is central for anarchist theory.
The state makes society readable to itself by simplifying it into administrative categories.
It turns tangled, local, historical, and relational practices into maps, surnames, land titles, tax units, legal identities, police files, and standardized measures.
These simplifications are not neutral descriptions of social reality.
They are interventions into social reality.
They cut into situations, discard relations that cannot be administered, and preserve the parts that can be made governable.

The state calls this process order.
Anarchism has reason to call it disorder.

The disorder does not consist in immediate chaos.
It consists in the imposition of the already-settled onto the not-yet-settled.
A legal category arrives before the relation has finished determining itself.
A property title arrives before the land's possible relations are allowed to appear.
A police category arrives before the conflict can be repaired by those who live inside it.
A border arrives before movement, kinship, ecology, and subsistence have been allowed to determine their own forms.
The state stabilizes one possible continuation by force, then treats the forced stabilization as proof that this was the real order all along.

This is the basic metaphysical error of the state: it mistakes archived actuality for ontological priority.

Pierre Clastres is useful here because he refuses the evolutionary assumption that stateless peoples are simply peoples who have not yet developed the state.
In *Society Against the State*, the absence of a state can be an active political accomplishment: practices that prevent centralized coercive power from separating itself from the social body.
Graeber makes the same assumption harder to maintain at anthropological scale.
Statelessness is not emptiness.
It can be a form of organization against the state-form.

Indigenous anarchist theory sharpens this further.
The issue is not only whether the state commands too much, but whether state recognition itself captures political life by forcing it through categories the state can administer.
Audra Simpson's work on refusal, Glen Coulthard's critique of recognition politics, Leanne Betasamosake Simpson's account of grounded resurgence, and Indigenous Action's anti-colonial anarchism all refuse the idea that freedom is achieved when the state finally recognizes the right identity, nation, claim, or injury.
Recognition can be a technique of settlement.
It can take living political relation and make it appear as a case within the state's archive.

This is why the question of violence has to be reframed.
The state does not abolish violence.
It classifies violence.
It legitimizes some violence as policing, war, imprisonment, border enforcement, debt collection, eviction, and jurisdiction.
It criminalizes other violence as disorder.
It hides still other violence in procedure: paperwork, eligibility rules, waiting periods, permits, court dates, administrative denials.
The result is not peace.
It is a managed distribution of force.

Anarchists do not have to deny that non-state life can contain violence, domination, abuse, revenge, coercion, or collapse.
It can.
The serious claim is not that removing the state removes conflict.
The claim is that centralized authority is not a neutral solution to conflict.
It often preserves the conditions that make violence durable, while depriving people of the means to respond directly to the relations in which violence occurs.

Against this, anarchist practice points toward direct accountability, mutual defense, restorative and transformative justice, federation, voluntary association, customary obligation, affinity, land-based resurgence, and forms of coordination where those affected by a situation retain the power to determine its continuation.
None of these are magic.
All can fail.
But their failure is not the same as the state's failure.
Their failure remains within the field of relation that must be repaired.
The state's failure is different: it removes determination from that field, archives one imposed continuation, and then returns as if the archive itself were order.

The strongest version of anarchism is therefore not the claim that people are naturally good and the state merely corrupts them.
Nor is it the claim that abolishing the state would produce an empty space in which harmony spontaneously appears.
The stronger claim is that human beings already produce order through relation, habit, memory, care, conflict, kinship, custom, subsistence, defense, and direct coordination.
The state captures some of that activity, freezes it into administrative forms, monopolizes legitimate force, and then claims to be the source of order itself.

Anarchism denies that claim.
Order does not begin with command.
Order begins in the open field of possible relations, before those relations have been reduced to files, borders, titles, contracts, crimes, and cases.
The state is past-facing disorder because it governs that open field from the archive of prior domination.
Political anarchism is the attempt to keep social determination where it actually occurs: in the living relations through which people meet, conflict, repair, defend, and continue one another without handing the power of continuation to an institution that arrives late and calls its lateness law.
