The position that a mathematical proof is rigorous if and only if it can be expanded into a sequence of formal logical steps in some axiomatic system has long been the default account of rigor in mathematics. It has the appeal of being precise: there is, on this view, an unambiguous test for whether a proof is rigorous, namely whether the formal expansion exists. The position is influential enough that "rigorous" and "formalizable" are often used interchangeably in mathematical exposition.

The position has been substantively criticized, principally by Yacin Hamami, Jeremy Avigad, and others working in the philosophy of mathematical practice. The critique is not that formalization is bad. It is that formalization is one register among several that the actual practice of mathematics uses, and that the criterion misidentifies which register is doing the rigor work in any given proof.

## What the critique establishes

Hamami's "Mathematical Rigor and Proof" (*Review of Symbolic Logic*, 2022) develops a notion of rigor in which a proof is rigorous when its constituent steps are *high-level inferences* — patterns of mathematical reasoning the practice has institutionally accredited — and the reader can recover the *plan* of the argument. The formal expansion exists in principle, but no working mathematician produces it; what they actually do is reason at the level of plans and high-level patterns, and the rigor lives there.

Avigad ("Reliability of mathematical inference," *Synthese*, 2020) makes a parallel observation about reliability: the reasons we trust a mathematical inference are practical and institutional (peer review, replication, applicability, integration with surrounding theory), not the existence of a formal expansion that nobody has actually produced. Macbeth's account in *Realizing Reason* (Oxford, 2014) of mathematical writing as the accumulation of conceptual content, not just chains of derivation, points in the same direction.

## Why this term names a defect

When a community treats formal correctness as the sole criterion of rigor, three things follow. First, all the registers a proof actually uses — diagrams, examples, narrative explanations, citations, computational checks — get demoted to "informal" status, even when they are doing the load-bearing work. Second, the actual cognitive work of proof construction (recognizing which apparatus to apply, recognizing what the structure of the situation is) becomes invisible because none of it shows up in the formal expansion. Third, communication of the proof to anyone who would benefit from it becomes harder, because the registers most readers can engage with have been ruled out as not really part of the proof.

The framework's positive criterion is [medium-specific-rigor](teaching/explanations/medium-specific-rigor.md): each register held to the rigor proper to it, with formal correctness one rigor among several, valuable in its own register and transported into others with honesty about what is and is not preserved.
