Audre Lorde (1934–1992) described herself, in the line her work made famous, as "a Black, lesbian, mother, warrior, poet."
The compound was the analysis: each term carries its own history of suppression, and refusing to suppress any of them in order to fit into a single movement's frame was, for Lorde, the precondition of any politics worth having.
Lorde was a poet first — eight collections — and a theorist of feminist political life whose essays did the work that the U.S. women's movement of the 1970s and 1980s could not do without.

## What this subdomain takes from her

- **The master's tools will never dismantle the master's house.** From the 1979 talk delivered at NYU's Second Sex Conference and collected in *Sister Outsider*: "What does it mean when the tools of a racist patriarchy are used to examine the fruits of that same patriarchy?
  It means that only the most narrow perimeters of change are possible and allowable."
  The argument is not that the inherited categories of feminist theory should be abandoned but that they cannot, on their own, do the analytical work the tradition needs — and that new categories, born of the experience the inherited categories make invisible, have to be developed.
  The line is one of the most cited in the tradition; it is also one of the most flattened.
  Lorde's full point requires the second half: difference is not a deficit to be transcended but the analytical resource from which adequate theory has to be built.
- **The erotic as power.** From "Uses of the Erotic: The Erotic as Power" (1978): the erotic is not pornography (which is its perversion) but the deep capacity for joy, satisfaction, and full living that capitalist work-discipline systematically suppresses.
  To live in contact with the erotic is to refuse the diminished forms of life that capitalist society offers and to demand more — in work, in love, in politics.
  The essay is one of the founding texts of feminist analysis of affect and labor; it is upstream of the affect-theory and care-ethics literatures that follow.
- **The uses of anger.** From "The Uses of Anger: Women Responding to Racism" (1981): anger between women, expressed accurately and listened to, is "an information and an energy" that can be turned into transformation.
  The essay is a direct intervention in the white-feminist managerial response to Black women's anger as disruptive; Lorde reframes the anger as analytical content the movement cannot do without.
- **The transformation of silence into language and action.** From the 1977 talk of that title: silence does not protect; the things one is most afraid to say are the things that, once said, make survival rather than acquiescence possible.
  The essay is foundational for feminist analysis of voice, witness, and the political work of speaking from one's own conditions.
- **Difference as creative function.** Across Lorde's essays: difference is not a problem to be managed but the fact from which any adequate coalitional politics has to start.
  "It is not our differences that divide us.
  It is our inability to recognize, accept, and celebrate those differences."
  The framing is the ground for the subdomain's refusal of the universal-feminist-subject move and is the upstream source for much of the coalition-political theory that follows.

## Key works

- *The First Cities* (1968) — first poetry collection.
- *Coal* (1976) — poetry; the breakthrough collection.
- *The Black Unicorn* (1978) — poetry.
- *The Cancer Journals* (1980) — memoir and analysis of breast cancer, mastectomy, and the politics of illness.
- *Zami: A New Spelling of My Name* (1982) — biomythography.
- *Sister Outsider: Essays and Speeches* (1984) — the central theoretical essays.
  Reference note: [`lorde1984`](../../../../../../references/lorde1984.md).
- *A Burst of Light* (1988) — essays.
