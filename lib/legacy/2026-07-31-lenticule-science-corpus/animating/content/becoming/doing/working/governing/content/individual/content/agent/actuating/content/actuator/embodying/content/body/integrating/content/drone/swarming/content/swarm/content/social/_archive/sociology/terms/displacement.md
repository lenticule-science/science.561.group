Displacement is the forced removal of a people from the place that sustains their way of life.
It is not merely geographic relocation — moving bodies from one location to another — but the severing of the relationships between people, land, language, and practice through which [culture](./culture.md) reproduces itself.
A people displaced from their territory lose not just land but the material basis for the ceremonies, subsistence practices, kinship networks, and [collective memory](./collective-memory.md) that constitute them as a people.

The concept encompasses a range of mechanisms: military conquest and removal (the Trail of Tears, the Kurdish village destructions of the 1980s–90s), legal dispossession (the Dawes Act converting communal Indigenous land into alienable individual plots), economic displacement (industrialization driving rural populations into cities), and administrative displacement (the Turkish state destroying 3,000 Kurdish villages during the 1990s counterinsurgency, forcing millions into urban areas where Kurdish language, culture, and social structure could not sustain themselves).

Edward Said (1984) distinguished exile from other forms of displacement: exile is the condition of being cut off from one's roots, from one's land, from one's past.
It is "the unhealable rift forced between a human being and a native place."
But Said also noted that exile can produce a double perspective — the ability to see both the homeland and the place of refuge with critical distance, belonging fully to neither.

Displacement intersects with [genring](theories/cybernetic-postliberalism/terms/genring.md) in two ways.
First, displacement produces the conditions under which genre figures stabilize.
A people displaced from their land cannot contradict the genre figure assigned to them by continuing to live visibly in the way the genre denies.
The genre stabilizes because the people are no longer there to interrupt it.
Second, displacement produces [diaspora](./diaspora.md) — communities that maintain identity across distance, developing new forms of cultural life that no genre figure can contain.
