Genring is the reduction of a living people to a fixed literary type.
It is not a metaphor for stereotyping but a specific political operation with three steps: **selection** (extracting visually striking, emotionally resonant features from a people's life — dress, diet, habitat, ritual), **codification** (assembling those features into a active character template and circulating it through novels, performances, images, and festivals until it becomes self-reinforcing), and **substitution** (the template replaces the people, so that encountering the people means encountering the character).

The output of genring is a **genre figure**: a character template complete with costume, setting, emotional register, and narrative arc.
The Noble Indian, the Hearty Bavarian, the Mountain Kurd, the salt-of-the-earth rural American — each is a genre figure produced by the same operation, serving the same function: guaranteeing that authentic folk life exists somewhere, so that the nation can claim it.

Genring differs from stereotyping in precision and consequence.
A stereotype is a lazy generalization that dissolves on contact with reality.
Genring is precise: it produces a specific kind of knowledge — knowledge of the character — that functions *as* knowledge of the people.
The false equation is what makes genring dangerous.
It provides the feeling of understanding while destroying the capacity to encounter the actual person standing in front of you.
When a Lakota mathematician, a Bavarian socialist, or a Kurdish filmmaker appears, they are not censored — they are illegible.
The genre has no character for them.

What genring destroys is [heteroglossia](../../../../../../science/theories/linguistics/theories/semiotics/terms/heteroglossia.md) — the coexistence of multiple voices, worldviews, and ways of being within any living community.
It collapses multi-voicedness into monologue, replacing internal debate, generational conflict, and political complexity with a single authorized character.
It also eliminates the [temporal middle](./temporal-middle.md) — the position of inheriting, continuing, and transforming a tradition — because genre figures are defined by stasis.
They are "unchanged since ancient times" or "traditional since time immemorial."
There is no genre position for someone who is doing what every living person does: continuing.

Genring is a form of what James C.
Scott (1998) calls [legibility](../../critical-theory/theories/abolition/theories/state-repression/terms/legibility.md) production — the simplification of complex social reality into categories a state can administer.
But genring is legibility with a narrative engine.
The simplified image is not merely a category but a character inhabiting a story, and the story generates obligations: to protect the character, to maintain the genre, to exclude whatever threatens its [coherence](./genre-coherence.md).
