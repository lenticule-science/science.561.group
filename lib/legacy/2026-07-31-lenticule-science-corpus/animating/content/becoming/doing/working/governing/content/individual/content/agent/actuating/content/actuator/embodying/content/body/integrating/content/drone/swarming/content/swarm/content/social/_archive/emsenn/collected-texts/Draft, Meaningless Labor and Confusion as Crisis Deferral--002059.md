You work on a product that doesn't need to exist.
Or you write copy for something that sounds like it might save a little bit of time for someone somewhere.
Or you manage people doing either of those things.

You're not a decision-maker.
But you’re also not someone who can afford to say no to decisions.
You're structurally adjacent—middle tier in a high-tier system.
Which is why you’re here, scrolling.
Trying to figure out what the hell is going on.
Trying to understand the system while also participating in it.
Trying to stay informed enough to be good, but not so overwhelmed that you have to stop.

This isn’t a moral failing.
It’s a **class position**.
And it was built—materially, energetically, ideologically—to keep you confused.

You aren’t confused because you're uninformed.

You're confused because you’ve been trained to treat _understanding_ as your role in the system.

You were raised—educationally, professionally, algorithmically—into a structure that positioned curiosity as virtue.
Not because it leads to action, but because it keeps you **occupied**.
Keeps you seeking.
Keeps you _good_.

This wasn’t a trick played just on you.
It’s a structural feature of white-collar life under late surplus capitalism: you're taught that your job is to interpret the world, not intervene in it.
Your labor is soft analysis—framed in ethics, optimized in OKRs, buffered by platforms that call scrolling “engagement” and not what it really is: **metabolizing contradiction until you forget you’re doing it**.

You're not the only one doing this.
But you are in a shrinking group of people **paid** to keep doing it—whether that’s inside a startup’s design team or a university’s policy center.
It’s the same work, distributed differently: to interpret, not to disrupt.
To generate clarity that never threatens the system clarity is supposed to describe.

And the weird part?
It used to work.

Back when the system had enough surplus to fund slow planning.
Back when there were buffers between idea and impact, between ideology and resource, between _what you thought_ and _what it cost to think it_.

Understanding used to feel productive because **it was subsidized**.

Now that subsidy’s collapsing.
And so is your clarity.

There’s a number you’ve probably seen floating around: **EROEI**.
Energy Returned on Energy Invested.
It tells you how much usable energy you get out of a system compared to what it takes to run it.

When oil first got tapped in Pennsylvania, the EROEI was over 100:1.
You could burn one barrel’s worth of effort and get a hundred back.
That’s not profit—that’s _surplus_.
And surplus doesn’t just mean more stuff.
It means more **slack**.
More distance between input and outcome.
More layers between cause and effect.

That’s what built industrial modernity: railroads, refrigerated logistics, fertilizer-based agriculture, transoceanic war, cloud computing.
But it also built the thing you live in every day: the ability to think professionally.
To design systems, draft roadmaps, run scenarios, “stay up to date.” None of that is spiritually innate.
It’s energetically supported.

> **High EROEI didn’t just make things move.
> It made thought scalable.**

It created a class of workers whose job was to interpret.
Product designers, researchers, academics, media strategists, policy analysts—people whose labor wasn’t directly productive, but _made other people’s labor legible_.
That’s not a criticism.
That’s what surplus is _for_.
It funds meaning.

And the logic held, for a while: If there’s enough slack in the system, you can afford a few layers of modeling.
You can predict instead of react.
You can plan.
You can think.

You were trained in that logic.
That was the deal.

You ask good questions.
You try to understand.
You try to stay ahead of the curve.
But no one told you the curve was drawn by **energetic capacity**, not moral commitment.

The system you were taught to understand was never just intellectual.
It was an artifact of high-return energy—and it’s eroding underneath you.

The surplus is shrinking.
That’s not a metaphor.
It’s thermodynamics.

Oil doesn’t gush anymore.
The cheap stuff’s gone.
What’s left is harder to find, harder to process, harder to distribute.
Deepwater rigs.
Tar sands.
Shale plays.
Solar and wind are real, but they require enormous up-front infrastructure—grids, batteries, rare earth metals—and we haven’t built the industrial system that builds the industrial system.

The global energy return is dropping.
The average EROEI that once propped up this world?
No longer double digits in many cases.
Some “solutions” like corn ethanol hover near 1:1 when you count the real costs.
That’s not energy.
That’s **drag**.

And drag changes everything.

Because all those interpretive systems—academia, journalism, policy, UX, ethics-in-tech workshops—were built with the assumption that there’d always be enough margin to think abstractly.
To coordinate slowly.
To revise.

But surplus is what made **complexity cheap**.

And now it’s expensive again.

> When the energy contracts, so does cognition.

Not because people got lazy or distracted.
Because the physical conditions that supported complex, buffered, distributed thinking have degraded.
The brain still runs on glucose.
But _infrastructure_ supports the ability to plan, to doubt, to model, to compare futures.

Without that infrastructure, cognition doesn’t stop.
It just bends.
It gets tighter.
Faster.
Shallower.
More certainty, less ambiguity.
More reaction, less consideration.
You’ve felt this.
It’s not burnout.
It’s **cognitive infrastructure collapse**.

Your job used to be to interpret complexity.
Now you're asked to _do that same job_—faster, with fewer supports, in systems that are running out of slack.

And you still think you’re failing.
Like if you just focused a little harder, you'd get your clarity back.

But clarity was never just mental.
It was structural.
And the structure is breaking.

Here’s what happened:

As energetic surplus declined, the systems that once handled abstraction professionally—academia, media, entertainment, policy—started to offload the cost of thinking onto everyone else.

Studios used to fund writers' rooms.
Now they A/B test trailer reactions on TikTok.

Think tanks used to release long PDFs with footnotes.
Now they track engagement on threads and call it “real-time sentiment.”

Newsrooms used to investigate.
Now they “curate the discourse.”

> This wasn’t innovation.
> It was **triage**.

When energy was cheap, you could centralize abstraction.
You could pay people to think slowly, at scale, with buffers between their models and the real world.
You could fund _sense-making_.

But those systems had an overhead.
They depended on slack: time, energy, institutional coherence.

So when the surplus started slipping, the model changed.
Not by decision, but by necessity.

The labor didn’t disappear—it got **distributed**.

Now the same cognitive work is done by unpaid volunteers: doomscrollers, mutuals, knowledge workers between meetings.
A thousand browser tabs trying to metabolize complexity in real time. _Not_ because they’re failing to focus—but because the system handed them the bill.

> **Cognition got crowd-sourced.
> Not to empower, but to offload.**

What used to be surplus-supported institutional analysis is now just... your timeline.
Your DMs.
Your side conversations.
Your overwhelmed attempts to "stay informed" while producing takes, while keeping your job, while staying coherent.

This is what a low-informational-EROEI environment looks like: You burn your own attention trying to generate clarity that used to be externally produced.
And the system skims the product—engagement, discourse, trend velocity—and calls it democratization.

But nothing was democratized.
The cognitive load was just **outsourced**.

Here’s the part the system won’t say out loud:

Once surplus dried up and sense-making got crowd-sourced, **the people most confused became the ones most likely to demand clarity from others**.

Not as a request.
As a condition of belief.

Because if your entire class position is built on your ability to understand, to stay informed, to interpret events and “remain critical”—then any knowledge that resists you becomes a threat.
Not just to your worldview, but to your self-image.

So you ask for help.
You ask for patience.
You ask for one more explainer, one more thread, one more reading list.
You say, “I’m trying to understand,” and you mean it.

But trying to understand isn’t neutral.
Not when **you’re demanding clarity from people who were never confused**.

Indigenous thinkers.
Black feminists.
Disabled organizers.
Land defenders.
Survivors.
People whose knowledge systems were never built on surplus in the first place.
People who live in clarity because they’ve had to.
Because the system never offered them abstraction.
Only consequence.

> And now they’re asked to translate that clarity for you.
> To package it.
> Render it.

That’s not learning.
That’s **epistemic extraction**.

It’s the same logic as resource extraction: take what you need from where it’s active, move it to where things are collapsing, and call it growth.
Only now it’s clarity instead of coal.

> You scroll until you hit a thought you can’t immediately process, and instead of sitting with that rupture, you ask it to **become legible to your confusion.**

Not out of cruelty.
But out of reflex.
Because the system taught you that interpretation was your job.
And if something resists interpretation, it must be missing something.

This is how confusion becomes an instrument of control.
It doesn’t silence the people with clarity.
It just **demands they explain themselves until they’re too tired to resist.**

At some point, the ideology was supposed to give out.

Not all at once.
But gradually—when the growth slowed, when the energy costs rose, when the abstraction machines failed to deliver anything real.
That should’ve been the moment when liberal universalism, global development, infinite growth, transhumanist techno-optimism—**all the high-EROEI ideologies**—started to recede.

Mood boards.
Postures.
Frameworks used to **signal complexity**, not to engage with it.

> The content of the idea was no longer functional, but its **format** still performed value.

And when the surplus disappears, the ideology has to keep moving, even if it no longer knows where it’s going.

So it rides confusion.

> Because if you stay confused, you never have to admit the model stopped working.

This is the last function of the interpretive class: To **preserve the interface** of understanding long after the content stops delivering.

Because to let go of that would mean admitting: The system trained you to think—but not to stop thinking when the thinking turned empty.

Not everything breaks when the buffers go.

Systems that don’t depend on abstraction to function—because they’re embedded in the land itself, in the relationships between people, species, water, and time.

You see it in feminist economies of care: Work that’s not optimized for output, but for survival.
Not "efficient" by design—but highly effective in contexts of low resource and high interdependence.
This is cognition that works **when surplus isn’t available**, because it never assumed it would be.

These systems:

- Don’t demand universalism

They metabolize constraint.
Not because they’re better.
Because they’re **real**.

> **They were designed to produce results under pressure.**

Because they were never dependent on massive energy flows, or on thousand-mile supply chains, or on cognitive labor funded by empire.

In interruption.
In precarity.

That’s not romantic.

The only reason they look “hard to understand” from where you’re standing is because **you’re still looking through the lens of systems that were subsidized to make sense.**

You’ve been taught to treat confusion as a temporary condition.
A fog you pass through.

If you just gather the right frameworks, find the right thread, listen to the right voice—it’ll all clarify.

The real question was never “What’s happening?” It’s: **What ways of thinking still function when the margin disappears?** When the cognitive infrastructure that upheld abstraction fails?
When the interpreter can’t buffer the contradiction anymore?

The answer’s not going to be found in higher resolution.

Because the interpreter class—the one trained to ask the good questions, write the hot takes, curate the epistemic moodboard—is not being asked to understand the collapse.
