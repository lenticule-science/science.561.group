# adult-supremacy

Adult-supremacy is the political arrangement in which adults hold power over children and young people and in which that power is treated as natural, inevitable, and justified.
The term draws a deliberate parallel to white supremacy and male supremacy: it names not individual prejudice but a system — a set of interlocking institutions, norms, and ideologies that concentrate political, economic, and social power in the hands of adults and exclude the young from decisions that affect their lives.
Generated by: age-hierarchy — adult-supremacy is the hegemonic form, the whole structure of rule that adultism enforces and childism legitimizes.

The *NO!
Against Adult Supremacy* zine series, circulated through anarchist networks, has been a primary venue for developing the concept.
Its contributors argue that adult-supremacy is not a metaphorical extension of other forms of domination but a foundational one: the first hierarchy most people ever encounter is that of adult over child, and it trains them to accept hierarchical social relations as normal.
From this angle the family — as an institution in which adults exercise near-total authority over children — functions as the primary site where obedience to authority is learned and naturalized, the schoolroom of every later submission.

Adult-supremacy operates through several reinforcing mechanisms: legal exclusion (children cannot vote, contract, or choose their own guardians), economic dependence (children are denied the right to work while their domestic and educational labor goes uncompensated), epistemic dismissal (children's testimony, preferences, and judgments are treated as less credible than adults'), and physical coercion (corporal punishment remains legal in many jurisdictions, and children have fewer protections against confinement than adults).
Each of these presents itself as protection, which is part of how the arrangement secures consent even from those it subordinates.

The concept sits among its three siblings under age-hierarchy: adultism (the discriminatory practices through which adult power operates), misopedy (the ideological antipathy toward children and childhood), and childism (the prejudice against children as a group).
Where adultism names the practice and childism names the prejudice, adult-supremacy names the political system — the arrangement of power itself.
Toby Rollo's work connects adult-supremacy to settler-colonialism: if the colonial relationship is structured by the logic of adult-supremacy — the assumption that the mature, rational, civilized subject is entitled to govern those deemed immature — then adult-supremacy is not one oppression among others but a template for domination as such, the model from which the colonizer's claim to govern the colonized is drawn.
