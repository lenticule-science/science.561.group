# settler-colonialism

Settler-colonialism is the form of colonialism organized around the replacement of Indigenous peoples with a settler society on Indigenous land.
It differs from extraction or exploitation colonialism, which takes labor and resources while keeping the colonized population in place as a workforce ruled from a distance.
Settler-colonialism instead seeks to eliminate the Indigenous population — through killing, removal, assimilation, or legal erasure — and to replace it with a new, permanent settler society that claims the land as its own.
Patrick Wolfe's compressed formulation names its driving principle: the "logic of elimination."
Generated by: colonialism — settler-colonialism is the colonial form whose aim is not to rule the native but to be rid of the native and take their place.

The concept's central insight, again from Wolfe, is that "invasion is a structure, not an event."
Settler-colonialism is not something that happened in the past and left present-day consequences; it is something happening now, sustained by ongoing practices: the legal apparatus of property and sovereignty, the policing of Indigenous land and bodies, the educational systems that naturalize settler presence, the economic arrangements that treat Indigenous land as a resource base, and the cultural myths that empty the land of its prior inhabitants.
The invasion did not end when the fighting stopped; it continues in every institution that keeps the settler in place and the native displaced.
Lorenzo Veracini's comparative work and Roxanne Dunbar-Ortiz's historiography of the United States as a settler-colonial project develop the structure across cases.

This structural analysis has direct consequences for what undoing settler-colonialism requires.
If it were merely a historical injustice, it could in principle be addressed through acknowledgment, apology, and reform within existing institutions.
If it is an ongoing structure, then it has to be dismantled — which means the return of land, not merely the recognition of historical wrongs.
This is why Eve Tuck and K.
Wayne Yang argue, in "Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor" (2012), that decolonization cannot be reduced to curriculum reform, diversity programming, or the easing of settler conscience through what they call settler moves to innocence.
The demand for land return is incommensurable with settler property law because settler property law is itself the legal technology of the dispossession.

A point this library holds firmly: settler-colonialism is a structure of domination, and the concepts that answer it — decolonization, land-back, recognition-refusal — are defined against it.
But Indigenous life, governance, and sovereignty are not products of settler-colonialism; they precede it and persist through it.
The structure tried to interrupt forms of life and relation to land that were already there, and the constructive alternative the Indigenous critical tradition names — resurgence, in Leanne Betasamosake Simpson's term, and the grounded normativity Glen Coulthard describes — rebuilds from that prior ground rather than from anything the settler order supplies.
Settlerism, the framing under which this library elaborates the structure at length, develops its continuous, displacement-and-replacement character; genocide, downstream of this concept, names the completion of its eliminatory logic.
