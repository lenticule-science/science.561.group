Abolition is the political horizon the contemporary [black-radical-tradition](../_index.md) most consistently orients toward.
The word is older than its current usage — it carries the inheritance of nineteenth-century anti-slavery — and the contemporary tradition has put real work into reclaiming it from the framing that treats abolition as a one-time historical event completed in 1865 (in the United States) or 1888 (in Brazil).
This essay tracks what abolition names now, distinguishes it from prison reform, and addresses two recurring confusions.

## The Du Boisian inheritance

The contemporary use of "abolition" is a deliberate pickup of Du Bois's term *abolition democracy* in *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935).
For Du Bois, the failure of Reconstruction was not principally that slavery was insufficiently abolished; it was that the constructive program a real abolition would have required — public education, land redistribution, public health infrastructure, civil-rights enforcement — was attempted, partly built, and then dismantled by counter-revolution.
Abolition democracy named this constructive program.

The contemporary tradition, primarily through [Angela Davis](../figures/angela-davis.md), reactivates the Du Boisian framing.
Davis's *Abolition Democracy* (2005) makes the argument explicitly: contemporary abolition is not principally about closing prisons; it is about building the social infrastructure that would make the political function the prison currently serves obsolete.
Closing prisons without building the infrastructure produces displacement of harm, not its reduction.

## Davis: the prison-industrial complex and abolition as analytic

[Angela Davis](../figures/angela-davis.md)'s *Are Prisons Obsolete?* (2003) makes the analytical move that lets abolition function as a research program rather than a slogan: it reframes "the prison" from a neutral instrument of justice into a historically specific institution with constituencies, beneficiaries, and growth dynamics — the prison-industrial complex.
Once that reframing is in place, the question shifts from "how do we improve prisons?" to "what are prisons for, who benefits from their expansion, and what would replace the social functions they currently absorb?"

The Thirteenth Amendment exception clause ("except as a punishment for crime") becomes load-bearing.
Davis reads the post-1865 history as the reorganization of slavery through criminalization — the convict-lease system, chain gangs, post-civil-rights-era mass incarceration — rather than its abolition.
This reading reframes mass incarceration as a continuation, not a recurrence.

## Gilmore: abolition geography

[Ruth Wilson Gilmore](../figures/ruth-wilson-gilmore.md) extends the abolitionist horizon into spatial register. *Abolition Geography* (2022) collects two decades of work arguing that abolition is principally about *the production of life-supporting place*.
The carceral state is the spatial expression of organized abandonment; the abolitionist response is the construction of dense civic infrastructure — schools that work, clinics that work, housing that works, food systems that work, conflict-resolution practices that do not route through cages.

Gilmore's framing makes a strong claim: the difference between reform and abolition is not principally a difference of degree (reform = small change; abolition = big change) but of orientation.
Reforms that strengthen the carceral apparatus or legitimate it consolidate it; reforms that reduce its scale and capacity without strengthening it weaken it.
The latter Gilmore (with Critical Resistance) calls *non-reformist reforms* — a phrase borrowed from André Gorz and given a new analytic.

## Two recurring confusions

### 1. Abolition as instantaneous

Abolition is sometimes read as the demand that prisons be closed tomorrow morning.
The position is rhetorically punchy and analytically wrong.
The contemporary tradition treats abolition as a long-term horizon: the construction of conditions over decades, in which carceral capacity is reduced as alternative infrastructures absorb the social functions the carceral state currently absorbs.
The horizon is not a date; it is an orientation that distinguishes which interventions count as building toward abolition and which count as consolidating the system.

### 2. Abolition as carceral-only

Abolition is sometimes read as a position about prisons specifically.
In the contemporary tradition's usage, the carceral state is one expression of a broader pattern — what Gilmore names organized abandonment, what Robinson names racial capitalism, what [Saidiya Hartman](../figures/saidiya-hartman.md) names the afterlife of slavery.
Abolition is the constructive politics adequate to that broader pattern.
Prison abolition is an entry point, not the whole.

## What this subdomain takes as the working frame

Abolition, in this subdomain's working usage, names: a constructive political horizon, oriented to the production of life-supporting infrastructures, that treats carceral institutions as historically specific expressions of racial capitalism's organized abandonment, and that distinguishes interventions by whether they reduce the scale and capacity of those institutions or consolidate them.
The horizon is intergenerational.
Its tests are practical: does this intervention reduce racial-capitalist capacity, build alternative infrastructure, and strengthen the political relations that can sustain both?
