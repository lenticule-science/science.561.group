# enclosure

Enclosure is the process of converting a commons — a collectively governed, shared resource — into private property.
The English enclosure acts of the fifteenth through nineteenth centuries are the paradigmatic case: parliamentary legislation and direct landlord action converted common pastures, forests, and fields into privately owned parcels, displacing the rural population from the land that had sustained them.
Stripped of independent access to subsistence, the dispossessed had no choice but to sell their labor for wages.
Generated by: **capital** in its accumulating mode — enclosure is one of the chief mechanisms by which capital fences off what was held in common and turns it into a source of accumulation.

Marx analyzed enclosure as a central instance of primitive accumulation, the historical process that created the preconditions for capitalist production.
The point of his analysis is that enclosure was not a natural evolution from feudalism to capitalism but an act of organized dispossession backed by law and force.
Land that had supported communal life through customary rights — the right to graze animals, gather wood, glean fields, take game — was made into exclusive private property, and the customary economy that depended on those rights was destroyed.
E.P. Thompson, in his social history of the English working class and of customary law, documented that enclosure dismantled not merely an economic arrangement but a whole way of life: communal governance, seasonal rhythms of work and rest, and the dense web of customary entitlements that had given the poor a foothold on the land.

Enclosure is not only historical, and recognizing its continuing forms is one of the concept's main uses.
Contemporary enclosures include the privatization of public services and utilities, the extension of intellectual property regimes — patents, copyrights, trademarks — over knowledge, seeds, and genetic material, large-scale land grabs in the Global South, and the commodification of digital and informational spaces.
The informational version is especially salient in surveillance capitalism, which encloses the previously un-owned residue of behavior, attention, and social life, fencing in for private extraction what no one had thought to own.
Each of these converts a shared or public resource into a privately controlled asset, reproducing the original logic in a new domain.

The connection to settler colonialism is direct and structural.
The colonization of Indigenous lands is enclosure on a continental scale: the conversion of collectively stewarded territory into private property through treaties, allotment, and outright theft follows the same logic as the English enclosures, extended and intensified through racial and imperial violence.
To see colonization as enclosure is to see that the dispossession at capitalism's origin and the dispossession at colonialism's heart are not two separate processes but the same operation working at different scales and against different peoples.

As a node, enclosure stands in exact opposition to the commons it consumes, and so it is the negative against which the reciprocal concepts in the tradition define themselves.
Where enclosure fences off and privatizes, the commons holds in common; where enclosure encloses knowledge and meaning, knowledge-sovereignty and the open commons of culture resist that fencing.
Enclosure is what those forms exist to refuse, and naming a privatization as enclosure is itself a way of asserting that what is being taken was once, and could again be, held together.
