# discourse

Discourse, in Michel Foucault's sense, is the set of rules, practices, and institutional conditions that determine what counts as a meaningful statement in a given domain.
It is not language in general, and not simply "talk about" a topic, but the specific regime that governs what can be said, by whom, from what institutional position, and with what authority.
A discourse is defined not by its subject matter — medicine, law, psychiatry — but by the rules of formation that produce its statements: the conditions under which a claim becomes intelligible rather than nonsensical.
Foucault developed the concept across *The Order of Things* (1966) and *The Archaeology of Knowledge* (1969), the works of his "archaeological" period.

A discursive formation, in Foucault's analysis, has several dimensions that together fix what is sayable.
There are the positions from which authorized speakers can make statements — the licensed physician, the certified expert, the judge.
There are the institutional sites where statements are produced — the clinic, the laboratory, the courtroom.
There is the conceptual grid that organizes the domain — disease categories, legal precedent, diagnostic criteria.
And there are the strategies by which statements are combined into arguments, theories, and bodies of knowledge.
Together these determine what can be said and what falls outside the thinkable altogether.

Medical discourse, for example, is not merely talk about bodies.
It is the configuration of qualified speakers, institutional sites, diagnostic categories, and standards of evidence that makes certain statements about bodies possible and others unthinkable.
A medieval physician's claim that a patient's humors are imbalanced is not wrong in the way a modern misdiagnosis is wrong.
It belongs to a different discursive formation — one in which "humoral imbalance" is a valid category and "bacterial infection" is not yet an available thought at all.
Discourse, in this sense, sets the horizon of the true before any particular statement is judged true or false.

Discourse is the site where power/knowledge operates most directly.
To control a discourse is to control what can be treated as true — not by censoring statements but by structuring the conditions under which statements become intelligible in the first place.
Foucault treats discourses not as reflections of a pre-existing reality but as productive forces: they generate the objects they appear merely to describe.
"The delinquent," "the homosexual," "the mentally ill" are not types that existed before criminology, sexology, and psychiatry classified them; the discourses produced these categories, and the categories in turn reorganized the institutions that produced them.
This is why discourse is the medium of subjectification: a person becomes a recognizable kind of subject only by being placed within a discourse that makes that kind of person sayable.
To change what people can be, on this account, one has to change what can be said.
