# settler-moves-to-innocence

Settler moves to innocence are the rhetorical and political strategies by which settlers attempt to relieve themselves of the guilt and complicity of living on stolen land without actually returning that land or dismantling settler-colonial structures.
The concept was developed by Eve Tuck and K.
Wayne Yang in "Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor" (2012), borrowing the phrase "moves to innocence" from Janet Mawhinney's earlier work on white complicity and turning it specifically toward the settler's relationship to Indigenous dispossession.
Generated by: settler-colonialism — these moves are the structure's mechanism of self-exculpation, innocence claimed in place of the repatriation that decolonization would actually require.

Tuck and Yang catalog several characteristic moves.
There is the claim of distant or partial Indigenous ancestry, deployed to establish a personal belonging that dissolves the settler's outsider status.
There is the adoption of "decolonization" as a metaphor for other goals — decolonizing the syllabus, the mind, the diet — which lets the word do emotional work while leaving land untouched and unmentioned.
There is the treatment of settler guilt as a psychological problem to be resolved through self-work and confession, as though the harm were an inner condition rather than a material arrangement.
There is the move that equates all forms of oppression with colonization, dissolving the specificity of land theft into a general progressive solidarity.
And there is the fantasy of Indigenous adoption or inclusion — of being welcomed, absorbed, forgiven — that would resolve the settler's illegitimacy without confronting its basis in occupied territory.

The decisive point is that these are not merely individual moral failings to be scolded.
They are structural features of settler-colonialism, because the settler order requires ongoing legitimation, and moves to innocence supply it.
Each one converts the incommensurable demand for land return into something manageable — a reform, an attitude, a curriculum change, a feeling — and in doing so it secures the structure against the only demand that would actually unsettle it.
The settler gets to feel decolonial precisely so as not to have to be.
This is why the concept functions less as an accusation than as a diagnostic: it lets one see how readily even sincere anti-colonial sentiment is recruited to the maintenance of the colonial order.

Within this library settler-moves-to-innocence is the shadow cast by decolonization: it names everything that presents itself as decolonization while refusing decolonization's material content.
Identifying a move to innocence does not by itself resolve anything, but it clarifies what decolonization requires and what it does not — and it sharpens the insistence, shared with the entries on decolonization and land-back, that the demand at issue is the return of land and life, not the easing of the conscience of those who hold it.
The constructive alternative does not lie in better feelings but in the relinquishment the moves are designed to avoid.
