# bureaucracy

Bureaucracy is an administrative form built out of offices — a graded hierarchy of impersonal positions, governed by written rules and documented files, in which the work of administration is carried out by trained specialists who hold their posts as a full-time vocation.
Generated by: bureaucracy is what offices become when they are systematized into a single machine.
Max Weber, who made the concept the founding object of organizational sociology, described it through an ideal-type that combines several features: fixed jurisdictional areas ordered by rules; a hierarchy of superordination and subordination; administration based on written documents that separate the office from the person; appointment (not election) on the basis of demonstrated expertise; the office as a full-time calling demanding the official's whole working capacity; general, learnable rules applied impersonally — *sine ira et studio*, "without anger or favor"; and officials who are personally free, salaried, pensioned, and advance by career, because they *hold* the office rather than *own* it.

It is the conjunction of these features, not any one alone, that distinguishes bureaucracy from the administrative forms it historically displaces.
Patrimonial stewards may be expert and full-time but never separate the office from the person; charismatic followings may keep records but never generalize their rules; household administration may be hierarchical but binds through personal loyalty rather than impersonal rule.
Bureaucracy is, on Weber's account, the characteristic carrier of rational-legal authority — the apparatus that fits and reproduces the modern belief that commands are valid because they issue from a properly constituted office following proper procedure.

Weber's central claim about it is *technical*, not moral.
Bureaucracy is technically superior to other administrative forms — faster, more precise, more predictable, more economical at scale — and once the mass state, the industrial firm, the political party, and the standing army all require the capacities it provides, bureaucratization becomes the most unambiguous and nearly irreversible trend of modernity.
But Weber held this superiority entirely compatible with bureaucracy's substantive costs.
Its formal, procedural rationality tends to crowd out substantive value — the question of what ends are worth pursuing — and the apparatus that is so efficient at any task can be turned to monstrous ends as readily as benign ones.
This is the source of his famous image of the "iron cage" (*stahlhartes Gehäuse*): a rationalized order that encloses those within it.

Later scholarship complicated the picture in two directions.
The neo-institutional tradition (Meyer and Rowan, 1977) observed that real organizations frequently adopt the *appearance* of bureaucratic structure as ceremony — a legitimating myth — while their actual operations run on something quite different, so the ideal-type describes no particular organization exactly.
The labour-process tradition (Braverman, Burawoy) read bureaucratic rule less as neutral efficiency than as a specific form of control over work under capitalism, the firm's own internal government of its workers.
Against the late-century vogue for "post-bureaucratic" management, Paul du Gay (*In Praise of Bureaucracy*, 2000) defended the impersonal office, arguing that its very impersonality carries a distinctive ethic — treating cases by rule rather than by favor — that its critics too easily sacrifice.

Within this cluster bureaucracy stands directly downstream of the office: it is what authority, once lodged in impersonal positions, builds when those positions are stacked, specialized, and bound together by files and rules.
It is the administrative body of the modern state and the modern corporation alike, and the foil against which any project of horizontal, non-hierarchical coordination must define itself.
