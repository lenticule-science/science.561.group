# Rubber-Hose Realism: From Koko the Clown and Mickey Mouse to the Aesthetic Toolkit of the U.S. 4th Psychological Operations Group

## Abstract

This article traces a visual and affective genealogy from early American animation — specifically Max Fleischer's Koko the Clown in *Out of the Inkwell* and Walt Disney's Mickey Mouse — to the contemporary audio-visual products of the U.S. Army's 4th Psychological Operations Group (Airborne), focusing on recruitment and influence videos such as *Ghosts in the Machine*.

Drawing on cultural-imperialism theory, semiotics, and military information-operations doctrine, the paper argues that what I call **[rubber-hose realism](../terms/rubber-hose-realism.md)** — a mode in which cartoon elasticity is used to stage [consequence-free violence](../terms/consequence-free-violence.md), moral weightlessness, and reversible bodies — has become a durable U.S. soft-power grammar.
This grammar is visible first in Fleischer's live-action/animation hybrids that allow Koko to traverse diegetic levels, then in Disney's stabilization of Mickey as a globally marketable, Fordist mascot, and finally in the uncanny, collage-driven aesthetics of contemporary PSYOP videos.

Rather than claiming a direct causal line or conspiratorial continuity, the article shows how state influence operations and early animation share formal strategies that manage audience affect: [strategic infantilization](../terms/strategic-infantilization.md), dissociation, and [moral displacement](../terms/moral-displacement.md).
The conclusion suggests that the 4th PSYOP Group's embrace of retro animation tropes and surreal editing is not a stylistic novelty, but a continuation of a long U.S. project in which the visual language of cartoons helps normalize, aestheticize, and make cognitively palatable the projection of military and political power.

**Keywords:** Koko the Clown; Mickey Mouse; rubber-hose animation; PSYOP; cultural imperialism; media aesthetics; soft power.

## 1. Introduction

On 18 November, widely celebrated as "Mickey Mouse Day," fans commemorate the 1928 premiere of *Steamboat Willie*, the short that the Walt Disney Company treats as Mickey's official on-screen debut.
This annual celebration reinforces Mickey's status as a benign emblem of childhood, nostalgia, and corporate magic.

At the same time, the U.S. Army's 4th Psychological Operations Group (Airborne) releases highly stylized recruitment and informational videos, most famously the *Ghosts in the Machine* series, which deliberately deploy unsettling imagery, rapid montage, fragmented voice-over, and a collage of historical and pop-cultural references.
The Army itself describes these videos as "thought provoking" works intended to dramatize "emotional manipulation" and invite viewers into the world of psychological operations (Army 2024).

Popular reactions to these PSYOP videos often focus on their "weird" or "creepy" aesthetic.
What is missing is an account of how this aesthetic participates in a much longer history of American visual culture — especially early animation — and how it supports the strategic goals of psychological operations as defined in U.S. doctrine: to influence emotions, motives, reasoning, and ultimately behavior of foreign audiences (FM 3-05.301 2007).

This paper proposes a focused, genealogical question:

> How do the visual and moral grammars of early American animation, exemplified by Koko the Clown and Mickey Mouse, prefigure and inform the contemporary aesthetic of 4th PSYOP Group media products?

By reading *Out of the Inkwell*, early Mickey shorts, and *Ghosts in the Machine* together, I argue that a specific cluster of techniques — rubber-hose animation, live-action/animation interpenetration, cartoon physics, and the staging of consequence-free violence — constitutes a shared aesthetic repertoire I describe as **rubber-hose realism**, a regime sitting on a [moral physics](../terms/moral-physics.md) substrate that runs across the [state-aesthetic continuum](../terms/state-aesthetic-continuum.md) from commercial entertainment to overt psychological operations.
This repertoire has moved from commercial entertainment to corporate soft power to overt psychological operations.

## 2. Historical Background: From Inkwell to Mouse

### 2.1 Koko the Clown and the Fleischer "Inkwell" Aesthetic

Max Fleischer's *Out of the Inkwell* series (1918–1929) introduced "the Inkwell Clown," later named Koko, animated via rotoscope from live-action footage of Max's brother Dave in clown costume.
The shorts characteristically feature:

- Koko literally emerging from the inkwell onto the animator's desk.
- The animator ("The Boss") drawing, erasing, tormenting, or negotiating with Koko.
- Interplay between live-action and animation in the same frame.

Formally, these films pioneered the *Fleischer Style*: rubber-hose limbs, extreme elasticity, morphing bodies, and spatial distortions.
Crucially, this elasticity is not merely visual; it structures how cause and effect work in the cartoon world.
Koko can be dismembered, flattened, or shattered only to reconstitute himself in the next shot.
Violence rarely has lasting consequence; bodies are reversible, and physics is subordinate to gag logic.

Koko's significance is underscored by the recent selection of *Koko's Earth Control* (1922) for preservation in the U.S. National Film Registry as "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant," in part because of its apocalyptic scenario in which Koko inadvertently gains control over global disaster and chaos.
Already in the 1920s, then, Koko's antics dramatize a world in which catastrophic events are triggered and reversed as visual play, defusing their moral weight through cartoon form.

### 2.2 Mickey Mouse and the Fordist Cartoon Mascot

Mickey's on-screen breakthrough occurs with *Steamboat Willie* (1928), one of the first cartoons with fully synchronized sound.
Like Koko, early Mickey is drawn in rubber-hose style; his limbs bend and stretch without joints, his body acts as a musical instrument, and animals and objects alike are conscripted into slapstick violence.

Over the twentieth century, Mickey becomes not just a character but a global brand and, in Watts's account, a vehicle for a "genial cultural imperialism" that exports middle-class U.S. values worldwide (Watts 1997).
Disney's post–World War II films and television programs — supported by the company's earlier cooperation with U.S. wartime propaganda efforts — position Mickey as the friendly face of American modernity.

Dorfman and Mattelart's *How to Read Donald Duck* famously extended this critique, arguing that Disney comics encode imperialist and capitalist values "behind the innocent, wholesome façade" of cartoon animals, making them powerful ideological tools precisely because they present themselves as harmless entertainment for children (Dorfman & Mattelart 1971).

Even critics of the cultural-imperialism thesis acknowledge that Disney's studio functions as a major agent of soft power: its characters shape global imaginaries of family, labor, race, and nation (Griggs 2019; GMJ 2024).
Mickey, in particular, serves as a sanitized, endlessly merchandisable emblem of U.S. benevolence.

## 3. Theoretical Framework

This study brings three strands of theory into conversation:

**Cultural Imperialism and Media Imperialism.** Work on cultural and media imperialism argues that U.S. media products — particularly Hollywood and Disney — serve as vectors of ideological power, normalizing U.S. economic and political dominance through attractive images and narratives (Dorfman & Mattelart 1971; Griggs 2019; GMJ 2024).

**Semiotics and Moral Physics in Animation.** Following Dorfman and Mattelart and subsequent critiques, I treat cartoons as texts whose visual codes (elastic bodies, reversible violence, absent parents, endless mobility) naturalize particular "moral physics": how harm, responsibility, and consequence are imagined.

**Psychological Operations and Narrative/Memetic Warfare.** U.S. PSYOP doctrine defines missions as operations using information to influence emotions, motives, and reasoning of foreign audiences, integrated into broader "full spectrum" dominance of the information environment (FM 3-05.301 2007).
Recent Army commentary stresses narrative as a central dimension of operations and laments U.S. failures to leverage narrative effectively (Ross & Rutland 2022).

These frameworks allow us to examine the 4th PSYOP Group's media not only as recruitment tools but as aesthetic artifacts that mobilize inherited cartoon grammars to perform affective and moral work.

## 4. Methodology

Given the absence of direct archival evidence linking specific animators or studios to PSYOP teams, this article deliberately avoids claims of intentional plagiarism or line-by-line influence.
Instead, it employs a comparative textual analysis:

**Primary cultural texts.**

- *Out of the Inkwell* shorts featuring Koko, with particular attention to *Koko's Earth Control*.
- Early Mickey Mouse shorts (e.g., *Steamboat Willie*), as well as later black-and-white episodes where rubber-hose aesthetics remain dominant.

**PSYOP media.**

- The *Ghosts in the Machine* recruitment videos produced by or for the 4th PSYOP Group, as documented by official DoD hosting, Army public-affairs coverage, and journalistic commentary (Army 2024; Task & Purpose 2024).

**Doctrinal and critical texts.**

- U.S. PSYOP doctrine (FM 3-05.301 2007) and related documents.
- Cultural and media-imperialism scholarship concerning Disney and U.S. soft power (Dorfman & Mattelart 1971; Watts 1997; Griggs 2019; GMJ 2024).

The method is genealogical rather than strictly historical: it traces how particular visual and narrative functions of rubber-hose animation reappear in a new institutional setting (the military), even if mediated by decades of stylistic evolution.

## 5. Analysis

### 5.1 Rubber-Hose Realism and the Moral Physics of Early Animation

Rubber-hose animation, standard in U.S. animation in the 1920s and 1930s, is defined by limb elasticity and curving, jointless motion.
As a visual system, it enables:

- Extreme bodily distortion without visible injury.
- Rapid transformations of objects (e.g., a character becoming an instrument).
- Continuous motion that erases clear boundaries between characters and environment.

In *Out of the Inkwell*, these traits are foregrounded by the live-action framing: Koko escapes the page, rearranges the studio, and even manipulates the animator's tools.
The diegetic barrier between fictional world and "real" world is repeatedly breached.

This produces a distinct **[moral physics](../terms/moral-physics.md)**:

- **Consequence-free violence.** Characters are beaten, exploded, or dismembered, yet reassemble without injury.
  Harm is visually spectacular but narratively negligible.
- **Reversibility of catastrophe.** In *Koko's Earth Control*, global disaster (earthquakes, storms, chaos) can be started and stopped by a comically simple control interface, trivializing apocalypse into a problem of operator discipline rather than structural violence.
- **Elastic subjectivity.** Characters' identities and bodies are stretchy: facial expressions, bodily integrity, and even species boundaries fluctuate to fit the gag.

These features do not merely entertain; they teach viewers — especially children — to experience violent or catastrophic events as aesthetic surfaces that can be reset.
Violence becomes a rhythm, not a rupture.

### 5.2 From Koko to Mickey: Sanitizing the Uncanny

Disney's early Mickey shorts share many of these traits: rubber-hose limbs, musicalized violence, and the use of animals as instruments or props.
However, Disney systematically sanitizes Koko's uncanny presence:

- Where Koko emerges from the inkwell into the animator's studio, Mickey is embedded in closed fictional worlds (riverboats, concert stages, barns).
- The animator's godlike role disappears; instead, the studio itself — its logo, opening fanfare, and later castle imagery — frames Mickey as the curated product of a benevolent corporation.

This shift facilitates Mickey's role as a Fordist mascot: a active, recognizable icon that can be mass-produced on merchandise, park signage, and television, embodying Disney's "fun, family, and fantasy" brand identity (Watts 1997).

At the same time, Disney's integration into U.S. wartime propaganda — producing training and persuasive films for multiple branches of the armed forces during World War II — cements the alignment between cartoon aesthetics and state messaging.
Animation's aptitude for simplifying complex technical or moral issues is explicitly harnessed for national mobilization.

In cultural-imperialism terms, Mickey thus becomes the "friendly face" of a larger apparatus that naturalizes American economic and political dominance through endearing narratives and imagery (Dorfman & Mattelart 1971; Griggs 2019).

### 5.3 The Aesthetic of *Ghosts in the Machine*

The 4th PSYOP Group's *Ghosts in the Machine* and its sequel are officially framed as recruitment videos that dramatize the psychological dimension of modern warfare.
The Army's own write-ups present them as "thought provoking," deliberately mysterious films that circulated first on a seemingly anonymous YouTube channel, generating viral curiosity before being acknowledged as official military products (Army 2024).

Journalistic coverage emphasizes their "haunting" and "unsettling" qualities: rapid montages of historical footage, glitches, symbolic imagery (masks, puppets, screens within screens), and voice-overs that speak of manipulating emotions and narratives (Task & Purpose 2024).

Although these videos are not literally animated in rubber-hose style, they adopt several animation-like strategies:

- **Layered ontologies.** Screens within screens, reflections, surveillance feeds, and archival clips echo the inkwell conceit: there is always another level of representation manipulating the one below.
- **Elastic causality.** Scenes of riot, conflict, or crisis are cut into symbolic juxtapositions rather than linear narratives.
  Causes and consequences blur; events seem to arise from abstract forces.
- **[Affective looping](../terms/affective-looping.md).** Repeated images, distorted voices, and cyclical sequences create a sense of being trapped in a loop — akin to cartoon gags that replay variations on the same fall or explosion.
- **Strategic infantilization and surrealism.** The videos juxtapose playful or childlike imagery (toys, simple symbols) with serious geopolitical stakes, producing a disorienting tone that recalls early cartoons' mix of naive surface and latent violence.

What emerges is a **[soft surrealism](../terms/soft-surrealism.md)**: the sense that reality itself has become cartoonish, malleable, and subject to unseen authors.
This aligns closely with PSYOP doctrine's emphasis on shaping perceptions and emotional responses rather than conveying propositional information (FM 3-05.301 2007; Ross & Rutland 2022).

### 5.4 From Rubber-Hose to PSYOP: Infantilization, Dissociation, Moral Displacement

We can now articulate three key continuities between early animation and contemporary PSYOP media.

#### 5.4.1 Infantilization

Both Koko/Mickey and *Ghosts in the Machine* operate through infantilization — not necessarily by depicting children, but by addressing audiences in a mode associated with children's media:

- Simplified, iconic imagery.
- Overstated emotional cues (music, rhythm, repetition).
- A world where complex systems are personified or reduced to manipulable controls (Koko's disaster switch; the PSYOP analyst's interface).

In cultural-imperialism discourse, Disney's child-oriented characters function as "harmless" pedagogical tools for transmitting ideological norms (Dorfman & Mattelart 1971).
Similarly, PSYOP media uses a childlike aesthetic to disarm critical distance: if the threats and tools of influence appear as stylized images in a slick montage, they are easier to accept as natural features of the information environment.

#### 5.4.2 Dissociation

Rubber-hose animation trains viewers to dissociate visual harm from real harm: bodies stretch rather than break; death is rare and reversible; catastrophe is a gag to be reset.

PSYOP videos extend this dissociation by:

- Presenting real or realistic images of conflict, protest, or disaster in hyper-stylized form.
- Shifting quickly between registers (historic footage, symbolic imagery, abstract graphics), preventing viewers from dwelling on any one scene long enough for a concrete moral response to crystallize.

The viewer is encouraged to feel impressed, intrigued, or disturbed — but not to form a active ethical judgment about what is being shown or about the institution producing the video.

#### 5.4.3 Moral Displacement

Finally, both early cartoons and PSYOP media engage in **[moral displacement](../terms/moral-displacement.md)**: they move the locus of moral concern away from structural power towards individual behavior or attitude.

In Koko and Mickey cartoons, structural issues (class, race, empire) become background scenery or exoticized locales, while the narrative focuses on the hero's pluck or mischief.

In *Ghosts in the Machine*, the existence of psychological manipulation is acknowledged, even glamorized, but the moral question is reframed as: *are you clever enough, special enough, to join the manipulators?* rather than *should this apparatus exist at all?* (Army 2024; Task & Purpose 2024).

This displacement reflects broader patterns in U.S. soft power and media imperialism, where structural inequality and imperial violence are often recoded as problems of culture, mindset, or narrative framing (Dorfman & Mattelart 1971; Griggs 2019; GMJ 2024).

## 6. Discussion: Animation, Empire, and the Aesthetics of Influence

The triangulation Koko–Mickey–PSYOP reveals that animation is not merely a genre but an *epistemic style*: a way of organizing what counts as real, consequential, or laughable.

**From studio to state.** Fleischer and Disney developed techniques — rubber-hose bodies, live-action/animation hybrids, cartoon physics — that proved ideal for simplifying complex processes and diffusing anxiety about modernity.
These same techniques are attractive to state actors seeking to render war, surveillance, and psychological control as aesthetically manageable.

**Soft power as pre-PSYOP.** Disney's role in Cold War and post-war soft power — what Watts describes as a "cultural Marshall Plan" (Watts 1997) and what Dorfman and Mattelart expose as an imperialist pedagogy (Dorfman & Mattelart 1971) — can be seen as a proto-PSYOP apparatus: a massively distributed, privately funded psychological-influence system that prepares global populations to identify with U.S. narratives.

**Memetic warfare and retro aesthetics.** Contemporary military analyses increasingly discuss "[memetic warfare](../terms/memetic-warfare.md)," where internet memes serve as tactical tools of persuasion (Ross & Rutland 2022).
The retro, rubber-hose-adjacent aesthetic popular in online culture (revivals of 1920s cartoon styles in games and music videos) provides a ready-made pool of forms that PSYOP units can draw upon to signal cultural fluency while maintaining control over narrative framing.

Seen this way, the 4th PSYOP Group's media products are not bizarre outliers but logical inheritors of a century-long convergence between cartoon logic and imperial strategy.

## 7. Conclusion

This paper has argued that early American animation, especially *Out of the Inkwell*'s Koko the Clown and Disney's Mickey Mouse shorts, developed a visual and moral grammar — **rubber-hose realism** — that continues to inform U.S. psychological operations media today.

By examining the aesthetic and affective strategies shared between these seemingly disparate domains, we can see how:

- **Infantilization** makes complex geopolitical projects feel approachable and even playful.
- **Dissociation** separates visual harm from ethical judgment, enabling audiences to consume images of violence without confronting their full implications.
- **Moral displacement** shifts attention from structural power to individual cleverness, loyalty, or emotional resilience.

The 4th PSYOP Group's *Ghosts in the Machine* videos exemplify how this inherited grammar is redeployed under conditions of digital media and narrative conflict: they adopt an uncanny, collage-driven style that rhymes with early cartoons' live-action/animation hybridity, while openly thematizing psychological manipulation as both danger and career opportunity.

The goal here has not been to suggest a conspiratorial plot in which Disney secretly trains PSYOP officers, but to make visible a continuum of techniques that shape how audiences "learn to see" war, power, and themselves.
If Mickey Mouse Day celebrates a cheerful rodent who helped normalize a century of American soft power, then reading Koko, Mickey, and PSYOP together may help us understand how even the most whimsical aesthetics can prepare the ground for more overt forms of psychological warfare.

Future research could deepen this analysis by:

- Conducting close shot-by-shot comparisons between specific cartoons and PSYOP videos.
- Interviewing military media producers about their aesthetic influences.
- Extending the genealogy beyond U.S. contexts, for instance to Japanese or Korean animation and their own intersections with state soft power.

But even at this stage, the central insight stands: the ghosts in the machine were sketching with rubber-hose limbs long before the Army gave them a name.
