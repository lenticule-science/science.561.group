# platform

A platform is a digital infrastructure that positions itself between users and mediates their interactions, becoming the medium through which contemporary social life is conducted and, in the same motion, capturing that activity as an extractable asset.
The defining gesture is twofold: the platform offers a seemingly free medium for sociality — for relation, attention, expression, exchange — and it records the freely given activity that medium hosts, converting it into data it owns and monetizes.
Generated by: **liberalism** — the platform is the infrastructural form through which a late-liberal formation now conducts subject-formation, the management of contradiction, and the distribution of feeling.

Three bodies of work frame the concept.
Benjamin Bratton's *The Stack* (2015) reads platforms not as applications running on top of society but as *layers* in a planetary computational structure that has come to rival the nation-state as a primary organizing form of power; the platform is infrastructural, not supplementary.
Nick Srnicek's *Platform Capitalism* (2017) supplies the political-economic reading: the platform is a specific business form — built on network effects, two-sided markets, and the extraction of rent from intermediation — that became economically dominant under particular post-2008 conditions, and he distinguishes several types (advertising, cloud, industrial, product, lean).
Shoshana Zuboff's *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019) supplies the critical-popular account: platforms extract *behavioral surplus* — data about users in excess of what any service requires — compute it into predictions of future behavior, and sell those predictions in markets the user never enters.
José van Dijck and colleagues' *The Platform Society* (2018) traces how platform logics have spread beyond technology firms into health, education, transit, journalism, and public administration.

The crucial structural feature is the inversion of the apparent transaction.
The user experiences the platform as a free service in exchange for their attention; what actually occurs is the conversion of their sociality into a proprietary asset.
On Zuboff's account this proceeds in a cycle: the platform extracts behavioral data, computes prediction products from it, sells those predictions to clients (advertisers, insurers, employers, campaigns), and — because more accurate predictions command higher prices — redesigns itself to *shape* user behavior into more predictable patterns.
The fourth step is the analytic novelty: earlier observation regimes watched behavior without altering it, whereas the platform has an economic incentive to nudge, gamify, and habituate, tuning its ranking and notification logic toward producing the very patterns its predictions describe.
What is commodified, on this reading, is not labor power or attention alone but the behavioral residue of lived life itself — taken rather than sold, and owned by the platform.

These claims are contested, and a graduate student should hold the differences in view.
Critics such as Evgeny Morozov argue that Zuboff's single "surveillance capitalism" frame overgeneralizes genuinely distinct business models — Google's advertising, Apple's hardware-and-services, Amazon's retail-and-cloud, TikTok's recommendation — into one logic.
Marxist critics, Srnicek among them, hold that the surveillance frame neglects the broader political economy of financialization, unpaid labor, and persistent industrial production within which data extraction is one mechanism among several.
The dispute matters: it is the difference between treating the platform as a novel and total accumulation logic and treating it as one contingent form within capitalism's longer history.

For the analysis at hand, the platform matters as the medium where the other mechanisms of this branch now run.
It is the infrastructure through which subject-formation is staged at scale — opinion, identification, and the calibration of expectation conducted through engagement metrics that make some forms of sociality legible and render others invisible.
It is where Hochschild's feeling rules are enforced at engagement-metric speed, an affect that draws strong engagement amplified and one that draws weak engagement dampened, faster than any interpersonal correction could be.
And it is where affect governance and cruel optimism are delivered as feed.
The platform, in short, is the enclosure of what had been a freely shared commons of sociality: it offers the commons back as a service and extracts the life conducted within it as capital.
