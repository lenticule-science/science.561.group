# palingenetic-ultranationalism

Palingenetic ultranationalism is Roger Griffin's term, from *The Nature of Fascism* (1991), for the ideological core of fascism: the myth that a sacred nation has fallen into decadence and will be reborn through struggle, purification, and collective will.
"Palingenetic" comes from *palingenesis*, rebirth or regeneration.
Griffin's claim is that this rebirth myth is the ideological *sine qua non* of fascism — without it, fascism has no engine; with it, every other fascist feature finds its organizing center.
Generated by: **nationalism** — this is the fascist intensification of the broader doctrine, what ordinary national feeling becomes when it is fused with a myth of decline and redemptive rebirth.

The myth runs on a double temporal axis.
Backward: there was a time when the nation was pure, whole, and powerful.
Forward: that purity will be restored through the heroic action of the present generation.
The present is staged as the hinge — the moment of decision between continued decline and redemptive transformation — which lends fascist mobilization its characteristic urgency, its sense that everything is at stake now.
This is what makes the formation more than nostalgia: it pairs mourning for a lost golden age with a utopian promise of regeneration, and demands action in the name of the future nation.

The "ultra" in ultranationalism marks the break from ordinary nationalism.
Fascist nationalism is not civic patriotism or cultural pride; it is the assertion that the nation is an organic, sacred body whose existence is prior to and more real than the individuals composing it.
The individual has value only insofar as they serve the national body.
Those held to contaminate it — through racial impurity, political dissent, cultural deviance, or alleged biological unfitness — are recast as pathological elements to be expelled or destroyed.
The rebirth myth thereby specifies who belongs to the future and who must be eliminated so that future can arrive; this is the point where palingenetic ultranationalism connects to the machinery of purification and elimination that fascist regimes build.

Griffin's real contribution is to make fascism *falsifiable*.
A political formation that does not invoke national rebirth after decline — that does not posit a sacred people, a fall from grace, and a redemptive future secured through purification — is not fascism on this account.
It may be authoritarian, populist, theocratic, or otherwise dangerous, but it lacks the ideological nucleus around which the other fascist structures crystallize.
The myth can be spoken in many vocabularies — *Volk*, race, "Western civilization," the restored nation — while the structure underneath stays constant: declare the crisis, name the pure community, denounce the enemies said to be draining its vitality, reframe suffering as sacrifice, promise destiny, assert its inevitability.
Read against its neighbours, palingenetic ultranationalism takes the **volksgeist** and **the-folk** that **romantic-nationalism** produced and conscripts them as the cast of a redemption story; it is **political-myth** at its most lethal, the rebirth narrative for which **nationalism** had already supplied the people and the past.
