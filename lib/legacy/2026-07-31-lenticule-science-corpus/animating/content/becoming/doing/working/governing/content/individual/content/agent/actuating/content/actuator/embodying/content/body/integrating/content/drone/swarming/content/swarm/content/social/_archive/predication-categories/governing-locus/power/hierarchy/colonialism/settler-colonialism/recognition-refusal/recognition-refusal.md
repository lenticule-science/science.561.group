Recognition refusal is the rejection of the demand that Indigenous peoples legitimate their governance, knowledge, and sovereignty by translating them into terms the settler-colonial state can recognize.
Glen Coulthard (Yellowknives Dene) develops the concept in *Red Skin, White Masks* (2014), drawing on and inverting Frantz Fanon's analysis of colonial recognition in *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952).

Fanon argued that the colonized subject who seeks recognition from the colonizer enters a trap: the terms of recognition are set by the colonizer, and what is recognized is not the colonized subject as they are but a version of them that the colonial framework can accommodate.
Recognition does not end domination; it reformats it.
The colonized subject becomes legible to the colonial order — and legibility, as Scott's work shows, is the precondition for administration.

Coulthard applies this to the Canadian settler-colonial context.
Liberal multiculturalism offers Indigenous peoples "recognition" — land claims processes, self-government agreements, cultural heritage protections — but on terms that presuppose the legitimacy of the Canadian state, the sovereignty of the Crown, and the evaluative framework of liberal-democratic governance.
To be recognized, Indigenous governance must be translated into the language of rights, constitutionalism, and institutional procedure.
What cannot survive that translation — governance grounded in specific relationships to land, ceremonial authority, oral tradition, and kinship obligation — is either excluded or reduced to cultural decoration.

Recognition refusal says: we do not need your recognition.
Indigenous governance does not derive its legitimacy from the settler state's acknowledgment.
[Grounded normativity](./grounded-normativity.md) — the ethical and political knowledge that arises from Indigenous relationships to specific places and practices — is self-legitimating.
It does not require translation into universalist terms to be real.

Leanne Betasamosake Simpson's *As We Have Always Done* (2017) develops the constructive dimension: if recognition is refused, what replaces it is not withdrawal but resurgence — the practice of Indigenous life, governance, and knowledge on Indigenous terms, grounded in the relations that sustain them.
Resurgence does not seek a place within the settler framework; it operates outside it, generating political life from its own relational ground.

The concept connects directly to the critique of [epistemic universalism](../../../../../../../../philosophy/terms/epistemic-universalism.md).
The demand for recognition is a demand that Indigenous knowledge pass through a universalist evaluative framework — that it be articulable, assessable, and ratifiable in terms accessible to settler institutions.
Recognition refusal is the insistence that this demand is itself colonial.
[Opacity](../../../../../../../../philosophy/theories/caribbean/theories/creolization/terms/opacity.md) — the right not to be made transparent — is the epistemological face of recognition refusal.
