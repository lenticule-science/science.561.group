Luc Boltanski and Laurent Thévenot's *On Justification: Economies of Worth* — published in French in 1991 [boltanski-thevenot-1991] and in English in 2006 [boltanski-thevenot-2006] — is the founding text of *French pragmatic sociology* (also called the *sociology of critical capacity* or the *sociology of conventions*).
Its central contribution is a typology of *six orders of worth* (six *cités* or "polities" in the original French) that actors draw on to *justify* their actions, criticize others' actions, and resolve disputes in complex social situations.

The framework treats justification as itself a sociological phenomenon worthy of systematic analysis: people in disputes do not simply assert preferences; they appeal to general principles, and the principles they appeal to are not arbitrary but draw on a small number of historically-developed *orders of worth*, each with its own definition of what makes a person, action, or arrangement *worthy*.

## The six orders of worth

Boltanski and Thévenot, working from a close reading of canonical political-philosophical texts (Augustine, Bossuet, Hobbes, Rousseau, Smith, Saint-Simon), identified six orders of worth, each derivable from a foundational text and each instantiated by characteristic objects, persons, judgments, and tests:

| Order | Foundational text | Higher principle | Worthy objects | Characteristic test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Inspired** | Augustine, *City of God* | Grace, inspiration, creativity | Imagination, the unexpected | Singular event of inspiration |
| **Domestic** | Bossuet, *Politics from Holy Scripture* | Tradition, hierarchy, generation | Family, custom, established places | Trust, loyalty, propriety |
| **Civic** | Rousseau, *The Social Contract* | The general will, collective | Representatives, the public | Demonstration, mobilization |
| **Fame** (or Opinion) | Hobbes, *Leviathan* | Public opinion, recognition | Celebrities, reputation, signs | Visibility, public acknowledgment |
| **Market** | Smith, *Wealth of Nations* | Competition, price, desire | Wealth, transactions, deals | Closing the deal, monetary success |
| **Industrial** | Saint-Simon, *Catéchisme des industriels* | Productivity, efficiency, technical performance | Tools, machines, professionals, plans | Functioning effectively, measurable performance |

Each order has six structural elements, derived from a *political-grammar* the authors extract from each foundational text:

1. A **higher common principle** — what generates worth (e.g., grace, tradition, the general will).
2. A definition of **worthy and unworthy states** — what counts as a worthy person, object, or relation (e.g., inspired vs. routine; trustworthy vs. dishonorable).
3. A **dignity** — the basic capacity for worth shared by all members.
4. A repertoire of **subjects** — categories of agents (e.g., inventor, parent, representative, celebrity, buyer, professional).
5. A repertoire of **objects** — categories of artifacts (e.g., the unexpected, the heirloom, the ballot, the headline, the price, the plan).
6. A **test** that establishes worth and resolves disputes (e.g., the moment of inspiration, the demonstration of loyalty, the public assembly, the survey, the deal, the working machine).

Boltanski and Chiapello [boltanski-chiapello-2005] subsequently proposed a seventh order — **the projective city** (*la cité par projets*), with adaptability, connectivity, and project-based engagement as the higher principle — emerging from late-twentieth-century management literature.
A further candidate **green order**, with ecological sustainability as the higher principle, has been developed primarily by other authors in the surrounding French pragmatic-sociology and conventions literature (notably Lafaye and Thévenot's earlier work on environmental disputes and subsequent expansions); the green order is not a straightforward extension of the original 1991 typology by Boltanski and Thévenot themselves but a related development.

## Disputes, tests, and compromises

The framework's distinctive analytical move is its treatment of *disputes*.
When actors in a dispute appeal to the same order of worth, the dispute can be settled by the order's characteristic test: a market dispute is settled by who closes the deal, an industrial dispute by who has the working machine, a civic dispute by who has the votes. *Critique* in such situations operates *within* the order, contesting whether the test was properly applied.

Disputes become more complex — and more interesting — when actors appeal to *different* orders of worth.
The civic representative says: this decision should be made by the assembly.
The market actor says: this decision should be made by who pays.
The industrial actor says: this decision should be made by what works.
Each is internally coherent; they disagree about which order should be used to settle the matter.

Boltanski and Thévenot identify three resolution mechanisms:

1. **Clarification**: actors discover they are working in the same order and disagreeing about a test, which the order itself can settle.
2. **Critique**: actors deploy one order to criticize the application of another (e.g., "you say it's profitable, but it's destroying community life" — market vs. domestic).
3. **Compromise**: actors construct hybrid arrangements that satisfy multiple orders simultaneously without reducing one to another.
  The "industrial-civic compromise" of mid-twentieth-century Western welfare states is paradigmatic: the state both runs efficient programs (industrial) and represents the collective will (civic).

Compromises are *fragile*: they require ongoing maintenance and can be destabilized by *purifying critiques* that reject the hybrid in favor of one of its components.

## Reception and extension

The framework has been heavily used in:

- **Economic sociology**: David Stark's [stark-2009] *The Sense of Dissonance* applies it to firms, treating organizations as zones of productive friction among multiple orders of worth.
- **Comparative cultural sociology**: Lamont and Thévenot's [lamont-thevenot-2000] edited volume uses the framework to compare repertoires of evaluation across France and the United States.
- **Organization theory**: Cloutier and Langley [cloutier-langley-2013] map the framework onto the institutional-logics literature, treating institutional logics as cognate with orders of worth and noting points of complementarity and tension.
- **Legitimacy studies**: Patriotta, Gond, and Schultz [patriotta-gond-schultz-2011] use the framework to analyze public controversies, treating legitimacy claims as drawing on combinations of orders.

Thévenot's [thevenot-2007] later development moved beyond the public/justification register to study *cognitive formats* and *engagements* at intermediate scales — the "familiar engagement" of intimate relations, the "engagement in a plan" of practical activity — extending the framework into a more comprehensive pragmatic sociology of evaluation.

## Key conceptual distinctions

Three distinctions are doing significant analytical work:

1. **Orders of worth vs. interests, values, or ideologies.** Orders of worth are *test-bearing repertoires of justification*, not preferences (interests), normative commitments (values), or comprehensive worldviews (ideologies).
  They are deployed *situationally* in disputes; the same actor may invoke different orders in different settings.

2. **Justification vs. domination.** The framework was developed in part as a response to Bourdieu-style critical sociology, which Boltanski had previously practiced and increasingly found insufficient.
  Where critical sociology treats justifications as masks for power, pragmatic sociology takes seriously the work of justification as a real activity with real constraints — actors in a dispute cannot say *anything* and have it be accepted as a justification; the order of worth's grammar constrains what counts.

3. **Coexistence vs. reduction.** The orders are *irreducible* to one another — none is the master order from which the others derive.
  Compromises among them are real social achievements, not concealed dominance of one order over others.

## What the tradition supplies

Three distinctive contributions:

1. **A typology of justification repertoires.** The six (or seven, or eight) orders provide a finite, structured vocabulary for the kinds of justification actors actually use in disputes.
  The typology is empirically grounded in close textual analysis and ethnographic study.

2. **A theory of compromise.** The compromise apparatus gives analysts a precise way to describe how multi-order arrangements (welfare states, NGOs, hybrid organizations) are constructed and maintained, and how they can be destabilized.

3. **A non-reductive pluralism.** The framework's commitment that orders are irreducible — no master order, no reduction of one to another — supplies a vocabulary for normative pluralism that does not collapse into relativism (each order has its own grammar and tests) or hierarchical ranking (no order is privileged a priori).
