# Berlant

Lauren Berlant (1957–2021).
Their work analyzes how affect — the pre-cognitive registers of attachment, hope, exhaustion, and endurance — sustains people's participation in social arrangements that are actively harmful to them.
The central concept is cruel optimism: the condition in which the thing you desire is itself an obstacle to your flourishing, and the attachment persists because giving it up would mean losing the structure of desire itself.

Cruel optimism names a specific relation, not a feeling.
The object of attachment (a job, a relationship, a political project, a fantasy of the good life) is cruel not because it fails to deliver but because the attachment to it organizes the subject's sense of possibility — and that organization is what prevents the subject from pursuing conditions that might actually sustain them.
The attachment is not irrational.
It is the rational response to a situation in which the alternatives are worse: losing the attachment means losing the infrastructure of desire that makes the present bearable.

Berlant extended this analysis into a broader account of crisis ordinariness — the condition in which crisis does not arrive as rupture but persists as the ordinary texture of life — and slow death — the wearing out of populations through the management of chronic conditions (obesity, pollution, debt, precarity) that are not dramatic enough to register as catastrophe but cumulative enough to shorten and diminish life.
Affective infrastructure names the emotional and procedural frameworks through which people manage their ongoing attrition.
