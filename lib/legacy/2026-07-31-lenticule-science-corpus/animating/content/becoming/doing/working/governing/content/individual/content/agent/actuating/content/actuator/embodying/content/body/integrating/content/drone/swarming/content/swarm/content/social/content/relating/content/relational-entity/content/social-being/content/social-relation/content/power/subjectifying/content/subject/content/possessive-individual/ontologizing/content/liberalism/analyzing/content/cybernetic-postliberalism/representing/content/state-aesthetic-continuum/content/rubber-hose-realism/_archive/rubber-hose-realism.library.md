**Rubber-hose realism** is a moral-physics regime in which bodies are elastic, harm is reversible, and the world resets between scenes.
It takes its name from the rubber-hose animation style of the 1920s and 1930s — Fleischer's *Out of the Inkwell*, early black-and-white Mickey Mouse — in which characters' limbs bent without joints and their bodies could be stretched, flattened, dismembered, and reassembled within seconds.
The technique is the regime's origin; the regime itself is broader, extending across commercial entertainment, soft power, and overt psychological operations wherever [consequence-free violence](consequence-free-violence.md) is the dominant mechanism.
The regime is "realism" in the ironic sense: it is presented as the natural physics of how things are, not as a stylistic choice.
Subjects who have internalized it experience genuinely consequential harm as if it operated by the same reset logic.

## The five features

**Body elasticity.** Characters' bodies are renewable resources: stretched, compressed, dismembered, and restored without permanent alteration.
The body does not accumulate damage across scene boundaries.

**Death as temporary state.** Death in rubber-hose realism is a punctuation mark rather than a terminal condition: characters die and return; the dead are not mourned, or mourned briefly; absence does not compound.
This is consequence-free violence at the individual scale.

**World reset.** Catastrophic events do not alter the world's capacity to contain subsequent events.
A destroyed city is rebuilt by the next episode; a defeated army reconstitutes; a civilization that ended is replaced by one with the same affordances.
The world does not carry forward what happened in it.

**Violence as spectacle, not evidence.** Violence is maximal, elaborate, and aesthetically prominent — but its prominence is in the register of spectacle rather than evidence.
It does not serve as proof that the world has changed; it serves as an event the audience watches.

**Recursion.** The regime is self-reinforcing through [socially ontogenic media](socially-ontogenic-media.md) distribution.
Each encounter with the regime trains the subject to apply its moral physics; the training makes subsequent encounters feel natural; the naturalness prevents the regime from being perceived as a choice.
The rubber-hose realism subject experiences genuinely consequential harm as if the reset will come, because the reset has always come before.

## Political deployment

The CPL corpus identifies rubber-hose realism's contemporary deployment beyond entertainment in state-aesthetic and influence-operation contexts.
Recruitment media (military, police, institutional) frequently operates in rubber-hose realism: the violence of the institution is shown as spectacular but without the persistence that would make it appear as structural harm.
Soft power projections use rubber-hose aesthetics to make the exercise of force feel episodic and consequence-free.
The animation style that originated in 1920s entertainment has been recognized as a governance aesthetic and redeployed accordingly.

## Relation to the political subject

The most significant political consequence is what rubber-hose realism does to the subject's capacity to think about genuinely irreversible conditions.
Climate destabilization, colonial harm, carceral accumulation — these do not reset.
A subject whose moral physics has been trained by rubber-hose realism carries a residual expectation of reset that makes structural irreversibility feel like a logical impossibility rather than a material condition.
This is the deepest political effect: not that subjects become comfortable with harm, but that they cannot imagine harm that does not eventually undo itself. ---
