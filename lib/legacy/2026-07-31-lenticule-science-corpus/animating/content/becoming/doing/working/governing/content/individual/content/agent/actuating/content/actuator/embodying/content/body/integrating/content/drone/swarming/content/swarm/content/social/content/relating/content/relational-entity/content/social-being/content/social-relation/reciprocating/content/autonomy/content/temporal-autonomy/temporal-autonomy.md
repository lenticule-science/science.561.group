# temporal-autonomy

Temporal autonomy is the condition of collectively determining one's own relationship to time — the capacity of a community to deliberate, rest, celebrate, mourn, and organize on rhythms it sets for itself rather than rhythms dictated by capital, the state, or colonial time-discipline.
Generated by: autonomy — temporal autonomy is self-governance extended to time itself, the recognition that a people that does not control its own tempo does not fully govern its own life.

The concept comes into focus through what is destroyed when dominant institutions impose their own temporalities.
Capitalism imposes the temporality of accumulation: faster turnover, shorter cycles, just-in-time production, the relentless compression of every interval between investment and return.
The state imposes the temporality of administration: fiscal years, electoral cycles, regulatory timelines, the deadline that arrives whether or not the work is ready.
Settler colonialism imposed industrial time-discipline on Indigenous peoples as a technology of dispossession, replacing seasonal, ceremonial, and relational temporalities with the clock, the shift, and the wage — the historian E.P. Thompson traced how this clock-time was drilled into industrial workers in the first place, and colonial schooling and labor regimes carried it outward.
Each of these impositions narrows the temporal space in which alternatives can be organized, lived, and sustained.
To control a people's time is to control the room they have to imagine and build anything else.

The reason temporal autonomy is not a luxury but a structural precondition becomes clear in the practice of consensus decision-making.
Genuine consensus cannot be rushed: hearing every position, working through disagreement, and arriving at a decision everyone can actually live with takes the time it takes.
When a situation demands an immediate response, the structure that answers fastest is the one with a command chain — a single person who can issue an order without consultation.
This is why the demand to "be more efficient" is so often, beneath the surface, a demand to accept hierarchy: efficiency is measured as speed, and speed selects for vertical organization.
The slowness of horizontal process is therefore a feature rather than a defect; it is the time that non-domination costs, and a community that cannot afford that time has already surrendered its autonomy to whoever sets the clock.

This makes temporal autonomy a thread running through the wider field of reciprocal practice.
The refusal of work is in part a claim to temporal autonomy — the insistence on not being productive on capital's schedule.
Direct action operates on its own timeline, neither waiting for institutional permission nor racing to meet an institutional deadline.
Prefigurative politics holds out for the time that non-hierarchical process needs even when urgency presses for shortcuts.
Paul Virilio's study of speed — his "dromology" — argues that the acceleration of production, governance, and war systematically serves the state and capital; temporal autonomy is the counter-claim, the insistence that communities, not institutions, set the rhythm of their own social life.

Within the reciprocal relations, temporal autonomy is a specific dimension of autonomy, alongside knowledge sovereignty: where autonomy is the general capacity of a collective to govern itself, temporal autonomy is that capacity exercised over time, just as knowledge sovereignty is it exercised over knowledge.
It is the everyday ground beneath the slower-built reciprocal relations — consensus, the commons, confederacy — all of which require a community able to take the time that acting together as equals demands.
