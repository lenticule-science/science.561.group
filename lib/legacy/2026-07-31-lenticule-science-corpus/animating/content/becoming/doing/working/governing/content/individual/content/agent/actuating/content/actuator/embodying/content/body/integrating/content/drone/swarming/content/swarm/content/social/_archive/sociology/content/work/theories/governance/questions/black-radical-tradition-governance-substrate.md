# What structural accounts exist in BRT literature for refusal, fugitive governance, and abolition?

The predicated-governance [black-radical-refusal](../../../../../../../humanities/theories/sociology/theories/work/theories/predicated-work/theories/predicated-governance/objects/black-radical-refusal.md) object sketches three governance contributions of the Black Radical Tradition: refusal of normative grammars, fugitive governance, and abolition as positive construction.
This question asks for grounded accounts from BRT scholars that can populate the object's substrate parameters accurately.

## Why sourcing matters here

The BRT's governance insights are active theoretical claims about how governance operates under conditions of systematic oppression and how communities maintain — and create — governing forms outside and against imposed institutional systems.
The object's formal content (blocking cells, lusory subsystems, positive closure-rule replacement) must be derived from what the tradition *actually describes*, not from what a substrate-first approach would assume.
Scholars who carry these traditions have identified structural features that outside formalization could easily miss or distort.

## Specific questions

### On refusal as governance

1. Cedric Robinson's *Black Marxism* (1983) identifies the Black Radical Tradition as a tradition of refusing racial capitalism's normative grammars.
  What structural accounts does Robinson give of how refusal operates as a governance form — specifically, how communities maintained alternative normative orders rather than merely protesting the dominant one?

2. Saidiya Hartman's *Scenes of Subjection* (1997) and *Lose Your Mother* (2007) describe practices of refusal and alternative sociality among enslaved and formerly enslaved people.
  What structural features of governance — authority, accountability, norm-maintenance — appear in her accounts?

3. Are there documented accounts from Robinson, Hartman, or others of what makes refusal *governance* (constituting authority and accountability for a community) rather than merely individual non-compliance?

### On fugitive governance

4. Fred Moten and Stefano Harney's *The Undercommons* (2013) describes "fugitive planning and black study" as governance forms outside and against institutional structures.
  What governance operations — decision-making, norm-setting, accountability — do they describe as occurring in fugitive spaces?

5. What historical accounts exist of maroon community governance: the internal governance structures of communities of escaped enslaved people in the Americas (Palmares in Brazil, Maroon communities in Jamaica and Suriname, Great Dismal Swamp communities)?
  These are documented instances of fugitive governance with actual decision-making structures, authority allocation, and norm-enforcement.
  What are those structures?

6. The Underground Railroad network had coordination and decision-making structures.
  What historical accounts describe those structures?
  How was authority exercised, how were norms maintained, how was accountability managed in a network that had to operate clandestinely?

### On abolition as positive construction

7. Ruth Wilson Gilmore's *Golden Gulag* (2007) and subsequent work articulates abolition as the creation of "life-affirming institutions."
  What specific institutional and governance forms does she describe as constitutive of the abolitionist project?

8. Angela Davis's *Are Prisons Obsolete?* (2003) and *Abolition Democracy* (2005) describe the abolitionist project.
  What governance alternatives does she describe concretely, and what structural accounts does she give of how accountability would operate in abolitionist governance?

9. Robin D.G. Kelley's *Freedom Dreams* (2002) covers the visionary politics of the Black Radical Tradition.
  What governance forms — historical and imagined — does he document as part of that tradition?

### On substrate implications

10. Does the BRT literature contain accounts that would require a different substrate representation than the one sketched in the object? Specifically:
    - Is the refusal-pattern's blocking cell a good substrate representation of what the tradition describes, or does the tradition describe something structurally different?
    - Is the fugitive governance subsystem's lusory-closure representation accurate, or does the tradition's account require modifications?
    - Is abolition-as-positive-construction well-captured by the closure-rule-replacement framing, or is there a deeper structural claim about how governance forms are built and how they displace punitive systems?

## Notes for the answering researcher

Source from BRT scholars themselves — Robinson, Hartman, Moten, Harney, Davis, Gilmore, Kelley, and others within the tradition.
The goal is not to impose a substrate grammar on the tradition but to derive the substrate formalization from what the tradition describes.
If the tradition's accounts do not fit the current substrate vocabulary, that is a finding that should be reported, and the substrate should be revised accordingly.
Community-authorized governance accounts (from maroon communities, contemporary abolitionist organizations) are especially valuable.
