# Genre

A **genre** is a recognised, stable, conventionalised type of communicative artifact: a research paper, a sonnet, a court ruling, a recipe, a software README, a customer-support ticket, a tweet. A genre is identified by its rhetorical purpose, its typical structural moves, its characteristic [register](register.md) range, its participation in a discourse community, and the audience expectations it sets up.

## What a genre commits to

When a contributor writes in a genre, the genre commits to:

1. **A rhetorical purpose.** The communicative work the artifact is supposed to do — argue a position, report a finding, instruct a procedure, request an action.
2. **Structural moves.** A sequence (often partly ordered) of recognisable discourse moves that competent readers expect. A research paper has IMRaD or some local variant; a court ruling has facts-issue-rule-application-conclusion; a recipe has ingredients then steps.
3. **A register range.** The linguistic variety appropriate to the genre. A research paper admits a narrow band of formal registers; a tweet admits a much wider band including casual ones.
4. **Audience expectations.** What competent readers of the genre will look for, will skim past, will treat as boilerplate, and will read closely.
5. **A discourse community.** The group whose conventions the genre instantiates and whose feedback shapes its evolution (Swales 1990).

## Genre, register, and audience

Genre presupposes [register](register.md) and addresses [audience](audience.md): a genre is a stable triple (rhetorical purpose, register range, audience expectations) that a discourse community has conventionalised. Picking the wrong genre — a personal-essay register in a research paper, a research-paper structure in a tweet — is one of the discipline's most visible failure modes.

## Relation to upstream

Carolyn Miller's "Genre as Social Action" (Miller 1984, *Quarterly Journal of Speech*) is the foundational paper for genre-as-rhetorical-action (rather than as taxonomy). Charles Bazerman on the genres of scientific writing (Bazerman 1988, *Shaping Written Knowledge*). John Swales on genre and discourse community (Swales 1990, *Genre Analysis*). The newer Sydney School of systemic-functional genre theory (Martin & Rose 2008, *Genre Relations*) is also relevant.
