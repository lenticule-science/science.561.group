# racialization

Racialization is the process by which a population is produced as a racial category — the potential social and political work through which a group comes to be seen, treated, and governed as a race.
Where race names the system of ranked categories, racialization names the verb: the ongoing operation that extends racial meaning to a people, a practice, or a relationship that was not previously understood in those terms, and that sorts human beings into the slots the racial order maintains.
Generated by: race — racialization is the act that makes the category, the doing behind the thing.

The concept matters because it shifts attention from race as a static set of boxes to race as something continually accomplished.
Populations are racialized into existence and out of it.
The textbook example is the way the Irish, Italians, and Jews in the United States were treated as racially distinct and inferior in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and were later folded into "whiteness" as the politics of labor, immigration, and citizenship changed — they "became white."
The reverse also happens: groups previously unmarked can be racialized when a state or dominant group finds it useful to mark them, as with the racialization of Arabs and Muslims in the United States and Europe after 2001.
In each case the category did not describe a pre-existing natural kind; it was imposed, and the imposition had consequences for who could vote, own property, marry whom, and move freely.

Racialization is typically driven from above — by states, legal regimes, censuses, immigration law, policing, and the classificatory machinery of dominant institutions — which is part of what distinguishes racial categories from ethnic ones that a group may adopt for itself.
It often pairs a classificatory claim ("these people belong to this category") with a narrative content ("and this is what they are like"): the legal or administrative act fixes the boundary while a stock set of stereotypes fills it in.
The two operate together to claim and reduce a population in a single movement, marking it as a kind and ranking that kind below others.

Within this library racialization is the engine beneath the other race concepts.
Racial-formation, in Omi and Winant's sense, is the larger historical process within which particular acts of racialization occur and racial categories gain and lose their content.
Racecraft, in the Fieldses' sense, names what racialization hides from view — the way the act of racializing disappears behind the resulting belief that the races were simply there to be perceived.
To insist on the word racialization is to keep the act in view: race is not a fact about bodies but something done to people, by identifiable institutions, for identifiable ends, and therefore something that can be refused and undone.
