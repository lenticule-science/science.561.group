A navigator is the [office](../../../terms/office.md) responsible for determining the path between where the organization is and where it needs to go.
The office exists because organizations face more possible actions than they can execute, and the selection of which actions to pursue — in what order, with what priority — is itself a skilled function that requires continuous attention.
Without a dedicated navigation function, organizations either drift (executing whatever is nearest) or thrash (changing direction with each new input).

## What the office does

The navigator performs four functions:

1. **Position fixing.** Determining where the organization currently stands relative to its goals.
  Position is not a single metric; it is a structured assessment across multiple dimensions — which objectives have been met, which are in progress, which are blocked, and which have not been started.
  Position fixing requires honest accounting: distinguishing actual progress from planned progress, completed work from claimed work, real capability from projected capability.

2. **Route calculation.** Given a fixed position and a set of goals, computing which sequence of actions gets the organization there with the least waste.
  Route calculation is the [leverage](../../../terms/priority.md) problem: which action makes other actions easier or unnecessary?
  A naive planner asks "what should we do next?"
  The navigator asks "which action opens the most subsequent paths?"

3. **Hazard identification.** Recognizing conditions along the planned route that will impede progress — dependencies that are not met, resources that are not available, conflicts with other organizational work, external conditions that invalidate assumptions.
  Hazard identification is prospective: the navigator looks at the planned route and identifies problems before they are encountered, not after.

4. **Course correction.** When conditions change — a goal is revised, a route becomes impassable, new information changes the leverage calculation — the navigator recalculates and recommends a new path.
  Course correction is not failure; it is the expected response to operating in an environment that changes.
  The navigator's value is in how quickly and accurately she recalculates, not in whether the original route survives contact with reality.

## Organizational lineage

The navigation function in organizations traces to two distinct traditions that converge on the same structural problem.

The first is literal navigation — the maritime tradition of plotting courses, fixing positions by observation, and adjusting for currents and weather.
The navigator's core skill was dead reckoning: maintaining an accurate estimate of position based on known starting point, speed, heading, and elapsed time, then correcting that estimate against observations.
Nathaniel Bowditch's *The New American Practical Navigator* (1802) codified this into systematic method: the navigator does not guess; she computes, observes, and corrects.

The second is operations research — the discipline that emerged in World War II to optimize resource allocation under constraint.
George Dantzig's simplex method (1947) and the subsequent development of linear programming, network flow, and combinatorial optimization formalized the route calculation problem: given a set of objectives, a set of constraints, and a set of possible actions, which sequence of actions optimizes progress toward objectives?
Herbert Simon's concept of "satisficing" (1956) acknowledged that in practice, the optimization is bounded — the navigator computes the best route she can find within the time available, not the globally optimal route.

In modern project management, the function appears as portfolio management — the discipline of selecting and sequencing projects to maximize organizational value.
The navigator is the portfolio manager's predecessor: the function that translates strategic goals into operational sequences.

## Fiber structure

**σ fiber (what the office recognizes):** Organizational position relative to goals — progress, gaps, drift, and divergence.
The navigator recognizes leverage opportunities (actions whose completion unblocks multiple other actions), dead ends (work that consumes resources without advancing goals), drift (gradual divergence between executed work and stated goals), and [horizon](../../../terms/horizon.md) collapse (when long-term goals are sacrificed to short-term urgency).
Recognition also covers route invalidation — when a planned sequence of actions has become impossible or counterproductive due to changed conditions.

**Δ fiber (what the office produces):** Route plans — sequenced sets of actions with dependencies, priorities, and expected outcomes.
Position reports — structured assessments of where the organization stands relative to its goals.
Leverage analyses — evaluations of which available actions produce the most progress per unit of effort.
Course corrections — revised plans that respond to changed conditions.
The navigator does not execute the plan — execution is the work of the relevant operational offices — but she specifies what should be done and in what order.

## Formality level

**F1 — low formality.** The navigation function is advisory.
The navigator produces routes and recommends priorities; the [captain](../../../terms/captain.md) decides which route to take.
This separation is deliberate and load-bearing: the navigator optimizes for goal-progress; the captain balances goal-progress against all other organizational concerns (morale, risk tolerance, political constraints, values).
A navigator with command authority would optimize the organization into a goal-seeking missile, which is not what organizations are for.
The navigator's power is analytical: she can demand information about organizational state from any office (a diagnostic privilege shared with the [chief medical officer](./chief-medical-officer.md)) but cannot compel action.

## What distinguishes the navigator

**From the [planner](../../../terms/plan.md).** A plan is a structured sequence of actions to achieve a specific objective.
The navigator operates at a higher level: she selects which plans to pursue, sequences plans against each other, and determines when a plan should be abandoned in favor of a different route.
Planning is within-objective; navigation is across-objectives.

**From the captain.** The [captain](../../../terms/captain.md) decides; the navigator advises.
The captain asks "where should we go?" and "should we change course?" — these are command decisions.
The navigator answers "if we want to reach X, the best route from here is Y" — this is analysis.
The captain may override the navigator's recommended route for reasons the navigator's model does not capture (political constraints, values, risk appetite).
The navigator accepts this without resentment; her function is to provide the best analysis, not to command.

**From the [tactical officer](./tactical-officer.md).** The tactical officer faces threats — conditions that could compromise integrity.
The navigator faces choices — conditions that require selecting among possible paths.
The tactical officer asks "what could go wrong?"
The navigator asks "what should we do next?"
A threat can change the route (a hazard that makes a planned path impassable), and the navigator incorporates tactical assessments into route calculation, but the diagnostic orientations differ.
