# state-of-exception

The state of exception is the suspension of the normal legal order by sovereign decision — the emergency in which ordinary law is set aside, yet authority continues to act, so that the law is in force but its protective content is hollowed out.
Generated by: the state of exception issues from sovereignty, for it is exactly the decision to suspend the law that, on Carl Schmitt's account, reveals where sovereign power truly lies.
Martial law, emergency decrees, the suspension of habeas corpus, the indefinite detention of those deemed dangerous — these are its everyday faces, the moments when a government claims the right to act outside its own rules while still claiming to act as authority rather than as a usurper.

Schmitt's *Political Theology* (1922) placed the concept at the heart of legal and political theory.
Liberal constitutionalism, he argued, dreams of a legal order so complete that nothing falls outside it, but every order confronts cases its norms cannot anticipate, and someone must decide what to do when the norms run out.
That someone is the sovereign, who stands at once inside the order (as its supreme authority) and outside it (as the power who can suspend it).
The exception is therefore not a mere gap or failure but the structuring limit-case that exposes the true nature of sovereignty: "sovereign is he who decides on the exception."
For Schmitt the decision, not the rule, is primary — a deliberately anti-liberal claim, given grim demonstration when the Weimar Republic's own emergency article was used to dismantle it.

Giorgio Agamben took up and radicalized this analysis in *Homo Sacer* (1995) and *State of Exception* (2005).
His thesis is that the exception has ceased to be exceptional.
The temporary suspension that Schmitt theorized as a borderline case has, in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, become a durable technique of government — emergency rule that does not end, security legislation that normalizes the extraordinary, whole populations governed under a permanent suspension of ordinary guarantees.
Agamben locates the paradigm in the *camp*: a space where the normal legal order is withdrawn and human beings are reduced to "bare life," included in the political order only through their exclusion from its protections, killable without the act counting as a crime.
The refugee camp, the detention center, the offshore black site, the militarized border zone are his contemporary instances.

The stakes are considerable, because the state of exception marks the precise point at which the distinction between authority and naked force threatens to collapse.
So long as the suspension is genuinely temporary, contestable, and answerable, it can be defended as the legal order's own provision for its survival.
When it becomes permanent and unreviewable, the form of legality remains — officials act, documents are issued, the language of law persists — but its substance is gone, and what is left is power that has freed itself from the rules it claims to embody.
This is why critics treat the proliferation of emergency powers, surveillance regimes, and indefinite detention not as deviations from the rule of law but as a mode of rule in their own right.

In this cluster the state of exception is the dark hinge between sovereignty and bare life.
It is sovereignty's most revealing act — the proof of supremacy through the power to switch off the law — and the operation that, under the regime of biopower, produces lives caught in the order only by being abandoned by it, neither fully inside its protections nor placed cleanly outside its reach.
