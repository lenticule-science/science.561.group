A counselor is the [office](../../../terms/office.md) responsible for the fit between officers and their [duties](./duty.md) — whether the right agents are in the right positions, whether they have the [skills](../../../terms/skills.md) their assignments require, and whether the overall crew composition is adequate to the organization's needs.
The office exists because assignment and capability drift apart over time.
An officer installed when the work was simple may be overwhelmed when it grows complex.
A crew composed for one mission may be mismatched for the next.

## What the office does

The counselor performs three functions:

1. **Officer-duty fit assessment.** Evaluating whether the current holder of an office has the capability signature that the office's [role](./role.md) requires.
  Fit is not a fixed property — it changes as duties change, as officers develop or degrade, and as the organizational context shifts.
  The counselor tracks this moving target.
  A capability signature specifies what a role demands; the counselor's job is to assess whether the current bearer operatively satisfies it or whether gaps have opened.

2. **Training recommendation.** When fit is poor, the counselor distinguishes between cases where the officer should be reassigned (structural misfit — the capability signature cannot be acquired in reasonable time) and cases where the officer should be developed (gap — the capability can be acquired with specific investment).
  Training recommendations are prescriptive: not "this officer needs to improve" but "this officer needs to acquire capability $p$ by practicing $q$."

3. **Crew composition analysis.** Evaluating whether the set of filled offices and roles covers the organization's needs.
  Coverage gaps — positions that are vacant, understaffed, or filled by officers stretched across too many roles — are the counselor's diagnostic domain at the organizational level.
  This is the complement of individual fit assessment: individual assessment asks "does this officer fit this role?"; composition analysis asks "does this crew cover these missions?"

## Organizational lineage

The role traces to the personnel officer in military organizations and the human resources function in corporate governance, but both of these have been diluted by administrative accretion.
The counselor function — specifically, the assessment of person-position fit — has deeper roots in industrial-organizational psychology.

Hugo Münsterberg's *Psychology and Industrial Efficiency* (1913) formalized the matching problem: given a set of tasks and a set of people, which assignment produces the best outcome?
The answer required measuring both sides — task demands and human capabilities — and constructing a fit function.
Frederick Winslow Taylor's scientific management (1911) attempted this crudely through time-and-motion study.
The counselor function is the organizational expression of the matching problem, refined beyond Taylor's mechanical approach to include learning capacity, role conflict, and developmental trajectory.

Talcott Parsons' pattern variables (1951) provide the structural distinction: the counselor's domain is the interface between *achievement* (what the officer can do) and *ascription* (what the office requires by virtue of its position).
The counselor navigates the tension between these — an officer may be ascriptively placed (installed in the office) while achievement-deficient (lacking the capability the role demands), and the counselor must name this gap without dissolving the distinction between office and role.

## Fiber structure

**σ fiber (what the office recognizes):** Capability gaps — mismatches between an officer's demonstrated capabilities and the capability signature of their assigned role.
The counselor recognizes skill decay (an officer who could once perform a function but has lost the ability through disuse), role overload (an officer bearing more roles than can be simultaneously executed without conflict), developmental readiness (an officer who is approaching the capability threshold for a more demanding role), and compositional gaps (roles that are structurally necessary but unfilled or poorly filled).

**Δ fiber (what the office produces):** Fit assessments — structured evaluations of officer-role alignment with specific gap identification.
Training plans — prescriptions for capability acquisition, with priorities and sequences.
Reassignment recommendations — proposals to move officers between positions when fit is irremediable through training.
Crew composition reports — analyses of whether the organization's office-filling pattern covers its operational needs.

## Formality level

**F1 — low formality.** The counselor function is advisory.
The counselor produces assessments and recommendations; she does not have the authority to reassign officers or to compel training.
Reassignment is a command function — it requires the [captain](../../../terms/captain.md)'s authority.
Training investment is a resource function — it requires the quartermaster's allocation.
The counselor's power is diagnostic and prescriptive, not executive.
This is deliberate: separating assessment from execution prevents the counselor from optimizing for easy-to-fix gaps rather than important ones.

## What distinguishes the counselor

**From the [chief medical officer](./chief-medical-officer.md).** The chief medical officer assesses system health — whether components are degrading.
The counselor assesses person-position fit — whether the right people are in the right positions.
A healthy officer in the wrong role is the counselor's problem.
A degrading system component is the chief medical officer's problem.
The two functions overlap when an officer is degrading *because* of role misfit (burnout from overload, skill atrophy from underuse), but the diagnostic frame differs.

**From the [reviewer](../../../terms/review.md).** A review evaluates output — whether the work product meets quality standards.
The counselor evaluates the relationship between the producer and the production — whether the officer has the capability to produce the output reliably.
A review that finds poor output triggers the question of whether this is a fit problem (counselor's domain) or a process problem (engineering domain).
