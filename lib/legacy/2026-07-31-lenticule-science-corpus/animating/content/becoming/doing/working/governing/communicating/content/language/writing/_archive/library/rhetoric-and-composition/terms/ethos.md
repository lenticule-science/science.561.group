***Ethos*** is the rhetorical appeal to the speaker's or writer's credibility — the quality that makes an [audience](./audience.md) trust the communicator. Aristotle identified ethos as one of three means of persuasion, alongside [pathos](./pathos.md) (emotion) and [logos](./logos.md) (logic). In many contexts, ethos is the most powerful: an audience that doesn't trust the speaker won't be moved by logic or emotion.

Ethos is not a fixed attribute of the writer. It is constructed in the writing itself through:

- **Demonstrated knowledge** — the writer shows familiarity with the subject through specific detail, accurate claims, and engagement with relevant sources.
- **Fairness** — the writer acknowledges opposing views, represents them honestly, and addresses them seriously. [Wayne Booth](../../../../../../references/people/wayne-booth.md) argued that the willingness to engage with disagreement is the foundation of rhetorical trust [@booth1961].
- **Appropriate [tone](./tone.md) and [register](../../../terms/register.md)** — the writer sounds like someone who belongs in the conversation. Overformality signals insecurity; casualness in a formal context signals disrespect.
- **Clarity** — clear writing builds trust because it suggests the writer has nothing to hide. The [plain language](../../plain-language/terms/plain-language-movement.md) tradition is, at root, an ethos strategy: writing plainly says "I respect your time and intelligence."

In [copywriting](../disciplines/copywriting/index.md), ethos takes the form of [social proof](../../copywriting/terms/social-proof.md), testimonials, credentials, and specificity. In [essay writing](../disciplines/essay-writing/index.md), it takes the form of careful citation, fair [concession](../../essay-writing/terms/concession.md), and command of evidence. In [technical writing](../disciplines/technical-writing/index.md), it takes the form of accuracy, tested procedures, and professional formatting.
