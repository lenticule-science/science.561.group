Scansion is the analytic technique for marking, on the page, which syllables of a line of [metrical](./meter.md) verse are stressed and which are unstressed, so that the [meter](./meter.md) the line is keeping — and the places it departs from that meter — can be read off explicitly.
The word comes from Latin *scandere* ("to climb"), via the medieval grammarians' practice of climbing a line foot by foot to verify its measure.

To scan a line is to make its metrical contract visible.
The reader who can scan can say with precision: *this is iambic pentameter, with a trochaic substitution in the first foot and a feminine ending after the fifth.*
The reader who cannot scan can hear that the line is metrical and feel the substitutions, but cannot name what is happening or why a different line at a different point in the poem is doing something subtly different.

## The marks

The standard scansion marks date to nineteenth-century classical pedagogy and have not changed substantially since.

- A **breve** ( ˘ ) over a syllable marks it as unstressed.
- A **macron** ( ¯ ), or sometimes an acute accent, marks a syllable as stressed.
- A **vertical bar** ( | ) divides one [foot](./foot.md) from the next.
- A **double bar** ( ‖ ) marks a [caesura](./caesura.md), a deliberate pause inside the line.

A scanned line of iambic pentameter looks roughly like this:

```
  ˘   ¯  |  ˘   ¯  |  ˘   ¯  |  ˘   ¯  |  ˘    ¯
Shall I | com-PARE | thee TO | a SUM | -mer's DAY
```

In practice, scholars use whichever marking system is cleanest in their typesetting environment.
Capital letters for stressed syllables and lowercase for unstressed (*shall I com-PARE thee TO a SUM-mer's DAY*) is common in informal contexts and in introductory texts.

## Scansion is interpretation

Scansion is not a mechanical transcription of the line.
It is an interpretation — a claim about how the line should be heard.
Many lines admit more than one defensible scansion, and the disagreement is itself substantive.
Is the second foot of "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day" *com-PARE* (an iamb) or *COM-pare* (a trochee)?
The first reading keeps the meter; the second creates a substitution that emphasizes the act of comparison.
A skilled scanner does not simply count beats but argues for a hearing of the line.

This is why scansion is taught as a skill rather than a procedure.
Two readers can scan the same line two different ways, and the difference between their scansions is the difference between their readings of the poem.

## Why scansion matters

A reader who learns to scan acquires a tool the unaided ear cannot replace.
The ear notices that some lines feel different from others, but it does not always know why.
Scansion makes the *why* explicit.
A line that feels slow and weighty often turns out, on scansion, to contain a spondee or a cluster of stressed syllables.
A line that feels rushed often turns out to contain anapestic substitutions or a series of unstressed syllables in close succession.
A line that feels broken often turns out to have a [caesura](./caesura.md) falling at a metrically unexpected point.
Scansion turns a vague impression into a specific observation, and a specific observation can be checked, contested, and built on.

The skill rewards practice.
A reader who has scanned a hundred lines hears patterns the unscanning reader misses, and reads metrical poetry with proportionally more pleasure.

## What scansion is not

Scansion is not the same as recitation.
A scanned line is not a script for how to read the line aloud.
A reader who delivered every stressed syllable as a thumping beat would produce a parody of metrical reading, not a competent one.
Spoken delivery softens the metrical contract — it lets the meter run underneath natural speech rather than over it.
Scansion exposes the contract; recitation honors it without exposing it.
Both are needed, and they are different activities.

Scansion is also not a verdict on a poem's quality.
A perfectly regular line is not better than a line full of substitutions, and a line full of substitutions is not better than a regular one.
What scansion reveals is what the line is doing; the question of whether what it is doing serves the poem is a separate, harder question that scansion cannot settle on its own.
