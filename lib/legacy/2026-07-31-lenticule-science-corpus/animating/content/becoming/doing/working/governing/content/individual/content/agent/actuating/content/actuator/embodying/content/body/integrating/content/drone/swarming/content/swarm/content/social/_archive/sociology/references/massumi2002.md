Brian Massumi. *Parables for the Virtual: Movement, Affect, Sensation*.
Durham, NC: Duke University Press, 2002.
ISBN 978-0-8223-2897-7.

## Status

Skimmed; central reference for the autonomy-of-affect strand of [affect-theory](theories/critical-theory/theories/affect-theory/).

## Annotation

Massumi's case for the autonomy of affect: affect is not emotion.
Emotion is the captured, qualified, narrated form that registers in cognition and discourse; affect is the pre-cognitive intensity in the body before that capture.
The distinction is methodological — it asks the analyst to attend to what bodies do before what bodies say — and ontological — Massumi takes seriously that there is a register of social life that operates below the threshold of meaning and that cannot be reduced to the meanings that subsequently form.

The book draws on Spinoza (via Deleuze) for the conception of affect as variation in a body's power to act, on Bergson for the analysis of duration and movement, and on a wide reading across cybernetics, philosophy of science, and political theory.
The political stakes are present throughout: affect is one of the registers through which contemporary power operates, and ideology critique that ignores it misses the bulk of what holds compliance in place.

Massumi's account has been contested — Ruth Leys's "The Turn to Affect" (2011) argues that the strict autonomy thesis depends on a misreading of the neuroscience Massumi cites — and the contestation is part of the literature.
