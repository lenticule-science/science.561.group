**Scientific management** is Frederick Winslow Taylor's program for the systematic study and reorganization of industrial work, formulated in his consulting practice with U.S. machine shops and steel mills from the 1880s onward and most influentially codified in [The Principles of Scientific Management](taylor1911) and the earlier [Shop Management](taylor1903).
The program combined an empirical methodology (time-and-motion study), an organizational prescription (the separation of planning from execution), a compensation structure (differential piece rates), and an explicit ideology (the cooperative-not-conflictual character of properly-organized work).

## Taylor's four principles

Taylor's [1911 text](taylor1911) summarizes the program in four principles:

1. **Develop a science** for each element of work, replacing rule-of-thumb methods with systematic study (time-and-motion analysis of the operations, materials, and instruments).
2. **Scientifically select and train workers**, replacing the old practice in which workers chose their own work and trained themselves.
3. **Cooperate with workers** to ensure the scientifically developed methods are followed (wage incentives, functional foremanship, the "first-class man").
4. **Divide work and responsibility** between management (planning) and workers (execution): "the managers assume new burdens, new duties, and responsibilities never dreamed of in the past."

The fourth principle — *the separation of conception from execution* — is what later critics (most notably [Braverman](braverman1974)) identify as scientific management's defining political move.
Taylor presented it as cooperation; Braverman analyzed it as [deskilling](deskilling.md).

## The methodology

The empirical core of Taylorism was time-and-motion study.
A trained observer (the time-and-motion engineer) decomposed the worker's activity into elementary motions, measured each with a stopwatch under varying conditions, identified the *one best way* to perform each motion, and aggregated the optimized motions into a procedure that the worker would then be required to follow.
The aggregated procedure typically reduced cycle times by 30–80% in the cases Taylor and his disciples (Frank and Lillian Gilbreth, Henry Gantt, Carl Barth) reported.
Whether the cycle-time reductions were sustainable, whether they reflected genuine technical improvements or simply intensified labor, and whether the workers who executed them benefited from them in any way other than higher piece rates were contested then and remain contested.

## The diffusion

Taylor's influence was global and rapid.
By the 1920s, "scientific management" — sometimes under different names (Bedaux system, Bureau of Personnel Administration techniques, the Soviet *NOT* movement, German *Rationalisierung*, Japanese efficiency engineering) — was being applied across:

- U.S. industrial production (Ford's River Rouge being the canonical case, though Ford's *Fordism* extended Taylor in important ways).
- Soviet industrial planning, where Lenin explicitly endorsed Taylor's methods (separating Taylor's "scientific" methodology from its capitalist application) and where the Stakhanovite movement of the 1930s extended the rate-busting logic into a state-organized program.
- Italian fascist corporatism and German rationalization movements of the 1920s and 1930s.
- Mid-twentieth-century U.S. clerical and service-sector work, extended through industrial-engineering departments and management consultancies.

The descendants of scientific management include time-and-motion study (now industrial engineering), work measurement (predetermined motion time systems like MTM and MOST), lean production (where the *kaizen* methodology updates the Taylorist time-and-motion logic for the post-Fordist setting), and contemporary call-center work measurement and warehouse logistics.

## Distinguished from

- **Bureaucracy** ([Weber](bureaucracy.md)) — bureaucracy is an administrative form characterized by rule-bound impersonal authority across the whole organization; scientific management is a methodology specifically for the technical organization of *production work*.
  Bureaucratic and Taylorist principles often co-occur but address different problems.
- **Fordism** — Fordism extends Taylor through the moving assembly line (which paces work mechanically rather than through wage incentive), through the high-wage / mass-consumption coupling Taylor did not theorize, and through the vertical integration of production.
  Taylor focused on the labor-process internals; Ford on the integrated production-and-consumption regime.
- **Bureaucratic control** ([Edwards](manufacturing-consent.md)) — Edwards distinguishes *technical control* (the assembly line's pacing) from *bureaucratic control* (rule-bound personnel systems).
  Scientific management is part of the *technical-control* lineage.
- **Lean production / Toyotism** — lean production retains Taylor's methodology of detailed work analysis and standardized procedures while ostensibly returning to workers the discretion Taylorism took away (through quality circles, kaizen suggestions, andon cords).
  Whether lean genuinely reverses Taylorist deskilling or extends it under more participatory rhetoric is contested.

## Reception and reputation

Scientific management's reputation collapsed dramatically through the twentieth century.
By the 1970s, "Taylorism" was a term of opprobrium in management discourse, associated with mechanistic, dehumanizing work design that the human-relations school, the socio-technical-systems school, and the lean-production school had all ostensibly transcended.
Robert Kanigel's [The One Best Way](kanigel1997) supplied the standard biographical study, documenting both Taylor's substantive contribution and what Kanigel and other historians (Wrege and Greenwood, Wrege and Perroni, Stewart) have argued is a substantial gap between Taylor's self-presentation and his actual practice — including, on these historians' analyses, exaggeration and in some cases fabrication of his most-cited case-study results (notably the Schmidt pig-iron loading study).

The *substantive practice* of scientific management, however, has continued and intensified.
Contemporary algorithmic-management systems — treated in the [platform-and-algorithmic-work-survey](../texts/platform-and-algorithmic-work-survey.md) — implement Taylorist methodology with vastly more granular measurement and vastly less worker recourse than Taylor's stopwatch-and-instruction-card era permitted.
