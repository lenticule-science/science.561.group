Kurdistan provides a case study in four-state genring: a single living people subjected simultaneously to four incompatible genre figures, each produced by a different state apparatus (Turkish, Iraqi, Syrian, Iranian), each designed to serve that apparatus's specific political needs, and each internally coherent while being incompatible with the others.
The Kurd of the Turkish state's genre bears no resemblance to the Kurd of the Iraqi state's genre, the Syrian state's genre, or the Iranian state's genre — and none of the four resembles the actual heterogeneous Kurdish populations they purport to represent.

This four-state genring is an extreme case, but its extreme character makes the mechanics visible with unusual clarity.
What follows analyzes each of the four genre figures, identifies the political function each serves, and then identifies what all four share in common — the structural operation that the four incompatible genres are all performing.

## The Turkish state's genre: Mountain Turks

The Turkish state's genre figure for Kurdish-speaking people is historically the *Mountain Turk*: a population that is essentially Turkish, that happens to have developed a mountain dialect, but whose Turkish identity is the authentic core that proper governance can restore.
This genre served the Turkish state's homogeneity project — the project of producing a unified Turkish national identity in which the Ottoman Empire's ethnic heterogeneity was replaced by a coherent national type.

The Mountain Turk figure performs several political functions: it denies the existence of a Kurdish people as such (there are only Mountain Turks who have been misled about their identity), it provides a path for individual Kurds to achieve full political standing (by accepting their Turkish identity), and it frames Kurdish cultural and political expression as a mistake or a manipulation rather than as authentic political claim.

The Mountain Turk genre requires the suppression of whatever makes the Kurdish heteroglossia visible: language, cultural practice, political organization, historical narrative.
The suppression is not only coercive; it is also representational.
The genre makes the living Kurdish community illegible as Kurdish — it can only be seen as Mountain Turkish, or as political agitation, or as foreign manipulation.

## The Iraqi state's genre: Loyal Minority or Terrorist Threat

The Iraqi state's genre figure for Kurdish-speaking people has operated in two modes, switching between them according to political context: the *Loyal Minority* and the *Terrorist Threat*.
The Loyal Minority figure portrays the Kurdish community as a protected ethnic minority within the Arab nationalist state, whose ethnic distinctiveness is acknowledged and whose political loyalty to the state is the condition of protection.
The Terrorist Threat figure portrays the Kurdish community as a fifth column, as foreign-backed destabilizers, as enemies of Arab unity.

The switching between these two genre modes is politically functional: the Loyal Minority mode is activated when the state wants to signal pluralism and when the Kurdish community's military or economic resources are useful; the Terrorist Threat mode is activated when the state wants to justify military operations or when the Kurdish community's political aspirations conflict with state priorities.
The switching itself is structurally important — it means the Kurdish community can never achieve stable political standing, because the genre can always be switched.

## The Syrian state's genre: Stateless Irregulars

The Syrian state's genre figure for Kurdish-speaking people is the *Stateless Irregular*: a population without legitimate territorial claim, present in Syria but not properly Syrian, whose political organization is a security problem and whose cultural existence is tolerable only within the limits the state sets.
The Stateless Irregular figure performs the specific function of denying citizenship claims: the genre positions the population as guests rather than members, as temporary presences whose claims are contingent on state tolerance.

The Syrian state denied citizenship to hundreds of thousands of Kurdish residents through administrative processes that used the genre to disqualify claims — identifying the Kurdish community as stateless (and thus not entitled to Syrian citizenship) rather than as a national minority with claims of prior residence.

## The Iranian state's genre: Tribal Periphery

The Iranian state's genre figure for Kurdish-speaking people is the *Tribal Periphery*: a population of tribal communities in the mountainous western border regions, authentically tribal in their social organization and therefore appropriately governed through tribal customary arrangements, not through the fully civil governance appropriate to the urban Iranian population.

The Tribal Periphery genre performs the function of the temporal-middle elimination: the living Kurdish population of Iran — urban, educated, politically sophisticated, with a complex internal diversity — is forced into a genre that represents them as pre-modern tribal communities.
Whatever does not fit this genre — the Kurdish lawyer, the Kurdish academic, the Kurdish women's organization, the Kurdish labor movement — is illegible within the genre.

## What all four genres share

Despite their incompatibility with each other, the four genre figures share the same structural operation: the elimination of the [temporal middle](../teaching/explanations/temporal-middle.md).

Each genre requires the Kurdish community to occupy a position defined by stasis — Mountain Turk (essentially Turkish, not a developing distinct culture), Loyal Minority / Terrorist Threat (defined entirely by relation to the Arab state's needs), Stateless Irregular (without political development or historical claim), Tribal Periphery (pre-modern, not-yet-developing).
In each genre, the Kurdish community cannot be a living community that inherits, develops, and transforms its tradition.
It can only be a fixed type whose characteristics are defined by the state apparatus's needs.

The four genres are incompatible as descriptions but compatible as political operations.
Each performs the elimination of the temporal middle; each renders the Kurdish community's self-determined development illegible; each produces a genre figure that serves the state apparatus's requirement that the Kurdish community not achieve political standing as a living people with historical claims.

## The [soft surrealism](../teaching/explanations/soft-surrealism.md) the four-state genring produces

When all four genre figures circulate simultaneously in international discourse about the Kurdish situation — when international media draw on all four state narratives, when academic analysis is conditioned by access to state archives that encode the four genres, when geopolitical analysis treats each state's genre as the relevant frame for that state's territory — the result is a specific form of [soft surrealism](../teaching/explanations/soft-surrealism.md).

The international observer who has encountered all four genres knows that the Kurd in Turkish political discourse bears no resemblance to the Kurd in Iraqi political discourse, which bears no resemblance to the Kurd in Syrian or Iranian political discourse.
The observer knows that the genre figures are genres — that they are not descriptions of a single people but political productions serving incompatible state interests.
But the observer also has no access to the Kurdish heteroglossia itself except through the state genres that have substituted for it.
The result is the characteristic soft-surrealist condition: the observer knows the genre is a genre, but has no position from which to see what the genre has substituted for.

This is not unique to Kurdistan.
It is the condition the four-state genring produces in extreme form, making visible what genring produces more subtly in cases where only one state apparatus is producing the genre.
