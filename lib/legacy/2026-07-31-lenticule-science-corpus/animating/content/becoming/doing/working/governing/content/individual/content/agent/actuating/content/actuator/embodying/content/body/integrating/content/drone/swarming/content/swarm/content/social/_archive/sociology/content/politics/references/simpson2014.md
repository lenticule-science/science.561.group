# Simpson 2014 — Mohawk Interruptus

**Simpson, Audra.
(2014). *Mohawk Interruptus: Political Life Across the Borders of Settler States*.
Durham, NC: Duke University Press.**

## Annotation

Audra Simpson is a member of the Kahnawà:ke Mohawk community and a political and cultural anthropologist at Columbia University. *Mohawk Interruptus* combines political theory with ethnographic work among the Kahnawà:ke Mohawks, a reserve community in what is now southwestern Quebec, examining how they sustain political sovereignty through centuries of settler colonialism by refusing American or Canadian citizenship.

The book develops "refusal" into a generative political and methodological concept.
Politically, refusal is the everyday work of declining to be absorbed into the settler state's category of citizen — refusing the passport, refusing the recognition, refusing the apologies that come as substitutes for the return of land.
Methodologically, refusal is also a stance the ethnographer can take: refusing to write in ways that would compromise tribal sovereignty.

Simpson's central theoretical contribution is the demonstration that *one sovereign political order can exist nested within a sovereign state* — not as a federal subdivision, but as a separate polity whose sovereignty is in continual tension with the encompassing settler state's claims.
The argument generalizes beyond Kahnawà:ke to the structural condition of Indigenous nations within settler states across North America and elsewhere.

## For the politics domain

The book is the primary reference for treating *refusal* as a polity-constituting move, not merely a tactic.
Refusal here is the structural means by which Indigenous sovereignty is exercised within and against settler-colonial encompassment.
It is also the primary reference for [nested sovereignty](teaching/explanations/sovereignty.md) as a structural condition.

## Relationship to Lakota material

Simpson writes from Kahnawà:ke Mohawk experience, not Lakota.
The framework, however, is directly relevant to contemporary Lakota political life: the refusal frame illuminates the structural meaning of Lakota refusals — of the Black Hills compensation award, of certain forms of federal recognition, of incorporation into settler-state political categories.
See [lakotaean-politics](theories/lakotaean-politics/_index.md) for the Lakota-specific treatment; that subtheory's authority rests with Lakota knowledge holders, with Simpson's framework cited rather than transposed.
