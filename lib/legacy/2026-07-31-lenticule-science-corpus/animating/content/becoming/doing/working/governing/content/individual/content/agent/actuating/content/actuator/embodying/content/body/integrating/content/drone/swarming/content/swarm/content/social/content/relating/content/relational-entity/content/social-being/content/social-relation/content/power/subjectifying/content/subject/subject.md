# subject

Generated by: power, subjectifying — power is productive, not merely repressive: it makes subjects, it does not only constrain them.

A subject, in Michel Foucault's sense, is a person as produced by the institutions, practices, and discourses that act on them.
The word carries a deliberate double meaning that Foucault exploits.
A subject is both the one who acts — the thinking, willing subject of philosophy — and the one who is acted upon — the subject of a king, the subject of an experiment.
Foucault used the term to name this double bind: people are constituted as agents through the very same processes that constitute them as objects of power.
In his 1982 essay "The Subject and Power," he described the central concern of his work as "not power, but the subject" — the question of how human beings are made into subjects through specific historical practices.

Subjectification (or subjectivation) names that making.
It is the production of subjects through practices of classification, examination, and normalization.
The school does not receive a ready-made "student"; it produces one through enrollment, placement testing, grading, and daily routine, and the student's self-understanding — "I'm good at math," "I'm a troublemaker" — takes shape inside the categories the institution uses.
The prison does not receive a "criminal"; it produces a "delinquent," a type of person with a profile, a case file, and a predicted trajectory.
The hospital does not receive simply a sick person; it produces a patient, a diagnostic case, a data point in an epidemiological pattern.
In each instance the institution does not encounter a finished self and then constrain it; it brings a particular kind of self into being.

Subjectification works along two axes at once.
The first is categorization by others: the institutional classifications — delinquent, gifted, normal, pathological — that position a person within a field of knowledge and governance.
The second is self-formation: the ways people come to understand themselves through the categories institutions make available.
The person who lives through a psychiatric diagnosis, who reads their own worth through a credit score, who interprets their desires through the available identity categories — each is participating in their own subjectification.
Foucault is careful to insist that this is not false consciousness and that the subject so produced is entirely real.
The point is that it is produced rather than given, contingent rather than necessary: the categories through which people are known, and through which they know themselves, are artifacts of institutional practice, not descriptions of what people simply are.

Two clarifications keep the concept from collapsing into a theory of oppression.
First, subjectification is not the same as domination, even though it can involve it: a person can be subjectified as capable, healthy, or normal — categories that carry privilege — and the mechanism is the same.
Power does not only say "you may not do that"; it says "you are this kind of person," and it produces the knowledge that makes the claim stick.
Second, because the subject is made through power/knowledge and discourse — and is intelligible only inside a discourse — to contest a regime of power is partly to contest the subject-forms it produces.
This is the sense in which Foucault's subject is the productive face of power, the point where power, knowledge, and discourse converge to constitute selves.
The liberal specification of this subject — the individual imagined as the proprietor of its own person — is taken up under the heading of the possessive individual.
