# power-knowledge

Generated by: power — power is not merely repressive but productive: the knowing that classifies, examines, and documents is itself an exercise of power.

Power/knowledge (*pouvoir/savoir*) is Michel Foucault's thesis that power relations and fields of knowledge are mutually constitutive.
The two are not merely correlated; they imply each other.
There is no knowledge that is not produced within relations of power, and no exercise of power that does not at the same time produce knowledge.
Foucault developed the idea most directly in *Discipline and Punish* (1975) and across his Collège de France lectures and interviews of the 1970s, collected in English as *Power/Knowledge* (1980).
The thesis cuts against two common assumptions at once: that knowledge is neutral (scientists discover facts; politicians may misuse them) and that knowledge is inherently liberating (the more we know, the freer we become).
Both, for Foucault, miss the entanglement.

The examination is the paradigmatic mechanism.
As practiced in the school, the clinic, the prison, and the workplace, the examination is simultaneously an exercise of power over the examined subject and a method of producing knowledge about that subject.
The teacher who gives a test exercises authority over the student and produces data — scores, rankings, classifications — that become part of the student's institutional identity.
The physician who examines a patient exercises clinical authority and produces diagnostic knowledge that determines treatment.
In each case the knowledge is not incidental to the power; it is the very mechanism through which the power operates.
To know a person, in these settings, is already to position them within a field that can act on them.

The knowledge produced through such procedures feeds back into techniques of management and classification.
Test scores generate categories — gifted, average, remedial — that channel students into different institutional tracks.
Diagnostic categories — neurotic, psychotic, personality-disordered — determine what treatments are administered and by whom.
Criminological profiles — the recidivist, the juvenile delinquent — shape sentencing, surveillance, and rehabilitation.
These categories are not neutral descriptions of pre-existing types; they are products of the institutional practices that observe and shape their objects at the same time.
The category and the population it names come into being together.

This is why Foucault treats power/knowledge as structural rather than personal, and why it carries real political stakes.
Contesting a regime of power requires contesting the knowledge that sustains it: one cannot dismantle the prison while leaving intact the criminological categories it produced, or challenge psychiatric authority while accepting the diagnostic framework that constitutes it.
The categories outlast the individuals who occupy positions of authority because they are embedded in training programs, professional norms, case files, and databases.
Power/knowledge is the apparatus through which power produces subjects (subjectification), trains bodies (discipline), and, at the level of populations, governs life (biopower); discourse is the medium in which it operates.
It is Foucault's standing answer to the question of how domination can work without a villain — through the ordinary, respectable machinery of knowing.
