# boundary

A boundary is the socially produced and policed line between an entity or group and what lies outside it — the distinction between us and them, member and stranger, inside and outside, that marks where one social formation ends and another begins.
Boundaries are not natural edges waiting to be noticed; they are made, maintained, and enforced through ongoing social work, and they can be drawn in different places by different people for different purposes.
Generated by: **relational-entity** — a boundary is the interface through which an entity meets its field, the worked line that distinguishes it from what it is not.

Sociology treats boundaries as potential accomplishments rather than given facts, a view crystallized in Michèle Lamont and Virág Molnár's influential review "The Study of Boundaries in the Social Sciences" (2002).
Lamont distinguishes *symbolic boundaries* — the conceptual distinctions people use to categorize objects, practices, and other people, the lines of taste, morality, and worth by which groups decide who is decent, respectable, or "our kind" — from *social boundaries*, the objectified, durable differences in resources and opportunities that those distinctions become when they are widely agreed upon and built into institutions.
In her *Money, Morals, and Manners* (1992) and *The Dignity of Working Men* (2000), Lamont shows ordinary people drawing moral and cultural boundaries to dignify themselves and to keep others at a distance — the worker who looks down on the lazy or the dishonest, the professional who looks down on the uncultured. *Boundary-work* is the term for this continual labour of drawing and defending the line.

Charles Tilly made boundaries central to the explanation of inequality.
In *Durable Inequality* (1998) he argues that the most persistent inequalities are *categorical*: organized around a boundary between paired and bounded categories — citizen and foreigner, white and Black, male and female — rather than around a smooth gradient of individual difference.
Once such a categorical boundary is installed, mechanisms of *exploitation* (the powerful capturing value from the labour of those across the line) and *opportunity hoarding* (those inside the boundary monopolizing access to resources) make the inequality durable, because it no longer depends on the traits of particular individuals but on the maintained line between categories.
The boundary does the work, and the boundary is socially produced and enforced — at borders, in hiring, in who is admitted and who is turned away.

The stakes are that where the line is drawn, and who polices it, distributes life chances and dignity.
The same population can be partitioned by a hundred different boundaries, and which one is made to matter — race, religion, language, papers, accent, neighbourhood — is a question of power, not of nature.
This is the harm that the nationalism branch performs in the extreme: the **volksgeist** and **the-folk** draw a boundary around the authentic people and cast the impure outside it.
Read against its neighbours, the boundary is the **relational-entity**'s worked edge, a thoroughly **contingent** line (drawable elsewhere, erasable, contestable) maintained through acts of **witnessing** and recognition that decide who is acknowledged as inside and who is denied entry.
To denaturalize a boundary — to show it was made and could be unmade — is among the most consequential moves a relational social analysis can perform.
