## What this subdomain takes from her

- **The archive's structural absence of Black women's interiority.** Hartman's central methodological insight is also the central Black-feminist analytical fact: the archive of slavery and its aftermath was produced by the slave system and by the institutions that succeeded it, and what it records is what mattered to slaveholders, planters, courts, censuses, and police.
  Black women's interior lives — what they thought, wanted, refused, hoped — are not in the archive in any straightforward way.
  They have to be inferred at the limit, against the grain of the documents that purport to describe them.
  The analytical fact is the precondition of the methodological response.
- **Critical fabulation.** From "Venus in Two Acts" (*Small Axe*, 2008) and developed across *Lose Your Mother* (2007) and *Wayward Lives, Beautiful Experiments* (2019): the methodological response to the structural absence of Black women's interiority from the archive.
  Critical fabulation is to write at the limit of the archive — to fabulate without falsifying, to imagine the inner lives the records cannot supply while marking the speculation as such, to refuse both the recovery narrative (which would falsify) and the silence (which would let the archive's violence stand unchallenged).
  The method is one of the central methodological inheritances of contemporary Black feminist scholarship.
- **The wayward.** *Wayward Lives, Beautiful Experiments* (2019) reads the lives of young Black women in early-twentieth-century Philadelphia and New York as practitioners of a freedom that historiography has not learned to recognize — refusal of work, refusal of family, refusal of the narratives prepared for them, the invention of forms of life from inside conditions that should have made them impossible.
  The waywardness is not failure; it is method.
  The book is a sustained Black-feminist counter-historiography.
- **Refusal of recovery.** Across Hartman's work, a refusal of the redemptive narrative — the move that takes a life destroyed by slavery and tries to rescue it through historiographic recovery.
  The refusal insists that the loss is the loss, that no narrative work undoes it, and that the analytical task is to live with the unredeemability.
  The refusal is constitutive of the kind of Black-feminist thought that does not console.
