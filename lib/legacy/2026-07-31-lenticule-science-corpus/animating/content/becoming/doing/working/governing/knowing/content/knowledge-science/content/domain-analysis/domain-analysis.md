# Domain-analysis

*Domain-analysis* is the framework in information science, due to Birger Hjørland and Hanne Albrechtsen (1995), that treats a **domain** as "a body of knowledge, defined socially and theoretically as the knowledge of a group sharing ontological and epistemological commitments." A domain is not a natural kind waiting to be discovered; it is a discourse community held together by shared assumptions about what exists, what counts as evidence, and how knowledge is produced.

## What the concept commits to

The framework's load-bearing move is sociological. Traditional classification assumes subjects exist independently and the classifier's job is to mirror them. Domain analysis says the opposite: what counts as a subject depends on who is asking and why. A domain's boundaries, internal divisions, and relevance criteria all reflect the commitments of the community that produces and uses the knowledge. This makes domain analysis inherently **comparative** — understanding one domain means understanding how its commitments differ from those of neighbouring domains.

Hjørland (2002) identified eleven approaches to domain analysis: producing literature guides, constructing special classifications and thesauri, indexing and retrieving specialties, empirical user studies, bibliometric studies, historical studies, document and genre studies, epistemological and critical studies, terminological studies, studies of structures and institutions in scientific communication, and domain analysis in scientific cognition. Each approach reveals different aspects of a domain's structure. No single approach is sufficient — the framework's claim is that the eleven projections jointly characterise the domain.
