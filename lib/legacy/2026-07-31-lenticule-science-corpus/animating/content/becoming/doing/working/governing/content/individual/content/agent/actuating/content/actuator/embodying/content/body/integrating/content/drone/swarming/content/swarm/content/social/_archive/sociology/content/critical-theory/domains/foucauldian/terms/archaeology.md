Archaeology is Foucault's method for analyzing the conditions that make a body of knowledge possible in a given historical period, without treating those conditions as steps in a story of progress.
Where a conventional history of ideas traces how one thinker influenced another — how Darwin built on Malthus, how Pasteur corrected Lister — archaeology asks a different question: what rules of formation had to be in place for these statements to appear at all?

Foucault introduced the method in *The Order of Things* (1966) and formalized it in *The Archaeology of Knowledge* (1969).
The unit of analysis is not the author's intention or the text's meaning but the statement (*énoncé*) — the basic function by which something enters discourse as sayable.
A statement is not a sentence or a proposition but a position within a discursive formation: it depends on who can say it, from what institutional site, using what concepts, and according to what strategies of evidence and argument.

Archaeology proceeds by identifying discontinuities rather than continuities.
Foucault argued in *The Order of Things* that Western knowledge passed through three distinct *epistemes* — configurations of knowledge that define what can be ordered, classified, and known in an entire period.
The Renaissance episteme organized knowledge through resemblance (signatures, sympathies, analogies).
The Classical episteme organized it through representation and taxonomy (ordered tables, visible features).
The Modern episteme organized it through history and hidden depths (development over time, underlying structures).
These transitions are not gradual refinements but breaks — moments when the rules governing what counts as knowledge change at a deep structural level.

The method is deliberately anti-teleological.
It refuses to read the past as a series of approximations leading toward the present's superior knowledge.
The Classical naturalist and the modern biologist are not doing the same thing more or less well.
They operate within different epistemes — different configurations of what it is possible to know.
Archaeology maps the synchronic structure of a discursive formation at a given moment: what are the rules? what do they permit? what do they exclude?
The complementary method — genealogy — traces the diachronic forces that produce and destabilize those rules over time.
