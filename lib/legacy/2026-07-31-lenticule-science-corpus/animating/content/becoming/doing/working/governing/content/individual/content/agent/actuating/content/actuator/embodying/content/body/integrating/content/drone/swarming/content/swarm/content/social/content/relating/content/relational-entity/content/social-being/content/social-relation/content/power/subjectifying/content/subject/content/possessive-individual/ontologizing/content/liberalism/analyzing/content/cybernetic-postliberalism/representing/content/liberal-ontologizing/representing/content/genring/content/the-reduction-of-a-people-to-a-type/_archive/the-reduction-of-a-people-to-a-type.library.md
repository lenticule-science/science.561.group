[Genring](../teaching/explanations/genring.md) — the reduction of a living people to a fixed literary type — is an operation with a specific history, specific mechanics, and specific political effects.
This text traces that history, analyzes the mechanics, and specifies the political effects with enough precision to support the analytical use the concept gets throughout predicated sociology.

## The history: Orientalism and its method

Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) is the foundational text for the genealogy of genring.
Said argued that the Western scholarly and literary tradition had produced "the Orient" not as a description of actual places and peoples but as a *discourse* — a structured set of representations, presuppositions, and conventions that organized Western knowledge and practice in relation to Eastern peoples and places.
The Orient of the Orientalist discourse was not the Arab, Turkish, Persian, or Chinese; it was a figure — sensual, irrational, despotic, static, mysterious — that served the Western imaginary's needs.

The mechanics Said identified: *textual attitudes* (treating representations of the Orient in prior texts as more authoritative than actual encounters with actual people), *strategic location* (the positioning of Western knowledge as the authoritative vantage point from which the Orient can be known), and *strategic formation* (the way prior texts constrain what subsequent texts can say — the inertia of genre).
These mechanics are the mechanics of genring.

The political function Said identified: Orientalism was not merely a scholarly error.
It was a *governing discourse* — the knowledge-form through which colonialism organized its governance of the colonized.
The Oriental who was irrational, despotic, and static required rational, ordered, and progressive Western governance.
The genre produced the political conclusion.

## The mechanics in detail

[Genring](../teaching/explanations/genring.md) operates through four mechanical steps, each of which can be analyzed in specific cases.

**Feature selection.** From the full heteroglossia of a living people — the actual diversity of individuals, perspectives, practices, and historical moments — specific features are selected for representation.
The selection is not random.
Features are selected for their *narrative utility*: visual distinctiveness (the feathered headdress, the veiled woman, the lederhosen), emotional resonance (the dignity of the noble savage, the exoticism of the Oriental, the heartiness of the peasant), and reproducibility without context (the features can be represented without their originating relational and historical context).

**Template assembly.** The selected features are assembled into a coherent character template: a stable personality, a stable appearance, a stable set of values and behaviors, and a stable narrative arc.
The template has to be coherent enough to be recognizable across diverse representations.
Karl May's Winnetou (1893) assembled the features of the Noble Indian into a template so stable that it has been reproduced, recognizably, in European children's literature and film for over a century.

**Template circulation.** The assembled template is circulated through media, literature, performance, and institutional practice until it achieves genre status — until audiences *recognize* the type and expect to see it reproduced.
The circulation produces the template's own authority: the more it circulates, the more it seems like an accurate representation, because it is the representation audiences have been trained to recognize.

**Substitution.** Once the template has achieved genre status, it *substitutes* for the people it purports to represent.
Audiences encounter the genre figure rather than the people; the people are made legible through the genre figure.
The living person who does not fit the genre — who deviates from the template — becomes unintelligible or is read as an aberration.

## What genring destroys

Vine Deloria Jr. (1969) named the political effect from the inside: "We are like actors in a play.
We exist to play out our parts; when we deviate from the script, they want to send us back to our reservations."
The living indigenous person who writes mathematics, organizes a labor union, practices urban farming, or does anything outside the genre's script becomes invisible to the genre's logic.
Not suppressed — the genre does not need to suppress deviation — but *illegible*: the deviation cannot be read as what it is because the genre has no character for it.

What genring specifically destroys is the [temporal middle](../teaching/explanations/temporal-middle.md): the position every living person actually occupies, of inheriting a tradition while also transforming it, of being both shaped by the past and actively shaping what comes next.
The temporal middle requires acknowledging that a people is *living* — that they have history, complexity, and a present that is not identical to their past.
The genre denies this acknowledgment.
The genre requires the people to be static — because the genre figure's value to the imaginary is precisely its stability.
A dynamic people whose character is changing is not a useful genre; it cannot anchor the imaginary in the way a static type can.

The destruction of the temporal middle is also the destruction of the possibility of meaningful relation.
You cannot have a relationship with a genre figure; you can only consume one.
The living person behind the genre is rendered inaccessible by the genre's substitution.
This is why Deloria describes the experience as being treated as an actor rather than a person.

## What [attributing](../teaching/explanations/attributing.md) does in this process

[Attributing](../teaching/explanations/attributing.md) is the micro-level operation through which genring operates in individual interactions: the practice of assigning to a person the characteristics of their attributed genre, regardless of their actual behavior, history, or statement.
Genring produces the type; attributing applies the type to specific persons.

The attributing operation closes the feedback loop that sustains the genre.
When a person is encountered, they are attributed the genre's characteristics; their actual behavior is interpreted through the genre's template (behavior that fits is noticed; behavior that doesn't is explained away as exception or fraud); the attribution is confirmed by the encounter; the genre is reinforced.
The genre's self-reinforcing quality is produced by the attributing operation that applies it to individuals.

## The political operation

The reduction of a people to a type is not merely a representational error.
It is a political operation with specific material consequences.

**Legal and policy consequence.** Legal and policy frameworks that operate through type-categories apply to people through the attribution operation.
Indigenous people who do not fit the type face disqualification from legal protections and treaty rights that are typed to a specific genre of indigeneity.
This is the legal dimension of genring's destruction of the temporal middle.

**Narrative consequence.** The political narratives through which a people's claims are legible are genre-constrained.
Claims that fit the genre of the authentic-native (land-based, traditional, non-modern) are legible to the settler imaginary; claims that don't fit — the indigenous software developer's claim to sovereignty over digital infrastructure, the indigenous urbanite's claim to continued cultural membership — are not legible within the genre.

**Relational consequence.** The genre substitution prevents the development of actual political relationships.
You cannot negotiate with a genre; you can only manage it.
Settler-state political processes that address indigenous peoples through the genre figure rather than through actual political relation are not negotiating — they are managing a genre.
