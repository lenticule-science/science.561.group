# Register

A **register** is a variety of language calibrated to a particular communicative situation: who is addressing whom, about what, through what medium, with what social distance and formality. In Halliday's systemic-functional account (Halliday 1985, *An Introduction to Functional Grammar*) register is parameterised by **field** (the subject matter), **tenor** (the relationship between participants), and **mode** (the role of language — spoken, written, visual, embedded in action).

A register is not a vocabulary list and not a style — it is a *situated configuration of linguistic choices* across phonology, lexis, grammar, and discourse pattern. A change of register is a change of communicative situation; a register-mismatched artifact (formal grammar in casual mode, casual lexis in formal field) is misread.

## What "writing in a register" commits to

A register commits to:

1. **Lexical choices.** The vocabulary admissible in this situation — technical vs. general, latinate vs. germanic, in-group vs. out-group jargon.
2. **Grammatical choices.** Sentence complexity, voice (active/passive), modality (must/may/might), nominalisation rate.
3. **Discourse pattern.** Turn-taking, paragraph structure, expected genre conventions, the implicit "shape" of a contribution.
4. **Pragmatic defaults.** Which Gricean maxims are foregrounded, what counts as polite or impolite, what reading style the audience will apply.

## Register vs. genre

[Genre](genre.md) is a stable, recognisable type of artifact (a research paper, a recipe, a technical specification, a tweet). A genre presupposes a register (or a small range of registers) and adds expectations about structure, length, and rhetorical moves. Register is the linguistic variety; genre is the conventionalised artifact-type.

## Relation to upstream

Halliday's systemic-functional grammar (Halliday 1985, Halliday & Matthiessen 2014) is the foundational source. Genre theory (Miller 1984; Bazerman 1988; Swales 1990) develops the register-genre distinction. Discourse register in pragmatics (Biber 1988; Biber & Conrad 2009) provides the empirical-typological apparatus. Bell's audience design (1984) connects register to addressee.
