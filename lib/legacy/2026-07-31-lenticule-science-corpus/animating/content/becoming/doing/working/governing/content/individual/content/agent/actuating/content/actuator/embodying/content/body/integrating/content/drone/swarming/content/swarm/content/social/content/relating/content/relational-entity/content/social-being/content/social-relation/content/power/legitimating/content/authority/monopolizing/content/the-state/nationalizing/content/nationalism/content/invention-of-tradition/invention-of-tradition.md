# invention-of-tradition

The invention of tradition is Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger's concept, from their 1983 collection *The Invention of Tradition*, for practices that present themselves as ancient and organically continuous but are in fact recent, deliberately constructed, and serving present political needs.
The Scottish Highland kilt, the pageantry of the British coronation, the Grimms' "German folk tales," Oktoberfest — each claims immemorial origins while being a product of the eighteenth or nineteenth century.
Generated by: **nationalism** — invented traditions manufacture the appearance of continuity that legitimates the nation, supplying the deep past the nation needs in order to seem natural rather than made.

Hobsbawm distinguishes three overlapping types of invented tradition: those that establish or symbolize social cohesion and group membership; those that establish or legitimate institutions and authority; and those whose main purpose is socialization — the inculcation of beliefs, values, and conventions of behaviour.
All three share one feature: they claim continuity with a suitable historic past while being substantially discontinuous with it.
The past they invoke is largely factitious, selected and stitched together to serve the present.
This is what distinguishes an invented tradition from a genuinely living one: the invented tradition is fixed and repetitive, asserting an unbroken line to antiquity, where a living tradition is an ongoing process of inheriting, interpreting, and transforming what was handed down.

The concept names a production mechanism, and the examples make the mechanism concrete.
The Brothers Grimm did not so much collect German folk tales as produce them, editing a heterogeneous body of stories — many from French-speaking, middle-class sources — into a corpus that confirmed the existence of a German **volksgeist**.
The *Trachtenverein* costume movement of the 1880s did not preserve Bavarian peasant dress; it produced the dirndl and lederhosen as consumable images of folk authenticity.
The Pahlavi regime did not discover the *Shahnameh*; it deployed the epic as proof of an ancient national spirit.
In each case the invention lies in the selection and in the claim of continuity, not in the raw material — which is why invented traditions are not lies in the simple sense.
They draw on real songs, real garments, real stories, then curate and recombine them until the result confirms what the nation needs to believe about itself.
They feel authentic *because* they contain authentic elements, rearranged to serve a purpose those elements never originally served.

The stakes are that invented tradition naturalizes the present by giving it a fabricated past.
It operates on **tradition** and on **collective-memory**: it takes the living process of transmission and the shared frameworks through which a people remembers itself, and freezes them into a fixed, official image presented as immemorial.
Read against its neighbours, the invention of tradition is the workshop that supplies **romantic-nationalism** and **the-folk** with their "ancient" costumes and customs, the practical complement to **political-myth**: where the myth narrates the nation's destiny, invented tradition stages its antiquity, manufacturing the continuity on which the myth's authority rests.
