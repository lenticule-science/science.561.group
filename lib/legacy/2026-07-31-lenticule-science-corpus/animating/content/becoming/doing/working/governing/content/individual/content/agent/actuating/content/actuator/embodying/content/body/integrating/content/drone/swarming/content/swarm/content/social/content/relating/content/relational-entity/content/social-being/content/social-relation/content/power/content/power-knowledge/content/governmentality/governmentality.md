# governmentality

Generated by: power-knowledge, governing through freedom — the hinge between discipline (over bodies) and biopower (over populations).

Governmentality is Michel Foucault's term for the rationality through which modern states govern populations — not primarily by commanding obedience but by shaping the conditions within which people conduct themselves.
Foucault fused "governing" and "mentality" to capture a precise point: government is not only what governments do but a way of thinking about how conduct should be conducted.
His shorthand for the object of governmental reason is the "conduct of conduct" — the directing of the ways in which people direct themselves.
He introduced the term in his 1977–78 Collège de France lectures, *Security, Territory, Population*, and extended it in the 1978–79 lectures, *The Birth of Biopolitics*.

Foucault traced the emergence of governmentality to the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when the problem of government separated itself from two older models.
Sovereignty had asked how a ruler could hold territory and command obedience — a relationship between sovereign and subject, mediated by law.
Pastoral care had asked how a shepherd could guide each soul toward salvation — a relationship between pastor and flock, mediated by conscience.
The new problem was distinct from both: how to manage a population — its health, wealth, productivity, and security.
Answering it required new forms of knowledge — political economy, demography, and statistics (Statistik, the "science of the state").
The population emerged as an object with its own regularities, measurable and therefore optimizable, and governing came to mean acting on those regularities rather than issuing commands to individuals.

Governmentality extends biopower by specifying its institutional logic.
Where biopower names the general fact that modern power takes biological life as its object, governmentality names the particular rationality through which that management is organized and justified — and different rationalities produce different subjects.
A liberal governmentality governs through the market and the framework of rights, and it produces the autonomous, self-interested subject who must be left free to pursue their own ends.
A neoliberal governmentality governs through competition and entrepreneurial norms, producing the self-investing subject who treats their own life as human capital to be managed.
A welfarist governmentality governs through social provision and risk-pooling, producing the citizen-client of the welfare state.
Each depends on specific configurations of power/knowledge that make its subjects visible, calculable, and administrable.

The concept's enduring importance is that it reframes freedom as a vehicle of power rather than its opposite.
Liberal and neoliberal government does not work by suppressing freedom but by working through it: it structures the field of possible action so that free subjects, pursuing their own interests, conduct themselves toward governable ends.
This is why governmentality serves as the hinge of Foucault's account of modern power — it connects the discipline that trains individual bodies to the biopower that regulates whole populations, supplying the rationality that organizes both into a coherent art of government.
The 1978–79 lectures pushed the analysis into neoliberalism specifically, examining how the Chicago School and German ordoliberalism reimagined the state–market relation not as the state's withdrawal but as its active construction of the conditions under which markets, and market-shaped subjects, can exist.
