# nation-state

The nation-state is the political form that fuses two distinct things: the *nation* — a people imagined as sharing language, culture, history, and destiny — and the *state* — a sovereign administration exercising authority over a bounded territory.
Generated by: the nation-state is the state in its nationalizing form, the state binding its territorial authority to the claim that its population is a single people.
The fusion insists that the two must coincide: each nation should have its own state, and each state should contain one nation.
This claim, which became the organizing principle of global politics after Westphalia (1648) and accelerated dramatically after the French Revolution, is among the most consequential fictions of modern history.

The leading theorists of nationalism have argued, against the form's own self-image, that the nation does not precede the state but is produced by it.
Ernest Gellner (*Nations and Nationalism*, 1983) contended that nationalism is not the awakening of dormant nations but the manufacture of nations by states that require culturally homogeneous populations to administer.
Modern industrial economies need mobile, interchangeable labour and centralized mass education; states therefore need citizens who speak one language, obey one law, and identify with one set of symbols.
The nation is the product of these administrative needs, not their cause.
Benedict Anderson (*Imagined Communities*, 1983) added that the nation is an "imagined community" — not a fiction in the sense of a falsehood, but a community constituted through shared imagination, made thinkable by print capitalism, maps, censuses, and museums rather than by any face-to-face acquaintance among its millions of members.

The form creates a structural problem for every people whose distribution does not match a state's borders.
The Kurds, spread across Turkey, Iran, Iraq, and Syria and a majority in none, are the paradigm case: the nation-state framework offers them only two intolerable positions — create your own state, which the four existing states resist with violence, or assimilate into the state you inhabit, which dissolves your identity.
Because the form demands that population and people coincide, it generates as its by-product those who do not fit: minorities, refugees, the stateless, and the displaced.
Where the imagined people is defined narrowly, the machinery of "making the population match the nation" shades into expulsion, forced assimilation, and at its extreme genocide.

The stakes are therefore double-edged.
The nation-state has been, on one hand, the vehicle of popular sovereignty and self-determination, the form in which colonized peoples won independence and citizens won rights.
On the other, its founding premise — one people, one territory, one state — is never true of any actual territory, and the work of making it appear true is the work of exclusion.
This is why some traditions reject the form itself rather than seek a better instance of it.
Democratic confederalism, the political philosophy associated with the Kurdish movement and practiced in Rojava, proposes governance through nested communes and councils that explicitly do not require territorial sovereignty or ethnic homogeneity to function.

Within this cluster the nation-state is the state married to the idea of a single people, and its characteristic remainders are statelessness — the condition of nations with no state of their own — and displacement, the forced clearing of territory to make the population match the imagined nation.
It sits downstream of the state and of sovereignty, and upstream of the most violent expressions of the friend-enemy distinction, where belonging to the nation and being marked as its enemy become matters of life and death.
