# George Sword — Recording of the Tóbtób Kiŋ Doctrine

George Sword (also called Long Knife; Lakota name *Mí Wakȟáŋ Yuhá* / similar variants in different sources) was the most systematic of Walker's informants.
A former wičháša wakȟáŋ who had converted to Christianity, Sword nevertheless agreed to record the Oglala theological tradition for Walker on the grounds that it would otherwise be lost.
He worked with Walker for over a decade.
The Tóbtób Kiŋ doctrine — the structure of Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka as sixteen aspects organized as four-times-four — is the central systematic content of Sword's recording.

## What Sword recorded

The doctrine in summary:

### The four Superior Wakȟáŋ (*Wakȟáŋ Aŋkáŋtu*)

1. **Wí** — the Sun.
  Ceremonial chief among the four superior. *"Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka Kiŋ"* in Finger's phrasing.
2. **Škáŋ** — the Sky / the Motion-principle.
  Functionally chief; supreme judge; giver of *naǧí*. *"Naǧí Tȟáŋka, the Great Spirit"* in Finger's phrasing.
3. **Makȟá** — the Earth.
  Mother; material ground; receiver of Íŋyaŋ's blood.
4. **Íŋyaŋ** — the Rock.
  First in cosmogonic origin; last in present-time rank; the self-emptied source.

### The four Associates (the next tier)

5. **Hanwí** — the Moon.
  Associate of Wí.
6. **Tȟaté** — the Wind.
  Associate of Škáŋ.
7. **Wóȟpe** — the Falling-Star, or the Beautiful-One.
  Daughter of Škáŋ; associate of Makȟá.
8. **Wakíŋyaŋ** — the Thunder.
  Associate (and counterpart) of Íŋyaŋ; the wakȟáŋ-bearing powers of the West.

### The four Subordinate (the third tier)

9. **Tatáŋka** — the Buffalo Bull.
10. **Hunúŋpa** — the Two-legged (often glossed as Bear, sometimes as Grizzly).
11. **Tatékiyutaŋ** — the Whirlwind.
12. **Wani** — the four winds.

### The four Wakanlike (the fourth tier; *like-wakȟáŋ*, lesser)

13–16.
Variable across recordings; Sword's specific listing here is less consistently preserved.

The structure is sixteen aspects total, organized as four-times-four (four superior, each with three subordinate-or-associated aspects), all together constituting Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka.

## The cosmogonic ordering

Sword embeds the Tóbtób Kiŋ in a cosmogonic narrative.
The order of emergence:

- *Before*: only Íŋyaŋ and Haŋ (the darkness).
- Íŋyaŋ's self-emptying produces Makȟá (from his blood) and Škáŋ (from the power-in-the-blood).
- Wí is created to warm Makȟá.
- Hanwí is created as Wí's companion.
- Tȟaté (the wind) and the four winds (Wani) emerge from Škáŋ.
- Wakíŋyaŋ emerges from the West, as the storm-power.
- Wóȟpe (the Falling-Star, or "the Beautiful-One") arrives later, the daughter-or-associate of Škáŋ.
- The Buffalo Nation and subsequently the human beings emerge.

The order is significant: the four superior are the first-emerged after the cosmogonic break; the associates come from them; the subordinates emerge in further differentiation.
The four-times-four structure mirrors the cosmogonic *order of differentiation* itself.

## On the rank-inversion

Sword's recording is precise: Íŋyaŋ is *first in cosmogonic origin* but *last in present-time rank* among the four superior. Škáŋ ranks highest in functional power; Wí highest in ceremonial chief-role; then Makȟá; then Íŋyaŋ.

The reason: Íŋyaŋ *gave-up-his-power* in the self-emptying.
He is now the spent-source — the hard, dry rock that *cannot give more*.
His cosmogonic priority does not translate into present-rank priority; the structure honors the cosmogonic origin but operates by the present-power configuration.

This rank-inversion is structurally important for the relational-framework reading: the *origin* of relation is Íŋyaŋ-emptied (the ⊤ position, the over-determined paradox-bearer); the *engine* of the present-tense relational field is Škáŋ (the ⊥ position, the under-determined supreme judge).
The cosmogonic source and the engine of ongoing operation are different positions in the bilattice.

## On the contemporary status of the doctrine

The Tóbtób Kiŋ doctrine as Sword gave it to Walker was already, in Sword's own time (late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries), being *displaced* by the Christianization push.
Sword's willingness to record it for Walker was motivated by the concern that it would otherwise be lost.
The boarding-school project and the systematic suppression of Lakota religion under federal policy until the 1978 American Indian Religious Freedom Act made open transmission of the doctrine dangerous for decades.

The contemporary Lakota tradition includes voices that affirm the Tóbtób Kiŋ doctrine as continuous Oglala theology (see Albert White Hat Sr., *Reading and Writing the Lakota Language* (1999); Vine Deloria Jr., *The World We Used to Live In* (2006)) and voices that treat it as one historical articulation among others.
The doctrine is *attested in the primary recording*; whether it is the contemporary holy-people's preferred articulation in 2026 is a different question best answered by the contemporary holders.

## What this subtheory commits to

This subtheory commits to the Tóbtób Kiŋ doctrine as the cosmogonic and metaphysical structure for the philosophical work.
The four superior Wakȟáŋ are read as the four positions of the relational field — the Lakota form of the four logic-values (⊥, t, f, ⊤) of the bilattice.
The commitment is to the *structural content* of Sword's recording, not to a polemical claim about contemporary religious authority.
