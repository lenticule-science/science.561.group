# Claim. Of the five available modes of the pentatonic minor scale, Mode I — with the fundamental as tonal center — is the only mode used functionally in the cedar flute repertoire.

**Claim.** The [pentatonic-minor-scale](teaching/explanations/pentatonic-minor-scale.md) admits five [modal](teaching/explanations/mode.md) rotations (see the prior claim [pentatonic-admits-five-modal-rotations](./pentatonic-admits-five-modal-rotations.md)), but in the documented [cedar-flute](teaching/explanations/cedar-flute.md) repertoire only one of these modes — Mode I, with the [fundamental](teaching/explanations/fundamental.md) (the all-holes-covered pitch, scale-degree 1) as the tonal center — appears functionally.
The other four modes are theoretically available on the instrument (the same six fingerings produce the same pitches regardless of which is treated as tonal center, and a player could in principle make musical sense of any of the five modes) but are not idiomatic in the documented repertoire and do not appear in the cadential or phrase-organizing conventions of the tradition.

**Argument.**

*Mode I is the functional tonic in cedar-flute repertoire.* Across the documented [courting-song](teaching/explanations/courting-song.md) repertoire — including the historical-recording sources (Densmore's BAE Bulletin 61 transcriptions, Belo Cozad recordings, Kevin Locke's repertoire, and the contemporary teaching tradition) — phrases consistently resolve to the [fundamental](teaching/explanations/fundamental.md) (scale-degree 1) at their [cadences](teaching/explanations/cadence.md); songs end on the fundamental; the cadential vocabulary (descending b3 → 1, descending 4 → 1, held tonic with [warble](teaching/explanations/warble.md)) is organized around the fundamental as terminal resolution.
The functional weight of the scale's pitches is therefore distributed in the Mode I pattern: 1 as stable tonic, 5 as stable dominant, b3 as characteristic minor-third approach-tone to the tonic, 4 as melodic pivot, b7 as upper peak.
This is the pattern of Mode I; it is not the pattern of any other mode of the same pitch collection.

*The other modes are not absent from the instrument but are not functionally present in the repertoire.* The same six [finger-hole](teaching/explanations/finger-hole.md) states that produce Mode I produce, by alternative tonal-center assignment, Modes II–V as well.
A player who chose to treat scale-degree b3 as the functional tonic would produce Mode II (the *major pentatonic*), with intervals 2,2,3,2,3 from the new tonic; treating scale-degree 4 as tonic produces Mode III; treating scale-degree 5 as tonic produces Mode IV; treating scale-degree b7 as tonic produces Mode V.
Such treatments are physically possible on the instrument.
They do not, however, appear in the documented repertoire as the conventional modal orientation of any song.
The functional tonic in idiomatic cedar-flute playing is the fundamental, and the mode is Mode I.

*Physical and historical reasons for the Mode I convention.* Two factors converge to make Mode I the conventional mode.
The first is *physical*: the [fundamental](teaching/explanations/fundamental.md) is the lowest pitch on the instrument (the all-holes-covered position), and it is the most acoustically stable pitch and the easiest to return to as the breath's energy decays through the [phrase](teaching/explanations/phrase.md).
The natural cadential pitch — the pitch the breath naturally moves toward as the [phrase](teaching/explanations/phrase.md) ends — is therefore the fundamental, and the natural functional tonic is the fundamental.
The second is *historical-musical*: the [wiilowan](teaching/explanations/wiilowan.md) vocal repertoire that the cedar flute [instrumentalizes](teaching/explanations/instrumentalization.md) uses Mode I, and the instrumentalization preserves the modal orientation of the source.
The flute, in inheriting the vocal repertoire, inherits its Mode I orientation.

*The claim is about practice, not possibility.* It would be incorrect to claim that the cedar flute cannot play Modes II–V — it can (the pitches are present, the instrument supports the alternative tonal-center assignments).
It would also be incorrect to claim that no player has ever tried Modes II–V — players experiment, and contemporary players in particular sometimes draw on the modal versatility of the instrument for cross-tradition or fusion contexts.
The claim here is specifically about the *documented core repertoire of the tradition*: in that repertoire, Mode I is the functional tonic, and the other modes do not appear functionally.
Whether the historical repertoire ever included songs in alternative modes that have since been lost is an open empirical question (see the question file [is-mode-iii-pentatonic-actually-used](../questions/is-mode-iii-pentatonic-actually-used.md)).

*Why this matters music-theoretically.* The functional-tonic claim is not a redundancy on top of the fact that the [pentatonic-minor-scale](teaching/explanations/pentatonic-minor-scale.md) is the scale of the instrument.
The scale-as-pitch-collection (the five pitches in their fixed acoustic positions) underdetermines the music — the same pitch collection could support any of five modal-organizations, each producing a musically distinct repertoire.
The choice of Mode I as the functional tonic is the *additional* music-theoretic specification that, combined with the scale, gives the cedar flute its melodic identity.
A theory that treated only the pitch-collection and not the modal-organization would be missing the music-theoretic specification that determines what the music actually sounds like.
