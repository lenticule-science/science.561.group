# Phrase

The phrase is a bounded melodic unit — the smallest melodic structure within a song that is large enough to constitute a complete musical "sentence" rather than a fragment.
A phrase begins with an attack (a first note articulated from silence or from the rest after the previous phrase), unfolds across a sequence of notes organized into a melodic contour, and ends with a [cadence](./cadence.md) — a recognizable closing gesture — after which a rest separates this phrase from the next.
The phrase is the unit at which the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) idiom is most naturally analyzed: a [courting-song](./courting-song.md) is a sequence of phrases, organized into [strophes](./strophe.md), repeating across the performance with [ornament-substitution](../../code/transformers/ornament-substitution.md) variation between strophes.

In the Lakotaean cedar-flute tradition, the phrase is *bounded by single-breath capacity*: each phrase is one exhalation, and the rest between phrases is the time the player takes to inhale.
This is not merely a practical constraint but a music-theoretic feature — the breath-bounded phrase is the defining temporal unit of the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) idiom, and the idiom's [rubato](./rubato.md) pacing, descending phrase-contour bias, and cadential vocabulary are all features of music organized around this breath-bounded structure.
A typical cedar-flute phrase runs between 4 and 10 seconds in continuous playing time, depending on the player's lung capacity, the song's tempo and density, and the specific phrase content.
Phrases longer than 10 seconds are rare and challenging (they push at the limits of comfortable lung capacity); phrases shorter than about 4 seconds are common only for terminal cadence-phrases or in some compact song-types.
The claim [phrase-bounded-by-single-breath](../learning/claims/phrase-bounded-by-single-breath.md) develops this music-theoretic point in detail.

The melodic contour of a phrase in cedar-flute repertoire is statistically *descending* — phrases tend to begin on a high scale-degree (often b7 or 8) and descend stepwise or by characteristic leaps through the scale to the [fundamental](./fundamental.md), where the phrase concludes on a [cadence](./cadence.md).
The descending bias has both a physiological grounding (the breath weakens through the course of a phrase, the [embouchure](./embouchure.md) relaxes, and the natural acoustic-and-physical tendency is for the pitch to fall as the breath fades) and a music-theoretic grounding (the [fundamental](./fundamental.md) is the tonal center to which phrases resolve, and the descending motion provides the most natural approach to that resolution).
Not all phrases are uniformly descending — peak-and-return phrases (ascending then descending) and "repeat-and-vary" phrases (two motivic sub-phrases at similar pitch levels) also appear in the repertoire — but the descending-cadence phrase is the most common and the most idiomatic.

A phrase typically contains 4 to 10 notes in continuous playing — the number depending on the phrase's tempo, the density of [ornaments](./ornament.md), the duration of held tones, and the player's choice of [breath-pulse](./breath-pulse.md) articulation.
The notes within the phrase are organized into the melodic contour described above; the inter-note timing is [rubato](./rubato.md) (flexible rather than metric); the articulation between notes is by [breath-pulse](./breath-pulse.md) or by finger-controlled ornament rather than by tongue-stroke.

The phrase concept in cedar-flute repertoire is fairly directly inherited from [wiilowan](./wiilowan.md) vocal practice — the [instrumentalization](./instrumentalization.md) of vocal song onto the flute preserves the phrase structure of the vocal source, with vocal phrase boundaries becoming flute phrase boundaries (and breath-rests in both).
In ensemble contexts where the flute is not present and the [voice](./voice.md) appears with [drum](./drum.md) (such as in [honor-song](./honor-song.md) performance), the phrase concept persists but is fitted onto a metric pulse — the phrase has a definite number of drum-beats, and the singer's [rubato](./rubato.md) within the phrase is constrained by the requirement to anchor at structural moments to the drum's pulse.

The [phrase-archetype-set](../../code/structures/phrase-archetype-set.md) structure documents the principal phrase shapes that appear in the cedar-flute repertoire, with their specific pitch-contours, characteristic ornament placements, and cadential conventions.
