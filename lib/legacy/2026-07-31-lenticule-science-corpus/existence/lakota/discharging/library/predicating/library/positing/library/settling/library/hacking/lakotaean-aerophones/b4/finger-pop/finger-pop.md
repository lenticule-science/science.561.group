# Finger Pop

The finger pop is a rapid open-then-close action on a single [finger-hole](./finger-hole.md) of the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md), producing a momentary lift of the sounding pitch before the held pitch resumes.
The pop is one of the most identifiable [ornaments](./ornament.md) of the cedar-flute idiom: it produces a sharp articulative chirp on top of an otherwise sustained tone, and its presence-or-absence and placement within a phrase are key markers of a player's style.

Mechanically, the pop works like this.
The player is sustaining a tone with some set of [finger-holes](./finger-hole.md) covered — say, all declared required covered, sounding the [fundamental](./fundamental.md).
The player briefly lifts a finger (typically not the foot-most-covered finger, but one of the higher fingers — the choice depends on the desired pitch interval of the pop), then immediately replaces it.
While the finger is off the hole, the air column is effectively shortened and the bore sounds the higher pitch corresponding to that shortened air column; when the finger returns, the bore returns to the lower pitch.
The result is a brief, sharp upward pitch deviation from the held tone, with the deviation lasting just long enough to be heard as a distinct pitch event (typically 30 to 100 milliseconds — short enough that it does not register as a "note" in the melodic line, but long enough that the pitch is perceptually identifiable).

The pop's pitch interval depends on which finger is lifted.
Lifting the finger immediately above the lowest-covered hole produces a pop to the next-scale-degree-up; lifting a finger further up produces a pop to a more distant scale-degree.
Most idiomatic pops are to small intervals — a pop from the held fundamental up to b3 (a minor third) is the most common single-pop, performed by lifting the foot-most finger; a pop from a held b3 up to 4 (a major second) is also common.
Larger-interval pops are possible but less idiomatic.

The pop's musical functions are principally three.
First, *articulation*: the pop provides a way to mark a structural moment within a sustained tone without retaking breath or changing the underlying melodic line.
The tone is sustained, the pop occurs, the tone continues.
Second, *rhythmic accent*: the pop, being a sharp pitch event, draws perceptual attention to the moment of its occurrence, and can be used to accent a beat or to mark a rhythmic structure within a [rubato](./rubato.md) phrase.
Third, *idiomatic flavor*: the pop is a sound that the cedar flute makes and other duct flutes generally do not (it requires the specific finger-hole layout of the cedar flute, the specific kind of fingertip-to-hole contact the cedar flute admits, and the specific breath-control regime the cedar flute encourages).
Its presence in a flute performance is a stylistic marker of the cedar-flute idiom.

The pop's placement within a phrase varies by performer and song.
Some players use pops sparingly, reserving them for specific structural moments (the start of a held tone, or a beat-accent within a sustained melodic figure); others use them more prolifically as part of a continuous ornamental texture.
The [ornament-substitution](../../code/transformers/ornament-substitution.md) transformer, which generates ornamental variants across strophes of a [courting-song](./courting-song.md), uses the pop as one of its principal substitution candidates: a plain held tone in one strophe may become a tone with one or more pops in the next strophe.

The pop differs from the [trill](./trill.md) in being a single brief event (the finger lifts once and returns) rather than a sustained alternation.
It differs from the [cut](./cut.md) in being a *lift* of a covered hole (producing an upward pitch deviation) rather than a *brush* of an uncovered hole.
It differs from the [finger-slide](./finger-slide.md) in being a discrete on-off event rather than a continuous-coverage event.
These four ornaments together — pop, cut, slide, trill — constitute the principal finger-controlled ornament vocabulary of the cedar flute.
