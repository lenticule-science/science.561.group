The cedar flute (Lakȟóta: *siyotaŋka*) is a wooden [duct flute](./duct-flute.md) of the Northern Plains, the principal melodic aerophone of Lakotaean musical tradition and the instrument carrying the vast bulk of the surviving solo wind repertoire.
It is built from a single straight section of softwood — almost always western red cedar in the historical and contemporary Plains tradition, occasionally juniper or other resonant softwoods — split lengthwise, hollowed in two halves, and rejoined to produce a single tube with a slow-air chamber (the "wind way" or *sound box*) at the upper end, a separate tone chamber below, and a partition (the "wall") between them.
A rectangular cutout in the top of the instrument bridges the two chambers, and a separately carved external block — frequently shaped to represent a bird, horse, or other figure — sits over this cutout and is bound to the flute body with leather or sinew.
The block directs the player's breath across a sharp edge (the "splitting edge" or *labium*), exciting the air column in the tone chamber.
This block-and-cutout system is the [duct-flute](./duct-flute.md) sound-production mechanism, and it is what distinguishes the cedar flute from end-blown bone whistles (which rely on the player's [embouchure](./embouchure.md) rather than a fixed duct) and from transverse flutes (which the Lakotaean tradition does not use).

The cedar flute carries six [finger-holes](./finger-hole.md) along the tone chamber, spaced so that, when all declared required are covered, the instrument sounds its [fundamental](./fundamental.md), and when the holes are uncovered in sequence from the foot of the instrument upward, the player produces the five additional scale-degrees of the [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md) (b3, 4, 5, b7) and the octave (8).
The exact pitch of the fundamental depends on bore length; surviving historical instruments and current makers most commonly produce fundamentals in the range of F#3 to A4, with F#4 and G4 perhaps the most common keys for contemporary courting flutes.
Overall length ranges from roughly 50 cm to 75 cm; the [bore](./bore.md) is approximately cylindrical, open at both ends, which determines the harmonic content available through [overblowing](./overblowing.md).

The cedar flute's primary repertoire is the [courting-song](./courting-song.md), a solo melodic genre in which the instrument [instrumentalizes](./instrumentalization.md) the [wiilowan](./wiilowan.md) vocal song form — taking a sung melody and rendering it as a flute line.
The tradition treats this instrumentalization as a definite musical operation, not a loose adaptation: a player who knows both the song and the flute can render the vocal melody on the instrument with consistent translation rules.
The flute is also used for personal musical reflection and, in living tradition, for performance and pedagogy outside the original ceremonial context.

Idiomatic technique on the cedar flute centers on a small but precise vocabulary of [ornaments](./ornament.md) — the [warble](./warble.md), [finger-pop](./finger-pop.md), [finger-slide](./finger-slide.md), [cut](./cut.md), [tap](./tap.md), and [trill](./trill.md) — and on the use of the [breath-pulse](./breath-pulse.md) to articulate individual tones within a continuous breath.
Phrasing is [rubato](./rubato.md), bounded by single-breath capacity, and song forms are [strophic](./strophic-form.md).
The instrument is not used in ensemble with metric percussion as a rule; its temporal substrate is non-metric breath-time, distinct from the drum-aligned timing of the whistle instruments.
