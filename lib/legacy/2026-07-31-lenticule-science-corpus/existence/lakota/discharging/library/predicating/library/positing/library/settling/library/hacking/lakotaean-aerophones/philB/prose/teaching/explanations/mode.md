A mode of a scale is what you get when you take the scale's pitch collection and treat a different one of its pitches as the tonal center — the *finalis*, the pitch to which phrases resolve and around which the scale's intervallic identity is organized.
Two modes can be the same pitch collection — the same notes, in the same physical positions on the instrument — and yet sound musically different, because the musical *function* of each pitch (whether it is the resting tone, an unstable tone, the highest stable point, the leading tone) is determined by its relation to the tonal center, not by its absolute pitch.

For an *n*-note scale, there are at most *n* available modes — one per pitch chosen as tonal center.
Some scales have fewer effective modes because of internal symmetry: a perfectly symmetric scale (like the whole-tone scale, with all intervals equal) has only one effective mode regardless of which pitch is treated as center.
The [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md), with its asymmetric interval pattern ⟨3,2,2,3,2⟩, has five distinct modes — none of the rotations produces the same interval sequence as the original.

The five modes of the anhemitonic pentatonic family that the [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md) belongs to are conventionally named in various ways across traditions; the names matter less than the identifying interval-sequence of each.
Taking the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md)'s scale-degrees 1, b3, 4, 5, b7 (with the octave as 8 = 1+12), the five modes are:

- **Mode I** — tonic 1, ascending intervals 3,2,2,3,2 — the "minor pentatonic"
- **Mode II** — tonic b3, ascending intervals 2,2,3,2,3 — the "major pentatonic" (taking b3 as the new tonic, what was b3 becomes the new 1, what was 4 becomes the new 2, and so on)
- **Mode III** — tonic 4, ascending intervals 2,3,2,3,2
- **Mode IV** — tonic 5, ascending intervals 3,2,3,2,2
- **Mode V** — tonic b7, ascending intervals 2,3,2,2,3

The Lakotaean cedar-flute repertoire principally uses Mode I — with the [fundamental](./fundamental.md) (the all-holes-covered pitch, scale-degree 1) as the tonal center to which phrases resolve.
This is a *use* convention rather than a constraint of the instrument: the same six fingerings produce the same pitches regardless of which is treated as tonal center, and a player could in principle make musical sense of any of the five modes on the same instrument.
The Mode I convention is musically grounded in two facts.
First, the [fundamental](./fundamental.md) is the lowest pitch on the instrument (the all-holes-covered position), and it is the most acoustically stable pitch and the easiest to return to as the breath's energy decays — it is therefore the natural cadential pitch.
Second, the [wiilowan](./wiilowan.md) vocal repertoire that the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) [instrumentalizes](./instrumentalization.md) uses Mode I, and the instrumentalization preserves the modal orientation of the source.

The question of whether Modes II through V appear at all in the actual played repertoire — even occasionally, as variation or as expressive contrast — is one of the open empirical questions of the theory; see the question file [is-mode-iii-pentatonic-actually-used.md](../../learning/questions/is-mode-iii-pentatonic-actually-used.md).
The theoretical availability of the alternative modes is uncontroversial; their actual presence in the repertoire is what remains to be documented carefully.

The general concept of mode — pitch collection plus choice of tonal center — extends beyond the pentatonic case to any scale with an asymmetric interval pattern.
The European diatonic modes (Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, Locrian) are the seven modal rotations of the major scale.
Indian raga theory works with similar but more elaborated modal principles.
The pentatonic case treated here is one of the simplest non-trivial cases of mode, but the principle generalizes: changing the tonal center changes the music, even when the pitches do not.
