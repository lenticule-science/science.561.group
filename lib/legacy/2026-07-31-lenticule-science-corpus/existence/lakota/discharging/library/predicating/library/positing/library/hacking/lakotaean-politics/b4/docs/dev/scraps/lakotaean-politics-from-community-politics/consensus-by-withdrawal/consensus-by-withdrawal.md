# Consensus-by-withdrawal

The Lakota decisional procedure: a council aims at consensus, and a person or tiyospaye that does not consent to a council decision has the right to withdraw rather than be coerced into compliance.
Withdrawal is not failure; it is the structural feature of the polity-form that makes coercive command impossible.

The phrase *consensus-by-withdrawal* is not a Lakota term; it is a descriptive label this subtheory uses for a structural pattern that appears across the Lakota political record.
The Lakota practice itself is not labeled with a single noun in the sources; it is enacted, not named.

## How it works

The pattern recurs at every scale of [council](omniciye.md):

1. A matter is brought before the council (a tiyospaye fire, the omniciye, the Oceti Sakowin's joint council).
2. Deliberation proceeds with listening, reflection, prayer, and ceremony.
  The pipe is passed when a decision is to be made binding.
3. Those who consent to the decision are bound by it.
4. Those who do not consent are not coerced.
  In the limiting case, a tiyospaye whose members do not consent to the larger council's decision can break camp and travel separately.
  A person within a tiyospaye whose dissent runs deeper than reconciliation can move to a different tiyospaye, or — in extreme cases — leave the people.

## Why this is not the same as majority rule with opt-out

The structural difference matters.
In majority rule, the procedure produces a decision that binds *all* members, and those who disagree must either comply or pay a sanction.
In consensus-by-withdrawal, the procedure produces a decision that binds *those who have consented*.
Withdrawal is not breaking the rules; withdrawal is exercising a recognized political right.

The consequence is that no council can decree something onto a person who has not consented.
The akicita's enforcement (see [akicita](akicita.md)) is real, but it operates within the bounds of council-delegated occasion-specific authority and on those who remain within the camp; it does not pursue those who have left.

## Why this is consensus and not anarchy

The procedure is consensus-oriented because withdrawal is a costly move that the polity is structured to minimize.
The kinship obligations that bind tiyospaye and naca and council members put intense relational pressure on reaching agreement that all can live with.
Withdrawal is available; it is structurally significant; but it is reserved for matters that genuinely cannot be reconciled.
The everyday operation of the polity is intense relational work toward agreement, not constant fissiparous departure.

This is also why the procedure does not produce paralysis.
The pressure of kin obligation, the seriousness of withdrawal as an option, and the binding force of ceremony together produce decisions that hold.
The polity has organized human collective life at the scale of seven oyate of the Oceti Sakowin across vast territory and centuries with this procedure; it is not a procedural curiosity.

## Relation to state-refusal

This is the structural mechanism that distinguishes the Lakota polity-form from the state-form most clearly.
A state's authority depends on being able to compel the dissenting party.
A polity built on consensus-by-withdrawal is structurally unable to develop into a state without losing its constitutive procedure.
Pierre Clastres' ([clastres1974](../../../references/clastres1974.md)) argument about [state-refusal](../../teaching/explanations/state-refusal.md) as active political achievement is, for the Lakota case, instantiated in this procedure.

## Continuity into the present

The procedure persists where Lakota political life is conducted under its own auspices — in traditional councils, in ceremony, in the long Oceti Sakowin response to the Black Hills compensation award (the people have continually refused to take the cash; the structural pattern of refusal-as-political-act is consensus-by-withdrawal at the polity scale).
It is in tension with the parliamentary procedures imposed by the Indian Reorganization Act constitutions, where binding-by-majority is the procedural form; that tension is itself an ongoing political question in Lakota communities.

## Sources

The procedural pattern is recurrently described, though seldom named, in the Lakota literature.
Walker's *Lakota Society* ([walker1982](references/walker1982.md)) documents the consensus orientation of council practice and notes the absence of majority-rule procedure.
Ella Deloria ([deloria-ella1944](../../../references/deloria-ella1944.md)) provides the kinship-obligation framing that makes the procedure work.
Contemporary Lakota writers — Marshall ([marshall2002](references/marshall2002.md)), Estes ([estes2019](references/estes2019.md)) — articulate the continuity into the present.
Pierre Clastres ([clastres1974](../../../references/clastres1974.md)) provides the comparative political-anthropology frame that makes the structural significance visible.
