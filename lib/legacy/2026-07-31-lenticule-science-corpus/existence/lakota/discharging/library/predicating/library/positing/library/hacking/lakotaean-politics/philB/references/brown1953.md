# Brown 1953 — The Sacred Pipe

**Black Elk.
(1953). *The Sacred Pipe: Black Elk's Account of the Seven Rites of the Oglala Sioux*.
Recorded and edited by Joseph Epes Brown.
Norman, OK: University of Oklahoma Press.
(Volume 36 of the Civilization of the American Indian Series.)**

## Annotation

Black Elk (Heȟáka Sápa, 1863–1950) was an Oglala Lakota wičháša wakȟáŋ (holy man), survivor of the Little Bighorn and Wounded Knee, and one of the most consequential transmitters of Lakota ceremonial knowledge into the recorded twentieth-century literature.
Joseph Epes Brown was a young scholar of comparative religion who lived with Black Elk and his family on the Pine Ridge Reservation in the late 1940s; Black Elk adopted him and gave him the name Chanumpa Yuha Mani (He Who Walks with the Sacred Pipe).

*The Sacred Pipe* is Black Elk's account of the seven sacred rites of the Oglala — the rites brought to the Lakota by Pte Saŋ Wiŋ (White Buffalo Calf Woman), with the sacred pipe as the foundational gift.
The seven are:

1. *Iníkağapi* — the sweat lodge / inipi
2. *Haŋbléčheyapi* — the vision quest (literally "crying for a dream")
3. *Wanáǧi Yuhápi* — the keeping of the soul
4. *Wiwáŋyaŋg Wačhípi* — the sun dance
5. *Huŋkápi* — the making-of-relatives ceremony (see [hunka](teaching/explanations/hunka.md))
6. *Išnáthi Awíčhalowaŋpi* — the preparing-of-a-young-woman ceremony
7. *Tȟapá Wakȟáyap* — the throwing of the ball

The book gives Brown's record of Black Elk's description of each rite — its origin, its components, its meaning.

## Status as a source

Black Elk's voice in this book is real but mediated.
The mediation includes: Black Elk's son Ben Black Elk's interpretation from Lakota into English; Joseph Epes Brown's editorial framing; and the wider question of how to read Black Elk's late-life accounts in light of his earlier Roman Catholic catechist work (a topic developed by Damian Costello and Michael Steltenkamp in subsequent scholarship).
Raymond DeMallie's *The Sixth Grandfather: Black Elk's Teachings Given to John G.
Neihardt* (Bison Books, 1984) is a companion critical-edition resource on the related Neihardt material (*Black Elk Speaks*).

The book is, with those mediation caveats, the most authoritative recorded account of the seven rites from a Lakota wičháša wakȟáŋ.

## Why this is a political reference

Several of the seven rites have direct political-substrate significance:

- *Huŋkápi* makes political kin (see [hunka](teaching/explanations/hunka.md)).
- *Wiwáŋyaŋg Wačhípi* (the sun dance) is the ceremony of the people gathered as a people; its political-cohesive role for the Oceti Sakowin is foundational.
- *Wanáǧi Yuhápi* (the keeping of the soul) bears on the polity's relations to its ancestors, which are not severable from the polity itself.
