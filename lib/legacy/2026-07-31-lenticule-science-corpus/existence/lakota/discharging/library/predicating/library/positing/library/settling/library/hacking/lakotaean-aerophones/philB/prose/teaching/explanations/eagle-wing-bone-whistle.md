# Eagle Wing-Bone Whistle

The eagle wing-bone whistle is a single-pitch end-blown aerophone fashioned from the hollow wing bone — typically the humerus, ulna, or radius — of an eagle.
It is the principal single-tone wind instrument of Lakotaean ensemble practice and one of the few aerophones in the tradition designed without [finger-holes](./finger-hole.md): the instrument sounds one [fundamental](./fundamental.md), and its theoretical content lies not in pitch variation but in rhythm, [timbral envelope](./ornament.md), and ensemble function.
From a music-theoretic standpoint the whistle is the paradigm case showing that a single-pitch instrument is not a trivial or pre-musical object — it has a developed vocabulary, a learnable grammar of articulation, and a specific role in the larger ensemble texture.

Construction is straightforward in principle and demanding in practice.
A hollow wing bone is cleaned, often plugged at one end with pitch or wax (sometimes leaving the bone open at both ends, depending on the desired sounding behavior), and given a small lateral cut or mouthpiece arrangement near the blowing end that creates an edge against which the player directs breath.
Unlike the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md), the whistle does not have an external block; the [embouchure](./embouchure.md) and the player's breath control take on the work of producing and shaping the tone.
Pitch is fixed by the bore length and the position of the plug (if any) — modifications a player cannot adjust in performance — so the whistle has, for the player, a single sounding pitch that varies only slightly in tuning across whistles and across players.

The fundamental sits in a high register — typically several octaves above middle C — producing a piercing, narrow-band tone audible over the texture of [drum](./drum.md) and [voice](./voice.md) in large outdoor ceremonial settings.
The whistle can be made to [overblow](./overblowing.md) to a higher [harmonic](./harmonic.md) by increasing breath pressure, producing a still-higher pitched flare; in some traditions this overblow is used cadentially or as a heightening device.
The whistle can also produce a [warble](./warble.md) — rapid alternation between fundamental and an upper harmonic via jet instability — which functions as an ornament and as a signal of intensity.

The whistle's musical content unfolds along three axes that the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) does not foreground.
The first is *timbral envelope*: each whistle articulation has a definite attack shape (sharp or rolled), a definite sustain (short or long), and a definite decay (clean or with warble).
Players draw from a small grammar of envelope types — a short sharp tone, a long sustained tone, a sustained tone with terminal warble, a pulsed sustain — and a "tune" on the whistle is a sequence of these envelopes.
The second is *rhythm*: whistle calls are rhythmically composed from a small set of figures (single, paired, triplet, sustained-with-pulse), and the rhythmic phrasing is what distinguishes one whistle pattern from another when the pitch axis is collapsed.
The third is *ensemble function*: the whistle is virtually always heard in ensemble with [drum](./drum.md) and [voice](./voice.md), and its phrasing is keyed to the metric structure of the drum and to specific structural moments in the song (phrase ends, dance-step changes, accent points within an honor song).

The instrument carries strong cultural and legal significance in contemporary practice — eagle parts are restricted under United States federal law and access is governed both by federal permit and by tribal practice.
These conditions are properly addressed in the [lakotaean-aerophonic-culture](../../theories/lakotaean-aerophonic-culture/_index.md) sub-theory and are not the music-theoretic content of the instrument; the music theory of the eagle wing-bone whistle is the grammar of envelope, rhythm, and ensemble function described above.
