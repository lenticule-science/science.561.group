# Claim. The standard cedar flute, with all declared required holes covered, sounds the fundamental; uncovering holes 1–5 sequentially produces scale-degrees b3, 4, 5, b7, 8 of the pentatonic minor.

**Claim.** On the standard six-hole [cedar-flute](teaching/explanations/cedar-flute.md), the all-six-holes-covered fingering sounds the [fundamental](teaching/explanations/fundamental.md) (scale-degree 1 of the [pentatonic-minor-scale](teaching/explanations/pentatonic-minor-scale.md)).
Sequentially uncovering [finger-holes](teaching/explanations/finger-hole.md) from the foot-most hole (hole 1) toward the head produces, in order, the scale-degrees b3 (uncover hole 1), 4 (uncover holes 1–2), 5 (uncover holes 1–3), b7 (uncover holes 1–4), and 8/octave (uncover holes 1–5).
The sixth hole, when used, extends the playing range upward into the second octave by [overblowing](teaching/explanations/overblowing.md) or by additional fingering.
This standard tuning is the conventional Plains cedar-flute tuning across makers and lineages.

**Argument.**

*The all-holes-covered fundamental.* When all declared required [finger-holes](teaching/explanations/finger-hole.md) are covered, the air column extends from the [duct-flute](teaching/explanations/duct-flute.md) wind way at the head all the way to the foot of the instrument, terminating at the open foot.
The [bore](teaching/explanations/bore.md), being approximately cylindrical and open at both ends, supports a fundamental standing wave whose wavelength is approximately 2L (with end-corrections), where L is the bore length from the splitting edge to the foot opening.
The [fundamental](teaching/explanations/fundamental.md) frequency is therefore v/2L (v = speed of sound).
This pitch is scale-degree 1 of the [pentatonic-minor-scale](teaching/explanations/pentatonic-minor-scale.md) — the [tonic](teaching/explanations/fundamental.md) — and is the pitch the entire scale is referenced to.

*Sequential hole-uncovering produces successive scale-degrees.* The conventional placement of the six holes along the [bore](teaching/explanations/bore.md) is what determines which scale-degrees the instrument produces.
The holes are positioned such that uncovering hole 1 (foot-most) shortens the effective air column to produce a pitch a minor third (3 semitones, see [interval](teaching/explanations/interval.md)) above the fundamental — scale-degree b3.
Successive holes are positioned to produce, in turn, the scale-degrees 4 (perfect fourth, 5 semitones above the fundamental), 5 (perfect fifth, 7 semitones), b7 (minor seventh, 10 semitones), and 8/octave (perfect octave, 12 semitones).
The hole positions are not the unique positions that would produce these scale-degrees — there is some range of variation across makers — but the positions are constrained by the requirement that the scale produced be the [pentatonic-minor-scale](teaching/explanations/pentatonic-minor-scale.md), and that requirement defines the conventional tuning.

*The pentatonic minor specifically.* The choice that the conventional tuning produces the [pentatonic-minor-scale](teaching/explanations/pentatonic-minor-scale.md) (as opposed to some other scale a six-hole instrument could produce) is a *historical-musical* fact, not an acoustic necessity.
A six-hole flute with holes placed at different positions would produce a different scale.
The choice of the pentatonic minor is grounded in the [wiilowan](teaching/explanations/wiilowan.md) vocal repertoire that the [cedar-flute](teaching/explanations/cedar-flute.md) [instrumentalizes](teaching/explanations/instrumentalization.md) — the vocal repertoire uses the pentatonic minor, and the flute is built to render that repertoire.
The flute's tuning is therefore *derived from* the vocal practice it serves.

*The sixth hole and range extension.* The sixth hole (closest to the head) is used in two principal ways in idiomatic playing.
First, it can be uncovered as part of the [overblowing](teaching/explanations/overblowing.md) embouchure-fingering for accessing second-octave pitches.
Second, on some flute traditions and some specific instruments, it is used for non-standard scale-degree access — a cross-fingering involving hole 6 can produce a scale-degree slightly different from the standard ones, useful for occasional non-scale tones or for fine tuning.
The standard tuning, however, makes principal use of holes 1–5, with hole 6 in an auxiliary role.

*Intonation, not just nominal scale-degrees.* The scale-degrees sounded are nominally those of the [pentatonic-minor-scale](teaching/explanations/pentatonic-minor-scale.md), but the actual [cents](teaching/explanations/cents.md)-values of the scale on a typical cedar flute are not 12-tone equal temperament.
The b3 typically sits 20–40 cents flat of 12-TET; the b7 typically sits 20–40 cents flat of 12-TET; the perfect fifth and the octave are close to their just values.
See the structure [cedar-flute-cents-table](../code/structures/cedar-flute-cents-table.md) for the detailed intonational profile.
