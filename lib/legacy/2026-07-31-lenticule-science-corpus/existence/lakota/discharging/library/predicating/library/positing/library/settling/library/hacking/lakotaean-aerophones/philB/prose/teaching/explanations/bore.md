The bore of a wind instrument is the resonating air column — the volume of air, enclosed by the instrument's tube, that vibrates to produce sound.
The bore's *length* sets the wavelength of the [fundamental](./fundamental.md) tone the instrument produces; the bore's *cross-sectional profile* (cylindrical, conical, irregular) determines which [harmonics](./harmonic.md) above the fundamental are admissible and how strongly; the bore's *end configuration* (open at both ends, open at one end and closed at the other, or open with a duct constraint) determines which harmonics actually appear in the [harmonic-series](./harmonic.md) the instrument can sound and which are suppressed.
The bore is therefore the central acoustic structure of an aerophone — what determines, before any consideration of [finger-holes](./finger-hole.md) or [embouchure](./embouchure.md), the basic pitch material the instrument is capable of.

The [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) has an approximately *cylindrical* bore — the cross-section is roughly constant from head to foot — and is *open at both ends* (the upper end opens via the [duct-flute](./duct-flute.md) wind way and the splitting-edge cutout, and the foot is open).
This bore configuration has a specific acoustic consequence: an open-open cylindrical bore admits all integer multiples of the fundamental as standing-wave modes.
That is, if the fundamental has frequency *f*, the bore can sound *f*, 2*f*, 3*f*, 4*f*, and so on as overtones — the full [harmonic-series](./harmonic.md).
(A *closed-open* cylindrical bore — closed at one end, open at the other, like a stopped pipe — admits only the *odd* harmonics: *f*, 3*f*, 5*f*, …) The cedar flute's open-open cylindrical bore is therefore acoustically rich, but in practice it sounds principally the [fundamental](./fundamental.md) on the player's normal breath pressure and the second harmonic (octave) on [overblowing](./overblowing.md); higher harmonics are physically available but not idiomatic in the playing tradition.

The bores of the bone whistles ([eagle-wing-bone-whistle](./eagle-wing-bone-whistle.md), [crane-bone-whistle](./crane-bone-whistle.md), [elk-whistle](./elk-whistle.md)) are also approximately cylindrical — the wing bones and antler tines they are made from happen to be nearly straight tubes of slowly varying cross-section.
The bone-whistle bores are short relative to the cedar flute's, which sets their [fundamental](./fundamental.md) in a high register, and they are open at both ends in the standard construction (or closed at one end by a plug, which lowers the fundamental for a given length and shifts the bore's harmonic admissibility from open-open to open-closed).
The single-pitch character of these instruments is a consequence of their having no [finger-holes](./finger-hole.md), not of their bore acoustics — the bore would admit [overblowing](./overblowing.md) to higher harmonics, and in some playing traditions the warble or terminal flare exploits exactly that capacity.

For music-theoretic purposes the bore is the structure from which pitch material is *derived* in the Lakotaean aerophone tradition.
The [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md) is not imposed on the cedar flute from above; it is the scale that emerges from the conventional [finger-hole](./finger-hole.md) placements on a cylindrical-bore open-open duct flute of the cedar tradition's standard dimensions.
The cents-deviations of the actual scale degrees from 12-tone equal temperament are likewise consequences of the bore-and-hole geometry — small whole-number harmonic ratios are easier to sound and more stable than equal-tempered ratios on a cylindrical bore, and the scale that emerges from conventional hole placement reflects this physical fact.
A music theory of the cedar flute that treats the [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md) as primary and the bore as background reverses the actual derivation: the bore is primary, and the scale is what the bore-with-conventional-holes produces.
