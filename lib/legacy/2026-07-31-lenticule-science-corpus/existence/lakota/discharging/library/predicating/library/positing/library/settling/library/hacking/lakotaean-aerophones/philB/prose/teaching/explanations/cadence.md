# Cadence

A cadence is a phrase- or song-ending gesture that resolves the melodic motion of the phrase to a stable tonal position — typically the [tonic](./fundamental.md) scale-degree — and signals the phrase's terminus.
The cadence is the canonical structural marker of melodic closure: a listener recognizes that a phrase has ended (rather than being interrupted, or merely paused mid-motion) by hearing the cadential gesture.
Cadences in the Lakotaean [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) tradition form a small, named, and identifiable vocabulary, and the choice of cadence is one of the music-theoretic decisions a player makes for each phrase.

The principal cadences of the cedar-flute idiom are:

- The *descending minor third* cadence — the canonical cadence — in which the phrase concludes on the scale-degree b3 (the minor third above the tonic), followed by a descent to the fundamental scale-degree 1.
  The b3-to-1 motion is the most idiomatic cadential gesture in the repertoire, and it is the cadence that the typical [courting-song](./courting-song.md) phrase resolves on.

- The *descending perfect fourth* cadence, in which the phrase concludes on the scale-degree 4 (the perfect fourth above the tonic), followed by a descent to the fundamental scale-degree 1.
  The 4-to-1 motion is less common than the b3-to-1 motion but still idiomatic; it provides a slightly less close approach to the tonic (the 4 is a more distant interval than the b3) and a slightly different musical character.

- The *descending perfect fifth* cadence, in which the phrase concludes on the scale-degree 5 (the perfect fifth above the tonic), followed by a descent to the fundamental scale-degree 1.
  The 5-to-1 motion is the strongest of the descending-to-tonic cadences in interval-content (the perfect fifth is the most acoustically stable cadential interval) but appears less frequently in cedar-flute repertoire than the b3-to-1 and 4-to-1 cadences, perhaps because the perfect-fifth descent traverses several intermediate scale-degrees and the cadential descent is therefore less compact.

- The *held tonic with warble* cadence, in which the phrase concludes on the fundamental scale-degree 1, held for an extended duration, with a [warble](./warble.md) ornament applied to the held tonic.
  This cadence can stand alone or can follow one of the descending-to-tonic motions above; it is the most characteristic cadential figure of the cedar-flute idiom in its full expressive form, and it provides a natural decay shape for the phrase as the breath fades through the warble.

The cadential vocabulary is small — these four principal cadences cover most of what appears in the documented [courting-song](./courting-song.md) repertoire — and the choice among them is a music-theoretic decision with definite consequences.
A phrase ending on the b3-to-1 motion sounds more conclusive than one ending on the 4-to-1 motion; a phrase ending with a held-tonic-warble sounds more definitively terminal than one ending with a clean tone.
Players use the cadential vocabulary to mark different levels of closure: an internal phrase within a [strophe](./strophe.md) may end on a less-conclusive cadence (the 4-to-1) while the strophe-final phrase ends on a more-conclusive cadence (the b3-to-1 with held-tonic-warble); the song's final strophe may end on the most-conclusive cadence (a held-tonic-warble extended slightly longer than usual, with the breath fully decayed).

The cadence concept extends naturally to the vocal [wiilowan](./wiilowan.md) repertoire, where the same cadential motions appear — and where the cedar-flute cadential vocabulary is partly inherited from the vocal source.
The [warble](./warble.md) cadential ornament is the principal flute-specific cadential figure without a direct vocal analogue (vocal music has cadential figures, but the warble's specific jet-instability mechanism is flute-idiomatic).
