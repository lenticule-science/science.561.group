An ornament is a small melodic or articulative decoration applied to a base tone in a musical phrase — a brief deviation from the plain tone that adds expressive, idiomatic, or stylistic content without changing the phrase's underlying melodic identity.
The ornament is the unit of expressive variation in the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) idiom and in many other musical traditions; the vocabulary of named ornaments available to a player constitutes one of the principal music-theoretic objects of the tradition.

The Lakotaean cedar-flute repertoire admits a small but precise vocabulary of ornaments — the [warble](./warble.md), [finger-pop](./finger-pop.md), [finger-slide](./finger-slide.md), [cut](./cut.md), [tap](./tap.md), [trill](./trill.md), and [breath-pulse](./breath-pulse.md).
Each is a distinct articulatory action, with a definite acoustic effect, a definite typical duration (most ornaments are well under a second), and definite rules about where in a phrase it can appear.
The ornament vocabulary is the musical analogue of a small inflectional system: a base melodic figure can appear in plain or ornamented form, and the ornaments are the inflections that produce the ornamented variants.
The [ornament-substitution](../../code/transformers/ornament-substitution.md) transformer is the music-theoretic formalization of this process.

Ornaments are not interchangeable.
Each ornament in the cedar-flute vocabulary has a *placement*: a specification of which positions within a phrase it can occupy.
Cadential ornaments — the [warble](./warble.md), the slide-to-tonic, the held tonic — belong at the [cadence](./cadence.md), at the phrase's terminal scale-degree.
Mid-phrase ornaments — the [cut](./cut.md), [tap](./tap.md), [trill](./trill.md) — decorate scale-degrees within the phrase's interior.
The [breath-pulse](./breath-pulse.md) is special: it functions as both a syllable-marker (articulating one melodic unit from the next within a continuous breath) and as a low-grade ornament (giving the underlying tone an expressive pulse rather than a continuous sustain).
The [finger-pop](./finger-pop.md) and [finger-slide](./finger-slide.md) appear in various positions depending on the player's idiom and the song's requirements; the slide is particularly idiomatic at phrase-beginnings (as an approach to a scale-degree from below) and at cadences (as a glide into the terminal tonic).

The function of the ornament vocabulary is twofold.
First, it provides the player with a means of *expressive variation* within a fixed melodic frame: the same melodic phrase can be played plain on one strophe and ornamented variously on subsequent strophes, with the ornaments carrying the expressive content while the melody remains identifiably the same.
This is the principal mechanism of [strophic-variation](../../code/transformers/strophic-variation.md) in [courting-song](./courting-song.md) performance.
Second, it provides the music with *stylistic identity*: a flute performance lacking the cedar-flute ornament vocabulary — say, a synthesizer rendering of the same pitches and rhythms — sounds *not like cedar flute music* even when the underlying melody is identical.
The ornaments are what make the music sound the way it sounds.

Ornament vocabulary in the cedar-flute tradition partially overlaps with ornament vocabulary in [wiilowan](./wiilowan.md) vocal performance — vocal ornaments include glissando (vocal sliding), melismatic decoration, vocal pulses, and timbral inflection.
The [instrumentalization](./instrumentalization.md) of vocal song onto the cedar flute uses flute ornaments to render vocal ornaments, with broad correspondences (vocal glissando → flute slide; vocal melisma → flute cut or trill; vocal pulse → flute breath-pulse) and some non-trivial cases (the flute's [warble](./warble.md) has no direct vocal analogue and is added at the cadence in flute performance regardless of whether the vocal source had a warble-like ornament).

Other musical traditions have their own ornament vocabularies, and the named ornaments of the cedar-flute tradition partially overlap with named ornaments in other duct-flute traditions (the European recorder's vocabulary of ornaments shares some general categories — slides, trills, cuts — with the cedar flute's), but the specific ornaments and the rules for their use are distinctive to the Lakotaean tradition and are not interchangeable with other traditions' vocabularies.
