Overblowing is the technique by which a player of a wind instrument drives the [bore](./bore.md) into a vibration mode higher than the [fundamental](./fundamental.md), causing the instrument to sound at a [harmonic](./harmonic.md) of the fundamental rather than at the fundamental itself.
Mechanically, this is accomplished by increasing the breath pressure (and, on embouchure-controlled instruments, by adjusting [embouchure](./embouchure.md) tension and aperture) above the level that sustains the fundamental, into the regime that supports the next standing-wave mode of the bore.

For an open-open cylindrical [bore](./bore.md) — the configuration of the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) and most Plains bone whistles — the second [harmonic](./harmonic.md) is the *octave* above the fundamental (2×*f*).
Overblowing to this second harmonic is therefore an *octave up*: the same [finger-hole](./finger-hole.md) state that produces the fundamental's pitch at normal breath pressure produces a pitch one octave higher when overblown.
On the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md), this means the player can in principle access the full [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md) in two octaves — the first octave at normal breath pressure with hole-states 1 through 6, and the second octave by overblowing those same hole-states.
In actual playing tradition, the second-octave range is used as an occasional expressive extension rather than as the principal melodic territory; the canonical [courting-song](./courting-song.md) sits in the first octave with the second octave appearing for the climactic moment of certain song-types and for the occasional ornamental flourish.

Overblowing to the third [harmonic](./harmonic.md) (3×*f*, an octave-plus-a-fifth above the fundamental) is physically possible on a cylindrical-bore aerophone but requires considerably more breath pressure and embouchure control than the octave overblow, and it is not idiomatic on the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) — the third-harmonic register is not part of the standard repertoire.
On the [eagle-wing-bone-whistle](./eagle-wing-bone-whistle.md) and other end-blown bone whistles, where the [embouchure](./embouchure.md) gives the player more direct control over the air-jet, third-harmonic overblowing occasionally appears as a high "flare" articulation for climactic moments.

Overblowing has musical consequences beyond just the higher pitch it produces.
First, the timbre of an overblown tone differs from the fundamental — the overblown tone is brighter, often slightly thinner, with different harmonic content above the sounding pitch — so an overblown note is a distinct timbral object, not just a higher fundamental.
Second, the breath pressure required to sustain an overblow is higher and the attack must be more decisive — overblown phrases tend to be shorter and more sharply articulated than fundamental-register phrases.
Third, the *transition* between the fundamental register and the overblown register is itself a musical event: a clean octave-jump on a single fingering, accomplished by a sharp increase in breath pressure, is a characteristic gesture available on the cedar flute and used at moments of phrase climax or melodic peak.

Overblowing is also the mechanism behind the [warble](./warble.md).
The warble occurs when the player's breath pressure is held precisely on the boundary between the fundamental and second-harmonic regimes, where the air-jet is unstable and "flips" between the two modes at audio rate.
The same physics that enables clean overblowing — the bore's admission of the second harmonic — enables the warble as an unstable-jet ornament.
A player who can overblow cleanly has the breath control to position the breath precisely on the warble threshold; a player who cannot will produce overblow flares unintentionally where a warble was intended, or vice versa.
