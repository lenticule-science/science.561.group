The tap is a brief percussive finger-action on a [finger-hole](./finger-hole.md) of the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) that articulates the currently-held tone with a rhythmic accent, with minimal or no audible change in sounding pitch.
Where the [finger-pop](./finger-pop.md) and the [cut](./cut.md) produce noticeable pitch deviations through brief changes in the air column's effective length, the tap produces *primarily* a percussive event — a brief mechanical perturbation of the air column that creates an audible articulation without significantly displacing the pitch.

The tap is the most subtle of the cedar-flute's finger-controlled [ornaments](./ornament.md), and its identification as a distinct named ornament — as opposed to a marginal effect of imprecise fingering — depends on the player tradition.
Some players and some teaching lineages distinguish the tap as a deliberate articulative device with specific placement rules; other lineages absorb the tap into a broader category of "articulative finger-action" alongside the pop and the cut, with the tap-versus-pop distinction emerging only as a continuous variable (how much pitch change the brief finger-action produces).
For the purposes of this theory, the tap is named distinctly because it is musically distinguishable: a tap produces a percussive accent without changing the perceived pitch, while a pop produces a percussive accent *with* a momentary upward pitch deviation.

Mechanically, the tap is produced by a fingertip striking a [finger-hole](./finger-hole.md) — whether covered or open — sharply enough to mechanically perturb the air column without re-configuring it significantly.
A tap on a *covered* hole produces almost no acoustic effect from a fluidic-flow standpoint (the hole was covered, the finger strikes it briefly harder, the hole remains effectively covered), but the mechanical impact transmits through the wood and produces a soft percussive sound, faintly audible above the sustained tone.
A tap on an *open* hole produces a slightly more noticeable effect: the brief finger-strike momentarily disturbs the air-column termination at that position, producing a slight pitch flutter or a brief noise component on the sustained tone.

The tap's musical functions are narrower than those of the more pitch-displacing [ornaments](./ornament.md).
The principal function is *rhythmic articulation*: the tap marks a beat or rhythmic structure within a held tone without disrupting the melodic identity of the tone (since the pitch barely changes).
A skilled player can use a sequence of taps to articulate a rhythm against a single sustained scale-degree, producing a percussive layer on top of the sustained melodic content.
This is analogous to the vocal tradition's use of [breath-pulse](./breath-pulse.md) to articulate within a single sustained vocal tone — the tap is the finger-controlled analogue of the breath-controlled pulse.

A secondary function is *texture*: a sequence of soft taps on a covered hole adds a subtle rhythmic noise-component to a sustained tone, slightly enriching the tone's spectral character without changing its melodic content.
This use of the tap is more often associated with quiet, intimate cedar-flute playing than with louder ceremonial or performative contexts.

The tap is distinguished from the [finger-pop](./finger-pop.md) by the absence (or minimal presence) of pitch deviation: a pop has a clear momentary higher pitch, a tap does not.
It is distinguished from the [cut](./cut.md) likewise by the absence of pitch deviation in the opposite direction.
It is distinguished from the [trill](./trill.md) by being a single brief event rather than a sustained alternation.
The boundary between the tap and the more pitch-displacing ornaments is somewhat fluid — a "hard tap" on a covered hole that briefly partially-unseals it is close to a soft pop, and a "soft pop" is close to a hard tap — and players' ornament vocabularies vary in how sharply they draw the tap-versus-pop distinction.
