**Algorithmic management** is the exercise of managerial functions — task allocation, performance evaluation, pricing, scheduling, discipline, deactivation — by software systems rather than (or alongside) human supervisors. The term emerged in the mid-2010s in studies of platform-mediated work (Uber, TaskRabbit, Deliveroo, Amazon Mechanical Turk, Instacart) and has since extended to cover algorithmic systems deployed in conventional employment settings (warehouse logistics, call centers, white-collar productivity-monitoring). Alex Rosenblat and Luke Stark's [foundational paper](rosenblat-stark-2016) on Uber drivers and Rosenblat's [Uberland](rosenblat2018) supplied the field's empirical and conceptual ground; Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin's [Algorithms at Work](kellogg-valentine-christin-2020) provided the standard theoretical synthesis.

## The six R's

Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin's synthesis identifies six dimensions along which algorithmic systems exercise managerial control — explicitly reframing Edwards's [contested terrain](edwards1979) for the algorithmic setting:

1. **Restricting** — narrowing the worker's choice set (which tasks appear, which destinations are visible, which actions the interface permits).
2. **Recommending** — directing the worker toward platform-preferred actions through highlighting, prompts, default options, and gamified incentives.
3. **Recording** — capturing detailed behavioral data: location, speed, acceptance rates, response times, customer interactions, sometimes facial and bodily measurements.
4. **Rating** — evaluating performance in real time through customer ratings, completion metrics, response-time scores, and aggregate algorithmic risk scores.
5. **Replacing** — substituting workers easily through the platform's matching function, with automated deactivation triggered by rating thresholds or policy violations.
6. **Rewarding** — adjusting compensation through algorithmically determined dynamic pricing (surge, peak, multipliers), bonuses, and tier-based incentives.

Each R names a managerial function that human management has long performed; what is novel is the *speed*, *granularity*, *opacity*, *unaccountability*, and *unilaterality* with which algorithmic systems perform them. A human manager who deactivated a worker had to give reasons, hear appeals, and live with the worker's response; an algorithm's deactivation arrives by email and is generally non-appealable.

## Worker resistance

The same paper catalogs the corresponding repertoire of worker resistance — *algoactivism* in the field's vocabulary — that has emerged:

- **Gaming the rating system** (timing acceptances and cancellations strategically; cultivating customer relationships to secure positive ratings; coordinating with other workers to manipulate aggregate metrics).
- **Information sharing** through driver forums (Reddit, Facebook groups, WhatsApp networks, dedicated apps like Driver Help) that pool platform-knowledge management does not provide.
- **Collective action** through driver associations, work stoppages, mass deactivations, regulatory campaigns, and litigation challenging worker classification.
- **Exit and multi-homing** — working across multiple platforms simultaneously to extract bargaining leverage no single platform's algorithm can fully capture.

The algorithmic-management literature insists, against accounts that treat algorithmic control as totalizing, that workers retain meaningful agency and that the contested terrain is genuinely contested. The algorithm's apparent omniscience and unaccountability are limits to be worked around, not absolute conditions.

## Beyond the platform

The original empirical referent — gig-platform work — has been increasingly extended to cover:

- **Warehouse logistics** (Amazon's quota systems, package-handling productivity tracking, restroom-time monitoring).
- **Call centers** (real-time emotion analysis, talk-time optimization, post-call coaching prompts).
- **White-collar productivity monitoring** (keystroke logging, attention tracking, Microsoft Teams "productivity score," ActivTrak-style dashboards).
- **Healthcare and education** (Epic productivity dashboards for clinicians, learning-management-system metrics for instructors).
- **Algorithmic hiring** (resume-screening AI, video-interview analysis, predictive assessment tools).

Ifeoma Ajunwa's [The Quantified Worker](ajunwa2023) is the most comprehensive contemporary mapping of the broader regime, making the case that the algorithmic-management apparatus has expanded far beyond the gig-platform setting and now operates across most of the U.S. workforce in some form, while remaining largely outside the labor and privacy law frameworks that regulated earlier surveillance forms.

## Distinguished from

- **[Scientific management](scientific-management.md)** (Taylor) — algorithmic management is the contemporary descendant of Taylorism. The methodology (detailed measurement, separation of conception from execution, prescribed procedures, output-tied pay) is recognizably Taylorist; what is novel is the *vastly greater granularity*, the *real-time pacing*, the *automation of the supervisor*, and the *typical absence of the worker-employer relationship that Taylorist workplaces presupposed*.
- **Bureaucratic control** (Edwards) — bureaucratic control operates through stable rules and personnel systems negotiated over time and accessible to grievance procedures; algorithmic management operates through opaque decision systems whose rules are typically proprietary, dynamically updated, and not subject to procedural challenge.
- **Decision-support systems** — earlier-generation IT systems supplied *information* to human managers who then made decisions; algorithmic management *makes the decisions* and supplies them to workers (or to the lower-tier humans who execute the algorithm's outputs). The locus of decision-making is the analytical difference.
- **The platform itself** — the platform is the larger institutional form (the firm, the legal entity, the consumer-facing interface, the matching infrastructure); algorithmic management is the specific *managerial functioning* the platform performs over the workers it intermediates. A platform that classified workers as employees and deployed human managers would still be a platform; it would not exhibit algorithmic management.
