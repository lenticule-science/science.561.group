A **coordination mechanism**, in Henry Mintzberg's sense, is one of a small number of basic means by which organizations coordinate the work of their members. Mintzberg's [The Structuring of Organizations](mintzberg1979) — the classic synthesis of mid-twentieth-century organization theory — identified five mechanisms in the original formulation; later work [mintzberg1989] extended the list to six. The mechanisms are the elementary vocabulary of organizational design: any organization's coordination apparatus is some combination of them, and the appropriateness of any given mechanism depends on the scale, complexity, uncertainty, and substantive content of the work being coordinated.

## The six mechanisms

1. **Mutual adjustment.** Coordination by informal communication between the workers doing the work. Two carpenters building a cabinet talk about how to fit the joinery; a small startup team discusses what to build next over coffee. The mechanism is fast, flexible, and responsive to local information; it scales poorly because every additional participant adds combinatorially many communication links.
2. **Direct supervision.** Coordination by a supervisor who issues instructions to the workers and monitors compliance. The foreman tells the assembly-line workers what to do and checks they do it; the manager assigns tasks to the team and reviews the output. Effective at small-to-medium scale; breaks down when the supervisor's span of attention is exceeded or when the work's substantive content exceeds the supervisor's competence.
3. **Standardization of work processes.** Coordination by specifying *how* the work is to be done — the steps, sequences, and procedures every worker performing the task must follow. The Taylorist instruction card, the surgical checklist, the call-center script, and the franchise operations manual are paradigm cases. Effective when the work is well-understood and stable; brittle when conditions change faster than procedures can be updated.
4. **Standardization of outputs.** Coordination by specifying *what* the work must produce — the dimensions, performance characteristics, or quality standards the output must meet — while leaving the *how* to the worker's discretion. The contract with deliverables, the engineering specification, the academic paper's submission criteria are examples. Effective when outputs can be specified without specifying processes; requires the worker to have the competence to determine the process.
5. **Standardization of skills (and knowledge).** Coordination by training workers to a common standard, then trusting the trained worker to coordinate themselves with others trained to the same standard. The medical residency program, the bar-admitted lawyer, the licensed electrician are paradigm cases: when two surgeons meet in an operating theater, they coordinate not because either supervises the other but because both have internalized the same protocols through extended training. Effective for professional and craft work; requires a substantial training infrastructure outside the firm.
6. **Standardization of norms (the sixth mechanism, added in 1989).** Coordination by inculcation of shared values, commitments, or worldviews that orient the work without specifying its operations. Mission-driven organizations (religious orders, ideological political parties, certain non-profits, some startups) coordinate through shared *commitment* rather than through procedures, outputs, or skills. The mechanism is hard to manufacture and easy to lose.

## Conditions of fit

The six mechanisms are not interchangeable. Mintzberg's central design claim is that each mechanism is *appropriate to* particular conditions:

- **Mutual adjustment** fits very small or very innovative work where the situation is novel enough that no procedure could be specified in advance.
- **Direct supervision** fits small-to-medium scale work where one competent supervisor can attend to all the workers and the work is stable enough to be supervised.
- **Standardization of work processes** fits high-volume, well-understood, repetitive work where the gain from optimization outweighs the loss of flexibility.
- **Standardization of outputs** fits work where the output can be specified independently of the process and the worker has the competence to determine the process.
- **Standardization of skills** fits professional and craft work where extensive prior training can substitute for in-firm coordination.
- **Standardization of norms** fits mission-driven work where shared commitment carries the coordination load procedure cannot.

Most real organizations combine mechanisms. A hospital uses standardization of skills (the trained physician), standardization of processes (the surgical checklist), direct supervision (the resident-attending relationship), mutual adjustment (the case conference), standardization of outputs (the discharge criteria), and standardization of norms (the professional ethic). The proportions vary by unit and by case.

## The classical-organization-theory background

Mintzberg's coordination-mechanism framework synthesizes a longer tradition. James Thompson's [Organizations in Action](thompson1967) had developed the typology of *interdependence* (pooled, sequential, reciprocal) that calls for different coordination structures (rules, plans, mutual adjustment respectively). March and Simon's [Organizations](march-simon-1958) had analyzed *coordination by program* (standardization) versus *coordination by feedback* (mutual adjustment) under different uncertainty conditions. Mintzberg's contribution was to fuse these and adjacent literatures into a single design vocabulary that practitioners and analysts could apply.

The framework has been criticized for over-tidying a messy phenomenon, for under-theorizing the political dimension of coordination (treating coordination as a technical optimization problem rather than as a contested terrain), and for treating organizations as bounded entities when contemporary work increasingly crosses organizational boundaries. The mechanisms remain, however, the standard analytical inventory in organizational analysis.

## Distinguished from

- **[Bureaucracy](bureaucracy.md)** — bureaucracy is a particular *form of organization* characterized by a particular combination of coordination mechanisms (heavy on standardization of work processes and direct supervision via the office hierarchy, light on mutual adjustment and standardization of norms). Coordination mechanisms are the elementary components; bureaucracy is one institutional configuration of them.
- **[Scientific management](scientific-management.md)** — scientific management is a specific methodology for implementing standardization-of-work-processes coordination, originally for industrial production and subsequently extended to other domains. It is one tradition of one mechanism, not the whole vocabulary.
- **[Role](theories/governance/terms/role.md)** — a role is the organizational position grouping the [capabilities](capability.md) and duties of an incumbent; a coordination mechanism is the means by which the work of role-incumbents is coordinated with each other. The two address adjacent but different questions: roles partition the work, coordination mechanisms integrate the partitioned work.
- **Authority structure** — the authority structure specifies who can issue binding directives to whom; the coordination mechanism specifies how work is integrated. Authority structures and coordination mechanisms are independent dimensions: the same authority structure can integrate work through any of the six mechanisms.
