# Comparative-historical analysis

*The method family that explains macro-historical outcomes — revolutions, state formations, regime transitions, the rise and fall of social movements — by systematic comparison across historical cases, attending to sequence, conjuncture, and path dependence.*

The method's modern form was consolidated by Barrington Moore (*Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy*, 1966), Theda Skocpol (*States and Social Revolutions*, 1979), Charles Tilly, and the generation that followed (Mahoney, Rueschemeyer, Thelen). Earlier sources include Marx's analyses of nineteenth-century French politics, Weber's comparative sociology of religion and authority, and the historical-sociological tradition of Bloch and Braudel.

The method's commitments. Cases are not data points; each is a structured configuration whose internal coherence the analyst must understand before comparison is possible. Comparison across cases is then organized by a logic — Mill's methods of agreement and difference, in Skocpol's classical formulation; or, in more recent work, set-theoretic methods (qualitative comparative analysis) that handle multiple causal pathways and conjunctural causation. Sequence and timing matter: revolutions are not the same kind of thing wherever they occur; what came before and what was happening simultaneously elsewhere is part of the explanans.

Path dependence is the method's signature concept. Outcomes at time *t* constrain what is possible at time *t+1*; small differences early can lock in divergent trajectories. The analyst's job is to trace those trajectories without smoothing them into a single covering law.

Limits. The method works best at intermediate-N (a handful to a few dozen cases). It is data-intensive — each case requires substantial historical and archival work. It struggles when cases are non-independent (as in diffusion or world-systemic processes), and it is often pressed by quantitative critics on the issue of selection on the dependent variable.

Tilly's late work pushed the method toward the analysis of *mechanisms* — recurrent micro-causal processes that compose into macro outcomes — as a way of squaring case-specific richness with comparative generalization.
