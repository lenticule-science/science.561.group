# Action

*The role concept for what people **do** in society — meaningful conduct oriented toward, with, or against others. Action is sociology's basic unit of analysis when the discipline asks how the social world is brought about and held together by what people do.*

Weber defined social action as conduct to which the actor attaches subjective meaning, and which takes account of the behavior of others. The definition does the work of separating *action* from mere reflex or behavior: a sneeze is not action; a deliberate cough to interrupt a speaker is. Weber's typology — instrumentally rational, value-rational, affectual, traditional — was the first systematic claim that action is not one thing but a structured family.

The schools diverge. Parsons's structural-functionalism reads action as enacting roles inside a normative order. Symbolic-interactionism (Mead, Blumer, Goffman) reads action as the moment-to-moment construction of meaning between actors. Bourdieu reads action as the play of [habitus](habitus.md) and disposition inside a [field](field.md), neither pure choice nor pure structure. Practice theorists (Schatzki, Reckwitz) read action as the performance of socially shared practices. Marxism reads action under capitalism as labor — and labor as the activity through which the actor is alienated from what they make.

Action's twin concept is [structure](structure.md). The action–structure question is one of the discipline's recurring axes: do social arrangements determine what people do, or do people's actions produce and reproduce the arrangements? Most contemporary frameworks (Giddens's structuration, Bourdieu's habitus) refuse the dichotomy and treat action and structure as mutually constitutive.
