# Habitus

*The role concept, after Bourdieu, for the durable, generative system of dispositions — perceptual, evaluative, action-generating — that an actor acquires through prolonged exposure to a position in social space and that organizes that actor's subsequent practice without conscious calculation.*

Habitus is Bourdieu's central move for refusing the action–structure dichotomy. Habitus is *embodied structure*: the structure of the social world inscribed in the body as taste, posture, sense of what is comfortable, sense of what is "for people like us." Habitus generates action — but action that is "regulated improvisation," not mechanical execution. The actor is not following rules but is *disposed* to act in certain ways; the disposition is the residue of where the actor has been positioned in a [field](field.md).

The concept does several jobs at once. It explains why people from a given class fraction tend to share tastes, gestures, and judgments without coordination. It explains why mobility between classes is hard even after the formal barriers fall: the new arrival lacks the habitus to move through the new space without showing the seams. It explains the *misrecognition* that lets unequal arrangements appear natural: the dominant habitus reads itself as universal, and what is in fact a positional disposition presents itself as taste, intelligence, or merit.

Critics within and outside the Bourdieusian tradition have pressed: how plastic is habitus? How does it change? Lahire has argued that the unitary habitus is too coarse — actors carry plural, sometimes-contradictory dispositions reflecting movement across plural fields. Black-feminist sociology has used the concept to analyze the embodied costs of moving between fields whose habituses are racially and gender-coded incompatibly.

Habitus is paired with [field](field.md) (the structure habitus enacts within) and [capital](capital.md) (the resources whose distribution gives positions in the field their structure).
