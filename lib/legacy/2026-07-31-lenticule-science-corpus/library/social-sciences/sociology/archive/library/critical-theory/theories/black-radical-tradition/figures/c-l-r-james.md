C. L. R. James (1901–1989) is the figure through whom the [black-radical-tradition](../_index.md) holds open the claim that revolutionary self-activity, not vanguard direction, is the form of politics adequate to mass emancipation. Born in Trinidad, he wrote across history, political theory, novel, drama, and cricket criticism, organized in Trotskyist and pan-Africanist movements in Britain and the United States, and was deported from the U.S. in 1953.

## What this subdomain takes from him

- **The Haitian Revolution as the first successful anti-colonial revolt.** *The Black Jacobins* (1938) is the load-bearing text. James reframes the Haitian Revolution from the marginal event of dominant historiography into the central event of the Age of Revolutions: enslaved people in Saint-Domingue overthrew slavery, defeated Napoleonic France, and founded a Black republic — and they did so by acting, not by being acted upon. The book establishes the analytical priority of the enslaved as historical subjects.
- **Self-emancipation.** The political principle the tradition reads out of Haiti and out of James's later organizing: the emancipation of the oppressed is the act of the oppressed themselves. Vanguardism, however well-intentioned, reproduces the structure of leadership and led that revolution is supposed to abolish. The Johnson-Forest Tendency James co-founded with Raya Dunayevskaya pushed this principle against the Trotskyist orthodoxy of their day.
- **The autonomy of Black struggle.** James argued, against much of the U.S. left in the 1940s, that the Black movement in the United States possessed an autonomous revolutionary logic that did not need to be subordinated to a class-based program — that, on the contrary, it had its own historical trajectory and its own leadership. This claim is the proximate source for one strand of the tradition's argument that Black liberation is the vantage point, not a special case.
- **History as analysis of revolutionary capacity.** James writes history to recover what people did when they were not supposed to be capable of doing it, and to make that recovery available as evidence for what people can do now.

## Key works

- *The Black Jacobins: Toussaint L'Ouverture and the San Domingo Revolution* (1938; revised 1963)
- *World Revolution, 1917–1936* (1937)
- *Notes on Dialectics* (1948)
- *Mariners, Renegades and Castaways* (1953)
- *Beyond a Boundary* (1963)

## Where his work figures elsewhere

James is the proximate source for the tradition's reading of revolution as self-activity and for [Cedric Robinson](cedric-robinson.md)'s argument in *Black Marxism* that the Black radical tradition predates and exceeds Marxism. *The Black Jacobins* sits behind the subdomain's treatment of slave revolt as analytically prior to abstract theories of revolution.
