On how contemporary settlerism is about governing the affective relationships around deferred action to maintain control over, through, and with climate breakdown.

Climate breakdown is not simply a material unraveling—it is a crisis of relation, temporality, and affect. While once settler colonialism enacted dominance through territorial seizure and extractive imposition, contemporary settler governance secures itself through the management of _when_ things are allowed to end, _who_ must wait, and _how_ waiting feels. That is: it governs through deferral. The violence is not only in what is done to land and people, but in what is not yet done, what is delayed, what is promised. In the era of collapse, settlerism becomes an apparatus of suspended decay.

Deferral operates here as both tactic and ontology. As Elizabeth Povinelli (2011) describes in _Economies of Abandonment_, late liberal governance organizes life around the endurance of what ought not be endured—what she calls “quasi-events,” crises that never quite resolve and thus reproduce the conditions of their own governance. Climate catastrophe is the perfect quasi-event. It is slow, distributed, always arriving but never quite arrived—just urgent enough to justify new technologies of surveillance, debt, extraction, and settler futurity, but never catastrophic enough to dismantle the structures that produce it. This is not accidental. Climate breakdown is not mismanaged; it is _governed_.

That governance is affective. Drawing from Lauren Berlant’s theory of “cruel optimism” (2011), we might say that settlerism sustains itself by tethering subjects to the fantasy that action—green infrastructure, carbon credits, net zero pledges—_will_ eventually come. It scripts the apocalypse as a deferred spectacle, always to be acted upon tomorrow, elsewhere, by someone else. In this way, deferral is not passive—it is productive. It produces affective attachments to a future that will not arrive, and distributes responsibility in a way that protects the structural integrity of settler governance while giving the appearance of participatory care.

But unlike traditional apocalyptic imaginaries that hinge on rupture, deferral governs through continuity. It does not deny collapse; it _manages_ it, smoothing it into routines of liberal responsiveness—press conferences, recycling programs, ESG initiatives, slow funding cycles. This produces what we might call _managed decay_: the calibrated disintegration of life-worlds that remains intelligible to policy, to markets, to settler time.

Settler time, as Mark Rifkin (2017) argues, is not just linear—it is proprietorial. It enacts a temporal order where some bodies are granted futurity and others are suspended in waiting. In the context of climate breakdown, this manifests as triage logic: entire geographies are allowed to burn, flood, or desiccate so long as the centers of governance remain stable. The uneven distribution of decay is not collateral damage—it is the mode of operation. In this way, settlerism no longer just owns land; it owns time, deciding whose world can end and whose must endure.

This is not simply a politics of inaction, but a politics of affective choreography. Settlerism produces scenes of care—technical reports, diplomatic summits, activist performances—that rehearse the aesthetic of concern while operationalizing delay. These performances orient subjects toward hope, despair, outrage, resolve—but always in ways that reabsorb potential disruption into existing structures. As Sara Ahmed (2010) notes, emotions do not simply reside in bodies—they circulate, attach, stick. In the settler climate regime, emotion is stickied to postponement, to the promise that collapse is negotiable if one just feels the right way about it.

In this schema, refusal becomes unintelligible. To reject deferral—to name collapse as present, unmanageable, ungovernable—is to risk being labeled apocalyptic, nihilistic, or irrational. But perhaps what is needed is precisely a refusal of the deferral apparatus: a disarticulation from settler temporality and its promises. This would not mean fleeing the future, but reinhabiting the present as already contested, already broken, already alive with other possibilities.

Because not all relations are governed by decay. Outside settler time, many Indigenous and Black life-worlds have long known how to live with the ruins, how to compost loss into new forms of relation. These are not redemptive narratives—they do not save the world—but they do open it. They refuse to treat time as a resource to be hoarded or managed, and instead attend to relation as what persists through rupture.

To end, then: the climate is not waiting. It does not care whether the liberal subject feels prepared. It is not suspended. It is decaying, now. And within that decay are already the seeds of other relations—if we stop deferring them.
