W. E. B. Du Bois (1868–1963) is the figure the [black-radical-tradition](../_index.md) treats as its anchor — not because the tradition begins with him (it does not; the tradition's own self-understanding traces back to slave revolt) but because his writing makes the tradition's claims legible as a sustained intellectual program. He held the first PhD in history awarded to a Black person by Harvard (1895), co-founded the NAACP, edited *The Crisis*, helped organize the Pan-African Congresses, and ended his life as a citizen of Ghana.

## What this subdomain takes from him

- **The color line.** "The problem of the twentieth century is the problem of the color-line" (*The Souls of Black Folk*, 1903). Read in this subdomain not as prediction but as analytical claim: the global organization of the modern world is structured by a racial division, and any social theory that treats race as a derivative or secondary phenomenon is failing to describe its object.
- **Double consciousness.** The condition of seeing oneself "through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity." The tradition takes this less as a psychological description than as a structural one — a name for the epistemic condition of those whose social existence is constituted from the outside by a hostile gaze, and the analytical leverage that condition provides.
- **The general strike of the enslaved.** *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935) reframes the Civil War: the mass desertion of plantations by enslaved people was a general strike that decided the war and inaugurated a brief experiment in interracial democracy. This reading reverses the dominant historiography that treated the enslaved as objects of emancipation rather than as its agents. Cedric Robinson reads *Black Reconstruction* as the moment the Black radical tradition becomes self-conscious as a tradition.
- **Abolition democracy.** Du Bois's term for the constructive program Reconstruction attempted and the counter-revolution destroyed: the building of public institutions (schools, hospitals, land redistribution) that would have made a post-slavery democracy substantive. The term is reactivated by [Angela Davis](angela-davis.md) as the constructive horizon of contemporary abolition.

## Key works

- *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903)
- *Black Reconstruction in America, 1860–1880* (1935)
- *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899)
- *Dusk of Dawn* (1940)
- *The World and Africa* (1947)

## Where his work figures elsewhere

The tradition's central terms — racial capitalism, the prison fix, fugitivity, the undercommons — are downstream of frames Du Bois established. *Black Reconstruction* is the proximate source for Robinson's *Black Marxism* and for Davis and Gilmore's abolition writing.
