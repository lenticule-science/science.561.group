Green anarchism is the current within anarchism that treats the ecological crisis as constitutive rather than incidental. Where productivist currents in the broader left have taken industrial development as the precondition for liberation (or have at minimum left the ecological question for after the revolution), green anarchism reads industrial civilization itself — including its state, capital, and large-scale technological infrastructure — as among the structures whose continuation is incompatible with the continuation of life on the planet.

The current is internally varied. *Social ecology* (Murray Bookchin and the Institute for Social Ecology) gives the most institutional and constructive form: the ecological crisis is the crisis of hierarchy itself, and a free society would necessarily be an ecologically rational one, organized through directly democratic municipal confederations. *Anarcho-primitivism* (John Zerzan, the milieu around *Fifth Estate* and *Green Anarchy*) gives the most radical form: civilization as such — the agricultural transition, division of labor, symbolic culture — is the catastrophe whose unwinding is the work. Between them sits a wide field of practices, communities, and analyses that share the ecological-political commitment without committing to either pole.

Indigenous land-defense movements and Indigenous political-philosophical traditions are central interlocutors for contemporary green anarchism, and the relation deserves more careful articulation than this subdomain currently carries (a follow-up). The settler-colonial frame ([`settler-colonialism/`](../../../povinellian/theories/settler-colonialism/)) is constitutive: any green-anarchist program in occupied territory that does not begin from Indigenous sovereignty over the land it claims to defend reproduces the structure it claims to refuse.
