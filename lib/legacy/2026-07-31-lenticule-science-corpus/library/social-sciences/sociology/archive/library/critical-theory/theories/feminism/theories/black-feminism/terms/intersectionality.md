*Intersectionality* is the term Kimberlé Crenshaw introduced in two foundational law-review essays — "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" (*University of Chicago Legal Forum*, 1989) and "Mapping the Margins: Intersectionality, Identity Politics, and Violence Against Women of Color" (*Stanford Law Review*, 1991) — for the analytic that the experience and situation of Black women cannot be analyzed as the sum of "race oppression" + "gender oppression," because the categories themselves are produced through their intersection.

The 1989 essay's central case study is *DeGraffenreid v. General Motors* (1976): five Black women sued GM for discrimination, alleging that the company's seniority-based layoff policy disproportionately affected Black women because GM had not hired Black women into the relevant positions until the 1970s. The court refused to recognize the claim. White women had been hired (so GM was not guilty of sex discrimination); Black men had been hired (so GM was not guilty of race discrimination); the court held that the plaintiffs could not "combine statutory remedies to create a new 'super-remedy.'" Crenshaw's analysis: the legal categories of "race discrimination" and "sex discrimination" were each modeled on the paradigm case of one axis at a time (white women, Black men), and the intersection — the case Black women actually occupied — fell through the categorical seams. The legal categories were structurally incapable of describing the structure that produced the plaintiffs' situation.

The analytic Crenshaw named has roots that precede the term. The [Combahee River Collective](../figures/combahee-river-collective.md)'s 1977 statement names "interlocking systems of oppression" and analyzes the position from which Black women's situation is produced — the same position Crenshaw analyzes a decade later in legal terms. [Patricia Hill Collins](../figures/patricia-hill-collins.md)'s [matrix-of-domination](matrix-of-domination.md) systematizes the same analytic at the sociological level. [Audre Lorde](../figures/audre-lorde.md)'s insistence on the analytical primacy of difference and [bell hooks](../figures/bell-hooks.md)'s analysis of "imperialist white-supremacist capitalist patriarchy" are doing the same work in the essay-theoretical register. Crenshaw's term carries the analytic into legal theory and from there into the broader U.S. political vocabulary; Collins's later work (*Intersectionality* with Sirma Bilge, 2016; *Intersectionality as Critical Social Theory*, 2019) develops it into a systematic critical social theory.

The dilution of intersectionality into a demographic checklist — the additive sum the analytic was designed to refuse — is itself a problem the contemporary literature treats as part of the term's reception history. The dilution is what Collins's work since 2000 is largely arguing against. Used rigorously, intersectionality names a structural analysis: the categories of race, class, gender, and sexuality are produced together by interlocking systems of domination, and any analysis that treats them as separate axes that can be summed misidentifies what the structure is and how it operates.

The convergence with the [`kyriarchism`](../../../../queer/theories/kyriarchism/_index.md) analysis in the sibling queer subdomain is direct: kyriarchism is the analysis of the same interlocking structure, named from a different starting point in feminist theology. The two terms describe the same object.
