In the [[settler ontology]], within [[selfhood]], [[emotion]] is a [[form]] of [[private property]]: it belongs to the [[individual]], arises from within them, and is considered [[legitimate]] because of its [[subjective immediacy]]. 

This formation is not [[inherent]] but [[culture|cultural]], as [[explain|explained]] by [[Sara Ahmed]]. In [[The Cultural Politic of Emotion]], she shows that emotions do not simply emerge from within individuals and move outward into the world. Instead, emotions are [[relational practice|relational practices]] that both [[orient]] [[subject|subjects]] toward each other and [[generate]] the [[social field]].

**Sara Ahmed** critiques this framing in _The Cultural Politics of Emotion_, showing that emotions do not simply emerge from within individuals and move outward into the world. Instead, emotions are **relational practices** that both orient subjects toward others and produce the contours of the social. When someone feels “hurt,” that feeling is not just a report of an interior state—it is a **claim on the world**, one that draws boundaries between self and other, between justice and harm. Emotions “stick” to bodies and figures, Ahmed argues, meaning that social norms teach us whose feelings matter and whose feelings are disruptive, irrational, or excessive.

In settler-colonial formations, the dominant subject (typically white, liberal, cis, and settler) is conditioned to believe that their emotions are **morally clarifying**—they reveal when something is wrong. They also believe their emotions are **descriptive**: to say “I feel this way” is to imply that _this is how things are_. Within intimate relationships, this belief translates into a relational norm in which the dominant partner expects their emotional state to **set the conditions of engagement**. “I’m not ready,” “I’m too overwhelmed,” “I need to explain how I was feeling” all function as emotional vetoes that regulate what kinds of conversations can happen, and when.

To interrupt this, we must shift from an ontology of emotion as sovereign territory to an ontology of emotion as **relational response**. This does not mean denying the reality of emotion, but understanding that emotions are not innocent or purely interior—they are **conditioned, patterned, and productive**. Following **Judith Butler**, we can say that emotions are **performative**: they do not merely describe a state of being; they _bring social reality into being_. To cry, to explain, to say “I didn’t mean to”—these are not just expressions of feeling, but **acts** that do things in the world. They shape the space of relation by authorizing some truths while foreclosing others.

**Frantz Fanon**, in _Black Skin, White Masks_, describes the white subject’s emotional reality as structurally protected and socially scaffolded. The colonized, in contrast, is not permitted the same emotional range—not because they do not feel, but because their feelings are constantly **misread, pathologized, or reinterpreted through the colonizer’s framework**. This produces a double-bind: the colonized must anticipate the dominant subject’s emotional reaction, and _modulate_ their own accordingly, in order to stay in relation. Their emotional labor is not oriented toward self-expression, but toward **surviving someone else’s sovereignty**.

In contrast to this asymmetry, many Indigenous and relational traditions treat emotion not as the interior property of a sovereign self, but as a **co-emergent field phenomenon**—something that arises _in the between_, through proximity, movement, silence, and listening. As **Leanne Betasamosake Simpson** and others have argued, relation is not built through confession or explanation, but through **reciprocal attunement**—learning to sense when another is withdrawing, overextending, contracting, or offering. In such a field, emotion is not the justification for retreat or re-centering, but a signal to shift rhythm, deepen listening, or pause without fleeing.

### **Mechanism of Interrupting Sovereign Emotion**

To de-sovereignize emotion, one must cultivate a capacity for **affective reflexivity**—a skill of watching one’s emotional state not as a command to act, but as one possible layer of the relational field. The settler partner, in this new ontology, must learn to recognize that “feeling overwhelmed” is not a justification for withdrawal or explanation. It is a condition to be named and held lightly—_with others_, not against them.

This includes practices like:

- **De-centering** emotional immediacy: “I feel X” is not a mic-drop but an offering, to be contextualized alongside other truths.
    
- **Naming emotion as _positional_, not ontological**: “I feel unsafe” is not always a report of threat—it may be a signal of ego disruption.
    
- **Interrupting the instinct to explain from within affective urgency**, understanding that this urgency is part of a recursive power dynamic that structurally invalidates the other’s pain.
    

In the decolonial relational field, emotion becomes not the basis for withdrawing from relation, but the medium through which relation becomes visible. It is neither sovereign nor suppressed. It is **felt together**, carried across asymmetry, and metabolized in the presence of others—not wielded to restore self-legibility at the cost of silencing the other.

## **II. Feeling Harm Across Difference: Interrupting the Explanatory Loop**

To feel harm across difference is to remain _with_ rupture—not as something to be quickly resolved or rendered coherent, but as an **encounter with alterity** that cannot be absorbed into the self without loss. In settler-liberal relation, harm is typically managed through a logic of _sameness_—a shared agreement on what counts as harm, what its cause was, and how it can be repaired. The result is a patterned instinct to **reframe the other’s account into one’s own terms**—to understand it by translating it into a familiar emotional or cognitive framework.

This instinct is most visible in the explanatory reflex: when harm is named, the dominant subject seeks not to feel it, but to **re-narrate** it. “I didn’t mean to,” “I was trying to help,” or “I felt the same way” are not mere statements of intent. They are acts of epistemic capture—gestures that **fold difference back into the settler’s own frame**. They imply that relation requires shared emotional reality as a precondition, rather than as a possible outcome of encounter.

Against this, we ask: What would it mean to **feel harm without understanding it fully?** What would it mean to allow harm to remain partially opaque, and still remain in relation?

To approach this question, we must take seriously the traditions that teach us that **difference is not a problem to be solved**. In **Édouard Glissant’s** work on _opacity_, he argues that the desire to understand the other fully—to reduce them to terms the self can grasp—is itself a colonial impulse. He writes: “It is not necessary to understand everything in order to live with one another.” This is not a rejection of connection, but a redefinition of it. Relation, for Glissant, is not the disappearance of difference—it is **dwelling with it**.

**Saidiya Hartman’s** notion of _critical fabulation_ offers another model. In her work on enslaved women whose lives are occluded from the historical record, Hartman refuses the extractive imperative to make their stories “whole.” Instead, she **lingers in the gaps**—not to fill them, but to respect their weight. This practice is instructive in relational contexts as well: we are not owed perfect mutual transparency. In fact, demanding it may reproduce the very dynamics of control we claim to oppose.

**Leanne Betasamosake Simpson** similarly articulates a politics of **refusal**, where not all knowledge is for everyone, and where the labor of translation is not always owed. This has profound implications for how harm is engaged in asymmetrical relations: the person naming harm should not be required to **make it legible** to the one who enacted it. To do so would be to subordinate the truth of harm to the affective and epistemic capacity of the dominant.

From **Jessica Benjamin**, a relational psychoanalyst, we inherit the idea of _mutual recognition_—but crucially, she distinguishes this from mirroring. Recognition is not understanding the other as oneself. It is **holding the other as real in their difference**—as a center of perception, affect, and moral claim _independent_ of your own interpretive frame.

### **Mechanism: Suspending the Demand for Translation**

To feel harm across difference requires the **suspension of the demand for symmetry**. In this practice, the settler subject must resist the impulse to reconfigure the event in a way that preserves their own moral coherence. Instead, they must let the harm **stand**—in its language, its logic, its affective weight. This does not mean agreeing with every account, but recognizing that **explanation is not owed**, and that emotional discomfort is not a signal to clarify, but a **cue to listen differently**.

This practice can be supported by:

- **Pausing the narrative reflex**: noticing when the desire to say “Here’s what I meant” or “That’s not how I saw it” arises, and allowing it to pass unspoken.
    
- **Acknowledging limits of knowing**: saying “I don’t fully understand, but I accept that this was your experience” without trying to make it your own.
    
- **Letting the harm remain irreconcilable**: resisting the internal pressure to resolve tension too quickly, and instead dwelling in the relational field that rupture opens.
    

Crucially, this is not passive listening. It is an **active affective labor** of _holding open space_ for something that will not resolve on the settler’s terms. This kind of presence is not trained by liberal selfhood—it must be **cultivated through practice**, often in tension with the dominant subject’s instincts and acculturation toward comfort, clarity, and closure.

This is what it means to feel harm across difference: not just to acknowledge the event, but to allow it to **reshape the field** without insisting that it conform to your emotional logic. It is a refusal of explanatory capture, and an offering of **epistemic humility**—a practice of remaining, even when one cannot explain.

## **III. Coherence Through Rupture: Making Relation Without Control**

In the liberal-colonial imaginary, **coherence is treated as a prerequisite for relation**. To be in relation is to first be a coherent self—emotionally regulated, narratively intact, and morally legible. This assumption underlies most dominant models of intimacy, conflict resolution, and communication: that people must “know who they are” in order to show up, that repair must be built on mutual agreement, and that dissonance is a problem to be smoothed out, not a generative field of transformation.

This demand for pre-existing coherence is not innocent. It reflects a settler-colonial ontology of the self as **bounded, sovereign, and epistemically complete**. Within this ontology, rupture is experienced as a threat: a sign that relation has failed or that one party is being unreasonable, unstable, or unsafe. The result is a relational form in which dissonance is managed through techniques of closure: explanation, reframing, retreat, or moral defense.

But what if rupture is not the failure of relation—but its **opening**?

**Gloria Anzaldúa** offers a foundational rethinking of this question through her concept of _la facultad_ and _mestiza consciousness_. For Anzaldúa, coherence is not something one possesses in advance of relation—it is something that emerges through the **pain of contradiction**, through the borderlands where incompatible logics collide. The self is not undone by rupture; it is _reforged in its light_. She writes, “a massive uprooting of dualistic thinking in the individual and collective consciousness is the beginning of a long struggle, but also the beginning of integration.”

To enact relation through rupture, we must reimagine coherence not as **interiority secured**, but as **field responsiveness**—a capacity to remain oriented to the other _even as the self is disoriented_. Here, coherence is not a moral posture but a _dynamic equilibrium_, co-constructed across shifting terrains of affect, history, and positionality.

This aligns with **Karen Barad’s** theory of **intra-action**, where entities do not pre-exist their relations but are **constituted through them**. In Barad’s agential realist framework, coherence is not prior—it is what emerges from within the entangled processes of mattering. This idea disrupts the liberal fantasy of autonomous selfhood and replaces it with a **relational ontology**, in which boundaries, meanings, and moral responsibilities are **negotiated through the encounter itself**.

Similarly, **Leanne Betasamosake Simpson’s** articulation of _grounded normativity_ offers a framework for coherence that is **local, collective, and context-responsive**. For Simpson, ethics and coherence arise not from universal principles or self-contained identities, but from being in reciprocal motion with land, kin, and others. This is not coherence as fixed identity, but **coherence as fidelity to relation**—staying in tune with the web of obligations and interruptions that make up the world.

In psychoanalytic terms, **Jessica Benjamin** names a similar movement when she speaks of _thirdness_: the shared, co-created relational field that survives and holds the tension between two desiring, fallible, partially knowable subjects. Thirdness is not agreement—it is the willingness to sustain the space _in which subjects can be transformed by their encounter_ without collapsing into fusion or retreating into defense.

### **Mechanism: Allowing Coherence to Emerge Through the Encounter**

To enact a relation in which coherence is made through rupture, not assumed in advance, requires a profound shift in **what is valued during conflict**. The goal is not to return to emotional stability or mutual understanding as quickly as possible, but to **stay in motion within the disorientation**, and to allow the disorientation itself to teach.

This shift involves several interrelated practices:

- **Letting go of control over what the relation is “about.”** The narrative may shift, split, collapse. Let that be part of the process.
    
- **Valuing orientation over resolution.** Ask: Are we still turning toward each other, even if we do not agree or understand?
    
- **Practicing field-attunement.** Noticing where rupture creates possibility: new language, new distance, new forms of interdependence that don’t collapse into sameness.
    
- **Holding relational memory.** Recognizing rupture as part of a longer story, not a final break. Coherence is a _pattern_, not a proof.
    

This is what it means to practice **post-sovereign coherence**. The self is not abandoned—it is decentered, **re-situated as one participant in a larger choreography** of meaning. Coherence becomes not a precondition for participation, but an _emergent rhythm_, co-authored through moments of discomfort, disorientation, and non-resolution.

To live this way is to refuse the colonizing gesture of explanatory control. It is to allow rupture to _mark us_, and to orient our response not toward self-repair, but toward **the care of the relational field itself**, even when that field feels unstable, unresolved, or unfinished.

## **Conclusion: Relational Praxis Beyond Sovereignty**

To interrupt the recursive cycles of relational harm structured by settler-colonial emotional dominance, one must do more than change behavior. One must change **ontological commitments**—what one believes relation is, where it begins, and what it requires. The traditional liberal model of relation—sovereign selves in symmetrical exchange—is not neutral. It is a settler form. It reproduces itself through emotional re-centering, explanatory capture, and a preconditioned demand for coherence. These are not incidental problems. They are **infrastructural features** of a relational world built to protect dominance under the guise of mutuality.

The alternative is not simply “being better.” It is **being different**—constructing a relational form in which:

- **Emotion is not sovereign**, but one participant in a shared field;
    
- **Harm is not immediately translated into sameness**, but allowed to remain felt across difference, even when unresolvable;
    
- **Coherence is not a precondition for participation**, but a possible outcome of staying in relation through rupture.
    

These are not techniques. They are not “tools” in the instrumental sense. They are **disciplines of presence**, **forms of attentional labor**, and **acts of refusal**—refusal to center the self as truth, refusal to make others explain their harm until it feels safe, refusal to collapse complexity into comfort.

The theoretical traditions that support this model are diverse, but they converge on a few core insights:

- From **Black feminist thought** (Hartman, Spillers, Moten), we learn to value opacity, contradiction, and the labor of remaining in relation with that which does not resolve.
    
- From **Indigenous theory and practice** (Simpson, Tuck, Million), we learn that reciprocity cannot be coerced or rushed, and that relational life is built on _reorienting_, not mastering, the self.
    
- From **relational psychoanalysis** (Benjamin), we understand that true encounter is not agreement but **mutual non-dissolution**—the ability to remain in the presence of another without needing them to disappear into our framework.
    
- From **poststructural and agential realist theory** (Anzaldúa, Barad), we are reminded that the self is not pre-formed, but comes into being through encounter. Relation is the site of world-making, not merely its reflection.
    

This work is deeply political. It is not about individual transformation for its own sake. It is about **withdrawing energy from the settler-colonial infrastructures of feeling** that govern whose pain is centered, whose explanations are believed, and whose harm is suspended in ambiguity. It is about **refusing the logics that require coherence before care**, or clarity before belonging.

What emerges instead is a **recursive ethics of presence**. A way of staying in motion, not because one is certain, but because one is _committed to the field of relation as something more real than the self alone_. This is not an abandonment of self—it is an invitation to remake the self, continually, in the shared space of difference.
