# Field

*The role concept, after Bourdieu, for a structured space of positions — defined relationally, by the distribution of the relevant [capitals](capital.md) — within which agents struggle for the stakes specific to that space. Academic fields, artistic fields, political fields, journalistic fields, religious fields each have their own structure and stakes.*

A field is not a population, an institution, or a network. It is a *space* — closer to a force-field in physics than to a sphere of activity in ordinary speech. Positions in the field are defined by what kinds and quantities of capital their occupants hold relative to others. Action in the field is structured by those positions: the dominant defend their position, challengers contest it, and the stakes specific to the field (academic prestige, artistic legitimacy, political authority) are the prizes.

Each field has its own *doxa* — its taken-for-granted assumptions about what is worth pursuing, what counts as a serious move, who is a recognized player. Each field has its own conversion rates between capitals. Each field has its degree of autonomy — the extent to which it operates by its own logic versus being dominated by another field (typically the economic).

The field concept is sociology's response to two problems at once. It refuses the picture of action as either pure interest-maximization (because what counts as interest is field-specific) or pure normative compliance (because positions in the field have stakes the actor has reason to pursue). And it refuses the picture of structure as a single overarching system: there are many fields, with different logics, in shifting relations of dominance.

[Habitus](habitus.md) is what allows an actor to play in a field without conscious calculation; the habitus has been calibrated by prior exposure to that field or to a homologous one. Lacking the appropriate habitus, an actor with the right capital can still be "out of place."

Critical extensions: Bourdieu's field theory has been pressed (by Hartmann, by Black-feminist sociology, by postcolonial theory) on whether its analyses of mobility under-credit the racial and colonial structuring of who can enter a field at all. The field is not innocent of who is allowed in.
