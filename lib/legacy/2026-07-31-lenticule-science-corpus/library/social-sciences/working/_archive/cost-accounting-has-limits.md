A system can count every penny and minute that it has been built to recognize. That does not mean it can account for every form of labor, every source of value, every avoided harm, every future benefit, or every condition that made the visible work possible.

Arlene Kaplan Daniels makes the first cut in “Invisible Work” (1987). She argues that common sense tends to define “real work” as paid work, which means unpaid and background labor, especially women’s domestic and community labor, disappears from view. That matters here because the accounting system does not discover what work is. It inherits a social definition of work. If “work” means what appears as wage labor, billable time, or a cost center, then huge amounts of socially necessary labor are excluded before accounting even begins.

Arlie Hochschild makes the next cut in The Managed Heart (1983). She names emotional labor: the work of managing feeling, expression, tone, patience, warmth, calm, deference, reassurance, and composure as part of a job. That work can be absolutely necessary to producing value, especially in service, care, management, teaching, hospitality, retail, administration, and conflict-heavy workplaces. But it is not naturally visible as a discrete unit. The customer did not leave angry. The team did not fracture. The meeting did not collapse. The worker absorbed the tension. The accounting system sees the completed shift, not necessarily the emotional regulation that made the shift succeed.

Anselm Strauss gives the organizational version in “Work and the Division of Labor” (1985) and “The Articulation of Project Work” (1988). He calls attention to articulation work: the work of making other work line up. Scheduling, coordinating, translating between people, repairing handoffs, noticing dependencies, smoothing conflicts, remembering context, deciding what has to happen before something else can happen. This is not a side issue. Cooperative work only works because articulation work is constantly happening. But if the accounting taxonomy is organized around final deliverables, production units, tickets closed, or hours assigned to formal tasks, articulation work appears only indirectly. It appears as the absence of breakdown.

Kjeld Schmidt and Liam Bannon make this central to computer-supported cooperative work in “Taking CSCW Seriously” (1992). Their point is that cooperative work is not just a bunch of individuals using tools in parallel. Cooperative work requires support for the interdependence between tasks. That means the “real work” of a workplace includes the coordination layer that lets formally assigned work cohere. A database can force every worker to enter every task, but then the work of making the database entries meaningful becomes another layer of articulation work. You do not eliminate the invisible work; you move it.

Lucy Suchman’s “Making Work Visible” (1995) is especially important because the phrase sounds like a simple good: make the work visible, then value it properly. But Suchman’s point is more dangerous than that. Making work visible is not neutral. It is an intervention. To make work visible is to choose a representation of work, choose what counts as evidence of work, choose who gets to describe the work, choose what becomes auditable, and choose what consequences attach to visibility. Visibility can help workers, but it can also discipline them. “Make it visible” is never just a technical move.

Susan Leigh Star and Anselm Strauss sharpen this in “Layers of Silence, Arenas of Voice: The Ecology of Visible and Invisible Work” (1999). They do not treat visibility and invisibility as simple opposites. They show that work becomes visible or invisible inside an ecology of indicators, records, voice, silence, backgrounding, and institutional attention. Some work is visible to coworkers but not managers. Some is visible to clients but not payroll. Some is visible only when it fails. Some is visible as “personality,” “helpfulness,” or “being a team player,” not as labor. So the claim “we can quantify labor costs” is not wrong exactly. It is just downstream of the more important question: which indicators make which labor visible to whom?

Geoffrey Bowker and Susan Leigh Star give the classification argument in Sorting Things Out (1999). Every classification system has residuals: “other,” “miscellaneous,” “not applicable,” edge cases, exceptions, informal notes, workarounds, things everyone knows but the form cannot say. Residuals are not just bugs in bad classification systems. They are structural. A classification system simplifies the world so an institution can act. But the world is always more detailed than the classification. So when a cost accounting system classifies work, it necessarily produces a remainder. That remainder is not “nothing.” It is the work the system cannot or will not classify.

Wendy Espeland and Mitchell Stevens make the quantification argument in “A Sociology of Quantification” (2008), building on their earlier work on commensuration. Quantification means translating different things into a common metric. That is powerful because it lets institutions compare, rank, audit, budget, and decide. But the price of that power is simplification. To turn different kinds of activity into a common number, the system must strip away context. A minute of mentoring, a minute of machine operation, a minute of crisis prevention, a minute of grieving with a patient, and a minute of repairing a coworker’s mistake can all become “labor time,” but that does not mean they are the same kind of value-producing act. The sameness is produced by the accounting system.

This is where the cybernetic point comes in. W. Ross Ashby’s law of requisite variety in An Introduction to Cybernetics (1956) says, in effect, that only variety can absorb variety. A regulator has to have enough internal variety to respond to the variety of the system it regulates. Conant and Ashby’s “Every Good Regulator of a System Must Be a Model of That System” (1970) adds that a good regulator must contain a model of what it regulates. Apply that to labor accounting: if the actual variety of human work is larger than the categories in the accounting system, the system cannot distinguish all the relevant differences. It must collapse different acts together. It must miss some differences. It must produce invisibility.

That is why “we can add more categories” does not solve the problem. More categories can make different things visible, but a practical accounting system still cannot contain the full variety of human work. If you add a category for mentoring, then someone has to decide what counts as mentoring. If you add a category for safety, then safety work starts being performed for the safety metric. If you add a category for coordination, then the coordination of the coordination category becomes background work. The residual moves. It does not disappear.

James C. Scott gives the political version of this in Seeing Like a State (1998). States and large institutions make the world legible by simplifying it. They create maps, cadasters, censuses, standard names, standard categories, standard measures. This lets them tax, govern, plan, extract, and administer. But Scott shows that legibility often destroys or ignores metis: practical, local, situated knowledge. In a workplace, metis is the worker’s practical knowledge of how things actually get done: who knows what, which machine lies, which manager needs warning, which form is fake, which shortcut is safe, which rule cannot be followed literally without breaking production. Perfect accounting wants legibility. But production often depends on metis that legibility cannot capture without transforming or destroying it.

Then Goodhart and Campbell explain why the problem gets worse when the accounting becomes consequential. Goodhart’s law, from Charles Goodhart’s work on monetary management in the 1970s, is usually summarized as: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Donald Campbell’s “Campbell’s Law” (1976) makes the same point for social indicators: the more a metric is used for decision-making, the more it is subject to corruption pressure and the more it distorts the process it was supposed to monitor. In a workplace, once labor metrics determine pay, promotion, discipline, staffing, investment, or status, workers and managers reorganize around the metric. They do what is counted. They neglect what is uncounted. They game what is gameable. The metric stops merely describing work and starts producing a new work regime.

Marilyn Strathern and Michael Power explain the audit version of this. Strathern’s work on audit cultures, including “From Improvement to Enhancement” and the Audit Cultures volume, argues that audit changes the activity being audited. Power’s The Audit Society (1997) argues that modern institutions increasingly organize themselves around producing auditable records. This is crucial. The more intense the accounting system becomes, the more work is redirected toward satisfying accounting itself. The workplace now has accounting-work, compliance-work, documentation-work, dashboard-work, self-reporting-work, metric-maintenance-work. Some of that gets counted, some does not. But the fantasy that accounting simply observes work from outside is gone. The accounting system becomes part of the labor process.

So when someone says “you can quantify human labor costs down to the iota using databases and software,” the answer is: you can quantify what your recognition system is built to recognize. You can make a very precise record of the categories you chose. But precision inside a category system is not the same as truth about work.

This matters especially because the invisible remainder is power-structured. It is not random. Daniels shows unpaid and domestic labor disappears when work is defined around wages. Hochschild shows emotional labor is treated as personality or professionalism instead of labor. Strauss, Star, Suchman, Schmidt, and Bannon show articulation work disappears when systems focus on formal tasks and deliverables. Glenn’s “From Servitude to Service Work” (1992) shows paid reproductive labor is racially organized through long histories of servitude and service work. Federici’s Revolution at Point Zero (2012) argues that capitalism depends on treating reproductive labor as a background condition rather than fully recognized labor. The pattern is not accidental: the work that disappears is often the work done by women, racialized workers, care workers, migrants, lower-status staff, informal coordinators, and whoever lacks the authority to force their work into the official categories.

This is also why “some jobs are straight expense and generate no revenue” is not the killer point it seems to be. That sentence already accepts the accounting system’s theory of value. Compliance and safety look like “expense” if the ledger only recognizes revenue-generating transactions. But preventing a lawsuit, preventing a death, preventing burnout, preventing equipment failure, preventing regulatory shutdown, preventing reputational collapse, or preventing workers from maiming themselves is value. It often appears as nothing happening. No accident. No fine. No outage. No lawsuit. No scandal. No death. Accounting systems are notoriously bad at valuing counterfactuals because the value lies in the event that did not occur.

A safety worker may “generate no revenue” in the same way a bridge support “generates no traffic.” The statement is technically true only because the accounting frame has decided to attribute movement to the cars and not to the structure that keeps them from falling into the river.

The same applies to the line worker in the original example who notices an optimization and mentions it in a hallway. The accounting system might record their wage, their shift, their output, maybe even the manager’s process improvement later. But the observation itself came from situated experience. It came from attention. It came from accumulated metis. It came from being close enough to the work to notice something management did not know how to ask. If that observation becomes a process change, the accounting system can assign the value wherever its categories allow. Usually that means the manager, the department, the project, or the firm. But the causal origin of the value does not necessarily line up with the accounting attribution.

That is the key distinction: allocation is not attribution.

Job cost accounting allocates costs. It does not perfectly attribute value. It can say “this many paid hours went into this job.” It can say “this task was assigned to this cost center.” It can say “this project consumed this budget.” But value is relational. Value comes from dependencies among workers, tools, histories, infrastructures, social reproduction, local knowledge, avoided harms, future possibilities, and collective coordination. A database can model some of that. It cannot model all of it without becoming as complex as the world it is trying to measure.

Even systems like BlackRock’s Aladdin do not refute this. They prove the opposite. A giant financial data platform can unify data, risk models, portfolio operations, and accounting views. That is enormously powerful. But it is still a recognition system. It defines what counts as data, what counts as risk, what counts as a position, what counts as exposure, what counts as value, what counts as externality, and what time horizon matters. The more powerful the system is, the more consequential its categories become. But that is not neutral omniscience. It is high-capacity institutional legibility.

So the feminist-CSCW answer to “perfect cost accounting” is not anti-number, anti-database, or anti-software. The answer is more precise than that: every accounting system is a recognition system. Every recognition system compresses work. Every compression produces a residual. Every residual is politically distributed. More measurement can move the residual, but it cannot abolish it. Once the measure governs decisions, workers and managers adapt to the measure, and the measure becomes part of the system it claims to describe.

That means the real question is not “can we count labor?” Of course we can count labor, in many useful and harmful ways. The real question is: what theory of labor is built into the count? What kinds of work become visible? What kinds become background? Who is allowed to define the categories? Who benefits when the categories are treated as objective? Who performs the work of making the accounting system usable? Who handles the exceptions? Who absorbs the harms that the ledger externalizes? Who gets credit for collective value? Who is told they generated no revenue even though the revenue-generating work depends on them?

So I would put the point this way: capital can count every bean it has defined as a bean. It cannot prove that nothing else is food.

Perfect cost accounting is not perfect value accounting. It is just a powerful way of enforcing a particular ontology of work.
