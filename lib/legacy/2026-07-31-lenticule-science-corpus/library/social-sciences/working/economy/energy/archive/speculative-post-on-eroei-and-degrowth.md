EROEI—energy returned on energy invested—is the ratio that built modern life.

In the early days of oil, it was easy: dig a hole, get a geyser. For every 1 unit of effort, you got 100 units of usable energy. That surplus is what built highways, suburbs, grocery stores, the internet. You could run a whole civilization on 100:1.

But that kind of surplus doesn’t last. The oil’s deeper now. The fields are dry. The rigs are bigger. The payoff is smaller. Some of the stuff we call energy today is barely even profit—shale oil, tar sands, corn ethanol—4:1, maybe 3:1 after you count the hidden costs. Same goes for food systems that need fertilizer made from fossil fuels just to stay productive. Same goes for water you have to pump out of shrinking aquifers.

The whole system is still running. But it’s running on worse margins. Higher costs, lower returns.

And it wasn’t just oil. Early internet had a high EROEI too. One blog post could shift your worldview. One hyperlink rabbit hole could rewire your brain. You could stumble into a forum and come out changed. The return on a single hour online could be seismic—mind-expanding, community-forming, identity-making. High yield. Low input.

But that surplus didn’t last either.

Now? The search space is cluttered. The signal’s been paved over with content farms, engagement traps, algorithmic landfill. Everyone’s pumping memes through cultural refineries just to sell reaction-time futures. The attention wells are shallow. The feed keeps feeding, but it doesn’t nourish.

So here’s the turn: what if the same thing is happening to your _mind_?

Social media feeds are the dopamine equivalent of a tar sands operation: huge infrastructure, toxic runoff, microdrops of usable joy. And like any extraction industry, it’s all downhill once the easy stuff’s gone.

Before long, you’re not scrolling for enjoyment and novelty anymore. You’re _fracking for meaning_—scrolling to learn why you scroll, scrolling to understand your place in the world.

But to show you one meme, one thinkpiece, energy has to move through dozens of machines, cables, servers, and satellites. You get maybe half a second of reaction. You scroll to the next one. It costs a lot. It gives back very little.

That’s a low-return system. And worse, the more you consume, the less each one gives you. Attention flattens. Feeling dulls. You chase more for less. Just like the oil rigs do.

But not all experiences behave that way.

Some are high-yield, low-input. Sitting with a friend. Staring at clouds. Doing one thing slowly. Letting yourself be bored. These are cognitively convex—they don’t always pay off, but when they do, they can change your day. Or your life. One insight. One shift. One real sense of peace. All for almost no input cost.

A lot of folk are still using morality, not thermodynamics, to determine their relationship to the world around them. But the abundance of the world is not a function of your morality.

It was high EROEI that gave many people the material distance to make big plans. Martian colonies were seen as a given, on our way to becoming an interstellar species.

But the energy isn’t there like it used to be. The dream of space colonies is already adjusting—scaled back, deferred, quietly laughed off. The collective imagination has adapted to the collective potential.

And the individual imagination about the individual has to adjust, too. assumes philosophy is a high-return system. Like you can just keep feeding it time and takes and coffee and conferences and get truth out the other end. Like you’re still going to the moon.

But philosophy has an EROEI too. And the return’s getting lower.

It’s not enough to decarbonize. You have to _de-intensify_. Learn what costs more than it gives, and opt out.

Degrowth isn’t just an economic theory. It can be a sensory strategy. A spiritual EROEI recalibration.

You don’t need _less_ life. You need less **input** for more **return**. The ROI on silence is outrageous.

So yeah, decarbonize your grid, sure. But also decarbonize your brain. De-intensify. Rediscover _qualia per joule._
