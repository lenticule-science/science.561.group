The human singing voice is the source of the Lakotaean melodic repertoire — songs are vocal songs first, and the instrumental traditions of the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) and the whistle aerophones stand in determinate relation to vocal song rather than as instrumental traditions with their own melodic content. The cedar flute [instrumentalizes](./instrumentalization.md) [wiilowan](./wiilowan.md) vocal song. The whistle aerophones articulate within an ensemble whose primary continuous melodic stream is the voice. The voice is therefore present in the aerophone theory not as an aerophone itself (the singing voice is conventionally not classified as an aerophone in instrument taxonomy, despite being air-driven) but as the source and partner of the aerophone musical material.

The Lakotaean singing voice in its principal traditional contexts uses a forward-placed, high-energy, falsetto-capable timbre, often described in the ethnomusicological literature as "high and tight." Male singing in ensemble routinely operates in a high register near and above the natural break, with skilled singers moving fluidly between modal voice and falsetto without obvious register break — a vocal technique that allows a small group of singers to project across the open-air ceremonial contexts in which much of the music has been historically performed. Solo and intimate vocal contexts admit a wider range of vocal placement and register. Female voices in ensemble traditionally enter on specific structural moments in the song, typically an octave above the men's melody, contributing a stratified two-voice texture.

The Lakotaean voice's relation to pitch differs from European-classical voice in ways that matter for music theory. The Lakotaean voice is not bound to a 12-tone equal-tempered grid; tunings are flexible and context-dependent, and the same scale-degree may be sounded slightly differently in different phrases. Microtonal inflection — small expressive pitch deviations within a sustained tone — is a regular feature of the vocal style and one of the things the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) [instrumentalization](./instrumentalization.md) must address: the flute's pitches are quantized to its fingering chart, so the flute's rendering of a vocal melody is a *quantization* of the voice's more flexible pitch content. This is a music-theoretic operation with definite consequences, not a loss of musical content; see [vocal-to-flute-quantization](../../code/transformers/vocal-to-flute-quantization.md).

The voice carries text — typically in Lakȟóta language, sometimes including vocables (non-lexical sung syllables that carry melodic and rhythmic content without semantic content). The text-bearing role of the voice has specific consequences for [phrase](./phrase.md) structure: vocal phrases align to text-phrases, vocable phrases align to melodic phrase-units regardless of text, and the [instrumentalization](./instrumentalization.md) onto the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) must address how syllable boundaries become flute-articulation boundaries (typically: a syllable becomes a single flute note, with [breath-pulse](./breath-pulse.md) articulation marking syllable boundaries).

In ensemble with the [drum](./drum.md), the voice carries the primary melodic stream and operates within the drum's metric pulse. The voice's phrasing is freer than the drum's — singers may stretch or compress phrase-internal timing slightly while remaining anchored to the drum's beat at structural moments — but the overall temporal organization is metric, not the [rubato](./rubato.md) of the solo [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md). The whistle aerophones articulate within this same metric texture, and the [voice](./voice.md)–[drum](./drum.md)–whistle ensemble is the principal site of the [heterophony](./heterophony.md) and stratified-texture phenomena the theory analyzes.
