[[Max Stirner]] and [[Henry David Thoreau]], while both proponents of [[individualism]], would likely have struggled to maintain more than a respectful intellectual acquaintance due to their differing conceptions of [[autonomy]], [[ethics]], and interpersonal value.

Stirner, whose radical [[egoism]] is central to *[[The Ego and Its Own]]* ([[1845]]), viewed human relationships through a [[utilitarian]] lens. For him, interpersonal engagement was meaningful only insofar as it fulfilled his own interest: “My intercourse with men has meaning only when it rests on mutual use; otherwise it is repulsive to me.” His idea of [[friendship]], if it existed at all, would be anchored strictly in instrumental value—usefulness, benefit, or affirmation of ego. There was no place in his framework for moral obligation, altruism, or shared ethical purpose.

Thoreau, by contrast, embedded his self-reliance in a context of moral conscience. His retreat to [[Walden Pond]] wasn’t an escape from society but a recalibration of how to live meaningfully within it. He championed solitude but also actively resisted injustice, most notably in *[[Civil Disobedience]]* ([[1849]]), suggesting that he valued relationships grounded in ethical alignment and social concern. His famous line—"I never found a companion that was so companionable as solitude"—speaks less to misanthropy and more to his belief in authenticity over social obligation. Friendship, for Thoreau, would have required shared values and mutual reflection, not just mutual use.

Were the two to meet, their common resistance to external institutions—church, state, tradition—might have sparked engaging debate. But Thoreau’s principled activism and Stirner’s rejection of all normative constraints point toward incompatibility at the level of values. Thoreau might have found Stirner's egoism unmoored from moral clarity; Stirner, meanwhile, could have dismissed Thoreau's ethics as naïve idealism.

Thus, while they may have exchanged ideas with vigor and mutual respect, it is improbable that they would have been friends in any deep or enduring sense. For Stirner, friendship lacked intrinsic worth unless it served the ego. For Thoreau, it required a kindred moral spirit. In that divergence, the possibility of real friendship recedes.
