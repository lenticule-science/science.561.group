## **The Memo Is Not the Start**

On April 4, 2025, the U.S. Department of Education issued a memo to state education agencies. It requires districts receiving federal funds to certify that they do not participate in “prohibited” diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) activities. The memo does not define DEI. It does not list violations. It does not describe enforcement mechanisms. It does state that failure to certify may result in the loss of funding.

No new law has been passed.  
No specific content has been banned.  
But districts must respond immediately, and completely.

The memo does not need to define what is prohibited.  
Its function is to create a condition in which uncertainty becomes actionable.

Staff are not instructed to remove specific materials.  
They are required to ensure that no evidence remains that could be interpreted — now or later — as participation in something undefined.

What follows is not enforcement.  
It is anticipation.  
It is panic labor shaped by administrative risk.  
It is a system attempting to comply with something it cannot describe.

This is not where the story begins.  
The memo is not the first act.  
It is the inflection point — the moment uncertainty gains structure.

The memo does not introduce new beliefs.  
It disturbs existing coherence.

And in that disturbance, the system begins to rewrite itself.
## **What Is Removed Is Not Just Content**

District responses to the memo focus on language, documentation, and association. Staff are instructed to scrub websites, revise statements, delete materials, and review records for any appearance of noncompliance. DEI job titles are renamed or eliminated. References to bias, privilege, colonization, or systemic harm are removed from curriculum and training.

On the surface, this appears to be a content purge.

But the structures being removed are not only topics.  
They are frameworks for understanding cause, consequence, and relation.

It is one thing to teach that enslavement occurred.  
It is another to teach how it structured the economy, the legal system, the built environment.  
It is one thing to name settler colonialism.  
It is another to trace how land theft, genocide, and assimilation policies shape the present.

These frameworks are not supplementary.  
They are how students are taught to understand structure, not event — system, not accident.

What is being targeted are not opinions.  
What is being targeted are the tools that make systems legible.

These tools do not belong to DEI.  
They belong to every discipline that recognizes social life as historically constructed and materially unequal.  
Their removal is not a shift in tone. It is a disassembly of method.

To comply, districts are not just deleting slideshows.  
They are disconnecting students from the frameworks that explain how the world was built.

What disappears is not just what was taught.  
It is the ability to teach why.
## **Panic Labor as Epistemic Rewriting**

The memo requires certification. It does not define how certification is demonstrated.

In response, school districts begin deleting internal training materials. Policy statements are rewritten. Grant language is revised. Website pages disappear. Curriculum folders are renamed or emptied. Documentation of past programming is quietly archived or destroyed.

None of this is dictated by the memo.  
All of it is required by the memo’s structure.

There is no checklist.  
No review process.  
No confirmation of compliance.

The only way to ensure safety is to remove anything that could be interpreted — now or later — as evidence of having once engaged with a prohibited framework.

This is not merely precaution.  
This is disavowal.

And to disavow something structurally, it must be removed without record.  
Rewritten as if it never existed.  
Described in language that signals continuity, not revision.

This is not a revision of policy.  
It is the recursive rewriting of institutional memory under conditions of risk.

Panic labor is not temporary.  
It creates the archive that the next round of compliance will inherit.

And that archive will contain no trace of what was removed.
## **Instruction Continues Without Memory**

After the purge, schools remain open.  
Classrooms are staffed.  
Units proceed.  
Assignments are distributed.  
Grades are given.

Instructional operations appear unchanged.

But the coherence of those operations depended on frameworks that no longer exist in the materials.

Curriculum guides still reference essential questions, but the conceptual tools once used to answer them have been removed.  
Readings have been stripped of framing.  
Historical units cover timelines without tracing consequence.  
Literature is taught through theme, not context.  
Health is taught through anatomy, not identity.

Teachers are not instructed to conceal.  
But they are expected to continue teaching with materials that no longer reference the systems they once explained.

Instruction continues as if continuity is intact.  
But the logic that once connected lesson to world has been severed.

What remains is language.  
What has been removed is the ability to name.

Teachers adapt.  
They imply without naming.  
They contextualize without citation.  
They reframe in ways that cannot be documented.

This is not improvisation.  
It is survival within a curriculum that no longer remembers what it was designed to teach.
## **Recursive Misalignment**

Curriculum is structured around progression.  
Concepts are introduced, expanded, and connected across units and grade levels.  
Assessment tools assume that what is tested was previously taught — and that what is taught builds on what came before.

With the removal of core frameworks, these sequences are broken.

A government unit may include voting access, but no longer reference race.  
A biology lesson may describe chromosomes, but not gender.  
A literature discussion may track character development, but avoid naming structural violence or historical context.

Teachers are expected to meet standards that rely on concepts no longer supported by instructional materials.  
Students are expected to demonstrate understanding that was never named.  
Evaluations proceed based on frameworks that have been erased.

This is not an instructional gap.  
It is a recursive condition.  
Each year’s curriculum assumes the last one was whole.  
Each lesson assumes coherence where none remains.

Teachers work around this break in sequence.  
Some over-explain. Some redirect. Some reduce complexity to avoid contradiction.  
But there is no shared language for what has been lost.  
And no formal recognition that coherence is no longer structurally supported.

What looks like a functional curriculum is the repetition of a system that no longer contains its connective tissue.

It performs progression.  
But it cannot remember what it was progressing from.
## **Trace Compression and Professional Amnesia**

Teachers entering the profession after the purge receive no account of what was removed.

Their materials are complete only in appearance.  
Their training reflects current documents, current pacing, current standards.  
Supervisors review alignment, not history.  
Mentorship does not include what used to be said — only what must now be omitted.

There are no side-by-side comparisons.  
No archival annotations.  
No institutional mechanism for describing the change.

The removed frameworks are not debated.  
They are not named.  
They are not remembered.

This is not a failure to preserve content.  
It is a condition in which **pedagogical lineage** is structurally denied.

Lesson plans are revised not to reflect disagreement, but to remove the trace of prior analysis.  
Equity language is deleted without notation.  
Context is dissolved into neutrality.

Compliance becomes a teaching strategy.  
Not as ideology, but as formatting.

What new educators learn is how to operate inside the version of the system that remains.  
What they do not learn is what used to be structurally visible.  
What they cannot teach is what no longer has language.

This is not censorship.  
It is recursive forgetting.  
And it is producing a generation of educators whose professional memory begins after the break.

## **This Is a Coordinated Forgetting**

The memo does not need to define what it prohibits.  
It does not need to specify what was removed.  
It does not need to clarify what should remain.

What it requires is **that the conditions for remembering are rendered structurally unviable.**

Once memory is disconnected from the materials, the system begins to rewrite itself.  
Once the trace is lost, there is nothing left to question.

What remains is a procedural instruction:

- **Comply** without reference to what you used to teach.
    
- **Adapt** without acknowledging what was once necessary.
    

This is not the suppression of content.  
It is the denial of the capacity to recall what was.

The system does not need to erase every record.  
It only needs to eliminate the ability to remember the terms by which it was once structured.

Teachers are not censored.  
They are **disconnected** from the frameworks that once allowed them to name the structures they taught.

The system functions without contradiction,  
but it functions only because it **has forgotten**.

This is not a one-time action.  
This is not a change in administration.  
This is a **coordinated forgetting** — a process that removes not just content, but the ability to recall what was erased.

The memo is not the cause.  
It is the condition.
