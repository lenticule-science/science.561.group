I’ve been thinking about how to make a better carrot. Not a sweeter one, not a purple one, not a mini-snack-sized one. A _useful_ one. A carrot that could serve not just as food, but as a tool—a kind of natural toothbrush and colon brush at the same time. Which, if you step back from the weirdness of that sentence, is actually a fairly reasonable thing to ask of a root vegetable.

Carrots already do some of the work. They’re crunchy, fibrous, and mildly abrasive, which means they can help scrub your teeth a little while you chew. Dentists don’t usually say “eat carrots instead of brushing,” but they also don’t mind when people snack on them. The texture alone helps disrupt plaque, and there’s some mild stimulation of the gums. Carrots also have compounds that aren't exactly medicinal, but do interact with the body in meaningful ways—especially in the gut.

But what if we could push the carrot further? Make it more intentional? What would it take to grow a carrot that _functioned_ as both a tooth cleaner and a digestive cleanser?

Let’s start with the dental side. A good toothbrush works through mechanical abrasion—gentle scrubbing that removes buildup without damaging enamel. For a carrot to do that better, it would need a slightly coarser internal structure. That likely means increasing the **cellulose and pectin content** in the root tissue, particularly around the outer cortex. Essentially, we’d breed for crunch—not just general firmness, but microtexture. The kind of grit that can hold together under chewing and offer enough friction to polish without scraping.

Beyond texture, there’s chemistry. Carrots naturally contain compounds like **falcarinol** and **falcarindiol**, which have shown antimicrobial properties in lab settings. They may not be potent enough to replace mouthwash, but concentrated slightly through breeding or soil conditioning? They could add another layer of function—not just scrubbing the teeth, but shaping the oral biome. A kind of edible dental wipe.

On the other end, we have the gut. Carrots are already a solid source of **dietary fiber**, particularly insoluble fiber, which bulks up stool and promotes regular movement. But again, we can optimize. We’d aim for a carrot with a higher ratio of **insoluble to soluble fiber**, focusing on plant cell wall components that resist breakdown in the stomach and make it to the colon intact.

Why does that matter? Because insoluble fiber acts like a broom—it pushes things along, stimulates peristalsis, and helps clean the walls of the intestine as it moves. A carrot that does this more effectively becomes more than food. It becomes a _device_.

We might also want to look at the carrot’s **sugar profile**. Certain sugars—like those in the FODMAP group—can cause bloating or discomfort in some people. We’d want to reduce those if possible, or modulate them based on who the carrot is for. (A digestive carrot for IBS is probably not the same as a toothbrush carrot for children.)

And then, of course, there’s the question of whether this is done through breeding or gene editing or something in between. Traditional carrot breeding has already given us a spectrum of textures, sizes, and sweetness levels. The traits I’m talking about—abrasiveness, fiber profile, secondary metabolites—are already **expressed within existing carrot biodiversity**. It’s not about inventing new genes. It’s about dialing existing ones into a new combination.

What you end up with, then, is something humble and elegant: a carrot that you can eat as a snack, but that also brushes your teeth a little better _and_ leaves your gut happier than it found it. A small, passive tool. You don’t have to do anything extra—just eat the carrot. The brushwork happens while you chew, and again as you digest.

I’m not saying it’ll replace your toothbrush. But maybe it becomes a kind of _first brush_—a midday scrub that helps between cleanings. Or a functional food you give kids, knowing it’s doing a little more work than it seems. Or just a step toward foods with different embodied intentions.

There are worse things to grow.
