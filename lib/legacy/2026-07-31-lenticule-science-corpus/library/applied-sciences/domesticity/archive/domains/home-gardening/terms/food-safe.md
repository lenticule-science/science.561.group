Food-safe describes a material, surface, or substance that is suitable for contact with food without introducing harmful chemicals, residues, or contaminants. In [home gardening](../index.md) — particularly when growing [sprouts](./sprout.md) and [microgreens](../topics/microgreens/index.md) — the food-safe status of containers, growing media, and cleaning agents determines whether the harvest is safe to eat.

Common food-safe considerations for home growers include: whether a plastic container is rated for food contact (look for recycling codes 1, 2, 4, or 5 and explicit food-grade labeling), whether a growing medium contains fertilizers or treatments not approved for edible crops, and whether cleaning products leave residues that could transfer to sprouts during the [rinse cycle](./rinse-cycle.md). Glass jars and stainless steel mesh are generally food-safe by default; plastics and coated metals require verification.

The distinction between food-safe and [sanitized](./sanitize.md) is important: a container can be food-safe (made of appropriate materials) without being sanitized (free of microbial contamination), and vice versa. Home sprouting requires both — equipment made of food-safe materials that is sanitized before each use. This matters because sprouts grow in warm, moist conditions that favor bacterial multiplication, and the short cycle from seed to harvest means there is no cooking step to eliminate pathogens before consumption.
