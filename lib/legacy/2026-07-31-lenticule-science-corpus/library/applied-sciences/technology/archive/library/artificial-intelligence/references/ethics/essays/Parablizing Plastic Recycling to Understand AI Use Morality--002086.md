By the middle of the 20th century, the development of industrial plastics had been made inevitable by the body of research and techniques that had been accumulated. As a [[material]], it was incredibly lightweight and [[fungible]], even compared to [[metal]] or [[wood]]. Its ability to be used as a material in nearly any part of the manufacturing process made it ideal for industrial mass production. Its low-weight made it ideal for the logistics-intense consumerism and militarism that was developing in the United States.

During World War II, plastics were adopted as substitutes for rationed materials like rubber, metal, and glass. The U.S. military expanded research and industrial capacity for plastics, especially in electronics, packaging, and medical supplies. After the war, this infrastructure was preserved and expanded for Cold War logistics. By the Korean War, plastics were used across field equipment, communications, and transport systems. Civilian manufacturers adapted the same processes for mass consumer goods.

However, unlike the general materials that preceded it, plastic was not easily reusable or destroyable. Soon, waste plastic became a visible pollution in the American environment, inducing public concern.

Coastal communities raised alarms about the accumulation of plastic debris in waterways and beaches. Local anti-litter campaigns were often led by women’s civic groups and conservation clubs, which framed the issue in terms of environmental and community degradation. By the end of the decade, urban waste management systems were overwhelmed by disposable packaging, and the visible presence of non-decomposing plastics in public and natural spaces began to draw national attention.

As the production and use of plastics was necessary to maintain the national posture in the Cold War, and plastics had become a critical part of the economy, production could not be scaled down. Thus, the plastic waste, a crisis, had to be managed.

In response to growing public discomfort, the plastics and beverage industries launched the "Keep America Beautiful" campaign in 1971. Its most iconic ad featured a white actor in redface pretending to be a Native American, shedding a tear at the sight of roadside litter. The campaign taught the public that plastic pollution was “litter,” and that littering was a personal moral failure. Around the same time, industry began to promote recycling as a solution, despite internal research showing that large-scale recycling was neither economically viable nor technically feasible for most plastics.

This structured recycling and anti-littering as forms of impossible carework: work that cannot meet its stated goal but is still demanded and moralized. It aligns with Lauren Berlant’s theory of cruel optimism, where the hope of doing good sustains participation in systems that prevent meaningful change.

Subcultures developed around avoiding plastics—using glass, buying in bulk, moving off-grid. These were treated as forms of resistance but often relied on the same infrastructures. They provided aesthetic and moral alternatives without displacing the structure of production.

Thus, what was culturally understood as a form of environmentalism was a form of crisis management done by oil companies, to defer the anxieties of plastics pollution into moral individualism practiced as harm reduction. This aligns with a convergence of what Elizabeth Povinelli termed the quasi-event, a socially-legible moral crisis that is operationalized to delay or displace recognition of structural conditions, and Byung-Chul Han's critique of neoliberal responsiblity, where structural violence is individuated, internalized, and privatized, so that systemic harms appear and are felt as moral failures of individuals.

The cadence of this form of crisis management can be seen around Large Language Models and Artificial Intelligence. The public release of chat-interfaces like GPT-3 and image generators like Midjourney is felt as a harm, and so a logic of harm reduction has been engaged, resulting in an attitude of abstinence: do not use AI in your work or play, and do not support those who do.

But like with plastic, this personal refusal occurs after the systemic integration has already taken place. LLMs are now embedded across defense, surveillance, finance, logistics, and information operations. They are integrated into drone targeting systems, predictive policing, disinformation campaigns, and federal procurement frameworks. Individual abstention does not interrupt these deployments; it defers attention away from them.

This is again a form of impossible carework—an effort to perform moral coherence within a structure that has already locked in its harm. The desire to preserve one’s humanity becomes a form of attachment that blocks systemic analysis. Moral positions are routed through personalized relationships to technology, which obscure the scale and location of power.

What is being called “responsible AI use” is often a **morally satisfying misrecognition** of how AI is already structured into infrastructural violence. The refusal to prompt a chatbot or view AI art is framed as an ethical act, while the training data, labor regimes, and militarized applications remain untouched. As with recycling, harm is reformatted into a question of consumer ethics rather than systemic integration.

Critically, the public anxiety might be correct, as it was with plastics, but finding the truth of those anxieties is being misguided. Additionally, the specific concerns being voiced may be valid, but contextualized through misguidance into being misunderstood.

The public discourse has moralized these concerns around an individual's relationship to their humanity. This piece has analyzed the misguidance of these concerns.

Neither has sought the truth of the concerns, but they have made clear that there are at least two facets to the concerns. There must be an [[Analysis of the Liberal Subjects' Concerns of LLM Existence]] and an [[Analysis of the Liberal Subjects' Concerns of LLM Use]].
