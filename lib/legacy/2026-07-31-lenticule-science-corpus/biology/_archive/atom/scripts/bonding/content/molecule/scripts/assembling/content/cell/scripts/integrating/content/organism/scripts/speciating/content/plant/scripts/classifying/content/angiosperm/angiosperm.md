Angiosperm: a flowering plant, member of the clade Angiospermae (formerly Magnoliophyta). With approximately 300,000 described species across 416 families and 64 orders, angiosperms are the dominant land plants and have shaped terrestrial ecosystems for the past 130 million years. The name encodes the defining innovation: *angio* (vessel) + *sperm* (seed), referring to the enclosed ovule — the central reproductive novelty that distinguishes angiosperms from [gymnosperms](../../botany/terms/gymnosperm.md), which bear naked seeds.

The angiosperm lineage is monophyletic, originating in the Early Cretaceous. Modern classification follows the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG) framework, now in its fourth iteration (APG IV, 2016), which reorganizes the traditional Linnaean hierarchy on the basis of molecular phylogenetics. The two largest clades are the [monocots](./monocot.md) (~70,000 species) and the [eudicots](./eudicot.md) (~210,000 species), which together comprise >99% of angiosperm diversity.

The reproductive anatomy that defines angiosperms is the **flower** — a modified shoot bearing microsporophylls (stamens producing pollen) and megasporophylls (carpels enclosing ovules). After pollination and pollen tube growth, fertilization occurs via a unique process called **double fertilization**: one sperm nucleus fuses with the egg cell to form the embryo; a second sperm nucleus fuses with the polar nuclei of the central cell to produce the **endosperm**, a triploid nutritive tissue that fills the seed and sustains the developing seedling. This process is unique to angiosperms and provides a selective advantage: the endosperm is provisioned only after successful fertilization, avoiding wasteful investment in unfertilized ovules.

The **fruit** — a mature ovary enclosing seeds — is another angiosperm innovation that facilitates seed dispersal and protection. The combination of flowers, double fertilization, endosperm, and fruits makes angiosperms efficient reproducers with fast life cycles, strong parental investment in offspring, and diverse dispersal mechanisms. These traits, coupled with xylem vessels (more efficient than gymnosperm tracheids) and rapid leaf turnover, have made angiosperms ecologically dominant across nearly every terrestrial habitat.
