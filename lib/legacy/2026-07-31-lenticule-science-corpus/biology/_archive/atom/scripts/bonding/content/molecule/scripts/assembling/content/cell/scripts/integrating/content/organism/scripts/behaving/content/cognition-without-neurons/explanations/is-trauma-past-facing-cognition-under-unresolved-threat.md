# Does recurrent disaster shift cognition from future-oriented possibility to past-oriented trace management?

## The question

When human beings experience disaster not as a completed event but as a recurring or structurally unresolved condition, does cognition shift its temporal reference system?

More precisely: does recurrent ecological disaster produce a measurable shift from **prospective orientation** — acting from open possible futures — toward **retrospective orientation** — acting from the traces of prior catastrophe?

If so, is trauma the organism-level version of the same process that state archive, liberal crisis-management, and platformal affect-governance perform at social scale: the conversion of open becoming into past-facing control?

## Initial formulation

The ordinary threat cycle has a temporal structure:

1. threat appears;
2. defensive mobilization begins;
3. threat passes or is survived;
4. discharge completes;
5. baseline returns.

Trauma occurs when this temporal sequence fails. The threat has passed chronologically, but not physiologically. The past event continues to organize the present.

Structural crisis intensifies this: the threat has not passed at all. The organism is not misreading the present; the present really is organized by unresolved threat.

The question is whether recurrent disaster does something similar to cognition at collective scale. A burned forest is not only an event. It is a landscape made of prior event-traces: ash, absence, scarred trees, changed animal movement, altered water flow, seed release, soil change, hunger, migration, memory. The present becomes legible as the residue of catastrophe.

So the cognitive problem may shift from:

> What future can we grow toward?

to:

> What past event is still determining the present?

## Testable hypothesis

Populations exposed to recurrent ecological disaster should show increased reliance on past-event traces when interpreting present conditions and planning action.

This should appear across multiple levels:

1. **Physiological:** altered stress-axis regulation, elevated allostatic load, heightened threat-detection, reduced autonomic flexibility.
2. **Cognitive:** greater salience of memory, precedent, scar, warning, recurrence, and threat prediction; reduced capacity for open-ended prospective planning under unresolved threat.
3. **Narrative:** more frequent disaster-origin explanations: “this is because of the last fire/flood/drought/war.”
4. **Institutional:** increased archive, mapping, boundary-making, risk classification, insurance logic, policing, emergency-management procedure.
5. **Temporal:** compression of future horizons; expansion of past-reference horizons.
6. **Political:** greater susceptibility to domination when institutions offer past-facing order as protection from future uncertainty.

## What would count as evidence?

Evidence for the hypothesis would include:

- disaster-exposed populations showing measurable changes in cortisol rhythms, inflammatory markers, startle response, sleep, or vagal flexibility;
- shifts in language before and after disaster toward memory, warning, recurrence, risk, preparedness, and trace-reading;
- reduced long-horizon planning and increased short-cycle vigilance;
- increased institutional production of maps, records, boundaries, risk scores, insurance categories, emergency protocols, and policing after disaster;
- intergenerational persistence of these shifts when the disaster’s material traces remain unresolved;
- stronger effects where disaster is recurring or structurally unresolvable, weaker effects where recovery and discharge are materially supported.

## What would falsify or weaken it?

The hypothesis would be weakened if:

- recurrent disaster exposure does not alter temporal language, planning horizons, or threat-salience after controlling for material deprivation;
- physiological stress markers return to baseline when the ecological threat remains present;
- disaster-exposed groups maintain or increase open-ended prospective planning without corresponding increases in trace-management;
- institutional archival/risk expansion after disaster is explained entirely by bureaucratic capacity rather than by unresolved threat.

## Stronger version

The strongest question may be:

**Is trauma a temporal-orientation disorder produced when past actuality remains more predictive than open possibility?**

At individual scale, this appears as hypervigilance, flashback, rumination, and chronic activation.

At social scale, it appears as archive, border, title, emergency management, risk governance, policing, insurance, and platformal crisis narration.

At energetic scale, it appears when declining surplus collapses the distance that previously allowed future-oriented abstraction.
