Dengbej (Kurmanji: *dengbêj*, from *deng* "voice" + *bêj* "speaker") are Kurdish oral storytellers and singers who perform long, unaccompanied vocal narratives — epics, love stories, laments, historical accounts, genealogies, and improvisations — drawn from a communal repertoire that spans centuries. The tradition is the primary vehicle through which Kurdish history, law, kinship, and emotional life have been transmitted across generations in the absence of state institutions, written canons, or centralized literary authority.

A dengbej performance is [heteroglossic](../domains/semiotics/terms/heteroglossia.md) by structure. Each dengbej performs the same material differently. Audiences participate, contest, and redirect. No single performance is authoritative. The repertoire contains contradictory accounts of the same events — a battle epic may celebrate the warrior and mourn the widow in the same breath, a love story may honor the beloved and criticize the social order that separates the lovers. The form itself — unaccompanied voice, improvised within tradition, performed in community — refuses the boundaries of official literature and resists codification into a single authorized text.

This is why states target dengbej. Turkish suppression of Kurdish language in the 1930s imposed fines on every word of Kurdish spoken in public. The 1980 military coup nearly destroyed the tradition — most dengbej ceased performing. Iran's Pahlavi regime banned Kurdish music and oral performance alongside Kurdish dress and education. The suppression targeted not specific content but the form: a cultural practice that preserved multiple voices, multiple histories, and multiple ways of being Kurdish. Dengbej are dangerous to genring states because they are living archives of the [heteroglossia](../domains/semiotics/terms/heteroglossia.md) that [genring](../../../../humanities/domains/sociology/domains/cybernetic-postliberalism/terms/genring.md) must destroy.

The post-1991 revival of dengbej — through Kurdish-language television, diaspora festivals, cultural institutions in Iraqi Kurdistan, and the Dengbej House (*Mala Dengbêjan*) in Diyarbakir — preserves the tradition but also risks transforming it. Institutionalization can turn living heteroglossia into heritage: a bounded, conservable genre rather than a contested, transforming practice. When a dengbej performs at a cultural festival for an audience that includes UNESCO observers, the performance becomes a representation of Kurdish culture — a genre figure of Kurdish culture — rather than an enactment of it.
