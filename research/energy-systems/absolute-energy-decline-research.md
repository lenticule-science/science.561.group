# Absolute Energy Decline and the Growing Infrastructure Overhead

Research notes compiled 2025-03-31; scientific status revised 2026-10-17. **Research hypothesis:** Falling EROEI in some energy sources and a growing maintenance burden may reduce the energy and infrastructure service surplus available to society, even if some gross production indicators continue rising. The globally aggregated claim that **absolute net energy has already peaked** is **not established by the studies cited here**. This note distinguishes observed evidence, model forecasts, and its own proposed causal synthesis.

This question now also informs [Capacity Ownership in a Flow Economy](../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/10/17/2026-10-17-note-on-capacity-ownership-in-a-flow-economy.md), which asks how an economy could hold shares in sustainable, **typed productive capacity** rather than only title to assets. Energy surplus, service capacity, ecological health and rights to distribution remain different quantities. The older use of "depth" is retained here as a research description, **not** a proved identification with the Leader's **distal bypass** or with the separately existing [Obsmetrologia differential-bypass theorem](https://github.com/lenticule-science/obsmetrologia/blob/main/Obsmetrologia/Cohomology/DifferentialBypass.lean), which concerns closure/settling and regime agreement rather than physical energy accounting.

## The core problem in one paragraph

**Model hypothesis:** Declining net returns in some sources, depletion and maintenance requirements can squeeze future usable capacity. Delannoy et al. (2021) **projected** a 2024 net-energy peak for oil liquids under specified GlobalShift scenarios; this is not an observed peak of global energy. The IEA (2025) finds nearly 90% of *upstream oil and gas investment spending* since 2019 has been directed toward offsetting declining field production. This investment-share statistic is **not** the energetic cost-of-energy or a claim that oil uses 90% of its own output. A PNAS Nexus (2024) model estimates global agrifood EROEI of 0.91 in 2019 under one explicitly bounded numerator/denominator; different inclusions yield different ratios. Half the planet's topsoil is gone. Aquifers are depleting. Mineral ore grades are declining. Each of these pressures demands more energy input to maintain current output, and they compound: degraded soil needs more fossil-derived fertilizer from a system that requires more investment to maintain production from depleted fields, pumped with water from aquifers that require more energy to access. The infrastructure of "depth" — the apparatus between energy extraction and delivered services — has costs that the proposed economy must account for. The **magnitude and global direction of the remaining usable surplus** require an actual reconciled energy account, not an inference from these separate indicators.

## I. Net oil peak in a 2021 scenario model

Conventional crude oil production peaked in 2006 at ~70 mb/d (IEA WEO 2010; confirmed by Fatih Birol). Total liquids (including unconventional) continue to grow in GROSS terms, but:

**Model result — Delannoy et al. (2021, *Applied Energy*):** Under the study's GlobalShift scenario and modeled EROI assumptions, net energy from oil liquids is **projected to peak in 2024 at ~415 PJ/day**, and gross production for energy purposes around **2034 at 551 PJ/day**. The model reports substantial year uncertainty (standard deviation 6.6 years across scenarios for the net peak). The paper says the result must **not** be read as announcement of a coming peak; it cannot establish an observed 2024 peak in hindsight. It concerns modeled *oil liquids*, not all world usable energy. [Primary paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apenergy.2021.117843).

**Model/account-boundary estimate:** Delannoy et al. estimate oil-production energy inputs of 15.5% of oil liquids gross output for their contemporary base and project **50% by 2050** under their scenarios. Neither figure establishes a measured 2050 outcome.

IEA Oil 2025: supply capacity growth is "heavily frontloaded, slipping from 1.8 mb/d in 2025 to contraction after 2029."

## II. The maintenance trap (Red Queen Syndrome)

**IEA September 2025:** "Nearly **90% of annual upstream oil and gas investment since 2019 has gone to offsetting production declines** rather than meeting demand growth."

Without continued investment:
- Global oil production would fall **8%/year** (~5.5 mb/d each year)
- Natural gas would fall **9%/year** (~270 bcm each year)

These decline rates have **doubled since 2010** (when the figures were 3.9 mb/d and 180 bcm). The acceleration is itself accelerating. Tight oil/shale gas production would fall **>35% within 12 months** if investment stopped, and a further 15% the year after.

Total upstream investment in 2025: ~$570 billion. Mostly going to running in place.

The IEA explicitly calls this the "Red Queen Syndrome" — the system must run faster just to stand still.

## III. Agrifood output/input energy ratio and its boundary

**Measured-and-modeled global estimate — PNAS Nexus, December 2024:** Global agrifood **energy output to externally supplied energy input** was **0.91 in 2019** under the authors' principal boundary, compared with 0.68 in 1995 and 0.94 in 2014. They report 0.63 including losses, biofuel and feed (LBF) under an expanded account, but 1.03 under a different inclusion of losses in output. The reported 0.91 is therefore **not an intrinsic EROEI of agriculture regardless of accounting boundary**, nor evidence that sunlight's photosynthetic contribution has vanished. [Primary paper](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae524).

- Food processing: 40% of total food system energy use
- Fertilizer: 17%
- Livestock: 60% of energy inputs, <20% of food calories delivered

The authors contrast traditional farming systems with industrialized agrifood systems dependent on substantial external energy inputs. **Under their 0.91 account**, global food-calorie delivery is smaller than specified commercial energy inputs; some regions and system boundaries still return more than one unit. Describing **all** agrifood activity as a universally negative-energy system would confuse that defined account with global energy conservation.

**Soil degradation compounds this:**
- 50% of topsoil lost in 150 years
- 33% of arable land moderately to highly degraded (FAO)
- 90% of topsoil at risk by 2050 (FAO projection)
- 75 billion tonnes of soil eroded per year globally
- 100 million hectares of healthy land lost each year (UNCCD 2015-2019 data)

**Yield growth is slowing:** Global agricultural output growth fell from 2.68%/yr (2001-2010) to 2.08%/yr (2011-2019) — the lowest rate in six decades (USDA ERS). In 24-39% of maize, rice, wheat, and soybean growing areas, yields have either never improved, stagnated, or collapsed (*Nature Communications* 2012).

The implication: maintaining current food production on degrading soil with declining EROEI requires ever-increasing energy inputs (more synthetic fertilizer, more irrigation, more processing), drawn from an energy system that is itself consuming more of its output to maintain production. The food system and the energy system are in a compounding negative feedback loop.

## IV. Water as an energy sink

As aquifers deplete, water becomes an increasingly significant energy cost.

**Ogallala Aquifer:** Water levels have dropped 28-44 feet since mid-20th century. Wells must be drilled deeper, increasing pumping costs. Some wells are now unusable for smaller farmers.

**Punjab (India):** The most rapidly depleting major aquifer, losing 46 cm/year (2003-2012). 64.6 billion cubic meters lost in 17 years. 79% of irrigation is groundwater-fed, powered by subsidized electricity. The subsidy creates a feedback loop: free electricity → deeper pumping → faster depletion → need for even deeper pumping.

**Desalination as replacement:** Reverse osmosis uses 2.5-4.0 kWh/m3 with energy recovery (7-12 kWh/m3 without). As aquifers deplete, desalination scales up, and water transitions from a nearly free input to a significant energy cost.

## V. Mineral ore grade decline

**Copper:** Average ore grade declined from 5% (1900) to 0.62% (present). Mineral resources average 0.49%, indicating continued future decline. Lower grades mean exponentially more energy per unit of extracted metal.

**Mining costs:** Increased ~30% in the last five years (PwC Mine 2024). Mining already uses ~10% of global energy (Bardi).

**Michaux (GTK Finland, 2024):** Peer-reviewed analysis of mineral requirements for a full fossil fuel phase-out found that demand for nearly every necessary mineral would exceed not just production capacity but **known global reserves**. This is not a production bottleneck; it is a resource sufficiency problem.

**Bardi's Seneca Effect:** Mineral depletion follows Seneca dynamics — slow rise, rapid collapse. Easy, high-grade deposits were exploited first. Remaining deposits require exponentially more energy. The extractive industry has masked this through economies of scale, but those are reaching limits.

## VI. Forest loss

- Pre-industrial forest cover: ~6 billion hectares (57% of habitable land)
- Current: 4.14 billion hectares (FAO FRA 2025)
- One-third of all forests destroyed. Half of that loss in the last ~120 years.
- Tropics lost a record 6.7 million hectares of primary forest in 2024 (worst in two decades)
- For the first time, fires (not agriculture) were the leading cause — nearly 50% of tropical forest destruction

## VII. The renewable transition's energy overhead

**Capellan-Perez et al. (2019, *Energy Strategy Reviews*):** A fast 100% renewable transition by 2060 could decrease system EROEI from ~12:1 to **~3:1 by mid-century**, stabilizing at ~5:1. Rapid deployment "cannibalizes" existing energy output — all output fuels the embodied energy of future power plants.

**Kikstra et al. (2022, *Nature Communications*):** 1.5C-compatible transition pathways cause a **10-34% decline in net energy available to society** during the transition period.

**Brockway et al. (2024):** Fossil fuels at useful stage already deliver only ~3.5:1. The EROI equivalent for renewables is ~4.6:1. Renewables exceed this, suggesting the transition is energetically viable *in the long run*. But the transition period itself involves building new infrastructure while maintaining old infrastructure — doubling the overhead.

**Heinberg's observation:** "Renewable energy isn't replacing fossil fuel energy — it's adding to it." Global emissions keep rising. The transition is additive, not substitutive. The energy overhead of maintaining BOTH systems simultaneously is higher than either alone.

## VIII. The compounding structure

**Hypothesis (not a measured coupling law):** These pressures may interact through feedback. Whether their combined outcome is additive, multiplicative, threshold-driven or offset by substitution must be tested with a coupled dynamical model. Candidate causal paths:

1. Declining oil EROEI → more energy needed per unit of oil → less net energy for everything else
2. Soil degradation → more fertilizer needed → more natural gas consumed → faster EROEI decline
3. Water depletion → deeper wells or desalination → more energy consumed → less net energy for everything else
4. Ore grade decline → more energy per tonne of mineral → harder to build renewable infrastructure → slower transition → longer dependence on declining fossil fuels
5. Infrastructure maintenance → 90% of investment to offset decline → less capital for new capacity → slower transition
6. Renewable transition overhead → 10-34% net energy decline during transition → less energy for maintaining EXISTING systems during the period when they're being replaced

Each pressure worsens the others. A steepening-decline trajectory is a **model hypothesis**, not a consequence established by simply listing these paths. Identify the state variables, causal coefficients, feedback signs, timescales and comparison trajectories before claiming Regime III dynamics.

**Hagens' framing:** "We are drawing down the fossil hydrocarbon store 10-million-times faster than it was trickle-charged by daily photosynthesis millions of years ago." Congressional Budget Office: each $1 of deferred maintenance generates $4.50 in eventual costs through cascade deterioration. Energy-related infrastructure can also create delayed costs, but an identical compounding rate is not demonstrated by the cited dollar-denominated maintenance figure.

## IX. What this means for the paper

The current outline treats the energy data (Step 4) as background — well-established, not the contribution. The net-energy scenarios and measured investment costs sharpen two **conditional hypotheses**:

**1. The floor may drop under explicit scenarios.** The paper assumes there is surplus to work with, but that surplus can be pressure-tested against projected decline and rising maintenance requirements. But if net energy is declining in absolute terms while maintenance overhead is growing, the surplus available for cognitive scaffolding is shrinking from both sides: less total energy AND a larger fraction consumed by the system's own maintenance. The "distance" between extraction and return isn't just the EROEI ratio — it's the physical infrastructure (pipelines, refineries, data centers, grid, farms, water systems) that must be maintained just to keep the current system running. That infrastructure IS the depth. And it's getting more expensive while the energy to maintain it is getting scarcer.

**2. There may be no recovery path within the current energy paradigm.** The outline's Step 10 (implications) assumes that maintaining or restoring cognitive scaffolding is possible if societies choose maintenance, care, and short feedback loops. Under a demonstrated absolute net-energy decline, even well-chosen investments in scaffolding could be insufficient. The question becomes: is there enough net energy to maintain ANY institutional scaffolding for reflexive cognition at civilizational scale, or is Regime III terminal within the current energy paradigm?

The global agrifood ratio 0.91 (under one defined 2019 boundary) and the IEA's 90% **money-investment allocation** statistic concern **different numerators and denominators** and must not be multiplied or presented as if 90% of energy output were consumed. They are warning signals for an empirical model of future energy-service capacity, not sufficient evidence that global cognitive scaffolding is already in terminal decline. The [capacity-share thesis](../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/10/17/2026-10-17-note-on-capacity-ownership-in-a-flow-economy.md) specifically proposes to measure that **feasible service capacity** with ecological and rights constraints rather than equating capacity with a single energy ratio.
