Dmitry Orlov is a Russian-American engineer who emigrated from the Soviet Union as a child and made repeated extended visits back during and after the 1991 Soviet collapse, observing its social and economic aftermath directly rather than through report. His writing compares that experience against what he argues are structurally worse vulnerabilities in the contemporary United States.

## Core ideas

- **[The five stages of collapse](../../../../../services/history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/glossary/terms/five-stages-of-collapse.md).** *Reinventing Collapse* (2011, developed from a 2006 essay) and *The Five Stages of Collapse: Survivors' Toolkit* (2013) lay out a sequential model: financial collapse (faith that "the market shall provide" is lost, savings and financial institutions are wiped out); commercial collapse (goods and commodities become scarce as import and retail supply chains fail); political collapse (faith that "the government will take care of you" is lost as government services and legitimacy fail); social collapse (faith that "your people will take care of you" is lost as community and social institutions fail to fill the resulting void); and cultural collapse (faith in the decency of humanity itself erodes).
- **Comparative vulnerability.** Orlov's central provocative claim is that the United States, despite its greater wealth, was in some structural respects less prepared than the late Soviet Union had been — citing weaker informal-economy skills, greater car dependency and weaker public transit, higher household debt, and thinner social cohesion — meaning the stages could, in his account, proceed further and faster in an American collapse than they did in the Soviet one.

## What the 561 Group reads them for

Orlov's staged model gives the house a concrete, ordered account of how collapse can proceed unevenly across financial, commercial, political, social, and cultural domains rather than as a single undifferentiated event — a companion sequencing to [John Michael Greer](./john-michael-greer.md)'s catabolic-collapse model, which describes the uneven, lurching character of decline without staging it by domain in the same way. Orlov's financial-collapse stage, in particular, is read against the house's own [long-term-debt-cycle](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/long-term-debt-cycle.md) and [energy-return-on-investment](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/energy-return-on-investment.md) material as one account of what an "ugly deleveraging," in [Ray Dalio](./ray-dalio.md)'s vocabulary, looks like from the inside of the society experiencing it.
