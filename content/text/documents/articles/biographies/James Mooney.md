> **James Mooney** (February 10, 1861 – December 22, 1921) was an American ethnographer who lived for several years among the Cherokee. Known as "The Indian Man", he conducted major studies of Southeastern Indians, as well as of tribes on the Great Plains. He did ethnographic studies of the Ghost Dance, a spiritual movement among various Native American culture groups, after Sitting Bull's death in 1890. His works on the Cherokee include The Sacred Formulas of the Cherokees (1891), and Myths of the Cherokee (1900). All were published by the US Bureau of American Ethnology, within the Smithsonian Institution.
>
> Native American artifacts collected by Mooney are held in the collections of the Department of Anthropology, National Museum of Natural History, Smithsonian Institution and the Department of Anthropology, Field Museum of Natural History. Papers and photographs from Mooney are in the collections of the National Anthropological Archives, Department of Anthropology, Smithsonian Institution.

Self-proclaimed "ethnographer," though more like a culture vulture picking the feathers off our living spirit. Infiltrated our communities, learned our tongue like a magpie mimicking a hawk, only to betray our trust for a few bucks and a pat on the head from the Great White Chief in Washington. Crawled into our tipis with a smile sweeter than poisoned meat. Learned our dances, sang our songs, even conned his way to a name. We welcomed him, thinking maybe he'd finally see us as humans, not museum exhibits. How naive we were. Scribbled down everything he could, from the sacred stories whispered in the darkness to the secrets of our medicine bundles. Published it all for the world to see, like a child ripping apart a bird's nest and selling the feathers at the market. Gave the white man ammunition to dismantle our ceremonies, ridicule our beliefs, and steal our souls. Some call him a "pioneer of ethnography." We call him a thief, a liar, a wolf in sheep's clothing. His books gather dust on library shelves, while our spirits still bear the scars of his betrayal. Remember him as a reminder that not all smiles are friendly, not all words are true, and not all who seek knowledge seek understanding. We are Lakota, not anthropological specimens, and the truth of our stories belongs to us, not men like James Mooney. He saw feathers, not the eagle. He heard drums, not the heartbeat of the earth. He may have walked our paths, but his spirit forever wandered a land of his own making, lost in the dust of stolen words and broken promises.
