Philip Tetlock (1954-) is an American psychologist, currently at the University of Pennsylvania, whose research program on expert judgment is the empirical foundation the house's own forecasting discipline is built against. His two-decade study of expert political forecasters, published as *Expert Political Judgment: How Good Is It? How Can We Know?* (2005), tracked tens of thousands of predictions by hundreds of experts and found their aggregate accuracy barely exceeded chance, and in some conditions fell below simple extrapolation algorithms — the finding popularly summarized as experts performing no better than "a dart-throwing chimp."

## Core ideas

- **Foxes over hedgehogs.** Tetlock's 2005 study found that "foxes" — forecasters who draw on many analytical frames, hold views tentatively, and readily revise them — substantially outperformed "hedgehogs" — forecasters committed to one big, totalizing explanatory framework applied with high confidence. Confidence and public visibility were, if anything, inversely correlated with accuracy.
- **The Good Judgment Project and superforecasters.** Running IARPA-funded forecasting tournaments with Barbara Mellers, Tetlock identified a small subset of ordinary volunteers — "superforecasters" — who, using disciplined probabilistic reasoning, decomposition of questions into checkable components, frequent belief updating, and active search for disconfirming evidence, systematically outperformed professional intelligence analysts with access to classified information.
- **[Superforecasting](../../../../../../engineering-services-section/services/computer-engineering-services-section/services/computer-systems-engineering-services-section/services/computer-web-engineering-services-section/services/web-platform-services-section/services/web3-platform-services-section/web3-gambling-platform-services-section/services/kalshi-services-section/services/forecasting-service/content/text/glossary/terms/superforecasting.md) as method, not talent alone.** *Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction* (with Dan Gardner, 2015) argues the superforecasters' edge is substantially a trainable method — calibration (do stated probabilities match observed frequencies?) and resolution (how sharply do forecasts distinguish likely from unlikely outcomes?), scored with proper scoring rules such as the Brier score — rather than an innate gift.

## What the 561 Group reads them for

Tetlock's calibration evidence is the direct empirical basis for the house's own [forecasting-services-section](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/forecasting-services-section/README.md), which scores every recorded forecast against outcomes with the Brier score specifically because Tetlock's research demonstrated that plausible-sounding, high-confidence expert narrative is not a reliable substitute for scored, updating, probabilistic judgment. That finding cuts directly against the confident, specific, largely unscored forecasting style of [Peter Zeihan](./peter-zeihan.md), [George Friedman](./george-friedman.md), and [Ian Bremmer](./ian-bremmer.md): all three write in the hedgehog register Tetlock's research found to underperform, which is why the house reads their substantive claims as hypotheses to weigh and score rather than forecasts to adopt on the strength of their institutional authority. [Nate Silver](./nate-silver.md)'s Bayesian, continuously-updated approach is the discipline's closer cousin.
