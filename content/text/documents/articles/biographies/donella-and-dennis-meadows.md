Donella H. Meadows (1941-2001) and Dennis L. Meadows led, with Jørgen Randers and William W. Behrens III, the team that produced *The Limits to Growth* (1972) for the Club of Rome, one of the most widely read and most contested works of twentieth-century systems modeling.

## Core ideas

- **World3 and [limits to growth](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/limits-to-growth.md).** Built using the system-dynamics methods Jay Forrester developed at MIT, the World3 model tracked the interaction of population, industrial output, food production, resource depletion, and pollution under a range of policy scenarios. Its "standard run," commonly called the business-as-usual scenario, showed industrial output and population peaking and then declining sometime in the twenty-first century as accumulating resource and pollution constraints overwhelmed the economy's capacity to keep growing.
- **Reception and early criticism.** The 1972 book was widely and sharply criticized on release, including by economists such as Julian Simon and William Nordhaus, as an exercise in unfounded doom-mongering that underestimated technological adaptation, price-driven substitution, and the discovery of new reserves.
- **Later empirical checks.** Follow-up work — *Beyond the Limits* (1992) and *Limits to Growth: The 30-Year Update* (2004) — and independent empirical comparisons, notably Graham Turner's 2008 and 2014 papers comparing three decades of observed global data against the original World3 scenarios, found the real-world trajectory tracking fairly closely with the standard-run scenario rather than with the more optimistic alternatives.
- **The 2020s update.** Gaya Herrington's 2021 paper in the *Journal of Industrial Ecology*, "Update to limits to growth: Comparing the World3 model with empirical data," extended the comparison through 2019 data and found current trends most closely aligned with the "Business-as-Usual" and "Comprehensive Technology" scenarios — both of which project a slowdown or halt to global economic and industrial growth beginning around the 2020s to 2040s absent a significant deviation from the observed trajectory.

## What the 561 Group reads them for

World3's overshoot dynamic — growth proceeding past the point the underlying resource and pollution constraints can sustain, followed by decline rather than a smooth approach to a stable limit — is the house's clearest formal, quantitative predecessor to the qualitative EROEI-decline position held by [Nate Hagens](./nate-hagens.md), [Richard Heinberg](./richard-heinberg.md), and [Joseph Tainter](./joseph-tainter.md), and it is read alongside the house's own [sizing-without-a-bank note](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/04/2026-09-04-note-on-sizing-without-a-bank.md) and its concern with a rising absorbing barrier under climate breakdown: Herrington's 2021 update tracking toward a 2020s-2040s growth slowdown is, on the house's reading, the same physical constraint the oil-sanctions log's "declining energy return on energy invested" describes, arrived at independently through a decades-old, empirically re-tested systems model rather than through energy-accounting analysis.
