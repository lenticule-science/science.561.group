Richard Heinberg is an American educator and senior fellow at the Post Carbon Institute, and one of the most widely read popularizers of peak-oil and resource-limits analysis for a general audience.

## Core ideas

- **Peak oil as the opening case.** *The Party's Over: Oil, War and the Fate of Industrial Societies* (2003) lays out the peak-oil thesis — that global conventional oil production would reach a maximum and then decline, with severe consequences for an industrial economy built on assumptions of ever-cheaper, ever-more-abundant liquid fuel.
- **[Peak everything](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/peak-everything.md).** *Peak Everything: Waking Up to the Century of Declines* (2007) generalizes the argument: not only oil but coal, key minerals, topsoil, fresh water, and eventually global population face near-term production or availability peaks, so that the twenty-first century is better understood as a century of declines across multiple resource dimensions at once rather than a single-commodity story.
- **Growth's structural incompatibility with a finite planet.** *The End of Growth* (2011) and *Afterburn* (2015) argue that declining net energy returns undermine the technological-substitution and efficiency-gain arguments typically offered against resource-limits concerns — that a shrinking energy surplus, not merely raw resource depletion, is what makes continued economic growth on the post-1950 pattern impossible to sustain.

## What the 561 Group reads them for

Heinberg's peak-everything framing generalizes the single-resource peak-oil case into the same declining-net-energy position the house holds in the [2025-12-25 oil-sanctions log](../../../../../../public-services-section/services/personal-services-section/emsenn-services-section/content/text/logs/personal-logs/2025/12/25/2025-12-25-emsenn-personal-log-about-latest-oil-sanctions.md), which names "declining energy return on energy invested, brittle supply chains, ecological limits" as the physical and thermodynamic pressures that financial and legal layers manage rather than resolve. Heinberg sits with [Nate Hagens](./nate-hagens.md) and [Joseph Tainter](./joseph-tainter.md) as one of the house's three central citations for its own [energy-return-on-investment](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/energy-return-on-investment.md) position, and against the more optimistic connectivity- and technology-substitution-driven readings represented by [Parag Khanna](./parag-khanna.md).
