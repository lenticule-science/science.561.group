Nate Silver (1978-) is an American statistician who moved from baseball forecasting (the PECOTA player-projection system) to political forecasting, founding FiveThirtyEight in 2008. His central methodological commitment, laid out in *The Signal and the Noise: Why So Many Predictions Fail — but Some Don't* (2012), is explicitly Bayesian: a forecast is a probability distribution over outcomes, continuously updated as new evidence arrives, rather than a single confident prediction of what will happen.

## Core ideas

- **[Signal and noise](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/forecasting-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/signal-and-noise.md).** In data-rich domains — weather, elections, sports, seismology, economics — genuine predictive signal is mixed with far more noise than intuition suggests, and much of forecasting is separating the two rather than finding more data.
- **Forecast versus prediction.** Silver insists on a sharp distinction between a probabilistic forecast (a distribution: "70% chance of X") and a deterministic prediction (a single confident claim: "X will happen"), and argues that collapsing the two — treating "70%" as equivalent to "yes" — is the most common form of public forecasting failure, including much of the criticism FiveThirtyEight's 2016 US presidential election model received for giving Donald Trump roughly a 29% chance of winning, a probability higher than most competing models gave him, that critics nonetheless treated as a wrong prediction once he won.
- **Track record.** FiveThirtyEight's state-by-state election models correctly called the outcome in 49 of 50 states in 2008 and all 50 in 2012; the model's 2016 performance is a frequently cited test case for the forecast/prediction distinction, since a 29% outcome occurring is not, on Silver's own framework, evidence the model was wrong.
- **Extending the frame.** *On the Edge: The Art of Risking Everything* (2024) extends the same probabilistic, risk-calibrated reasoning to poker, venture capital, and other high-variance domains where explicit expected-value and bankroll-management thinking (Kelly-adjacent reasoning) governs decision quality.

## What the 561 Group reads them for

Silver's forecast/prediction distinction and his insistence on scored, updating probability estimates is one of the two empirical pillars — alongside [Philip Tetlock](./philip-tetlock.md)'s calibration research — that the house's own [forecasting-services-section](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/forecasting-services-section/README.md) is built on: the section's pipeline explicitly records a probability estimate and a confidence level rather than a binary call, and scores it against resolution with the Brier score once the outcome is known, precisely to avoid the "70% treated as yes" failure Silver names. *On the Edge*'s attention to risk-of-ruin-aware sizing under genuine uncertainty also connects to the house's own [sizing-without-a-bank note](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/04/2026-09-04-note-on-sizing-without-a-bank.md), though the two reach that territory from different starting assumptions about who has a bank to be bailed out by.
