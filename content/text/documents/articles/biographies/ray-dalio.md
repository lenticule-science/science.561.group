Ray Dalio (1949-) is an American investor who founded Bridgewater Associates in 1975 and built it into one of the largest hedge funds by assets under management. His public writing distills Bridgewater's internal macroeconomic frameworks into two related cyclical accounts of how economies and world orders rise, overextend, and unwind.

## Core ideas

- **[The long-term debt cycle](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/long-term-debt-cycle.md).** *Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises* (2018) distinguishes the familiar short-term business cycle (roughly five to eight years, governed by central-bank interest-rate policy) from a much longer debt "supercycle" (roughly fifty to seventy-five years), over which an economy accumulates debt faster than incomes grow until debt-service burdens become unsustainable, ending in a deleveraging event. Dalio classifies deleveragings as "beautiful" (a balanced mix of austerity, debt restructuring, wealth transfer via taxation, and money printing that keeps nominal growth positive) or "ugly" (depression or hyperinflation, when the mix is unbalanced).
- **The changing world order.** *Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order* (2021) applies an analogous cyclical reading — the "Big Cycle" — to the rise and decline of reserve-currency empires (the Dutch and British empires, and now the United States, with China as a rising challenger), tracked through measurable indicators: internal wealth and values gaps, debt levels, relative education and innovation, military strength, trade share, and reserve-currency status.
- **Mechanical, indicator-driven forecasting.** Dalio's method emphasizes that these cycles are not merely narrative but are tracked against quantifiable historical indicators across many prior cases (the Dutch guilder, British pound, and reigning powers before them), intended to make the framework a falsifiable diagnostic rather than a purely qualitative story.

## What the 561 Group reads them for

Dalio's long-term debt cycle is the clearest institutional-finance statement of the pattern the house's [2025-12-25 oil-sanctions log](../../../../../../public-services-section/services/personal-services-section/emsenn-services-section/content/text/logs/personal-logs/2025/12/25/2025-12-25-emsenn-personal-log-about-latest-oil-sanctions.md) reads directly out of energy sanctions and export controls: that finance is what makes worsening material conditions governable, converting "real shortages and declining returns into legible, deferrable risks" that can be "carried on balance sheets instead of resolved at the level of production or consumption." Dalio's deleveraging types name, at the level of an entire economy, the same choice between distributing pain now versus deferring it into a larger later reckoning that the house's [sizing-without-a-bank note](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/04/2026-09-04-note-on-sizing-without-a-bank.md) analyzes at the level of an individual or pooled sleeve of capital. Dalio's "changing world order" is also read as a debt-and-institutions-centered account of the same multipolar disorder [Ian Bremmer](./ian-bremmer.md)'s G-Zero framework names from a political-science vantage.
