Angela Yvonne Davis (born 1944) is an American political activist, philosopher, and academic. A member of the Communist Party USA and associated with the Black Panther Party, Davis was the target of an FBI manhunt in 1970 and spent sixteen months in jail before her acquittal in 1972. Her scholarship and organizing have focused on the connections between racism, [capitalism](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/sociology-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/capitalism.md), and the carceral state.

## Core ideas

- **Prison [abolition](../../../../../services/philosophy-services-section/services/black-philosophy-services-section/services/abolition-services-section/content/text/glossary/topics/abolition.md)**: Davis argues that prisons do not reduce harm but reproduce it -- concentrating poverty, racism, and state violence into institutions that generate further damage. In *Are Prisons Obsolete?* (2003), she contends that prisons have become so normalized that alternatives appear unthinkable, and that this normalization is itself a political achievement of the carceral state. Her [abolitionist](../../../../../services/philosophy-services-section/services/black-philosophy-services-section/services/abolition-services-section/content/text/glossary/topics/abolition.md) work connects directly to [Ruth Wilson Gilmore](../../../../../services/information-science-services-section/services/knowledge-services-section/services/research-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/ruth-wilson-gilmore.md)'s analysis of organized abandonment and organized violence.
- **Intersectionality of race, class, and gender in carceral systems**: Davis's analysis insists that the prison system cannot be understood through race alone, class alone, or gender alone. Mass incarceration targets Black and brown communities, working-class people, and women in ways that compound and reinforce each other. The carceral state is a site where [racial capitalism](../../../../../services/philosophy-services-section/services/black-philosophy-services-section/services/black-radical-tradition-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/racial-capitalism.md) operates through gendered and classed mechanisms simultaneously.
- **Black feminist politics**: in *Women, Race & Class* (1981), Davis traced the history of American feminism and its repeated failures to address the conditions of Black women, arguing that a feminism unable to confront race and class reproduces the very hierarchies it claims to oppose.

Angela Y. Davis (b. 1944) is the figure through whom the [black-radical-tradition](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/sociology-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/social-sciences/sociology/archive/library/critical-theory/theories/black-radical-tradition/_index.md) holds together philosophical analysis, mass political organizing, and the abolitionist horizon. Trained in philosophy under Herbert Marcuse at Brandeis and in Frankfurt, she taught at UCLA, was prosecuted and acquitted in 1972 in a case that became an international cause, and helped found Critical Resistance with [Ruth Wilson Gilmore](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/sociology-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/undated/ruth-wilson-gilmore.md) and others in 1997. Distinguished Professor Emerita at UC Santa Cruz.

- **The prison-industrial complex.** Davis (with co-organizers in Critical Resistance) gave currency to the term as an analytical name for the integrated system of prisons, policing, surveillance, sentencing law, prison-construction industries, prison-labor extraction, and the political economy that produces prison expansion as a profitable response to social crises. The term refuses the framing of "the prison" as a neutral instrument of justice and reframes it as an industry with constituencies, beneficiaries, and growth dynamics.
- **Abolition democracy.** Reactivating Du Bois's term: abolition is not principally the destruction of carceral institutions but the construction of the social, economic, and political conditions that would make those institutions unnecessary — universal health care, education, housing, meaningful work, conflict-resolution practices that do not route through cages. Abolition is therefore a constructive political program, not a negative one.
- **Race, class, gender as a single analytical field.** *Women, Race & Class* (1981) is the foundational text for the argument that the analytical categories of race, class, and gender cannot be separated and then re-articulated; they are produced together and must be analyzed together. The book is one of the primary sources for what later became known as intersectional analysis, though Davis's framing emphasizes the political-economic constitution of the categories rather than their lived experience.
- **The continuity of slavery and the prison.** Davis reads the Thirteenth Amendment's exception clause ("except as a punishment for crime") as the legal architecture that allowed slavery to be reorganized through criminalization rather than abolished. The convict-lease system, chain gangs, the contemporary prison: each is a historical phase of a continuous structure. This reading is the proximate source for the subdomain's treatment of mass incarceration as racial capitalism's spatial fix.
