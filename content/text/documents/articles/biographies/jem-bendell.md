Jem Bendell is a professor of sustainability leadership at the University of Cumbria, UK, whose 2018 occasional paper made "deep adaptation" a widely used term in climate-collapse discourse, together with a documented and unusually visible peer-review controversy.

## Core ideas

- **[Deep adaptation](../../../../../services/history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/deep-adaptation.md).** "Deep Adaptation: A Map for Navigating Climate Tragedy" (IFLAS occasional paper, July 2018) argues that near-term societal collapse due to climate change is likely, or in Bendell's phrasing probable to inevitable, within the lifetime of much of the current population, given known climate feedback loops and the inadequacy of policy response to date. Bendell frames the appropriate response around four R's: resilience (what social and physical structures to keep), relinquishment (what to consciously let go of before it is lost involuntarily), restoration (what past practices or attitudes to bring back), and reconciliation (making peace with mortality, uncertainty, and each other in that context).
- **The peer-review dispute.** Bendell's own account states the paper was submitted to a peer-reviewed journal (*Sustainability Accounting, Management and Policy Journal*) and returned for revision rather than accepted; rather than revise and resubmit, Bendell self-published it as an IFLAS occasional paper, after which it circulated widely and produced the large "Deep Adaptation Forum" community.
- **Scientific criticism and reception.** The paper drew sharp criticism from climate researchers, most visibly Thomas Nicholas, Galen Hall, and Colleen Schmidt's 2020 openDemocracy piece "The faulty science, doomism, and flawed conclusions of Deep Adaptation," which challenged specific claims (including a since-revised claim about near-term Arctic methane release) and argued the near-term-inevitable-collapse framing was not supported by the cited climate science and risked promoting fatalism. Bendell issued a revised version of the paper responding to some of the criticized specifics while maintaining the overall thesis.

## What the 561 Group reads them for

Bendell's deep-adaptation framework is read by the house as the clearest public statement of what it means to plan for a rising, rather than fixed, absorbing barrier: the house's own [sizing-without-a-bank note](../../../../../services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/04/2026-09-04-note-on-sizing-without-a-bank.md) states directly that "climate breakdown raises the water" and that "a sizing rule that reads a fixed barrier is wrong in the direction that kills" — the same structural point Bendell's resilience/relinquishment/restoration/reconciliation framework is built to answer at the level of a life or a community rather than a financial sleeve. The house takes the scientific criticism of Bendell's specific claims seriously rather than treating his near-term-collapse framing as settled, consistent with its general practice of reading contested forecasts as claims to weigh rather than facts to inherit — the same discipline applied to [Peter Zeihan](./peter-zeihan.md) and [Peter Turchin](./peter-turchin.md) elsewhere in this set.
