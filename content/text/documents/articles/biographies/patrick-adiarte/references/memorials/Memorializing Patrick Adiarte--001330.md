[[Patrick Adiarte]] died this week. I read it in passing and just sort of sat with it for a while. He was Ho-Jon on [[MASH]], which is a show I’ve been dipping back into lately—just picking through episodes, not in order. I’d seen a couple of the early ones recently, the ones where he’s still around.

It’s easy to forget how early _M*A*S*H_ changes. Characters appear, vanish, actors swap, tones shift. Ho-Jon gets a little mini arc in season one—he’s working around the camp, there’s a whole thing about getting him into college in the States, which kind of resolves, kind of doesn’t. Then he comes back later as a medic. Then he’s gone. I don’t think they ever explain it.

He wasn’t a main character, but he had a presence. Not flashy, not tragic, just... steady. Helpful. Always in motion. I liked the way Adiarte played him—there was a calmness that didn’t feel forced. He looked like someone who’d already adapted three times before the episode started.

When I saw the obituary, it mentioned _The King and I_, and I realized I’d completely forgotten he played Prince Chulalongkorn. It makes sense—he’s so young in that movie, and the whole production is so stylized, it’s easy to lose track of who’s who unless you’re looking closely.

I had already been thinking about _The King and I_ lately, weirdly. Not because of him—just because the story had started floating back into my head while thinking about how things are shifting internationally right now. The way power’s decentralizing again. Not a Cold War-style two-side thing, but this looser multipolar situation—China, the U.S., India, the Gulf, Russia, other configurations forming and dissolving all the time.

_The King and I_ sits in that kind of space, in a way. A Western teacher is brought in to help “modernize” Siam so it can hold off colonial interference. There’s this constant tension between adopting external systems and maintaining internal authority. The king wants to modernize without ceding anything. Anna wants to help, but she also brings all her assumptions with her.

I started rewatching parts of it. The spectacle is still there—everything framed like a stage, big gestures, stylized choreography. But then there’s the Prince. Chulalongkorn. Adiarte plays him like someone watching more than speaking. Not naive, not resistant—just alert.

There’s a moment near the beginning where he’s questioning what Anna teaches, trying to square her explanations with what he’s been told his whole life. He doesn’t push back loudly, but he clearly isn’t just absorbing everything at face value either. It’s already a negotiation.

By the end of the film, when the King dies, Chulalongkorn takes over. It happens quietly. There’s a room full of people, and he just steps in and starts making changes. He gets rid of the bowing rituals. He speaks evenly. There’s no swell of music under him. Just him deciding.

That performance holds up. Adiarte doesn’t play it big. He doesn’t try to telegraph anything. He just shifts into the new role like he’d been preparing the whole time. Which, watching it again, you realize he kind of was. Almost every scene with him is about watching, learning, keeping track.

I don’t know if it read that way when I first saw it. I think as a kid I mostly tuned into the songs and the bright colors. But this time around, it felt different. Adiarte’s performance feels like something that stayed low to the ground on purpose.

I’d forgotten how brief his career was. A few standout roles, then not much. It makes sense, given the time. There weren’t many parts being written for young Asian actors in 1950s and 60s Hollywood, and the ones that existed were mostly built around stereotypes. He got slotted into the “Asian student” or “foreign helper” roles. Sometimes with dignity, sometimes not. It faded quickly.

But there’s something about Chulalongkorn as a character that still works, especially now. A kid raised in the middle of contradiction, trying to hold tradition and change in the same frame. Figuring out how to inherit something without becoming what it already was.

And something about the way Adiarte carried that, especially in the last ten minutes of the movie. You can feel the calculation, but it’s not cold. It’s just... ready.

I didn’t expect his death to hit any particular note for me. I hadn’t thought about him in years. But now I can’t stop thinking about that last scene in _The King and I_. Him standing up straighter, speaking clearly, already thinking ahead of everyone in the room.

That’s all I’ve got.
