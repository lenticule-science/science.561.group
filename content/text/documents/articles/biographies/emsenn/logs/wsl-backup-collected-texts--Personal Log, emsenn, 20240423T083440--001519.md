Its something I've been trying to give some thought to, as I try and think about how to [[synthesizing|synthesize]] the ideas of [[regenerative horticulture]], [[degrowing]], [[sustainable resouce management]], [[Lakota insurrectionary anarcho-nihilism]], and the [[Pursuit Of Black Liberation and Indigenous Sovereignty]], through our [[praxis]]. And that means looking at [[Acer negundo|box elder]], a tree that's abundant along [[Phahin Sinte Wakpala]], and seeing what different relationships we can form with it. The people I asked about box elder, they view it as substandard [[wood stove fuel]], compared to the [[Ash tree]] that is found across a range of hills starting some 1000 meters north.

I've only been able to work with it some limited amount, given we were only on the land for a couple weeks before the recent cascade of emergencies broughtus to a town. But in that time, I first learned that the [[Acer negundo#Greenwood|greenwood]] can make a passable [[hatchet handle]], and from there was able to learn that there are a variety of [[tree fungus infection|fungal infections]] that is affecting every tree we examined.

Currently, the land around the creek has a significant accumulation of smaller branches that were cut off of larger branches, which were themselves used as firewood. This wood is piled up among the grass that's died back and dried, and this holds moisture in and keeps air circulation out, and that's the perfect space to keep a healthy population of all sorts of [[plant pathogen|plant pathogens]] thriving and ready to infect any new growth. And because there's such an abundance of infection, new growth dies back and falls down on its own in a couple years, keeping the trees locked in a cycle of illness.

We've been looking at how to change things so that this cycle can end, and new things can begin. There's many facets to this.

The accumulated wood is of a mix of qualities. Some was healthy and green when it was left: that stuff has turned into good dry wood, that burns relatively hot and smokeless. Some of it was part of rotten branches, so the wood has become almost cork light, its lignin has been so degraded by fungal activity. This stuff burns a bit smokey, to be expected, but made exceptionally light charcoals when we tried that. We've been making biochar out of different qualities of box elder wood, and are going to test how quickly the different products soak up water solutions, and how quickly they release what they pulled from the solution, but our intuition says those biochar chunks made from the rotten and dried box elder will work to fix nutrients quicker than average.

Which might be a way to move some of the land toward being suitable for gardening. Folk here say the soil is bad for growing stuff, and I understand how that's true. But from our perspective, it's bad in the best way: it has too much available nutrition for most plants, and they end up sick. There's other problems with the soil that the addition of biochar can help with. The soil is mostly silt and clay, both of which are very fine particles, both compacting and eroding with ease. Adding biochar - and other larger particles, like the waste left over making biochar and compost - will help give the soil some resistance to both those issues.
