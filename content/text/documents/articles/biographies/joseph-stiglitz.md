Joseph Stiglitz is an American economist at Columbia University who shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with George Akerlof and Michael Spence for their analyses of markets in which one side of a transaction knows something the other does not. Across a wide body of work in public economics, development, and macroeconomics, the piece of his contribution most directly relevant to group lending is his analysis of how lenders behave when they cannot fully observe a borrower's risk or effort.

## Core ideas

- **Credit rationing under asymmetric information.** With Andrew Weiss, Stiglitz showed that a lender facing borrowers of unknown risk may respond to excess demand for loans not by raising the interest rate to clear the market, but by rationing credit at a lower rate — because raising the rate further would drive away safer borrowers and induce riskier behavior from those who remain, lowering the lender's expected return rather than raising it.
- **Peer monitoring as a substitute for what the lender cannot observe.** In "Peer Monitoring and Credit Markets," Stiglitz argued that where a lender cannot cheaply monitor a borrower's effort or verify a borrower's true circumstances, a borrower's peers often can — and lending structured to make use of that peer information and peer pressure can reach lower default rates than the lender could achieve monitoring alone. This is the theoretical basis usually cited for why joint-liability group lending, as practiced by microfinance institutions, can work where individual lending to the same borrowers would not.
- **Screening and information asymmetry more broadly.** Across his work on insurance, labor, and financial markets, Stiglitz's recurring theme is that markets in which participants hold different information do not clear the way textbook competitive markets do, and that institutions — screening, signaling, rationing, monitoring — arise specifically to manage the resulting gap.

## Key works

- "Credit Rationing in Markets with Imperfect Information" (with Andrew Weiss, *American Economic Review*, 1981)
- "Peer Monitoring and Credit Markets" (*The World Bank Economic Review*, 1990)
