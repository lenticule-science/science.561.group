Henry Hansmann is an American legal scholar at Yale Law School whose work asks a question most economic theory takes for granted: why does a given enterprise end up owned by one class of patron — its investors, its customers, its suppliers, or its workers — rather than another? His answer, developed across several decades of work on corporate and nonprofit law, is that the pattern of ownership across the economy is not arbitrary but tracks which class of patron faces the lowest costs from owning the firm.

## Core ideas

- **The cost-of-ownership theory.** Ownership of a firm is assigned, in practice, to whichever class of patrons — investors, customers, suppliers, or workers — has the lowest combined costs of market contracting (dealing with the firm at arm's length, as a non-owner) and costs of ownership (monitoring the firm, making its decisions, and bearing its risk, as an owner). Different enterprises end up with different owners because the balance of these costs differs across industries and situations.
- **Why nonprofit enterprise exists.** In "The Role of Nonprofit Enterprise," Hansmann argued that nonprofit organizations arise where the ordinary market discipline of ownership breaks down — where customers cannot easily verify what they are buying — and the nondistribution constraint substitutes for that missing discipline by removing the owners' incentive to exploit the information gap.
- **Cooperative ownership as a cost-minimizing form.** On this theory, worker, consumer, and producer cooperatives are not ideological alternatives to investor-owned firms but a different, sometimes better, solution to the same problem: assigning ownership to the patrons for whom the costs of ownership are lowest, which in agricultural marketing, mutual insurance, and other settings can be the firm's suppliers or customers rather than its outside investors.
- **Comparative corporate law.** With Reinier Kraakman and other co-authors, Hansmann's *The Anatomy of Corporate Law* set out a functional framework for comparing corporate law across jurisdictions by the problems it solves — agency costs between shareholders and managers, between majority and minority shareholders, and between the firm and outside parties — rather than by each jurisdiction's own doctrinal categories.

## Key works

- "The Role of Nonprofit Enterprise" (*Yale Law Journal*, 1980)
- *The Ownership of Enterprise* (1996)
- *The Anatomy of Corporate Law: A Comparative and Functional Approach* (with Reinier Kraakman and others)
