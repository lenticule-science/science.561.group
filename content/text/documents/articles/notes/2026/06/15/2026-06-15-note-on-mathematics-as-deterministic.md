## Determinacy, Reproducibility, and the Boundary of the Universal: Why Mathematics Is Not House Style and Software Must Not Be Either

**Abstract.** Mathematics commands assent not by authority but because its results are determinate and reproducible: the same derivation, executed by any competent agent in any place or time, yields the same conclusion. Computation, properly construed, possesses the identical property, and from it derives both its theoretical coherence and its practical value. This essay argues that two common engineering pathologies are dual violations of this single principle. The first elevates a contingent local convention — "house style" — to the status of theory, illegitimately borrowing the authority of the necessary for the merely agreed. The second forfeits the universality that is computing's birthright by producing software that runs only in the environment of its authorship. Both confuse the local with the universal; the first by promoting convention to law, the second by failing to make a lawful capability actually portable.

### 1. The shared property

What distinguishes a theorem from an opinion is not the eminence of whoever states it but that its truth does not depend on who states it. A proof is a finite object whose validity can be checked by following its steps; the checking is, in principle, mechanical, and its outcome is invariant across checkers (Hilbert, 1925; Frege, 1884). This invariance — independence from the particular knower, the particular occasion, the particular instrument — is the source of mathematics' peculiar authority. It is also, exactly, the definition of reproducibility, and it is the property that the philosophy of science identifies as the criterion of objectivity itself: a claim is objective insofar as it is intersubjectively testable, reproducible by others who did not produce it (Popper, 1959). Determinacy and reproducibility are therefore not incidental virtues of mathematics; they are what makes it mathematics rather than rhetoric.

### 2. Why mathematics is not house style

The decisive contribution of the foundational period was to expel psychologism from logic and arithmetic: the laws of number are not generalizations about how human minds happen to operate, and a proof is not persuasive because it convinces a particular audience (Frege, 1884; cf. Husserl's parallel critique). A truth of arithmetic holds with necessity — it could not have been otherwise — and it holds everywhere. This modal character is precisely what a convention lacks. Lewis (1969) analyzed a convention as an arbitrary but self-perpetuating solution to a coordination problem: driving on the right is a convention because driving on the left would have served equally well, and its authority is nothing but the mutual expectation that others will conform. A convention is contingent (it could have been otherwise), local (it binds only where the agreement obtains), and groundless in the specific sense that it requires no justification beyond coordination.

To treat a house style — an indentation rule, a directory layout, a preferred import path — as though it were a theorem is therefore a category mistake in Ryle's (1949) sense: it assigns to an object of one logical type the properties of another. The house rule is contingent and local; the theorem is necessary and universal (Kripke, 1980). Dressing the former in the vocabulary of the latter — "law," "proof," "must" justified by derivation rather than by fiat — is an attempt to borrow the unearned authority of the necessary for the merely agreed. The error is not that conventions are worthless; coordination is indispensable, and a stated convention is a public good. The error is the misattribution of modal status. A convention that pretends to be a theorem is a counterfeit, and the counterfeit is detectable by the same test that certifies real mathematics: a theorem reproduces in every world, whereas the house rule reproduces only in the one repository where the local agreement happens to hold. What fails to reproduce outside its origin is, by §1's criterion, not theory at all.

### 3. Why computation shares mathematics' property

The reason this matters for software is that computation, correctly understood, is built on the same invariance. The Church–Turing thesis asserts that the class of effectively computable functions is independent of the model of computation chosen to define it: Turing machines, the lambda calculus, and recursive functions delimit the same class (Turing, 1936; Church, 1936). Computability is thus a machine-independent notion; what a program means, as a function from inputs to outputs, does not depend on the hardware that runs it. A deterministic algorithm, given the same input, yields the same output regardless of where it executes — the computational instance of reproducibility — and the discipline of referential transparency makes this determinacy a design property of programs themselves: an expression may be replaced by its value without changing the computation (Strachey, 2000). The high-level programming language was invented to lift programs off the particular machine so that the same source would compute the same function on different hardware (Backus, 1978), and the entire edifice of standards — the C language standard, the POSIX operating-system interface — exists to guarantee that a conforming program behaves identically across conforming implementations (ISO/IEC 9899; IEEE Std 1003.1). Computing inherits mathematics' authority precisely to the degree that it preserves mathematics' reproducibility.

### 4. Why non-portable software is the dual error

If the first pathology mistakes the local for the universal by inflation — promoting a convention to a law — the second commits the mirror error by deflation: it takes a capability that could be universal and confines it to the locale of its making. Software that runs only on the machine on which it was written, that presupposes the author's directory layout, ambient tools, or unpackaged neighboring modules, has surrendered the machine-independence that §3 identifies as computing's defining strength. The colloquial confession "it works on my machine" is the exact admission that the artifact is not reproducible — that its behavior depends on the unstated particulars of one environment rather than on its inputs alone. By the criterion of §1, such an artifact stands to genuine software as a proof only its author can verify stands to a proof: which is to say, it is not the thing it claims to be.

The engineering tradition has named and attacked this defect for decades. The motivation for portable languages was to defeat it (Backus, 1978); "write once, run anywhere" was its marketing slogan (Gosling, 1995); the twelve-factor methodology codified the requirements — explicit and isolated dependencies, configuration drawn from the environment rather than baked into the artifact, parity between development and production — that make an application deployable anywhere (Wiggins, 2011); containerization arose to package an application with its dependencies so that the deployment target need not replicate the author's host (Merkel, 2014); and the reproducible-builds and hermetic-build programs pursue the strongest form of the property, that the same source yield bit-for-bit identical artifacts independent of the build host, time, or path (Lamb & Zacchiroli, 2021). Each is an instrument for restoring to software the reproducibility that careless construction strips away. The common prescription is that a deployable unit must be *self-contained*: it must carry what it needs and depend only on a documented, standardized interface to its host, never on the incidental arrangement of the workshop in which it was assembled.

This is the precise sense in which a shipped program that imports a shared, in-repository helper module is defective. The dependency is not on a standardized, independently resolvable interface but on the contingent topology of one workspace. The artifact thereby ceases to be a function of its declared inputs and becomes a function of its birthplace; it cannot be lifted out and run elsewhere without reconstructing the workshop around it. That is the deflationary form of the same confusion diagnosed in §2: a unit that ought to be universal has been welded to the local. The remedy is not a matter of taste but of principle — bundle or vendor what the unit needs, depend only on published interfaces, and make each shipped thing deployable on any conforming system — because only a self-contained, standard-conformant artifact reproduces, and only what reproduces deserves to be called engineering rather than handicraft.

### 5. The unifying diagnosis

The two pathologies are one error viewed from opposite sides. Both arise from a failure to police the boundary between the contingent-local and the necessary-universal. Encoding house style as theory drags a convention across that boundary in the inflating direction, claiming for an arbitrary local agreement the world-spanning necessity that belongs only to what can be proved. Shipping environment-bound software fails to carry a capability across that boundary in the deflating direction, leaving in one locale a function that, by the Church–Turing invariance, was entitled to run anywhere. The disciplining principle in both cases is determinacy and reproducibility: a claim that reproduces in every competent hand is theory and may speak with necessity; a practice that reproduces only where a group has agreed is convention and must announce itself as such; and an artifact that reproduces only where it was born is broken and must be made to reproduce everywhere a standard interface exists.

This yields two corollaries with the force of method rather than preference. First, state conventions as conventions and theorems as theorems, and never derive the authority of the former from the rhetoric of the latter; a convention is justified by the coordination it secures, not by a proof, and presenting it otherwise corrupts the reader's ability to tell what is binding by necessity from what is binding by agreement. Second, make every deployable unit self-sufficient and standard-conformant, so that its behavior is a function of its inputs and a documented host interface, never of the machine that happened to assemble it. The first keeps the universal uncontaminated by the local; the second keeps the local from imprisoning the universal.

### 6. Conclusion

Mathematics is authoritative because it is determinate and reproducible: its truths do not depend on who derives them or where. Computing, when faithful to its foundations, is the same kind of thing — machine-independent in theory and, when built with discipline, deployment-independent in practice. The two errors under examination both betray this inheritance. To encode house style as theory is to counterfeit necessity from convention; to write software that runs only at home is to squander a universality that was already paid for by the very nature of computation. The cure for both is the recognition that gives mathematics its power and software its worth: only what reproduces beyond its origin is real, and the rest is local habit wearing the costume of law.
