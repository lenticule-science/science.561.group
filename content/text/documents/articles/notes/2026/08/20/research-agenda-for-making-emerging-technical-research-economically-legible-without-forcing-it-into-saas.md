# Research Agenda for Making Emerging Technical Research Economically Legible Without Forcing It Into SaaS

This agenda responds to the pasted brief: an emerging body of theory, software, public infrastructure, public writing, packages, prototypes, and agentic systems whose possible economic forms include B2B software, consulting, infrastructure, research services, editorial technology, agentic workflow tooling, standards work, or a hybrid.

---

# Executive thesis

The central problem is **economic legibility**: how an emerging technical/research project becomes understandable to institutions as something that solves a recognized problem, fits a budget, can be evaluated safely, and can be bought, funded, adopted, maintained, or partnered with.

This is **not mainly a branding problem**. Branding can clarify, but it cannot substitute for knowing who suffers the problem, who owns the budget, who bears the risk, what “success” means in operational terms, and what proof a buyer would need before committing. Steve Blank’s useful definition of a startup as an organization searching for a repeatable and scalable business model is relevant here, but the search may yield something other than a venture-backed software company: a research studio, standards lab, consultancy, grant-funded infrastructure project, open-source support model, editorial technology practice, or hybrid institution. See: [Steve Blank, “What’s a Startup? First Principles”](https://steveblank.com/2010/01/25/whats-a-startup-first-principles/).

The deeper research question is: **What institutional job is this work hired to do?** Until that is answered, “AI tooling,” “agentic editorial infrastructure,” “provenance systems,” “knowledge workflows,” “research operations,” and “public-interest infrastructure” are hypotheses, not categories. Gartner’s B2B buying work emphasizes that modern buying is nonlinear and involves problem identification, solution exploration, requirements building, and supplier selection, often across buying teams rather than a single decision-maker. See: [Gartner, “The B2B Buying Journey”](https://www.gartner.com/en/sales/insights/b2b-buying-journey). That matters here because an intellectually rich project may be admired by many people while being buyable by almost nobody.

The agenda should therefore proceed as **field research plus artifact production**: buyer interviews, language collection, workflow observation, prototype pilots, proof-of-value benchmarks, and pricing tests. Public writing can create credibility, but it only becomes pipeline when it connects to a specific institutional problem and an accountable next step.

---

# What the current situation already gets right

The current position has several real strengths.

First, it starts from **research and technical depth**, not a thin product wrapper. That can become a trust signal in markets where buyers care about epistemic accountability, provenance, governance, editorial judgment, technical reliability, or infrastructure durability.

Second, it already has multiple possible proof objects: public writing, packages, prototypes, agentic systems, and domain-specific theory. In B2B, proof objects matter because buyers often cannot evaluate abstract claims; they need artifacts that let technical evaluators, champions, and budget owners see what exists.

Third, the work is not yet overcommitted to a single commercial form. That is good. Many early technical projects prematurely declare themselves “SaaS,” then discover that the first real revenue is services, integration, support, audits, training, sponsored open-source development, or grants.

Fourth, the project appears to have **domain specificity** around editorial, provenance, knowledge, accountability, public-interest, and agentic workflow questions. Domain specificity is valuable because generic AI productivity tooling is crowded, while narrowly understood institutional pain can support trust, differentiation, and pricing.

Fifth, the public/research orientation can become a credibility base, especially for public-interest organizations, newsrooms, foundations, standards bodies, universities, and governance teams. But this only works if public work remains substantively useful rather than becoming a funnel-shaped performance.

# What the current situation under-theorizes

The major gaps are not aesthetic. They are commercial, organizational, and operational.

The work currently under-theorizes:

**Buyer identity.** “Newsrooms,” “research institutes,” “AI governance teams,” and “foundations” are not buyers. They are environments containing users, champions, blockers, legal reviewers, budget holders, procurement staff, and executives.

**Budget source.** A problem is not economically real until someone can name the budget line: editorial technology, innovation, compliance, AI governance, research operations, knowledge management, security, digital transformation, grant funding, communications, product, or consulting.

**Pain severity and urgency.** A buyer may agree that provenance, accountability, or agentic workflow design matters, while still ranking it below revenue, audience growth, compliance deadlines, security incidents, staffing shortages, or donor requirements.

**Switching costs.** Even bad spreadsheets, CMS hacks, Google Docs, Notion, Airtable, custom scripts, and human labor persist because they are embedded in routines. A new system must defeat not only competitors but inertia.

**Procurement friction.** Any software touching sensitive data, publication workflows, governance evidence, legal claims, or institutional memory will trigger questions about security, privacy, data retention, liability, uptime, access control, support, and vendor survival. CISA’s software acquisition guidance and NIST’s supply-chain risk work are reminders that modern software buying often treats vendors as risk surfaces, not just suppliers. See: [CISA, Software Acquisition Guide](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/software-acquisition-guide-government-enterprise-consumers-software-assurance-cyber-supply-chain).

**Proof of value.** “This is intellectually important” is not enough. Buyers need proof framed in their terms: time saved, risk reduced, evidence preserved, editorial corrections avoided, review cycles shortened, compliance burden reduced, institutional memory improved, or new work made possible.

**Delivery model.** The work could be software, service, research, training, standards contribution, audit, implementation, or infrastructure. Each implies different margins, support burdens, liability, pricing, and founder time.

**Category confusion.** A new category can create strategic advantage, but only if buyers recognize the problem as urgent. April Dunford’s positioning work is useful here: positioning is about showing how a product leads at delivering something a defined customer cares about, not inventing impressive language. See: [April Dunford, “A Quickstart Guide to Positioning”](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning).

**Trust and liability.** Agentic systems can create real operational risk: hallucinated outputs, unclear provenance, bad automation, privacy leaks, editorial mistakes, biased summarization, broken audit trails, and accountability gaps. NIST’s AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001 show that organizations increasingly need governance processes for AI risks, not just tools. See: [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework).

# Core B2B concepts we need to understand

## ICP / ideal customer profile

An **ICP** is a description of the organization most likely to have the problem, budget, urgency, trust fit, implementation capacity, and willingness to buy. It is not “anyone who could use this.” A good ICP might specify: “mid-sized investigative nonprofit newsrooms with recurring document-heavy investigations, limited engineering support, explicit AI-use policies, and foundation funding for editorial infrastructure.”

## Buyer, user, champion, economic buyer

The **user** touches the tool or service. The **champion** wants it to happen internally and helps navigate the organization. The **economic buyer** controls or can unlock budget. The **technical buyer/evaluator** assesses feasibility, security, architecture, integration, and maintainability. The **procurement/legal/security reviewers** can delay or block. MEDDIC-type qualification frameworks emphasize identifying metrics, economic buyer, decision criteria, decision process, pain, and champion for complex deals. See: [MEDDICC, MEDDPICC Sales Methodology](https://meddicc.com/meddpicc-sales-methodology-and-process).

## Painkiller versus vitamin

A **painkiller** solves an urgent, expensive, risky, or politically visible problem. A **vitamin** is nice, admirable, or strategically interesting but easy to defer. The research task is not to declare the work one or the other, but to discover where it is painful enough to buy.

## Category, positioning, and differentiation

A **category** is the market shelf buyers already understand or can be taught to understand. **Positioning** explains what the work is, who it is for, what alternatives it replaces, and why it is uniquely valuable. **Differentiation** is not “we are more thoughtful”; it is a buyer-relevant contrast against substitutes. Dunford’s warning is especially relevant: competitors include the alternatives buyers would use if your product did not exist, not only similar-looking companies. See: [April Dunford, “Positioning and Competition”](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/positioning-and-competition).

## Pipeline, funnel, demand generation, lead generation, sales motion

A **pipeline** is the set of active opportunities moving from initial relationship to possible revenue. A **funnel** is an analytic model of stages and conversion. **Demand generation** creates interest and trust around a problem. **Lead generation** identifies possible buyers. A **sales motion** is the pattern by which conversations become purchases: self-serve, founder-led, enterprise sales, partnerships, referrals, or service-led.

## Inbound, outbound, referrals, partnerships, founder-led sales

**Inbound** means people come to you through public artifacts, reputation, search, community, events, open source, or referrals. **Outbound** means you initiate contact. **Referrals** use trusted intermediaries. **Partnerships** reach buyers through organizations they already trust. **Founder-led sales** means the builders themselves learn the market by selling before delegating. Gartner’s recent buyer survey found that many B2B buyers prefer independent digital research and that irrelevant outreach causes active avoidance, which is a strong warning against spammy agentic outbound. See: [Gartner, 2025 B2B buyer survey press release](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-sales-survey-finds-61-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-buying-experience).

## Product-led, sales-led, service-led growth

**Product-led growth** means the product itself drives acquisition, retention, and expansion; OpenView defines PLG as making the product the primary driver of acquisition, retention, and expansion. See: [OpenView, Product-Led Growth](https://openviewpartners.com/product-led-growth/). **Sales-led growth** uses human sales to diagnose needs, build consensus, navigate procurement, and close. **Service-led growth** begins with consulting, audits, workshops, or implementation, then may produce repeatable software or infrastructure.

## Enterprise procurement and risk review

Procurement is not just purchasing. It is risk management. Buyers may ask: Who owns the data? Is there SOC 2 or ISO 27001? What happens if the vendor disappears? What is the SLA? Are outputs auditable? Can the system be disabled? Is there a DPA? Does it touch regulated or confidential material? CISA’s software acquisition work and NIST supply-chain guidance frame software acquisition as risk-informed decision-making. See: [CISA, Software Acquisition Guide](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/software-acquisition-guide-government-enterprise-consumers-software-assurance-cyber-supply-chain).

## Proof of concept, pilot, paid pilot, expansion

A **POC** tests whether something can work. A **pilot** tests whether it works in a real environment. A **paid pilot** tests value and commitment while compensating implementation labor. A good pilot has success criteria, scope boundaries, data constraints, a named buyer-side owner, and a path to either expansion or a clean stop. Practitioner guidance commonly distinguishes a POC proving “could work” from a pilot proving “does work.” See: [Amplify Partners, “The Technical Founder’s Guide to Pilots and POCs”](https://amplifypartners.com/blog-posts/the-technical-founders-guide-to-pilots-and-pocs).

## ACV, LTV, CAC, churn, sales cycle, payback period

**ACV** is annual contract value. **LTV** is lifetime value. **CAC** is customer acquisition cost. **Churn** is loss of customers or revenue. **Sales cycle** is time from first qualified opportunity to close. **Payback period** is how long gross profit takes to recover CAC; Stripe’s SaaS metrics guide and similar SaaS finance resources treat acquisition, retention, growth, and unit economics as core operating measures. See: [Stripe, Essential SaaS Metrics](https://stripe.com/resources/more/essential-saas-metrics).

## Services, consulting, SaaS, open core, agency, licensing, retainers, hybrid models

**Services/consulting** sell expertise and delivery. **SaaS** sells hosted software access. **Open core** offers an open-source core plus paid proprietary/source-available features; GitLab explicitly describes itself as open core with revenue from paid tiers. See: [GitLab Handbook, Stewardship](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/company/stewardship/). **Licensing** sells rights to use IP. **Retainers** sell ongoing availability/capacity. **Agency models** sell repeatable client work. **Hybrid models** combine public research, open source, services, grants, and commercial implementations.

# Pipeline research

A pipeline for this work should begin with **relationship research**, not lead scraping. Modern B2B buyers do substantial independent research, prefer hybrid/self-directed experiences, and often dislike irrelevant outreach; McKinsey’s B2B work emphasizes omnichannel buying, while Gartner’s 2025–2026 buyer data highlights rep-free preferences and avoidance of irrelevant supplier outreach. See: [McKinsey, B2B growth and omnichannel buying](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/five-fundamental-truths-how-b2b-winners-keep-growing).

## How to study pipeline

1. **Identify leads through problem evidence, not demographics.** Look for organizations publishing about AI policy, provenance, editorial workflow, knowledge management, document investigations, auditability, or governance burden.

2. **Separate audience from pipeline.** Audience reads. Community participates. Leads match a buyer hypothesis. Prospects have a problem and possible budget. Customers pay. Funders underwrite mission or infrastructure. Patrons support the work without being operational users.

3. **Use public artifacts as relationship openers.** A useful memo, benchmark, teardown, field guide, or open-source package is better than a pitch. The ask should be: “Is this problem real in your environment?” not “Do you want a demo?”

4. **Design discovery conversations.** Use consultative questions: current workflow, pain, cost of inaction, decision process, budget source, prior attempts, risk constraints, and what proof would matter.

5. **Qualify opportunities.** Do not proceed unless there is a named problem, identifiable owner, plausible budget, urgency, success criteria, and next step.

6. **Track relationships.** A CRM should record people, roles, institutions, problem hypotheses, trust history, consent boundaries, artifacts shared, and next useful action.

7. **Move from conversation to pilot.** Only propose a pilot when there is a real workflow, scoped data, success criteria, buyer-side owner, and a decision path.

8. **Move from pilot to paid work.** A pilot should end with one of four outcomes: expand, convert, pause with findings, or disqualify.

9. **Preserve trust after no.** Send useful notes, do not pressure, keep consent, and document why it was not a fit.

# Offer design

The first offer should probably **not** be finished software. It should be a paid learning instrument that produces value for the buyer and market knowledge for the builders.

| Offer | What buyer gets | Buyer | Pain addressed | How it proves value | Rough relative price | Delivery risks | Do not promise |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paid research memo | Tailored analysis of workflow/problem/category. | Foundations, research institutes, governance teams. | Unclear strategy or emerging risk. | Decision-useful memo. | Low to mid five figures. | Too abstract. | Implementation outcomes. |
| Technical audit | Review of provenance, AI workflow, docs, data/claims pipeline. | Newsrooms, nonprofits, AI governance teams. | Unknown risks and weak evidence trails. | Findings + prioritized remediation. | Low to mid five figures. | Access/data sensitivity. | Legal compliance guarantee. |
| Workflow analysis | Map current process and bottlenecks. | Media, research ops, KM teams. | Slow, fragile, undocumented workflows. | Before/after workflow map. | Low five figures. | Stakeholder availability. | Automation without process change. |
| Prototype integration | Working prototype in one workflow. | Technical teams, newsrooms, labs. | Need to see feasibility. | Demo on real data. | Mid five to low six figures. | Scope creep. | Production readiness. |
| Workshop | Shared language, training, problem framing. | Foundations, newsrooms, nonprofits. | Confusion around AI/provenance/workflow. | Outputs: map, risks, next steps. | Low five figures. | One-off inspiration. | Organizational transformation. |
| Implementation sprint | Time-boxed build/configuration. | Teams with clear workflow. | Need practical deployment. | Shipping artifact. | Mid five to low six figures. | Hidden integration complexity. | Long-term support unless contracted. |
| Standards consultation | Input on provenance/accountability/spec design. | Standards bodies, civic tech, foundations. | Need technical/field expertise. | Recommendations/reference implementation. | Low to mid five figures. | Politics of standards. | Consensus or adoption. |
| Data/provenance model design | Schema, trace model, metadata architecture. | Newsrooms, research orgs, governance teams. | Weak evidence lineage. | Usable model and examples. | Mid five figures. | Overengineering. | Universal standard. |
| Agentic workflow design | Human-in-loop agent workflow with controls. | Ops, editorial, research teams. | Manual repetitive work with accountability needs. | Controlled prototype + risk register. | Mid five figures. | Automation risk. | Autonomous correctness. |
| Documentation/editorial infrastructure package | Docs, templates, workflow, review process. | Developer tools, newsrooms, research labs. | Knowledge decay and editorial debt. | Working infrastructure. | Low to mid five figures. | Maintenance burden. | That docs stay current alone. |
| Retainer | Ongoing advisory/build capacity. | Foundations, nonprofits, labs. | Need trusted partner. | Monthly deliverables. | Monthly low to mid five figures. | Dependency. | Unlimited access. |

| Grant-funded public-interest build | Public infrastructure, research, documentation. | Foundations/public-interest funders. | Market failure but public need. | Public artifacts/adoption. | Grant-scale. | Sustainability after grant. | Self-sustaining revenue immediately. |

# Positioning and category research

| Frame | Makes legible | Hides | Who understands it | Who might buy | Competitors invoked | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Substitute category | Job it does now | Why buyers tolerate it | Where it fails | Switching requires | New offering must prove |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Spreadsheets | Track claims, sources, contacts, tasks. | Familiar, cheap, flexible. | Poor provenance, permissions, scale. | Migration, training. | Better structure without bureaucracy. |

| Generic AI tools | Drafting, summarizing, searching. | Easy, cheap, impressive. | Hallucination, privacy, no audit trail. | Governance and workflow wrapper. | Safer outputs and traceability. |

| Knowledge bases/search | Retrieval across docs. | Broad enterprise category. | Weak source accountability and workflow action. | Integration with repositories. | Better provenance and actionability. |
| Consultants | Diagnose and implement. | Trust, human expertise. | Expensive, non-repeatable. | Productized service. | Better reusable assets. |

The practical competitor is often not a company. It is **the existing mess plus the organization’s tolerance for that mess**.

# Risks and critiques

Be blunt: this direction could go wrong in many ways.

It could **commercialize the least interesting part** of the research because that is what buyers can understand.

It could mistake **visibility for demand**. People praising essays are not necessarily prospects. People requesting coffee are not necessarily champions. Champions are not necessarily economic buyers.

It could become a **guru funnel**, where public writing slowly turns into authority performance, paid access, vague frameworks, and parasocial demand generation.

It could create **agentic spam**. Using agents to identify leads and draft outreach is not automatically unethical, but automating relevance theater at scale is.

It could reproduce **platform incentives**: publish more, simplify more, polarize more, convert more, measure more, until the research becomes content sludge.

It could overfit to **enterprise procurement** and abandon public-interest goals. Enterprise buyers may pay more but demand roadmaps, controls, exclusivity, and support structures that reshape the work.

It could underprice expert labor and become a fragile consultancy. Conversely, it could overbuild software before the service pattern is understood.

It could sell “AI governance” into organizations that want legitimacy theater rather than accountable practice. The NIST AI RMF and ISO 42001 can support serious risk work, but they can also be used performatively if implementation is shallow.

It could confuse **public infrastructure** with **venture-scale software**. Some of the most important parts may need foundation funding, standards work, cooperative governance, or institutional sponsorship rather than subscriptions.

It could create a business the builders do not want to run: support queues, procurement questionnaires, enterprise security reviews, sales calls, invoice chasing, custom integrations, and customer success.

The antidote is not anti-commercial purity. It is disciplined inquiry: identify what can be sold ethically, what should remain public, what should be grant-funded, what should become software, and what should not be built.

# Prioritized roadmap

## Study first

1. Buyer language and pain: 30–50 discovery conversations across 4–5 segments.
2. Existing workflows and substitutes: observe how people currently manage claims, provenance, research, editorial workflows, AI governance evidence, or knowledge work.
3. Budget and authority: identify who can actually pay.
4. Procurement/risk: list security, privacy, liability, and support requirements by segment.
5. Proof: determine what evidence would move a skeptical buyer.

## Buyer categories to investigate first

Start with segments where the project’s depth is likely to matter and where trust is central:

1. Public-interest newsrooms and investigative nonprofits.
2. Foundations funding journalism, public-interest tech, AI accountability, or civic infrastructure.
3. AI governance teams in mission-sensitive organizations.
4. Research institutes and labs with evidence/provenance workflows.
5. Developer/open-source infrastructure projects if packages are already usable.

Do not start with broad enterprise knowledge management unless a very specific beachhead emerges. It is crowded, procurement-heavy, and easy to flatten the work into generic KM.

## Conversations to have first

Have discovery conversations with:

- 5 newsroom editors/product leads.
- 5 foundation program officers.
- 5 AI governance/compliance leads.
- 5 research ops/lab managers.
- 5 technical documentation/devtools/open-source maintainers.
- 5 consultants/agencies who already sell adjacent work.
- 5 skeptical procurement/security/legal people.

The goal is not to pitch. The goal is to learn what they already do, what breaks, what they have tried, what they spend money on, and what proof they would need.

## Public artifacts to prepare first

1. A clear “problem landscape” memo.
2. A demo showing one concrete workflow.
3. A buyer-language glossary.
4. A provenance/accountability risk checklist.
5. A pilot proposal template.
6. A public technical note explaining what the system does **not** guarantee.

## Offer to test first

Test a **paid workflow/provenance audit** or **paid research/workflow memo** before building a full product. It is concrete, low-risk, useful to buyers, and likely to reveal whether the project is actually solving a budgeted problem.

## What not to build yet

Do not build:

- A generic SaaS platform.
- A full CRM or pipeline automation system.
- A broad “AI governance platform.”
- A polished enterprise dashboard.
- Heavy integrations before a buyer commits.
- Autonomous agent workflows without auditability and human review.

## What should remain research

- Theoretical foundations.
- Public-interest critique.
- Open standards analysis.
- Ethical constraints.
- Evaluation methods.
- Public-facing field reports.

## What should become software

Only the parts that repeat across serious use cases:

- Provenance schemas.
- Audit trails.
- Workflow state tracking.
- Evaluation harnesses.
- Demoable agent workflows with human review.
- Reusable package components.
- Integration templates.

## What should become service

- Discovery and workflow mapping.
- Audits.
- Implementation sprints.
- Training/workshops.
- Standards consultation.
- Buyer-specific adaptation.

## What should become public infrastructure

- Open-source packages.
- Public benchmarks.
- Reference schemas.
- Governance templates.
- Field guides.
- Possibly a shared provenance/accountability toolkit if funders or institutions support it.

## Questions to answer before serious sales

1. Who has the pain?
2. Who owns the budget?
3. What do they call the problem?
4. What happens if they do nothing?
5. What are they already using?
6. What proof would change their mind?
7. What risks would block procurement?
8. What can be delivered in 2–6 weeks?
9. What can be repeated across buyers?
10. What must not be commercialized?
