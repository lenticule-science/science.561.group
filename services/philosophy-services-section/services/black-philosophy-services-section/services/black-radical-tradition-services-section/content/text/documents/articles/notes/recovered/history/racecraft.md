# racecraft

Racecraft is Karen E. Fields and Barbara J. Fields's term, from *Racecraft: The Soul of Inequality in American Life* (Verso, 2012), for the mental and social operation by which the act of racism is hidden behind the belief in race. The word is built on a deliberate analogy to witchcraft. In societies that believe in witchcraft, the practice of accusing, fearing, and punishing supposed witches produces a world in which witches are an experienced reality — yet no witch's power is actually doing the harm; the accusation and the response are. Racecraft works the same way: the practice of racism produces a world organized around race, and that organized world is then taken as proof that the races were there all along, natural kinds simply waiting to be perceived. Generated by: race — racecraft names the operation that makes race appear to be a fact of nature rather than a product of domination.

The Fieldses' central move is to keep racism and race distinct, and to insist on the direction of causation between them. Racism — the doing, the differential treatment of people sorted into groups — is the reality. Race — the belief that humanity is divided into ranked natural kinds — is the ideology that racism generates and then hides behind. The ordinary way of speaking reverses this: people say someone was discriminated against "because of their race," as though race were the prior cause and the discrimination its effect. Racecraft names exactly this reversal and exposes it as a sleight of mind. The discrimination comes first; the "race" is conjured to explain and justify it after the fact, and is then mistaken for its cause.

This matters because the reversal is not a harmless figure of speech. When inequality is attributed to race, the social action that produced the inequality vanishes from view, and what remains is a seemingly natural difference for which no one is responsible. Disparities in wealth, health, imprisonment, and life expectancy come to look like expressions of what the races simply are, rather than as the cumulative residue of identifiable practices carried out by identifiable institutions. Racecraft is, in this sense, an ideological technology of innocence: it converts the evidence of ongoing domination into evidence of pre-existing kinds, so that the structure that produces the harm need never be named.

Within this library racecraft is the concept that explains why race feels so solid despite having no biological foundation. Where racialization names the act of producing a racial category and racial-formation names the historical process that shifts the categories, racecraft names the cognitive trick that conceals both — the way the act disappears behind the belief, so that domination presents itself as the mere perception of a difference that was already there. To learn to see racecraft is to learn to read every claim about "what a race is like" as a report on what has been done to a people, and to whom.
