abstract: >
  This note proposes non-applicability cocycles as a research concept for
  studying refusal as governance rather than as non-compliance. Legal
  pluralism already teaches that multiple normative orders may occupy a single
  social field. Refusal studies, Indigenous political theory, Black studies,
  and abolitionist theory show that "no" can be a positive institutional act:
  a refusal of the terms on which an imposed order claims standing. The formal
  proposal here is that, when two governance patterns overlap and one asserts
  binding authority while the other recognizes the binding claim as
  non-applicable, the resulting obstruction should be tracked as an H^1-class
  on the boundary between patterns. This class is not a metaphor for
  disagreement. It is a record that the binding claim does not glue across the
  witness disciplines of the field.

## 1. Problem

The ordinary vocabulary for refusal is too often inherited from the order being
refused. A person or collective is said to disobey, violate, defect, resist,
opt out, fail to comply, or request exemption. Each of those descriptions may
be accurate inside some institutional grammar. None is adequate when the
refusal is not a failed performance of the imposed rule but a governance act
from another rule system.

The older social-theoretic problem is legal pluralism: a social field can
contain several operative normative orders, not one sovereign legal order with
minor local deviations. Sally Falk Moore's semi-autonomous social field made
this point by asking law-and-society research to study fields that generate
rules and induce compliance even while wider legal orders also press on them.
John Griffiths, Sally Engle Merry, and Brian Tamanaha developed legal
pluralism as a descriptive and analytic challenge to legal centralism. That
tradition gives the necessary first move: the state is not the only possible
source of law-like ordering.

The concept proposed here takes the next step. It asks how to name a very
specific obstruction that appears when one governance pattern asserts that a
rule binds and another governance pattern recognizes that same assertion as
not applicable. The point is not just that orders conflict. The point is that
the binding claim fails to become a shared section of the field.

## 2. Contribution

A non-applicability cocycle is a persistent boundary obstruction generated by a
refusal of applicability.

It appears when:

1. one governance pattern derives a binding status for an actor, relation,
   place, obligation, punishment, debt, office, identity, or jurisdiction;
2. another governance pattern sharing part of the same social field recognizes
   that derivation as non-binding rather than merely false, undesirable, or
   disobeyed; and
3. the overlap between the two patterns has no recognized coboundary that
   cancels the obstruction without destroying one pattern's witness discipline.

The resulting class is written informally as:

```text
[alpha] in H^1(boundary(E, R); A)
```

where `E` is the enclosing or external governance pattern, `R` is the refusing
pattern, `boundary(E, R)` is the disputed overlap on which binding is asserted
and refused, and `A` is the coefficient structure carrying the relevant
authority, obligation, recognition, and conflict values.

This is not a claim that every refusal is cohomological in a literal computed
sense. It is a research proposal: if governance is modeled as pattern-indexed
closure rules plus witness languages, then refusal of applicability should be
tracked by the same machinery used to track failed gluing. The class matters
because it marks where "this binds you" cannot be made into a shared fact of
the field.

## 3. Repository Source

The immediate source is the predicated-governance theory in this repository.
That theory treats governance as the normative substructure of predicated work:
not as a special state form, but as a rule-governed pattern for producing,
recognizing, and maintaining social facts.

Its key move is the notion of a governance pattern. A pattern has:

```text
(S_pi, W_pi, Rec_pi)
```

where `S_pi` is a closure-rule fragment, `W_pi` is a witness language, and
`Rec_pi` is a recognition discipline. Hohfeldian rights, consensus procedures,
restorative harm repair, kinship obligations, ayllu rotation, Ubuntu
restoration, Anishinaabe teachings, and Black radical refusal are therefore
not treated as local variations on one default legal grammar. They are
different governance patterns with different closure rules and recognition
disciplines.

The internal predicated-governance notes already identify the relevant row:
Black-radical refusal carries H^1 classes generated by non-applicability
cocycles. In that row, unresolved H^1 is not a defect in the formalism. It is
the substrate-algebraic record of structural refusal.

This note turns that row into a field-facing research concept.

## 4. Definition

Let `E` and `R` be governance patterns. `E` may be a state, school, court,
police agency, firm, platform, family regime, colonial office, or any other
pattern that asserts a binding status. `R` may be a fugitive, Indigenous,
abolitionist, kinship, mutual-aid, strike, religious, neighborhood, or other
pattern whose recognition discipline refuses that status.

Let `U_i` be local sites in their shared social field: encounters, documents,
routes, households, hearings, borders, work assignments, meeting places,
records, or other cells where recognition occurs. On each `U_i`, each pattern
can produce local judgments:

```text
E_i: bound_by(actor, declaration, institution)
R_i: not_applicable(declaration, actor, institution)
```

The two local judgments are not just contradictory propositions. They answer
different recognition questions. `E_i` asks whether the actor is bound under
the external pattern's closure rules. `R_i` asks whether that external closure
rule has standing inside the refusing pattern.

A non-applicability 1-cocycle is the obstruction appearing on overlaps
`intersection(U_i, U_j)` when the binding claim can be transported through `E`
but cannot be transported through `R` as a recognized obligation. In a
Belnap-style four-valued or lattice-valued setting, the disputed cell may be
marked conflict-valued: both the assertion of binding and the refusal of
applicability are present as witnessed information. The important datum is not
the conflict alone. It is the persistence of the obstruction after all
recognized local translations have been applied.

The cohomology class is non-trivial when the obstruction is not a removable
misunderstanding, missing record, ordinary appeal, internal veto, or recognized
exception. It is trivialized only by a lawful transformation inside the
relevant field: for example, the external order withdraws the binding claim;
the refusing pattern changes its recognition discipline; a mutually recognized
meta-pattern is constructed; or the disputed institution is made irrelevant by
an alternative governance fragment.

## 5. Distinctions

This concept is meant to be narrow.

It is not non-compliance. Non-compliance accepts the applicability of a rule
and fails or declines to perform it. A non-applicability cocycle records a
refusal that the rule has standing over the actor or relation in the first
place.

It is not an exemption. An exemption is granted inside the external pattern's
own jurisdiction. A non-applicability cocycle persists when the external
pattern's jurisdictional frame is itself refused.

It is not a veto. A veto is an internal power recognized by the decision rule
it blocks. Non-applicability is not necessarily an authorized move inside the
system asserting authority.

It is not pluralism in general. Legal pluralism names the coexistence and
interaction of multiple normative orders. A non-applicability cocycle names one
kind of obstruction produced at their boundary.

It is not resistance as attitude. The object is not an interior disposition,
preference, or identity. It is a witnessed failure of an asserted binding claim
to glue across recognition disciplines.

## 6. Legal Pluralism

Moore's semi-autonomous social field remains the nearest classical ancestor.
It gives social theory a way to see rule-generating fields that are neither
fully autonomous nor reducible to the official law pressing on them. Griffiths
then names legal centralism as an ideology and makes plural legal ordering a
descriptive problem. Merry develops legal pluralism for anthropology and
law-and-society research, especially where official and unofficial orders are
intertwined. Tamanaha later clarifies the term's history and warns against
conceptual overreach.

Non-applicability cocycles should be read in that lineage, not outside it. They
do not replace legal pluralism. They add a diagnostic for one recurrent
pluralist event: the moment when an official or dominant order says "this rule
binds" and another operative order says "that binding relation does not have
standing here."

This matters because much conflict in plural legal fields is not symmetric.
An official order may record refusal as deviance, crime, breach, truancy,
trespass, violation, contempt, debt, or incapacity. The refusing pattern may
record the same encounter as jurisdictional non-applicability, protection,
fugitivity, care, kinship obligation, strike discipline, ceremony, sanctuary,
or abolitionist construction. The analytical question is not which vocabulary
wins in advance. It is where the binding claim fails to glue.

## 7. Refusal Studies and Indigenous Political Theory

Audra Simpson's work on ethnographic and political refusal is central because
it refuses the premise that recognition by the settler state is the horizon of
political life. Refusal is not silence, lack, or failed speech. It is a method
for withholding, interrupting, and redirecting the terms on which knowledge and
citizenship are demanded.

Tuck and Yang's work on refusing research adds an ethical constraint for this
formal proposal. A theory of non-applicability cannot turn refusal into a new
extractive instrument. Some refusals protect what should not be made available
to the researcher's field of vision. A non-applicability cocycle should
therefore be treated as a limit record as much as an analytic record: it says
that a demanded translation has no standing, not that better instrumentation
would eventually capture it.

Coulthard's critique of recognition politics supplies the political stakes.
If recognition can reproduce colonial power by leaving the deeper structure of
domination intact, then a formal account of refusal must not make inclusion
into the dominant pattern the default resolution. The obstruction may be the
point. A cohomology class that remains non-trivial can be a record of a
governance pattern refusing absorption.

## 8. Black Radical Refusal and Fugitive Governance

The Black radical tradition is not reducible to refusal, but refusal is one of
the places where the concept becomes sharp. Cedric Robinson's account of the
Black Radical Tradition situates Black resistance in histories and social
formations not exhausted by European Marxist categories. Moten and Harney's
undercommons names fugitive planning and Black study as practices that do not
ask managerial institutions for the grammar of their own existence. Abolitionist
work by Angela Davis and Ruth Wilson Gilmore insists that abolition is not only
the negation of prisons and police. It is the construction of other
life-sustaining institutions.

That constructive abolitionist point is formally important. In the
predicated-governance notes, refusal does not simply delete an external rule.
It constructs a blocking cell or an alternative closure-rule fragment that
makes the external claim structurally non-binding. The punitive or
administrative order may still assert itself. The refusing pattern does not
therefore become a defective copy of that order. It has its own witness
language, its own recognition discipline, and its own account of what counts as
repair, safety, study, kinship, obligation, or survival.

The non-applicability cocycle names the boundary record of that difference.
It is not a way to domesticate fugitive governance. It is a way to avoid
mistaking fugitive governance for mere non-compliance with the dominant
institution's commands.

## 9. Formal Setting

The formal setting should be deliberately modest.

First, Belnap-Dunn four-valued logic supplies a reason not to explode in the
presence of conflict. In contested governance fields, one may have witnessed
support for a binding claim and witnessed support for a refusal of
applicability. Treating that cell as simply inconsistent loses the information
that both witnesses exist and that each is produced by a different recognition
discipline.

Second, cellular sheaf theory supplies the language of local data, restriction,
gluing, global sections, and cohomological obstruction. Hansen and Ghrist's
work on discourse sheaves shows how sheaves can model communication and
opinion dynamics on networks. Ghrist and Riess's work on cellular sheaves of
lattices and the Tarski Laplacian shows how lattice-valued sheaf data can
support a discrete Hodge-theoretic account where fixed points and global
sections matter.

The present proposal does not require importing those models wholesale into
social theory. It uses their general lesson: when local data cannot be glued
into a global section, the failure can be localized and studied. A
non-applicability cocycle is the governance-theoretic version of such a failure
where the non-gluing datum is the applicability of a binding claim.

Third, Searle's account of status-function declarations is useful as a foil.
If institutional reality often depends on declarations that assign status
functions, then non-applicability asks a prior pluralist question: under which
pattern's recognition discipline does the declaration have standing? A status
declaration may be valid inside one institutional order and non-applicable
inside another.

## 10. Research Design

The concept can be made empirical without collapsing into surveillance of
refusal.

Possible corpora include:

1. court, school, welfare, labor, border, prison, platform, or municipal
   records where an institution asserts binding authority;
2. community protocols, strike rules, sanctuary practices, mutual-aid records,
   abolitionist project documents, tribal governance materials, or refusal
   statements where another pattern rejects that authority's standing;
3. encounter records where both recognitions are present in the same field;
4. later transformations that remove, preserve, intensify, or re-route the
   obstruction.

The analytic procedure is:

1. identify the governance patterns without assuming one is default;
2. specify each pattern's closure-rule fragment, witness language, and
   recognition discipline;
3. locate boundary cells where binding is asserted by one pattern and
   non-applicability is witnessed by another;
4. distinguish ordinary contradiction from refusal of standing;
5. test whether the obstruction is removed by an accepted translation,
   exemption, appeal, reform, or shared meta-pattern;
6. if it persists, record it as a candidate non-applicability class.

The strongest cases will not be cases where a person merely says no. They will
be cases where the refusal is backed by a reproducible recognition discipline:
other members can tell when the refusal is valid, what it protects, what it
requires, and what would count as violating it.

## 11. Expected Payoff

For law-and-society, the concept gives legal pluralism a sharper local
instrument. It asks not only whether several normative orders coexist, but
where their binding claims fail to glue.

For political theory, it distinguishes refusal from dissent, exit, civil
disobedience, and exemption. A political order may be refused at the level of
applicability, not merely opposed within its own grammar.

For Black studies and abolitionist theory, it preserves the constructive
character of fugitive and abolitionist governance. It does not treat refusal as
absence. It treats refusal as a positive boundary act produced by a pattern
with its own recognition discipline.

For critical social ontology, it adds a pluralist correction to status-function
theory. Declarations do not float free. Their standing depends on governance
patterns capable of recognizing them.

For applied category and sheaf theory, it gives a social-theoretic use case in
which the relevant cohomology class is neither mere inconsistency nor ordinary
consensus failure. It is non-applicability of authority across plural witness
disciplines.

## 12. Limits

This proposal has strict limits.

It does not certify which refusal is legitimate. Legitimacy is produced inside
political, ethical, and historical struggles, not by naming a cohomology class.

It does not make Black radical, Indigenous, abolitionist, or fugitive practices
available for extraction. Some refusals are refusals of being studied under
dominant terms. A research method that violates that refusal has already missed
the object.

It does not claim that all normative conflict is H^1. Some conflicts are local
inconsistencies, missing facts, bad records, ordinary jurisdictional disputes,
or strategic performances. The concept is reserved for persistent boundary
obstructions in applicability.

It does not resolve the obstruction by default. In many cases, the demand for
resolution is itself the dominant pattern attempting to absorb the refusing
one. The class may remain because the refusal is doing work.

## 13. Thesis

The public paper should defend this thesis:

> A refusal of applicability is a governance act when it is produced by a
> recognition discipline capable of saying why an external binding claim has no
> standing. In plural governance fields, persistent refusals of applicability
> form boundary obstructions. These obstructions can be modeled as
> non-applicability cocycles: H^1-classes recording the failure of an asserted
> binding relation to glue across governance patterns.

The paper's strongest version is not "refusal is cohomology." The stronger,
more defensible claim is that cohomological language gives social theory a way
to keep track of a specific object that current vocabulary often smears
together with non-compliance, exemption, dissent, and jurisdictional conflict.
