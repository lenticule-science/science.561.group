# restorative-justice

Restorative justice is an approach to harm that centers repair over punishment. Instead of asking "what
law was broken, who broke it, and what punishment do they deserve?", it asks "who was harmed, what are
their needs, and whose obligation is it to meet those needs?" Generated by: restorative justice is the
refusal of law's punitive form — a way of responding to wrongdoing that turns away from the retributive
logic of the sanction and toward the repair of the relationships the harm has damaged. The shift
reframes the response to harm from a procedure directed at the offender into a process directed at the
relationship among offender, victim, and community.

In practice it takes many forms — victim-offender mediation, community conferencing, sentencing circles,
reparative boards — but these share a common structure: the people actually affected by the harm,
rather than the state acting in their name, participate directly in determining the response. The
offender is expected to acknowledge responsibility and to take concrete steps toward repair; the
victim's needs and experiences are centered rather than subordinated to the state's interest in
prosecution and punishment. Where the conventional criminal process treats a crime as an offence
against the state, to be answered by the state, restorative justice treats it as an injury to particular
people and relationships, to be answered by those it touched.

Restorative justice draws heavily on Indigenous dispute-resolution traditions — particularly Māori
practices in Aotearoa New Zealand and First Nations practices in Canada — that predate and operate
outside the retributive logic of colonial criminal justice. Its adoption into Western legal systems
therefore raises a pointed question of *recuperation*: when state institutions take up restorative
practices, do they transform those institutions, or do the institutions strip the practices of their
community-centered logic and redeploy them as a more palatable mode of social control? A sentencing
circle convened and controlled by a court is not obviously the same thing as one rooted in and
accountable to a community, and the worry is that the form survives while the substance — the genuine
devolution of authority to those affected — quietly does not.

Transformative justice presses the critique further. Where restorative justice seeks to repair harm
within existing social structures, transformative justice argues that the structures themselves produce
the harm and must be changed. If interpersonal violence is shaped by poverty, racism, patriarchy, and
displacement, then repairing the immediate interpersonal harm without touching the structural
conditions is insufficient — it returns people to the very circumstances that generated the violence.
Associated with abolitionist movements, transformative justice treats individual incidents of harm as
symptoms of structural violence and seeks responses that address both at once, refusing to separate the
question of this harm from the question of the conditions that produced it.

Within this cluster restorative justice stands as the principal alternative to the sanction and to
incarceration — the refusal of punishment as the paradigmatic answer to harm. It reaches back toward
forms of accountability grounded in community and relationship rather than in state coercion, and its
central tension is exactly the one between its transformative promise as a community practice and its
vulnerability to being absorbed, hollowed out, and reissued as one more instrument of the social
control it set out to replace.
