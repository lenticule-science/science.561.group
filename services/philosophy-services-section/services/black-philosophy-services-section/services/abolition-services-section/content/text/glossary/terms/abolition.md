Abolition is the political framework that demands the elimination — not the reform — of carceral and punitive institutions: prisons, police, immigration detention, the death penalty, and the broader apparatus of criminalization. Abolition holds that these institutions do not fail when they produce harm; they succeed. They function as designed: to manage populations rendered surplus or threatening by the political economy of [racial capitalism](../../../../../black-radical-tradition-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/racial-capitalism.md) through containment, punishment, and premature death.

The [genealogy](../../philosophy/disciplines/critical-theory/schools/foucault/terms/genealogy.md) runs from the abolition of chattel slavery through the recognition that slavery was not abolished but restructured. The Thirteenth Amendment's exception clause ("except as a punishment for crime") enabled the convict lease system, chain gangs, and the contemporary prison-industrial complex. [Angela Davis](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/knowledge-services-section/services/research-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/angela-davis.md), in *Are Prisons Obsolete?* (2003), argued that the prison is a continuation of the plantation: a racial institution that extracts [labor](../../../../../anarchism-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/labor.md), controls movement, and destroys social bonds along lines of race and class. [Ruth Wilson Gilmore](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/knowledge-services-section/services/research-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/ruth-wilson-gilmore.md) grounded this analysis materially, showing in *Golden Gulag* (2007) how California's prison expansion emerged from specific surpluses — of land, [labor](../../../../../anarchism-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/labor.md), capital, and state capacity — organized through the racial geography of [organized abandonment](../../../../services/state-repression-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/organized-abandonment.md). [Mariame Kaba](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/knowledge-services-section/services/research-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/mariame-kaba.md) insists that abolition is not a position on what should be destroyed but a practice of building what should exist: the social relations — care, accountability, material security — that make punishment unnecessary.

Abolition is therefore not only against prisons. It is a demand for the conditions under which carceral institutions lose their function: housing, healthcare, education, [food sovereignty](../../../../../../../../../biological-science-services-section/services/ecology-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/food-sovereignty.md), conflict resolution grounded in [mutual aid](mutual-aid.md) rather than punishment. This connects it to [prefigurative politics](prefigurative-politics.md): abolition must be practiced in the present, through the creation of non-carceral responses to harm, rather than deferred to a future moment of institutional reform.
