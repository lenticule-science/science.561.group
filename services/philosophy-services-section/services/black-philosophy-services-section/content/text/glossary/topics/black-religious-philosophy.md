# Black Religious Philosophy

Black religious philosophy studies divinity, evil, suffering, hope, moral authority, liberation, ritual, community, and the political uses and transformations of religious traditions in Black life. It includes philosophy of religion, theology, ethics, metaphysics, and social criticism, but it is not confined to Christianity or to academic theology.

Enslaved and free Black thinkers confronted a central contradiction: enslavers invoked Christianity while committing kidnapping, forced labor, family separation, rape, and murder. Frederick Douglass, Henry Bibb, Harriet Jacobs, and Anna Julia Cooper did not treat this merely as hypocrisy between private belief and conduct. Their arguments tested the moral authority of churches, scriptures, nations, and concepts of civilization against Black experience.

Later Black theology and womanist ethics developed different answers. James Cone made liberation from racial oppression determinative for theological meaning. Katie Cannon grounded womanist ethics in Black women's moral agency, survival practices, literary and religious resources, while challenging both white theology and male-centered Black theology. Black religious philosophy also includes secular critique, African-derived traditions, Islam, Judaism, humanism, religious naturalism, and practices that exceed inherited institutional categories.

## Boundary

Religious language must not be treated as an ornamental version of political theory, nor should it be assumed to express doctrinal consensus. Faith could sustain freedom, community, discipline, accommodation, revolt, patriarchy, and critique. The philosophical task is to identify the argument a source makes about authority, suffering, obligation, or possibility, not to assign a single political meaning to religion.
