Black philosophy is a plural field of philosophical activity developed from Black and African-descended life, histories, intellectual traditions, and world-making. It includes explicit academic philosophy but is not bounded by the modern university's decisions about genre, credential, or canon. Arguments may be carried in treatises, speeches, autobiographies, sermons, political organizing, jurisprudence, fiction, music, visual art, oral traditions, and collective practice when the evidence supports philosophical judgments about existence, knowledge, value, obligation, beauty, freedom, or social order.

The field is not a single doctrine. “Black” does not guarantee agreement, a radical politics, or a common method. Nor does Black philosophy mean only philosophy about race or racism. Black philosophers have addressed logic, education, religion, language, art, metaphysics, ethics, political economy, gender, sexuality, disability, ecology, technology, and ordinary life. Racialization and anti-Black domination shape many of these inquiries, but they do not exhaust their questions or answers.

Black philosophy overlaps with Africana philosophy, African philosophy, African American philosophy, Caribbean philosophy, Afro-Latin American philosophy, Black European philosophy, and other diasporic formations without being interchangeable with any one of them. “Africana philosophy” is commonly used as a metaphilosophical umbrella for philosophizing by African and African-descended peoples across Africa and its diasporas. “Black philosophy” places additional emphasis on Blackness as a lived, contested, political, cultural, and ontological formation. The boundaries remain subjects of argument rather than settled taxonomic facts.

This section therefore organizes the field through crossing dimensions: historical and geographic locations; philosophical problem-spaces; intellectual and political traditions; forms and sites of activity; and conditions of production, transmission, silencing, and recovery. No one dimension substitutes for the field. The Black Radical Tradition and abolition are developed parts of the current corpus, not containers for every Black philosophical project.
