The enslaved economy is productive activity and exchange undertaken by enslaved people outside the labor immediately required by enslavers. It included garden patches, poultry and livestock, hunting, fishing, cooking, craft production, wage work, self-hire, sale, barter, saving, and transfers of goods within families and communities.

The term is a modern historical description, not a single institution or a vocabulary known to have been shared by all enslaved people. Its forms differed by crop, region, skill, gender, law, plantation size, urban access, and whether labor followed a task or gang system.

The enslaved economy had a double character. It could provide food, choice, status, pleasure, family support, mobility, and a limited capacity to accumulate or purchase freedom. It could also subsidize slavery when enslavers reduced rations, clothing, or supervision because enslaved people supplied themselves during nights, Sundays, or holidays. Participation therefore demonstrates agency under constraint but does not by itself demonstrate economic independence or consent to slavery.

For labor theory, the enslaved economy shows that people distinguished work commanded for an enslaver from work directed toward their own ends. The same person could resist plantation output and work intensely in a family patch without contradiction.
