Own time is time that an enslaved person could direct toward self-chosen, familial, communal, religious, market, or leisure purposes rather than an enslaver's immediate command. Frederick Douglass described the limited ability to hire himself out as becoming “master of my own time.” Lowcountry task labor could also produce a customary boundary after an assigned task was completed, while Sundays, holidays, nights, and unauthorized absence furnished other contested intervals.

Own time was not free time in the full sense. Enslavers could revoke it, set excessive tasks, charge for self-hire, prohibit movement or trade, and force people to use the interval to supply food and clothing that the enslaver withheld. Work performed during own time could therefore increase an enslaver's profit. The term names a limited jurisdiction over time inside slavery, not an escape from slavery.

Nor should every hour after field or household duty be classified as own time. Solomon Northup described post-field chores, grinding, and food preparation. Charlie Pye's WPA account reports that women were required to spin a specified quantity after field work and were punished for failing. [Compulsory self-provisioning](./compulsory-self-provisioning.md) names after-hours labor commanded or made necessary by an enslaver's transfer of provisioning costs. Evidence of a residual interval, practical direction over its activity, and authority over its product or experience should be distinguished rather than assumed together.

The concept matters because it shows that temporal control was itself an object of Black labor struggle. A conflict about pace, task completion, hiring, visiting kin, gardening, worship, or rest was also a conflict about whether the enslaver's claim on the worker's day was morally unlimited.
