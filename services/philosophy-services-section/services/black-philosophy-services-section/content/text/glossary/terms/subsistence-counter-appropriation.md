Subsistence counter-appropriation is the recovery of food or other necessities from an extracting enslaver, justified by prior appropriation of the person's labor and by the enslaver's failure to provide enough for life. The term is newly introduced by the 2026-08-27 note on enslaved Black theories of labor; it is not presented as an antebellum phrase.

The underlying argument comes directly from Frederick Douglass and Henry Bibb. When Thomas Auld withheld adequate food, Douglass distinguished theft as such from taking what his own labor had already produced. He reasoned that the legal owner of his labor could not starve him and then treat self-provision as an independent wrong. Douglass initially limited the justification to taking from the enslaver, then considered the wider society's participation in depriving him of liberty and the just reward of labor.

Bibb made the boundary especially explicit when answering an accusation that he had stolen wheat. He held that an enslaved worker had a moral right to necessities from the abundance created by the worker's uncompensated labor, but said that taking a neighbor's property as a free person would be wrong. The convergence matters because it establishes a shared structure of argument across two witnesses: prior extraction and imposed deprivation alter the moral relation, while oppression does not erase every distinction about taking.

Counter-appropriation is narrower than the claim that any taking under oppression is justified. Its grounds are a prior extraction, a withheld necessity or earned good, and the preservation of the person subjected to that extraction. The concept exposes a conflict between legal property and moral ownership: an extracting legal order may call recovery theft while protecting the original taking.
