Rightful earnings is the moral claim that a worker holds to compensation produced through the worker's contracting, skill, and labor. The term is grounded here in Frederick Douglass's account of hiring himself as a caulker while enslaved in Baltimore. Douglass made his own contracts, performed the work, collected the wages, and concluded that the money was rightfully his. Hugh Auld's ability to seize the wage supplied coercive power, not moral title.

Rightful earnings differs from a general theory that all products can be assigned to isolated individuals. Douglass's argument concerned an immediate conflict between the person who performed and arranged paid work and an enslaver who contributed no labor but claimed the whole payment through ownership of the worker. The claim also rejected the enslaver's debt story: minimal food and clothing did not create an unlimited obligation because compelled prior service had already paid for those provisions.

The term is close to Douglass's own language but should not be attributed as a standard phrase used by all Black people enslaved in the antebellum South. It identifies one explicit labor principle whose wider resonance can be tested against other testimony.
