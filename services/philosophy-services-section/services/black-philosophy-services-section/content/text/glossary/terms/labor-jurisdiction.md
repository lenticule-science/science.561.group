Labor jurisdiction is the rightful authority to decide whether, when, where, at what pace, for what purposes, and toward whose benefit a person's capacities are used. It gathers several claims that Black witnesses to antebellum slavery expressed separately or together: a claim to wages, a boundary around one's time, authority over bodily movement, the direction of work toward family and community, control of pace, and the right to refuse a coercive command.

The term is a present analytic synthesis, not a phrase attributed to enslaved people. Henry Bibb's statement that a person has rights to wages, wife and children, liberty, happiness, and conscience supplies the clearest explicit integration. Frederick Douglass's arguments about contracting, earnings, own time, subsistence, and refusal supply a detailed account of several dimensions. Harriet Jacobs's family and bodily account shows why jurisdiction cannot be reduced to an individual wage relation. Solomon Northup's report of cotton quotas shows what it meant for an enslaver to seize authority over pace and interpret productive ability as debt.

Labor jurisdiction differs from treating self-ownership as the entire theory of freedom. Self-ownership can reject another person's property claim, but its commercial grammar can make personhood sound like ownership of a private asset. Labor jurisdiction emphasizes legitimate decision-making within relations. A person may direct work toward a child, spouse, household, or community without surrendering jurisdiction to them and without treating either person as property.

The concept also sets a non-productivist boundary. A person's jurisdiction does not depend on being productive, skilled, able-bodied, waged, or capable of supporting others. It describes authority over capacities, including the authority to rest or refuse, rather than making labor performance the source of human worth.
