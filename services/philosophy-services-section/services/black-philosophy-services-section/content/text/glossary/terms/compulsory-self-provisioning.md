Compulsory self-provisioning is labor that an extracting authority requires workers or their households to perform outside the principal workday in order to reproduce life and labor at the workers' own temporal cost. Under slavery this included cooking after field labor, producing clothing at night, maintaining cabins, and raising food because enslavers supplied too little or transferred provisioning duties to enslaved households.

The term corrects a misleading equation between work done after the field day and [own time](./own-time.md). An interval may be outside an overseer's immediate field command while still being structured by necessity, an explicit quota, or punishment. In Charlie Pye's WPA account, women were required to spin a specified amount after field work and were punished the next morning if they failed. Solomon Northup described field hands finishing daily cotton labor and then performing chores, grinding corn, and preparing the next day's food. These were second shifts, not simply autonomous enterprise.

Compulsory self-provisioning can coexist with limited labor jurisdiction. Celestia Avery reported that families on the Heard plantation raised vegetables, chickens, and other goods, but the enslaver claimed half of what they raised and prohibited sale. People nevertheless sold chickens clandestinely, bought fiddles, and organized reciprocal quilting. The same productive field could therefore contain imposed household reproduction, extraction by the enslaver, covert exchange, and community-directed work.

“Self” in this term identifies where the cost was placed, not an isolated individual. Provisioning was commonly household and communal labor, structured by gender and age. The arrangement allowed an enslaver to obtain the principal workday while displacing part of the cost of food, clothing, shelter, and recovery onto enslaved people's nights and social relations.
