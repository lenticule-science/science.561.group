Freedom ransom is payment made to terminate an illegitimate claim over a person, without recognizing that the recipient holds rightful title to the person or to the payment. It distinguishes a practical decision made under threat from consent to the property relation that created the threat.

Frederick Douglass used the underlying historical language. After friends paid Hugh Auld so that Douglass could return from Britain with greater security, Douglass described the payment as ransom and as money extorted by a robber. He could judge the payment prudent because liberty and political work were worth more than the sum while continuing to deny that Auld possessed a morally valid saleable interest in him.

William Grimes's 1825 narrative shows the labor structure of such ransom. After escaping, Grimes worked for years, built a household, and accumulated property in Connecticut. When agents of a former enslaver threatened re-enslavement, Grimes was compelled to transfer the product of those years to secure legal freedom and remain with his family. The slave claim thus reached beyond plantation labor and appropriated accumulated fugitive labor.

Moses Grandy's 1843 narrative shows that ransom could recur. After Grandy paid an agreed price, an enslaver destroyed his receipts and sold him; a later arrangement also failed before a third effort produced legal freedom. Grandy then used earnings to purchase his wife and son and sought funds for other relatives. The sequence separates practical payment from rightful title while showing that the coerced price could organize years of individual and collective labor.

“Self-purchase” names the legal and transactional form but can reproduce slavery's premise that a person is the object sold. Freedom ransom names the payer's contrary moral account. It does not imply that every person who purchased freedom described the transaction in these terms; the interpretation is explicit in Douglass and structurally supported by Grimes's description of compulsion and lost earnings.
