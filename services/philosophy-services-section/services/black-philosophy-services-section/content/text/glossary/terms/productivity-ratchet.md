A productivity ratchet is a coercive rule that converts demonstrated productive capacity or an exceptional output into a higher continuing minimum without granting the worker a corresponding claim to time, compensation, or control. The demand moves upward when the worker demonstrates greater ability but does not reliably move downward with exhaustion, injury, changed conditions, or the worker's own purposes.

The term is grounded here in Solomon Northup's description of cotton labor on Edwin Epps's Louisiana plantation. A new worker was driven to pick as rapidly as possible on the first day, and the measured output became the nightly expectation. Northup reported that exceeding a quota could cause the next day's task to be enlarged. Patsey's exceptional ability to pick cotton exposed her to punishment if she failed to deliver roughly twice the ordinary minimum. The capacity remained hers, but the enslaver asserted jurisdiction over its use and captured its gain.

Productivity ratchet is a modern analytic term, not Northup's or Patsey's phrase. Northup's account establishes the plantation rule and its consequences; it does not preserve Patsey's own stated theory of that rule. The example nonetheless corrects a simple claim that skill or productivity tends toward autonomy. Under a quota-and-punishment regime, greater capacity could intensify extraction.
