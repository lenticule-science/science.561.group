## The correction in one sentence

Black feminist scholarship changes the question “what theories did enslaved Black people hold about their labor?” by showing that slavery commanded a [compound labor regime](../../../../../../glossary/terms/compound-labor-regime.md): agricultural and skilled production were joined to domestic service, coerced reproduction, sexual availability, care, kin-making, and medical extraction, while the archive unequally authorized who could state what those relations meant.

This is more than the instruction to include women. If labor is defined first through a male worker, an employer, a wage, and an output, then pregnancy, nursing, child care, sexual coercion, family separation, household work, and bodily injury can appear as additions to the real theory. Enslaved women's testimony and Black feminist scholarship instead require those relations to participate in defining labor, personhood, property, obligation, and freedom from the beginning.

The historical record still does not yield one shared theory held by all enslaved Black women. Harriet Jacobs gives explicit arguments. Other women appear through court records, sale documents, medical writing, slaveholders' correspondence, objects, and retrospective interviews. Later scholars supply concepts that make structures visible, but those concepts are not retroactive quotations. This note therefore separates a historical actor's proposition, an interpretation of a practice, and a later analytic reconstruction.

## 1. Production and reproduction were one regime without being one activity

Jennifer Morgan's *Laboring Women* establishes that agricultural production and reproduction were jointly central to the organization and ideology of New World slavery. The point is not that childbirth can be reduced to work in the same sense as hoeing cotton. It is that enslavers calculated women's field capacity, pregnancy, birth, nursing, and children's inherited status within a connected order of accumulation. A theory of labor that excludes reproduction before inquiry begins therefore adopts the enslaver's public distinction between productive estate work and supposedly natural female capacity.

[Reproductive expropriation](../../../../../../glossary/terms/reproductive-expropriation.md) names the seizure of gestational, birthing, and life-sustaining capacities through a property regime in which a child's enslaved status followed the mother. It identifies an appropriation, not an identity. Enslaved women were not valuable only as reproducers, not every woman bore children, and reproduction never exhausted their labor or personhood. Morgan explicitly warns against approaching enslaved women only through reproductive capacity.

The 2024 scholarship organized under [reproductive racial capitalism](../../../../../../glossary/terms/reproductive-racial-capitalism.md) presses the relation further. Jennifer Morgan, Alys Eve Weinbaum, and the issue's contributors argue that conception, gestation, birth, childrearing, and the reproduction of social relations belong at the center of racial capitalism rather than at its domestic edge. This framework is later analytic vocabulary. It can illuminate the antebellum evidence, but it does not prove that an enslaved witness conceived her situation through the modern categories “racial capitalism” or “social reproduction.”

## 2. Sexual domination belonged to political economy

Harriet Jacobs argues that slavery reorganized sexual vulnerability and moral judgment. Dr. Flint's power over her movement, housing, labor, family, and possible sale made his sexual pursuit inseparable from ownership. Jacobs's constrained decisions cannot be evaluated through a choice model that first imagines equal parties and then asks whether a transaction occurred.

Adrienne Davis's [sexual political economy](../../../../../../glossary/terms/sexual-political-economy.md) supplies a later framework for this relation among law, markets, productive labor, reproductive appropriation, and sexual coercion. Alexandra Finley's “intimate economy” similarly places domestic, reproductive, and sexual labor within the domestic slave trade rather than in a private realm outside economic history. These concepts block two errors: treating sexual violence as an owner's private misconduct unrelated to accumulation, and treating every intimate relation as completely explained by market price.

Emily Owens's [presence of force](../../../../../../glossary/terms/presence-of-force.md) sharpens the evidentiary problem. Enslavers could use money, gifts, housing, or promises within a condition they already governed by captivity. The existence of an exchange does not settle whether consent existed; neither does the existence of force make every survival strategy identical. The inquiry must ask what powers structured the encounter, which alternatives were available, what the historical actor said, and what remains unknowable.

## 3. Kin-making was an end of labor and a field of seizure

Jacobs repeatedly directs work toward the support, education, and freedom of her children. Her grandmother baked at night to clothe her family. Jacobs worked in the North for her own support and her children's education while producing her narrative around household duties. Freedom in this account is not release from every obligation or all work. It is a change in authority over bodily movement, intimate relation, household labor, a child's future, and the destination of effort.

This evidence strengthens [kin-directed freedom work](../../../../../../glossary/terms/kin-directed-freedom-work.md), but it also limits any celebratory account of family. Slave law made kinship a site of sale, inheritance, coercion, and accumulation. Enslaved people made and defended relations that the legal order could deny, while enslavers exploited care and attachment. Motherhood cannot be presumed to produce political consciousness, and “the Black family” cannot be treated as a timeless structure outside gender, sexuality, violence, and historical change.

Hortense Spillers's later distinction between captive flesh and the socially recognized body, together with her account of ungendering, asks how captivity violently reorganized embodiment and kinship grammar. The concepts are powerful precisely when used as arguments, not slogans. They do not authorize calling every enslaved woman ungendered in the same manner or treating gender as absent from plantation organization. They identify a conflict between imposed availability and socially recognized personhood that particular sources must specify.

## 4. Medical knowledge could extract bodies as evidence

Deirdre Cooper Owens demonstrates that nineteenth-century gynecology depended on racial fictions about Black women's bodies and pain while using enslaved women as experimental subjects. This adds a domain that a plantation-output account misses: bodily capacity could be seized not only for crops, domestic service, or birth, but also for the production of medical knowledge.

Medical extraction is related to labor without being reducible to employment. A patient or experimental subject can be made productive for an institution even when no wage relation exists and no commodity is visibly completed. The medical record can then preserve the physician's technique while reducing the woman whose body made the technique possible to a case, injury, procedure, or name supplied by someone else.

Disability is therefore constitutive rather than supplementary. Moya Bailey and Izetta Autumn Mobley's Black feminist disability framework rejects an able worker as the unmarked human subject. Sami Schalk's [bodymind](../../../../../../glossary/terms/bodymind.md) names the material inseparability of mental and physical processes. For the labor inquiry, bodymind prevents pain, exhaustion, trauma, illness, cognitive difference, and care needs from being treated as external interruptions to an otherwise complete worker. It also prevents slavery's violence from turning disability into a metaphor that erases disabled people's lives.

## 5. The record has testimonial conditions

Every surviving statement has [testimonial conditions](../../../../../../glossary/terms/testimonial-conditions.md): authorship, genre, intended audience, editor or interviewer, time of recording, preservation history, and institutions that authorized the result as evidence. Jacobs wrote deliberately for an abolitionist public and worked with editor Lydia Maria Child. That mediation matters, but it does not make Jacobs absent from her own argument. WPA narratives preserve thousands of recollections, but they were gathered seven decades after emancipation through interviews, transcription, dialect conventions, and federal publication. That history does not make them useless; it identifies what kind of evidence they are.

Kristie Dotson's epistemic violence concerns a hearer's failure, through pernicious ignorance, to meet a speaker's vulnerability in communication. It is especially useful for asking how editors, interviewers, disciplines, and later readers receive Black women's testimony. It should not be applied merely because a desired statement is missing. A gap can result from prohibition, destruction, nonrecording, refusal, lost material, a researcher's search limits, or the absence of the proposition being sought. Those possibilities are not interchangeable.

## 6. Reading a hostile archive requires restraint and pressure

Marisa Fuentes's [reading along the bias grain](../../../../../../glossary/terms/reading-along-the-bias-grain.md) studies how a record's form and distortion disclose the power relations that made it. A court document that barely describes an enslaved woman may still reveal which movements were criminalized, whose injury mattered, and what officials needed to render legible. Reading the bias is not inventing the missing interior life. It is analyzing the document's production as part of its evidence.

Saidiya Hartman's critical fabulation confronts the repeated archival appearance of enslaved women through scenes of violation while refusing the fantasy that a historian can recover everything the archive did not preserve. Critical fabulation is Hartman's particular experimental method, not a decorative synonym for careful research. This note does not perform it. The note instead adopts an [interpretive ceiling](../../../../../../glossary/terms/interpretive-ceiling.md): the strongest conclusion a source can support after its testimonial conditions, genre, mediation, and alternatives have been considered.

An interpretive ceiling does not require timidity. A hostile record can support strong claims about the institution that produced it, even when it supports weak claims about a person's unstated intention. Repeated sale advertisements can demonstrate market classifications without revealing how each advertised person understood freedom. A medical report can demonstrate experimental extraction without preserving a patient's judgment of the procedure. A narrative's explicit moral argument can support more than a practice reported by an enslaver.

## 7. The resulting labor theory

The expanded evidence supports a more exact formulation of labor jurisdiction. Enslaved Black thought contested claims over at least six connected authorities:

1. authority over productive capacity and the result of work;
2. authority over gestation, birth, nursing, and the legal status assigned to children;
3. authority over sexual access and the meaning of constrained exchange;
4. authority to sustain, educate, protect, and remain with kin;
5. authority over pain, treatment, disability, and medical availability; and
6. authority to speak, withhold, interpret, and have testimony received as knowledge.

No single witness states this six-part theory in these words. It is the note's reconstruction across testimony and later Black feminist scholarship. Jacobs explicitly connects sexual coercion, children, work, moral judgment, and freedom; the reconstruction uses that argument as an anchor while marking where Morgan, Davis, Owens, Spillers, Cooper Owens, Dotson, Fuentes, Bailey, and Schalk supply later concepts.

The result also revises the idea that freedom means self-direction by an isolated worker. The evidence points toward relational jurisdiction: bodily and temporal authority exercised amid obligations and dependencies that a person can affirm, contest, or renegotiate. Care is not automatically liberating, because it can be coerced and offloaded. Dependence is not the opposite of freedom, because every life depends on relations and material support. The relevant question is who organizes those relations, under what authority, and toward whose continuance.
