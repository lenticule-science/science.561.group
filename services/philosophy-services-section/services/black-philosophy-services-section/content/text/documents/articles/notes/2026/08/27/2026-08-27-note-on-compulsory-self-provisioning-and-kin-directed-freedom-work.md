## The correction

The initial account of enslaved Black labor thought distinguished labor commanded for an enslaver from work performed during “own time.” That distinction remains necessary but is not sufficient. Work outside the principal field or household shift had at least three different structures:

1. an enslaver could explicitly command a second shift of cooking, spinning, cleaning, or maintenance;
2. an enslaver could withhold adequate provisions and thereby force households to spend residual time reproducing life and labor; or
3. enslaved people could exercise some practical discretion over production, exchange, care, rest, worship, pleasure, or freedom projects.

Only the third structure is fully described by [own time](../../../../../../glossary/terms/own-time.md), and even then the authority was insecure. The first two are better named [compulsory self-provisioning](../../../../../../glossary/terms/compulsory-self-provisioning.md): the displacement of reproduction costs onto enslaved people without surrendering the enslaver's claim to their principal labor.

The same expanded source set also changes the destination of labor. The relevant contrast is not only labor for enslaver versus labor for self. Moses Grandy, Elizabeth Keckley, Lunsford Lane, Harriet Jacobs, and Henry Bibb organize work around spouses, children, parents, and other relatives. [Kin-directed freedom work](../../../../../../glossary/terms/kin-directed-freedom-work.md) names labor and collective effort used to secure relatives' freedom and safety.

## 1. Night labor was not necessarily own time

Solomon Northup described cotton workers returning from a field day that ran from first light until darkness or later, carrying cotton to be weighed, completing assigned chores, grinding corn, cooking supper, and preparing the next day's food. The worker's residence was not outside the labor regime. Plantation extraction depended on an after-hours shift that made the next field day possible ([Northup, *Twelve Years a Slave*](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcmassbookdig.twelveyearsslave00nort/?st=gallery)).

A WPA narrative attributed to Charlie Pye reports an even more explicit command on the Ealey plantation in Georgia. After field labor, women had to spin a specified amount of thread at night; failure brought punishment the next morning. The account also says that Pye's mother plowed and cut logs, while his father made plantation shoes. Gender shaped assignments but did not confine women to domestic work or men to fields. “After hours” identifies clock position, not autonomy ([Federal Writers' Project, Georgia, part 3](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/mss/mesn/mesn-043/mesn-043.pdf)).

Harriet Jacobs's grandmother presents a mixed case. She received conditional permission to bake crackers at night, and the proceeds were required to clothe herself and her children. The activity created market knowledge and a family-directed resource, but it also relieved the enslaver of provisioning costs. The same labor could enlarge a household's practical capacity and subsidize the regime extracting it ([Jacobs, *Incidents*](https://anthology.lib.virginia.edu/work/Jacobs/jacobs-incidents)).

## 2. Residual time must be analyzed along three axes

Calling an interval own time should require evidence on three independent axes:

- **Interval**: had the principal assigned shift ended?
- **Direction**: could the person choose the activity, pace, companions, or rest?
- **Destination**: could the person or community decide how the product, earnings, or experience would be used?

An after-hours spinning quota satisfies only the first axis. A permitted garden whose produce the enslaver claims satisfies the interval axis and perhaps partial direction, but not destination. A clandestine sale can assert direction and destination while remaining exposed to punishment. A dance, visit, worship service, or period of rest may exercise jurisdiction without producing a commodity at all.

This three-axis analysis prevents “own time” from becoming an economic euphemism for the second shift. It also prevents a productivist assumption that the best evidence of autonomy is energetic market activity. Rest, pleasure, sociality, and care can be exercises of [labor jurisdiction](../../../../../../glossary/terms/labor-jurisdiction.md) precisely because the person declines to turn the interval into output for an enslaver.

## 3. Celestia Avery's account shows multiple jurisdictions in one household economy

In a 1937 Georgia WPA interview, Celestia Avery warned the interviewer that her account combined her own childhood memories with information transmitted by her grandmother, Sylvia Heard. That distinction makes the narrative useful without making it contemporaneous testimony from every person described.

Avery reported that enslaved families prepared their own meals, raised vegetables, made cloth, sewed, and maintained cabins after field labor. The Heard plantation required half of what families raised and prohibited sale. Yet people raised and secretly sold chickens, used money to buy fiddles, and held reciprocal quilting gatherings that moved from household to household until each had winter covering ([Avery interview, images 26–29](https://www.loc.gov/resource/mesn.041/?sp=26&st=text)).

No single label adequately describes all of this activity. Meal preparation and necessary clothing production reproduced the labor force. The enslaver's half-share extended extraction into the household economy. Clandestine sale asserted control over product and exchange. Reciprocal quilting directed labor through community obligation. Music and dancing directed earnings toward pleasure rather than subsistence or accumulation. The evidence therefore supports a field of contested jurisdictions, not a clean enclave outside slavery.

## 4. Own time could itself be criminalized

Lunsford Lane hired his time in Raleigh, North Carolina, paying his enslaver between one hundred and one hundred twenty dollars per year while manufacturing and selling tobacco products. Lane explained that state law prohibited an enslaved person from commanding his own time, although Raleigh authorities sometimes tolerated the practice. He recalled another man being seized and hired out for public benefit three successive times for exercising that prohibited control ([Lane, *Narrative*](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/rbc/rbaapc/15410/15410.pdf)).

Lane's evidence sharpens [own time](../../../../../../glossary/terms/own-time.md). The issue was not only leisure or the residue after a task. Command over time allowed contracting, production, saving, movement, and eventually family purchase. Legal suppression targeted a jurisdictional capacity whose economic success threatened the public performance of dependency. Tolerance was conditional: Lane observed that authorities were less likely to interfere when a person appeared not to be accumulating anything.

## 5. Freedom ransom was recursive

Moses Grandy's narrative shows why “self-purchase” is too mild a description. Grandy paid six hundred dollars toward freedom, saw the receipts destroyed, and was sold for another six hundred dollars. After another attempted purchase failed through an enslaver's financial arrangements, he finally obtained legal freedom through a third effort. The repeated payment did not progressively validate ownership; it repeatedly exposed the power to take earnings while withholding release ([Grandy, *Narrative*](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/narrative-of-the-life-of-moses-grandy-1843/)).

This is [freedom ransom](../../../../../../glossary/terms/freedom-ransom.md) with recurrence. Payment bought practical safety only when the coercive party honored it or an enforceable paper survived. The fraud was not an accidental imperfection in an otherwise legitimate labor contract. It was enabled by the premise that Grandy's work, earnings, and person remained claimable until an enslaver chose to relinquish them.

## 6. Freedom work moved through kin and cooperation

Grandy used earnings after his own release to purchase his wife and a son and sought funds for children and other relatives still enslaved. His narrative reports that his daughter Catherine paid twelve hundred dollars for herself, then worked with her sister Charlotte for one wage until they accumulated another twelve hundred dollars for Charlotte. The sisters' arrangement makes labor's destination unmistakably relational: Catherine's own legal freedom enlarged the capacity for a shared emancipation project.

Elizabeth Keckley's dressmaking presents another form of constrained accumulation. Keckley sought freedom for herself and her son, but reported that the Garland household claimed so much of her attention and depended so heavily on her earnings that she could not save the demanded price. Customers later assembled the payment through gifts and loans. Her productive skill simultaneously maintained the enslaver household, created a network of customers, and supplied the means eventually used for family freedom ([Keckley, *Behind the Scenes*](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/keckley-excerpt-from-behind-the-scenes-or-thirty-years-a-slave-and-four-years-in-the-white-house-by-elizabeth-1868/)).

[Kin-directed freedom work](../../../../../../glossary/terms/kin-directed-freedom-work.md) does not mean that family obligation was naturally harmonious or unlimited. Enslavers manufactured the emergency by separating families and pricing reunion. Nor was every freedom project familial. The concept identifies one repeatedly stated destination of labor that an individual account of wages misses.

## 7. Revised labor-regime typology

The evidence now supports at least six relations between work and authority:

1. **Estate-directed compulsory labor**: the enslaver sets activity, pace, and destination.
2. **Compulsory self-provisioning**: workers or households reproduce life outside the principal shift because an enslaver commands the work or withholds adequate provision.
3. **Tolerated own-account work**: the worker exercises limited discretion under revocable permission, fees, shares, or surveillance.
4. **Clandestine own-account work**: the worker claims direction and product without permission, under risk of punishment.
5. **Kin-directed freedom work**: labor, saving, travel, and cooperation aim at relatives' safety or legal release.
6. **Nonproductive jurisdiction**: rest, worship, pleasure, learning, visiting, and refusal assert that time and bodily capacity need not terminate in output.

These relations overlap. A garden can be compulsory provisioning, tolerated property, and a site of clandestine sale. Dressmaking can maintain an enslaver's household and build a freedom fund. A quilting gathering can produce necessary covering and sustain communal pleasure. Classification should follow the specific command, discretion, and destination in evidence rather than the activity's name.

## Source boundary

Lane, Grandy, and Keckley are retrospective published narratives; Grandy's was dictated to an antislavery activist, and publication shaped all three. The Avery and Pye material is more mediated still. Both WPA accounts were recorded roughly seventy years after emancipation; Avery explicitly mixed memory with grandmother-transmitted information, while Pye was nine at emancipation and the interviewer described his memory as hazy. Their testimony is used here to identify arrangements also visible elsewhere, not to supply verbatim collective belief.

The next pass should add testimony in which enslaved women explicitly judge imposed night work, pregnancy, nursing, care, illness, rest, and bodily exhaustion. This note establishes that those relations cannot be treated as secondary details of a wage theory; it does not yet recover enough first-person argument about them.
