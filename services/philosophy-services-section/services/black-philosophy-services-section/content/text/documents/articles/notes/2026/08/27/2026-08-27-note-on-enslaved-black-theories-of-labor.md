## The answer in one sentence

Black people enslaved in the antebellum South did not leave one uniform labor theory, but surviving testimony and practice repeatedly distinguish **labor as a human capacity directed by persons toward sustenance, kin, exchange, craft, and freedom** from **slavery as the violent seizure of the worker, the worker's time, and the products of work**.

That distinction matters. The evidence does not support the claim that enslaved people rejected labor as such. People who slowed compelled work also cultivated family plots at night, hired their skills for wages when possible, made and sold goods, cared for kin, and worked to purchase time or freedom. The recurring objection was not simply *work is bad*. It was that another person claimed unilateral authority over who would work, for what end, at what pace, under what violence, and who would receive the result.

## What can count as a theory here

The archive is structurally unequal. Enslavers produced ledgers, rules, and management prose in abundance. Enslaved people were often prohibited from literacy, and testimony that survives in print was shaped by abolitionist publication, editors, retrospective narration, or interview conditions. A theory therefore cannot be limited to a named treatise. For this inquiry, a labor theory is a sufficiently stable set of judgments about four relations:

1. who has the authority to direct work;
2. who has a moral claim on the time, product, or earnings of work;
3. what obligations work serves; and
4. when refusal, removal, or independent production is justified.

The strongest evidence is a person's explicit argument. Frederick Douglass supplies unusually clear arguments of that kind. Harriet Jacobs supplies explicit narration about work, family obligation, hiring, and self-support. Charles Ball's 1837 narrative supplies detailed observation of wage work and family patches within slavery. The WPA interviews add breadth but require special restraint: the Library of Congress warns that they were recorded seventy years after emancipation, often by white interviewers using leading or unskilled methods, and that written dialect was vulnerable to racial stereotype ([collection limitations](https://www.loc.gov/collections/slave-narratives-from-the-federal-writers-project-1936-to-1938/articles-and-essays/introduction-to-the-wpa-slave-narratives/limitations-of-the-slave-narrative-collection/); [language note](https://www.loc.gov/collections/slave-narratives-from-the-federal-writers-project-1936-to-1938/articles-and-essays/note-on-the-language-of-the-narratives/)). Practice can corroborate an argument, but practice alone does not disclose every participant's reason.

The terms introduced below are therefore marked by status. **Rightful earnings** is close to Douglass's own formulation. **Own time** was period usage and is visible in hiring and task arrangements. **Subsistence counter-appropriation** is this note's analytic name for a moral argument Douglass stated without that label. **Enslaved economy** is a modern descriptive term for independent production under slavery. None should be projected backward as a universal vocabulary shared by all enslaved Black southerners.

The expanded evidence is indexed in a [source dossier](./2026-08-27-source-dossier-on-enslaved-black-labor-thought.md). The dossier separates a witness's explicit proposition from an interpretation of reported practice so that a recurring pattern is not mistaken for a shared historical vocabulary. A companion [note on compulsory self-provisioning and kin-directed freedom work](./2026-08-27-note-on-compulsory-self-provisioning-and-kin-directed-freedom-work.md) corrects the assumption that all after-hours production was own-time work.

A second companion [note on Black feminist labor, reproduction, and epistemic authority](./2026-08-27-note-on-black-feminist-labor-reproduction-and-epistemic-authority.md) revises the inquiry's unit of analysis. It treats productive, reproductive, sexual, domestic, kin-making, and medical appropriation as connected but nonidentical relations, and it gives testimony and archival mediation their own epistemic constraints.

## 1. The worker, not the enslaver, holds moral title to earnings

Douglass's argument begins from agency rather than from the slaveholder's legal title. In Baltimore he found employment, made contracts, performed skilled caulking, collected the wages, and then had to surrender them to Hugh Auld. His sequence is itself a theory of acquisition: “I contracted for it, worked for it, earned it, collected it.” From those acts he concluded that the pay was rightfully his. Auld “had no hand in earning it,” and coercive power was Auld's only title. Douglass therefore called the taking robbery ([*My Bondage and My Freedom*, chapter 20](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/douglas01.asp)).

This is [rightful earnings](../../../../../../glossary/terms/rightful-earnings.md): the worker's activity generates a moral claim that the slaveholder's property law cannot erase. The argument also separates **power** from **right**. Auld could compel payment, but compulsion did not convert taking into entitlement. Slavery appears here not as a labor contract with bad terms but as an order that fraudulently converts force into a property claim.

Douglass also rejected the claim that maintenance paid the alleged debt. Food and clothing did not entitle Auld to the whole wage because Douglass's prior services were already supposed to cover those provisions. The accounting runs against slavery: the enslaver cannot first compel labor, then count minimal subsistence as an advance that makes the worker perpetually indebted, then seize every further earning as repayment.

## 2. A person has a claim to direct their own time

When Douglass negotiated permission to hire his time, the arrangement remained extractive: he carried the risk of finding work, bought his tools, clothed and fed himself, and still owed Auld a fixed weekly payment. Yet Douglass described becoming “master of my own time” as a gleam of hope. Control over contracting, movement, pace, and the residue after payment provided an opening toward escape even though it was not freedom.

[Own time](../../../../../../glossary/terms/own-time.md) names this practical claim to temporal direction. It appears beyond urban hiring. In the Lowcountry task system, completion of an assigned task could establish a boundary between the enslaver's demand and time used for a person's own purposes. Philip Morgan's study describes the task system's characteristic division between the master's time and the enslaved worker's time, while stressing that planters could exploit the arrangement as discipline ([Morgan 1982](https://www.latinamericanstudies.org/slavery/WMQ-1982.pdf)). Charles Ball described enslaved people using Sunday to work for wages and cultivating family “patches” treated in plantation practice as the property of particular families ([Ball, 1837](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/public/gdcmassbookdig/slaveryinuniteds00ball/slaveryinuniteds00ball.pdf)).

The claim was customary rather than secure. Enslavers could withdraw it, law could prohibit it, and the worker often had to perform a second shift to exercise it. “Own time” under slavery was therefore not time outside domination. It was contested time whose use made a limited assertion: the enslaver's demand was not morally infinite.

## 3. Work for family and community has a different end from commanded work

Independent production was commonly directed toward food, clothing, household goods, savings, celebrations, mutual help, and sometimes manumission. Harriet Jacobs recounted that her grandmother obtained permission to bake crackers at night after completing household work, on the condition that the profits clothe herself and her children. The condition benefited the enslaver by transferring provisioning costs, but the grandmother's labor also served a family claim that the enslaver's accounting did not originate ([Jacobs, *Incidents*](https://anthology.lib.virginia.edu/work/Jacobs/jacobs-incidents)).

The [enslaved economy](../../../../../../glossary/terms/enslaved-economy.md)—gardens, poultry, hunting, craft work, market exchange, and self-hire outside required labor—made this rival direction of work material. A Virginia historical synthesis describes enslaved people pushing back against attempts to monopolize their time and labor, passing some property within families, and working after hours to provision children; it also emphasizes that enslavers profited by reducing their own provisioning costs ([Encyclopedia Virginia](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/the-economy-of-the-enslaved-in-virginia/)). Juliana Rose Walker likewise finds economic negotiation, wage earning, and efforts to buy time throughout the WPA testimony, while warning that the resulting autonomy remained commodified and usually enriched enslavers ([Walker 2023](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-history-review/article/this-slavery-business-is-a-horrible-thing-the-economy-of-american-slavery-in-the-lives-of-the-enslaved/A1ABFFD821EA4E3D246070A2EA2B0718)).

The theory implicit in these arrangements is not simple possessive individualism. The product of own-time labor frequently moved toward kin and collective life. Labor's proper end could be provision, care, worship, celebration, or the enlargement of another person's freedom. A wage retained by one worker and food raised for a household both opposed the slaveholder's premise that every productive capacity terminated in the estate.

## 4. Taking necessities can be recovery, not theft

Douglass made the moral reasoning explicit when hunger forced him to take food. He distinguished stealing as such from taking necessities withheld by the person already appropriating his body and labor. Because his work produced the means of subsistence and the enslaver denied him enough to live, Douglass reasoned that supplying himself was “appropriating what was my own.” He later widened the argument: society helped the enslaver rob him of liberty and the just reward of labor, so legality could not settle the moral question ([*My Bondage and My Freedom*, chapter 15](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/douglas01.asp)).

[Subsistence counter-appropriation](../../../../../../glossary/terms/subsistence-counter-appropriation.md) is the analytic term used here for that reasoning. It does not mean that any taking by an oppressed person is automatically just. Douglass himself drew distinctions between taking from an enslaver and taking from unrelated people, and he grounded the former in prior extraction, withheld necessities, and self-preservation. The point is narrower and sharper: property law produced by an extracting order cannot by itself determine which party is stealing.

Henry Bibb independently makes the same bounded distinction. Answering an enslaver's accusation that he had stolen wheat, Bibb said that hungry workers had a moral right to take necessities from the abundance their own uncompensated labor produced. He explicitly distinguished that act from taking a neighbor's property as a free person, which he judged wrong. Bibb therefore strengthens the evidence that this was not merely Douglass's private rationalization: both grounded the claim in prior labor, deprivation, need, and the specific enslaving relation ([Bibb, *Narrative*, chapter 19](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/15398/15398-h/15398-h.htm)).

## 5. Refusal concerns command, pace, and destination—not an incapacity for work

Enslaved workers used absence, slowdowns, feigned illness, tool breaking, negotiation, flight, and revolt to obstruct the labor regime. The Library of Congress summarizes work slowdowns and absenteeism as means by which enslaved workers contested authority and negotiated labor conditions ([Resistance and Abolition](https://www.loc.gov/classroom-materials/immigration/african/resistance-and-abolition/)). Douglass observed the slaveholder's predictable accusation of laziness and reversed it: people denied wages, praise, and any motive but the lash had reason to evade commanded work, while slaveholders who lived from others' labor made “laziness” an apology for brutality.

This yields a theory of [coerced-labor refusal](../../../../../../glossary/terms/coerced-labor-refusal.md). Refusing the enslaver's work was compatible with intense work under self- or kin-directed purposes. The contrast discredits the plantation category of “lazy slave.” What the enslaver measured as deficient work could be a worker's judgment about illegitimate authority, excessive pace, an unjust destination of the product, or the preservation of bodily capacity.

Wartime mass flight to Union lines and withdrawal of labor later supplied the evidence from which W. E. B. Du Bois theorized a “general strike” of the enslaved. That phrase is a retrospective theoretical synthesis, not evidence that antebellum workers generally used the term. It belongs downstream from the practices examined here.

## 6. Skill and work remained human capacities under slavery

Craft knowledge could expand mobility and bargaining power. Douglass's caulking skill enabled him to contract, calculate value, move through Baltimore, participate in Black intellectual life, and finance escape. Blacksmiths, cooks, seamstresses, gardeners, boat workers, carpenters, healers, and other skilled workers used expertise for enslavers, but also for family provision, exchange, reputation, and limited autonomy.

Skill therefore had a double character. It could raise a person's sale price and intensify extraction, while also enlarging capacities the enslaver did not create and could not fully contain. No inference should turn skill into a simple road to freedom. Most skilled people remained enslaved, and privileges were revocable. The defensible proposition is that enslaved Black labor thought did not concede that the social meaning of a capacity was exhausted by its market value to an owner.

Solomon Northup's account of cotton picking on Edwin Epps's Louisiana plantation makes the extractive side more exact. A new picker was driven to the highest output possible on the first day; that output then became the minimum expected thereafter. Patsey's exceptional speed did not give her a claim to the additional product or a shorter day. It exposed her to punishment if she failed to reproduce a much larger quota than other workers. [Productivity ratchet](../../../../../../glossary/terms/productivity-ratchet.md) names this conversion of demonstrated capacity into a permanently enlarged compulsory demand. Under this regime, improvement did not reliably produce rest, higher earnings, or discretion. It disclosed more capacity for the enslaver to seize ([Northup, *Twelve Years a Slave*, chapter 12](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcmassbookdig.twelveyearsslave00nort/?sp=172)).

This evidence qualifies any easy identification of skill with autonomy. Skill sometimes widened mobility in urban hiring and sometimes tightened an output quota in plantation labor. The relevant question is not only whether a worker possessed skill, but who held authority to set the pace, interpret capacity, and receive the gain.

## 7. Gender and reproduction prevent a wage-only account

A theory built only from male wage hiring would reproduce an archival distortion. Enslaved women performed agricultural, domestic, craft, sexual, reproductive, and care labor under overlapping coercions. Slave law made a mother's status descend to her children, converting gestation and birth into accumulation for enslavers. Jacobs's narrative treats the protection and education of children as obligations that contest the enslaver's claim, and her grandmother's night work places family provision within that contest.

The evidence supports a bounded extension: labor's morally proper destination included relations that slavery refused to recognize, especially a mother's claim to care for children who the law treated as saleable increase. The note does not collapse motherhood into labor or presume that every reproductive act was consciously theorized in economic terms. It does insist that any account limited to field output and wages misses how slavery appropriated bodily capacity and how enslaved women directed work toward kin against that appropriation.

Henry Bibb states the connection in unusually compact form. Reflecting on being hired out as a child, he set a right to wages for labor beside rights to one's wife and children, liberty, happiness, and conscience. His list does not isolate a market right from social personhood. It identifies wage seizure, family seizure, and captivity of the person as coordinated denials ([Bibb, *Narrative*, chapter 1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/15398/15398-h/15398-h.htm)). Stephanie Camp's account of enslaved women's “rival geographies” adds a spatial dimension: movement, absence, gatherings, and bodily practices contested an enslaver's effort to contain life as well as labor ([Camp 2004](https://uncpress.org/9780807875766/closer-to-freedom/)). Daina Ramey Berry's “soul value” names another boundary on a labor account: enslaved people asserted worth beyond the wealth that enslavers extracted from Black bodies ([Berry 2021](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0144039X.2021.1896188)). These later analytic terms clarify the record; they are not substitutes for the witnesses' own statements.

## 8. Buying freedom could be judged as ransom rather than legitimate sale

William Grimes escaped slavery, worked for eight years in Connecticut, accumulated property, and then faced re-enslavement when his former enslaver's agents found him. Grimes described being stripped of roughly one thousand dollars in “hard earnings,” left without property while responsible for a family, and compelled to purchase freedom. The labor performed after escape did not become secure merely because it was waged: the continuing slave claim allowed an enslaver to seize its accumulated result ([Grimes, *Life of William Grimes*](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/grimes-life-of-william-grimes-the-runaways-slave-written-by-himself-by-william-1825/)).

Douglass supplied the decisive moral description when friends paid his legal owner after his escape. He treated the payment as “ransom,” comparable to money extorted by a robber, rather than recognition that an enslaver rightfully owned him. [Freedom ransom](../../../../../../glossary/terms/freedom-ransom.md) preserves that distinction. Payment could be a prudent means of securing a person's safety while the recipient's claim remained illegitimate. Self-purchase therefore does not prove consent to self-ownership as a saleable property relation. In these accounts it exposes slavery's recursive extraction: the person or the person's community must transfer value to end a claim that had no rightful foundation.

## 9. Labor jurisdiction joins the claims without reducing them to wages

[Labor jurisdiction](../../../../../../glossary/terms/labor-jurisdiction.md) is this note's synthesis for the rightful authority to decide whether, when, where, at what pace, for what purpose, and toward whose benefit a person's capacities are used. It is not a recovered antebellum phrase. It gathers relations that witnesses often stated separately: Bibb's wages, family, liberty, and conscience; Douglass's contracts, earnings, time, subsistence, and refusal; Jacobs's family-directed night work; Grimes's accumulated earnings and household; and Northup's account of imposed pace.

The term improves on “self-ownership” as the sole umbrella. Self-ownership can reveal slavery's denial of personhood, but it can also make freedom sound like a person privately owning a commodity called the self. Labor jurisdiction instead emphasizes authority within relationships. It includes individual bodily and temporal authority while recognizing obligations to children, spouses, kin, and community that witnesses treated as constitutive of freedom. It also shows why wages alone were insufficient: a worker might receive some pay while an enslaver retained the power to sell a spouse, revoke mobility, enlarge a quota, or seize the worker.

## 10. After-hours work divided into compulsion, limited jurisdiction, and freedom work

The first pass treated family gardening, market production, and other activity after the principal workday as evidence of own time. The expanded evidence requires a sharper division. Northup described cotton workers completing chores, grinding corn, and preparing food after the field day. Charlie Pye's WPA account says women were required to spin a specified quantity after field work and were punished for failure. [Compulsory self-provisioning](../../../../../../glossary/terms/compulsory-self-provisioning.md) names such a second shift: enslavers obtained the principal labor and displaced part of the cost of sustaining workers onto their remaining hours.

By contrast, Lunsford Lane described self-hire as command over his own time and noted that North Carolina law prohibited it. Celestia Avery's retrospective account combined imposed household provisioning with clandestine chicken sales, reciprocal quilting, music, and dancing. These cases show that a residual interval, practical direction over activity, and control of its product are distinct dimensions. Work after sundown was not automatically own time, and own time was not valuable only when used productively.

Moses Grandy and Elizabeth Keckley further show that the destination was not simply “self.” Grandy survived repeated fraudulent purchases of his own freedom, then worked to free his wife, son, and other relatives. His daughters Catherine and Charlotte combined their labor to free Charlotte after Catherine secured herself. Keckley's dressmaking maintained the enslaver household that claimed her while she sought freedom for herself and her son. [Kin-directed freedom work](../../../../../../glossary/terms/kin-directed-freedom-work.md) names this relational destination of labor ([Grandy](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/narrative-of-the-life-of-moses-grandy-1843/); [Keckley](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/keckley-excerpt-from-behind-the-scenes-or-thirty-years-a-slave-and-four-years-in-the-white-house-by-elizabeth-1868/); [Lane](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/rbc/rbaapc/15410/15410.pdf)).

## 11. The reconstructed theory stack

The surviving record supports thirteen connected propositions, with different degrees of explicitness:

1. **Persons are not made rightful property by legal declaration.** This is explicit in freedom narratives and necessary to the remaining claims.
2. **Doing and arranging work can generate rightful earnings.** Douglass states this directly.
3. **Coercive power does not generate moral title.** Douglass states this directly by naming force as the enslaver's only right.
4. **The enslaver's maintenance account does not establish an unlimited labor debt.** Douglass states the accounting argument directly.
5. **A worker has a claim to bounded own time.** Hiring, task completion, customary holidays, and conflict over withdrawal support this claim; its precise formulation varied by region and labor regime.
6. **Labor may properly terminate in subsistence, kin, community, or freedom rather than the estate.** Narratives and the enslaved economy strongly support this proposition.
7. **Taking from an extracting enslaver can be recovery within just earnings rather than theft.** Douglass states a carefully bounded version directly.
8. **Refusal of coerced labor does not imply refusal of productive activity.** The contrast between resisted plantation labor and energetic own-time work supports this proposition.
9. **Greater productivity does not create a greater labor debt.** Northup's account shows the opposite rule imposed by enslavers: demonstrated ability was converted into a larger punishable quota.
10. **Payment for legal freedom can be ransom without recognizing the enslaver's title.** Douglass makes this distinction explicitly; Grimes's loss of accumulated earnings shows its material structure.
11. **Wage, temporal, bodily, familial, spatial, and purposive claims are dimensions of labor jurisdiction.** “Labor jurisdiction” is a modern synthesis of connections made explicitly in Bibb and separately across the other testimony.
12. **After-hours production is not necessarily own-time work.** Northup and Pye show commanded second shifts; Jacobs and Avery show household provisioning that could sustain kin and subsidize slavery simultaneously.
13. **Freedom work can be kin-directed and collective.** Grandy, his daughters Catherine and Charlotte, Keckley, and Lane direct earnings and effort toward relatives' release rather than treating individual purchase as the endpoint.

These propositions resemble later vocabularies of self-ownership, labor entitlement, autonomy, moral economy, reproduction, and refusal. Those comparisons may be useful, but the direction of explanation matters. Enslaved Black thought is not evidence of how closely Black people approximated Locke, Marx, republicanism, or autonomism. It is a source of theory in its own right, formed under the practical problem of a regime that claimed the worker, the work, the time, the product, and the worker's descendants at once.

## Open questions

- How did labor judgments differ between the gang system and task system, city and plantation, Upper South and Deep South, and skilled hiring and agricultural labor?
- Which concepts can be recovered from Black religious speech, work songs, folktales, and naming practices without treating cultural expression as a transparent transcript of belief?
- How did West and Central African labor, property, kinship, and obligation concepts persist or change in particular southern Black communities?
- How did disabled, elderly, young, pregnant, and nursing people judge obligations that plantation accounting measured through productive capacity?
- When did own-time property strengthen collective survival, and when did enslavers successfully convert it into self-provisioning that subsidized slavery?
- What changes when the archive is organized around Harriet Jacobs's family and bodily account rather than Douglass's wage account?

## Source boundary

This note answers a narrow question with an initial source set. Douglass and Bibb provide unusually clear explicit moral propositions but cannot stand for every enslaved person. Jacobs and Keckley correct the wage-centered and male-centered frame but do not exhaust enslaved women's theories. Ball records plantation economic practice through a narrated and edited publication. Northup reports a named woman's conditions without giving access to Patsey's own stated interpretation. Grimes shows how a southern slave claim could appropriate northern fugitive labor. Lane and Grandy represent exceptional purchase and self-hire arrangements rather than general access to those routes. WPA testimony supplies scale and named examples but is retrospective and interview-mediated; the Avery and Pye material is explicitly bounded by age, inherited memory, and interviewer framing in the source dossier. Morgan, Camp, Berry, Walker, and the public-history syntheses supply analytic context; their terms are not silently attributed to antebellum speakers.

The next research batch should privilege enslaved women's direct statements about field pace, imposed night work, pregnancy, nursing, illness, disability, rest, and care. Until then, the thirteen propositions above are a defensible reconstruction, not a claim of consensus.
