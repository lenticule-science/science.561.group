An LLM agent is a large language model placed inside an interleaved observe-reason-act loop, rather than used for single-turn generation. The model receives an observation of some environment (a web page's DOM, a file tree, a game state, a conversation so far), produces a reasoning step and a chosen action in natural language, has that action executed against the real environment through a tool interface, and receives the resulting observation back — repeating until the task ends. Shunyu Yao et al.'s [ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629) (ICLR 2023) is the paper most credited with formalizing this loop for language models, interleaving chain-of-thought reasoning traces with executable actions so that reasoning can update the plan and actions can ground the reasoning in observed state.

The category sits downstream of two much older lines of work. Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig's textbook definition of an agent as anything perceiving an environment through sensors and acting on it through actuators supplies the general frame; the belief-desire-intention architecture of Anand Rao and Michael Georgeff's ["BDI Agents: From Theory to Practice"](https://cdn.aaai.org/ICMAS/1995/ICMAS95-042.pdf) (ICMAS 1995) supplies a much more specific proposal for how an agent should represent its own state and commitments. LLM agents are usually described using this older vocabulary — an agent's prompt-carried state is loosely called its "beliefs," its objective its "goal," its committed next step its "intention" — but they do not implement BDI's formal possible-worlds semantics or its discrete belief-revision and intention-commitment operators; a 2025 survey argues classical frameworks like BDI are "often rhetorically applied but are fundamentally mismatched to the stochastic, non-symbolic, and context-driven nature of LLM-based agents" (Mohamad Abou Ali, Fadi Dornaika, and Jinan Charafeddine, ["Agentic AI: A Comprehensive Survey of Architectures, Applications, and Future Directions"](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10462-025-11422-4), *Artificial Intelligence Review*, published online 14 November 2025; first posted as arXiv:2510.25445, October 2025 — not, as an earlier draft of this entry had it, Wang et al.'s "Multi-Agent Systems: From Classical Paradigms to Large Foundation Model-Enabled Futures," a different survey that does not contain this statement).

Subsequent work extended the basic loop along several axes: tool use proper — Timo Schick et al.'s [Toolformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761) (NeurIPS 2023) has the model teach itself which API calls to insert and where; self-critique — Noah Shinn et al.'s [Reflexion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366) (NeurIPS 2023) stores a verbal critique of a failed trajectory in episodic memory and conditions the next attempt on it; and persistent skill accumulation — Guanzhi Wang et al.'s [Voyager](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16291) (TMLR 2024) builds an ever-growing library of executable skills discovered through an automatic curriculum. Evaluating whether such loops actually work is the purpose of agent benchmarks such as SWE-bench, WebArena, GAIA, and AgentBench, and documenting where they reliably fail — memory, reflection, planning, action, and system-level cascading errors — is the subject of the 2025 AgentErrorTaxonomy survey (Zhu et al., arXiv:2509.25370).
