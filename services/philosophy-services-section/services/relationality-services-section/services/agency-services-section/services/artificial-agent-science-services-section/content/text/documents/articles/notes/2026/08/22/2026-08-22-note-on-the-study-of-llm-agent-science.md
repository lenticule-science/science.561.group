# Study of LLM Agent Science

This note is read for one purpose: to establish, for this sub-literature, which claims trace to peer-reviewed research versus industry-lab preprints and technical reports versus blog and trade commentary, so this library's own agent-operated practice cites the right kind of source.
This field's peculiarity is that much of its load-bearing work is arXiv-preprint or industry-lab tier — benchmarks and architectures released by frontier labs and university groups onto arXiv, sometimes later accepted to a peer-reviewed venue, sometimes not — and the study must label tiers explicitly rather than flattening them into a single undifferentiated "the literature says" register.
Method: web-verified citations.
Every load-bearing claim below carries a markdown-linked source — a DOI, an arXiv ID, an ACL-Anthology-style proceedings link, or a stable URL.
Anything not verifiable to a primary source is marked **[UNVERIFIED]** inline.

Tier key used throughout: **[peer-reviewed]** — accepted at a refereed venue (journal, ICLR/NeurIPS/AAAI-style conference, or a proceedings track with review); **[industry-lab preprint/report]** — released by a corporate or independent AI lab (OpenAI, Meta, METR, Sierra) without independent peer review, whether or not it later appears at a venue; **[arXiv preprint]** — an academic-group preprint with no located peer-reviewed acceptance at time of writing; **[textbook]** — a standard reference text, not a primary research claim.

## 1. The classical lineage: agents as a program category

Michael Wooldridge and Nicholas Jennings's ["Intelligent Agents: Theory and Practice"](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0269888900008122) (*Knowledge Engineering Review*, vol. 10, no. 2, 1995, pp. 115–152) is the founding survey of the field: it defines an agent as a computational system enjoying autonomy, social ability, reactivity, and pro-activeness, and organizes the design space into three families (deliberative/symbolic, reactive, and hybrid architectures). **[peer-reviewed, journal]**

Anand Rao and Michael Georgeff's ["BDI Agents: From Theory to Practice"](https://cdn.aaai.org/ICMAS/1995/ICMAS95-042.pdf) (Proceedings of the First International Conference on Multiagent Systems, ICMAS 1995, AAAI Press) is the canonical statement of the belief-desire-intention architecture: an agent maintains beliefs (a world model), desires (objectives), and intentions (a committed, partially-elaborated plan), with the paper reconciling the decision-theoretic and symbolic-reasoning traditions and the idealized-theory and practical-implementation traditions that had previously run in parallel. **[peer-reviewed, refereed conference proceedings]**

Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig's *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach* frames "agent" even more generally — anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts on it through actuators, evaluated by a performance measure over an environment, and organized along the PEAS (Performance, Environment, Actuators, Sensors) dimensions and a simple-reflex/model-based/goal-based/utility-based/learning agent hierarchy. This is the textbook-tier framing that both the BDI line and later LLM-agent papers implicitly assume when they say "agent" at all. **[textbook]**

## 2. The LLM-agent turn (2022–2023): reasoning-acting loops, tool use, reflection, memory

Shunyu Yao et al., ["ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629) (ICLR 2023; [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=WE_vluYUL-X)), interleaves chain-of-thought reasoning traces with executable actions against an external environment (a knowledge base, a web page, a simulated household), so that reasoning can update the action plan and actions can ground the reasoning in observed state. It was a top-5%-rated ICLR 2023 paper. **[peer-reviewed]**

Timo Schick et al., ["Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761) (NeurIPS 2023), shows a model can self-supervise which API calls to insert into its own generations and where, learning tool use (a calculator, a search engine, a calendar, a translation system) from a small number of demonstrations rather than hand-written heuristics. **[peer-reviewed]**

Noah Shinn et al., ["Reflexion: Language Agents with Verbal Reinforcement Learning"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366) (NeurIPS 2023; [poster](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2023/poster/70114)), has an agent verbally critique its own failed trajectory, store that critique as an episodic-memory text, and condition the next attempt on it — reinforcement learning implemented in natural language rather than in gradient updates. **[peer-reviewed]**

Guanzhi Wang et al., ["Voyager: An Open-Ended Embodied Agent with Large Language Models"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16291) (first posted to arXiv May 2023; accepted to *Transactions on Machine Learning Research*, March 2024, [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=ehfRiF0R3a)), builds a Minecraft agent around an automatic curriculum, a persistent skill library of executable code, and an iterative GPT-4-in-the-loop prompting mechanism, and is presented as the first LLM-powered lifelong-learning embodied agent to acquire skills without human demonstration. Because TMLR review is lighter-weight than a selective conference and the paper circulated and was cited for nearly a year as an arXiv preprint before that acceptance, this one straddles the peer-review line. **[peer-reviewed (TMLR), formerly arXiv preprint]**

Memory architectures are the weakest-anchored piece of this section. Charles Packer et al., ["MemGPT: Towards LLMs as Operating Systems"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08560) (arXiv:2310.08560, October 2023), proposes virtual-context management modeled on OS paging — moving information between an LLM's limited context window and external storage via self-directed function calls, with interrupts standing in for OS-style context switches. No peer-reviewed venue acceptance was located for this paper at time of writing. **[arXiv preprint]** Whether this specific architecture (rather than the general problem of externalized agent memory) is now load-bearing for production agent memory systems is **[UNVERIFIED]**.

## 3. Agent benchmarks

Carlos Jimenez, John Yang, et al., ["SWE-bench: Can Language Models Resolve Real-World GitHub Issues?"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770) (ICLR 2024; [proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/edac78c3e300629acfe6cbe9ca88fb84-Abstract-Conference.html)), evaluates models on 2,294 real GitHub-issue-to-patch problems drawn from 12 popular Python repositories, graded by whether the generated patch makes the repository's own test suite pass. **[peer-reviewed]**

John Yang, Carlos Jimenez, et al., ["SWE-agent: Agent-Computer Interfaces Enable Automated Software Engineering"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15793) (NeurIPS 2024; [proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/5a7c947568c1b1328ccc5230172e1e7c-Abstract-Conference.html)), is the companion system paper: it argues the interface an agent uses to touch a codebase (a custom edit/search/execute interface, not a raw shell) materially changes solve rate on SWE-bench, independent of the underlying model. **[peer-reviewed]**

Shuyan Zhou et al., "WebArena: A Realistic Web Environment for Building Autonomous Agents" (arXiv:2307.13854; ICLR 2024), ships four self-hostable, fully functional website categories (e-commerce, a Reddit-style forum, GitLab, a CMS) plus utility tools and knowledge resources, with 812 long-horizon natural-language tasks graded by execution-based checks on end state rather than surface-form action matching; the best evaluated agent (GPT-4 plus chain-of-thought) solved 14.41% of tasks against a 78.24% human baseline. **[peer-reviewed]**

Grégoire Mialon et al., ["GAIA: A Benchmark for General AI Assistants"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12983) (ICLR 2024, [poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2024/poster/18176)), poses 466 questions requiring reasoning, multi-modal handling, web browsing, and tool use that are conceptually simple for humans (92% human accuracy) yet were hard for GPT-4-plus-plugins at the time of publication (15%). **[peer-reviewed]**

Xiao Liu et al., "AgentBench: Evaluating LLMs as Agents" (arXiv:2308.03688; ICLR 2024, [proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/e9df36b21ff4ee211a8b71ee8b7e9f57-Paper-Conference.pdf)), spans eight distinct multi-turn environments — operating system, database, knowledge graph, digital card game, lateral-thinking puzzles, household tasks (ALFWorld), web shopping (WebShop), and web browsing (Mind2Web) — and finds a large gap between top proprietary models and open-source competitors of the time. **[peer-reviewed]**

Shunyu Yao, Noah Shinn, et al., ["τ-bench: A Benchmark for Tool-Agent-User Interaction in Real-World Domains"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045) (first posted arXiv:2406.12045, June 2024, from Sierra Research; accepted to ICLR 2025, [proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/1b126cc38b8638e07bef37e7b2bb72bf-Abstract-Conference.html)), simulates dynamic multi-turn conversations between an LM-simulated user and an agent that must follow domain-specific policy while calling APIs, scored by whether the resulting database state matches an annotated goal state; even GPT-4o-class agents succeeded on under 50% of tasks and showed high pass^k variance across repeated trials. This paper circulated for roughly seven months as an industry-lab preprint before its ICLR 2025 acceptance, correcting an earlier draft of this note that had marked it as unreviewed. **[peer-reviewed (ICLR 2025), formerly industry-lab preprint]**

## 4. Documented failure modes

Karthik Valmeekam, Subbarao Kambhampati, et al., ["PlanBench: An Extensible Benchmark for Evaluating Large Language Models on Planning and Reasoning about Change"](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/7a92bcdede88c7afd108072faf5485c8-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks.html) (NeurIPS 2023, Datasets and Benchmarks Track), adapts International-Planning-Competition-style domains (Blocksworld and a obfuscated-vocabulary variant, Mystery Blocksworld) to test whether LLMs can construct and verify plans and reason about the effects of actions, rather than pattern-match against commonsense scenarios that could be answered from world knowledge alone; the line of work (continued in Valmeekam et al.'s later evaluations of o1 on PlanBench) has repeatedly found LLM planning performance degrading sharply once superficial linguistic cues are removed. **[peer-reviewed, refereed benchmarks track]**

Thomas Kwa et al., ["Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499) (METR, arXiv:2503.14499, March 2025), defines a 50%-time-horizon metric — the human-professional completion time of the longest tasks a model can complete autonomously with 50% reliability — over a 170-task suite (HCAST, RE-Bench, and 66 novel shorter tasks) spanning seconds to tens of hours, and finds this horizon has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019, with a possible acceleration to every four months in 2024; the paper attributes the trend chiefly to reliability, error-adaptation, reasoning, and tool-use gains rather than raw task competence. METR is an independent AI-evaluation nonprofit, not an academic peer-reviewed venue; no peer-reviewed acceptance was located for this specific paper at time of writing. **[industry-lab technical report]**

Kunlun Zhu et al., ["Where LLM Agents Fail and How They Can Learn From Failures"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25370) (arXiv:2509.25370, submitted September 2025; under review at ICLR 2026 per its [OpenReview listing](https://openreview.net/forum?id=PFR4E8583W)), proposes AgentErrorTaxonomy, a five-dimension classification of agent failure (memory, reflection, planning, action, and system-level/cascading failure) grounded in a newly annotated dataset of failure trajectories (AgentErrorBench) drawn from ALFWorld, GAIA, and WebShop rollouts, and reports that a debugging framework built on the taxonomy improves task success by up to 26% relative to the strongest baseline. As of this note's writing the paper is a preprint under review, not yet an accepted peer-reviewed publication. **[arXiv preprint, under review]**

The general claim that per-step error rates compound multiplicatively across a long-horizon trajectory — so that a model reliable on short tasks degrades toward near-systematic failure as horizon length grows, and multi-agent pipelines can compound errors across agents in ways a single-agent sequential model does not capture — is consistent with both the METR time-horizon result above and the AgentErrorTaxonomy's system-level failure category, but no single peer-reviewed paper stating the general compounding-error law as such was located; treat the general formulation as **[UNVERIFIED]** pending a peer-reviewed anchor, even though its two supporting empirical results individually verify.

## 5. Did classical concepts survive contact with the LLM era?

Partially, and mostly as vocabulary rather than as architecture. Zixiang Wang et al., ["Multi-Agent Systems: From Classical Paradigms to Large Foundation Model-Enabled Futures"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18133) (first posted arXiv:2604.18133, April 2026; accepted to *IEEE/CAA Journal of Automatica Sinica*, vol. 13, no. 5, pp. 1007–1023, May 2026, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/JAS.2026.126113)), reviews classical multi-agent systems across perception, communication, decision-making, and control, against LLM-foundation-model-enabled systems that "lift collaboration from low-level state exchanges to semantic-level reasoning." **[peer-reviewed (journal), formerly arXiv preprint]** An earlier draft of this note misattributed the following direct statement of discontinuity to Wang et al.; it in fact belongs to a different paper. Mohamad Abou Ali, Fadi Dornaika, and Jinan Charafeddine, ["Agentic AI: A Comprehensive Survey of Architectures, Applications, and Future Directions"](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10462-025-11422-4) (*Artificial Intelligence Review*, published online 14 November 2025; first posted [arXiv:2510.25445](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.25445), October 2025), states plainly that classical frameworks such as the BDI model or perceive-plan-act-reflect loops "are often rhetorically applied but are fundamentally mismatched to the stochastic, non-symbolic, and context-driven nature of LLM-based agents." This is the clearest verified statement of discontinuity found for this note. **[peer-reviewed (journal)]**

Reading the two eras against each other: BDI's belief/desire/intention decomposition survives as loose descriptive vocabulary — an LLM agent's prompt-carried scratchpad state is sometimes still called its "beliefs," its objective its "goal" or "desire," its committed next-step plan its "intention" — but the formal BDI logics (the possible-worlds semantics Rao and Georgeff built their architecture on, and the interpreter cycle that selects and commits to intentions against a belief revision function) are not implemented by any of the LLM-agent systems surveyed in Section 2; ReAct, Reflexion, and Voyager instead run an interleaved prompt-observe-act loop over natural-language state with no discrete belief-revision or intention-commitment operators. Classical agent communication languages (FIPA-ACL and its performative-based message types — inform, request, propose) also did not carry forward as the substrate of LLM multi-agent coordination: current LLM multi-agent systems typically exchange free-text or JSON-schema-constrained natural-language messages, and 2025-era interoperability protocols (Model Context Protocol, Agent Communication Protocol, Agent-to-Agent Protocol, Agent Network Protocol) are new designs built around tool-calling and semantic content rather than adaptations of FIPA's speech-act formalism. An earlier draft of this note attributed this protocol grouping to a survey by Wang et al.; on inspection neither Wang et al. nor Abou Ali et al. (the two surveys cited above) discusses these four protocols, so the grouping and the non-adaptation claim both rest on no source verified in this note and are marked **[UNVERIFIED]**; a dedicated protocol-history search (a candidate primary source is arXiv:2505.02279, "A survey of agent interoperability protocols," not independently verified here) would be needed to ground this claim properly.

## 6. Relation to this library's own practice

This library is maintained by LLM agents running multi-agent study-and-verify workflows of exactly the kind Section 2's systems and Section 5's survey describe: a reasoning-acting loop over tools (file search, web search, file writes), episodic reflection carried in notes like this one rather than in weights, and coordination between agent instances that has no FIPA-ACL-style formal performative structure underneath its natural-language task handoffs. The tier-labeling method used throughout this note — distinguishing peer-reviewed results from industry-lab preprints and technical reports from unreviewed arXiv postings — is exactly the discipline the `news-media-science-services-section`'s [accountable-agentic-newsrooms memo](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/semiotics-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/undated/accountable-agentic-newsrooms.md) needs for its editorial-gate designs: an agentic newsroom that cannot tell a peer-reviewed SWE-bench result from a same-week uncorroborated preprint claim about agent capability cannot design a sound human-accountability boundary around what it publishes. The failure-mode literature in Section 4 (particularly the memory/reflection/planning/action/system taxonomy and the long-horizon error-compounding evidence) is the most directly transferable finding: an editorial gate for agent-produced copy should assume that reliability degrades with trajectory length and that errors compound across pipeline stages, which argues for gates placed at multiple points in an agentic newsroom's pipeline rather than a single end-of-pipeline check.

## 7. Gaps carried forward

- No peer-reviewed (rather than arXiv-preprint) source was found stating the general long-horizon error-compounding claim as a formal result; the two supporting studies (METR time-horizon, AgentErrorTaxonomy) are individually verified but the synthesis is **[UNVERIFIED]**.
- MemGPT-style memory architectures remain arXiv-preprint tier with no located peer-reviewed acceptance; whether a peer-reviewed successor or a formal comparison of memory architectures exists is unresearched here.
- τ-bench's peer-review status was re-checked during the 2026-08-22 verification pass: it was accepted to ICLR 2025 past its June 2024 arXiv posting and should now be cited as peer-reviewed rather than industry-lab-preprint tier.
- The claim that FIPA-ACL performative structure was not adapted (rather than independently reinvented) by 2025-era agent-interoperability protocols (MCP, ACP, A2A, ANP) was found during the 2026-08-22 verification pass to rest on no source cited in this note at all — the protocol grouping had been misattributed to Wang et al., whose paper does not discuss these protocols — so both the grouping and the non-adaptation claim remain **[UNVERIFIED]** and need a dedicated protocol-history search (arXiv:2505.02279 is an unverified candidate starting point).
- Voyager's straddling of arXiv-preprint and TMLR-peer-reviewed status (nearly a year between the two) is noted but not resolved into a single clean tier; readers citing it should specify which version (May 2023 preprint or March 2024 accepted version) they mean.
- No classical contract-net or mechanism-design-in-MAS literature was covered in this pass — the section README lists it under multi-agent science scope, but this note's coverage list (from its authoring instructions) was single-agent-weighted; a follow-up note should cover the classical coordination canon and the empirical LLM multi-agent-coordination literature the README also assigns to this section.
