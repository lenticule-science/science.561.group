A game is a voluntary activity conducted within agreed-upon boundaries — [rules](./rules.md), a playing space, a duration — where participants make choices that affect outcomes. The definition is loose on purpose: it must cover chess, tag, poker, and tabletop role-playing without collapsing into a list of specific [mechanics](./mechanic.md). What separates a game from other rule-governed activities (a recipe, a liturgy, a legal proceeding) is that the rules exist to create a space of meaningful choice rather than to produce a predetermined result.

Four features recur across activities widely recognized as games: rules constrain the space of legal actions; objectives give direction within it; uncertainty makes choices matter; and voluntariness — the [lusory attitude](../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/lusory-attitude.md) — distinguishes games from coercion. The full treatment, with worked examples and the boundary cases (puzzles, labor, open-ended play), is the lesson [What is a Game?](../../documents/articles/teaching-articles/references/reference-on-game.md). The published definitions this framework draws on run from Suits's constitutive-rules analysis through Caillois's classification to Salen and Zimmerman's artificial-conflict definition and Juul's classic game model.

A game in progress is described by its [game state](./game-state.md); its participants are [players](./player.md); the structured activity of engaging in games at all is [gaming](./gaming.md), and its unstructured parent is [play](../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/play.md).
