Player types are Richard Bartle's four categories of MUD player, from "Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs" (1996): achievers, who "regard points-gathering and rising in levels as their main goal, and all is ultimately subservient to this"; explorers, who probe the world's mechanics for their own sake; socialisers, for whom the game is a setting for relationships; and killers, who impose themselves on other players — "the more massive the distress caused, the greater the killer's joy at having caused it." The taxonomy sits on two axes: whether a player's interest points at other players or at the world, and whether their mode is acting-upon or interacting-with. The types echo Caillois's categories without reducing to them — the achiever's ladder is [agon](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/social-theory-science-services-section/services/ludics-science-services-section/services/game-ludics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/agon.md) formalized, the killer needs real distress the way [deep play](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/social-theory-science-services-section/services/ludics-science-services-section/services/game-ludics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/deep-play.md) needs real stakes — but Bartle's cut is by orientation, not by structure of play.

The paper's real content is dynamical, and it is only visible in a [persistent world](./persistent-world.md). Because the population never resets, the four types form a standing ecology: achievers breed killers "in a typically Malthusian fashion"; too many killers drive off the achievers they feed on and then starve out themselves; socialisers exhibit both positive feedback (they attract each other) and a fatal negative one (they attract killers, risking "a downward spiral in numbers"); and "massively increasing the number of explorers is the only way to reduce the number of killers without also reducing the player numbers in other groups." Bartle identifies three stable configurations — a killer/achiever game-like world, a pure-socialiser world, and a balanced world he considers "extraordinarily difficult to achieve and maintain" because it demands sustained player management "that isn't usually available to MUD administrators." Balancing a world is thus population dynamics, a governance problem rather than a design problem.

Bartle's later work roots all four types in one soil. *Designing Virtual Worlds* (2003) argues that identity-construction is "the fundamental, critical, absolutely core point of virtual worlds... It's why achievers achieve, explorers explore, socializers socialize, and killers... batter you with a stick," and distinguishes three depths of self-investment: the avatar (a doll), the character (a simulacrum), and the persona — "you're not assuming an identity, you are that identity... if you're killed in a fight, you don't feel that your character has died, you feel that you have died." Persistence deepens the investment mechanically: "the longer you play a character, the more attached to it you become... you would be very reluctant to throw it all away. Instead, you ride out the storm." A world that never resets is one where a self, once grown, becomes too costly to discard — which is where the stakes of [mimicry](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/social-theory-science-services-section/services/ludics-science-services-section/services/game-ludics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/mimicry.md) quietly stop being mimicry.
