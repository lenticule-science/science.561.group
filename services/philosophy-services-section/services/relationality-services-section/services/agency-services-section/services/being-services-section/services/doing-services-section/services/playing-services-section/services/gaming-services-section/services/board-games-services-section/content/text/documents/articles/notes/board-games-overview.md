Audience: readers who want a structural introduction to board games before studying specific traditions.

Learning goal: explain what makes board games distinct within the games module and identify how public position creates strategy.

## Worked example: one move changes the whole position

Imagine a simple grid board with one token on the center space and four open neighboring spaces. If a player moves a blocking piece onto one of those neighboring spaces, the position changes immediately for everyone. No hidden card has been drawn. No die result has rewritten the state. The change is visible on the board, and every later move must now respond to that new arrangement.

This is the board-game logic in miniature. A move matters because it changes a shared spatial field. Players reason from position: where pieces are, which spaces are occupied, which routes remain open, and which local relations can spread into larger advantages.

Board games are games organized around a persistent shared surface. That surface may be a [grid](../../../glossary/terms/grid.md), a track, a network of connected spaces, or a map with named regions. What unifies the family is not one exact shape of board but the fact that play depends on visible positional relations.

This makes board games one of the clearest forms of public [game state](../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/game-state.md). In many card games, important information remains hidden in players' hands. In many role-playing games, part of the state lives in a shared fiction. In board games, the board itself usually carries the most important information. That visibility changes the kind of judgment the game rewards: route planning, enclosure, timing, blocking, local tactical reading, and whole-board evaluation.

Most board games use [pieces](../../../glossary/terms/piece.md) that occupy, move between, or are placed onto spaces. The meaning of a move depends on [adjacency](../../../glossary/terms/adjacency.md), movement permissions, and board topology. On a square grid, orthogonal neighbors matter differently than diagonal ones. On a track, the important question is sequence. On a regional map, the important question may be borders and access points rather than equal-sized spaces.

These differences produce recognizable structural families inside board games:

- Race structures reward efficient movement along a path or track.
- Territorial structures reward enclosure, influence, or control of zones.
- Conflict structures reward attack, defense, blocking, and positional exchange.

Many named games combine more than one of these. [Weiqi](../../../../../services/weiqi-services-section/content/text/glossary/topics/weiqi.md) is territorial at the strategic level and tactical at the local level [@britannica2026go]. *Chess* is a conflict-centered positional game with differentiated pieces [@britannica2026chess]. *Pachisi* treats the board mainly as a race path [@britannica2026pachisi]. *Monopoly* turns board movement into economic encounter [@britannica2026monopoly]. That is one reason board games are analytically useful: a single visible surface can support many kinds of pressure at once.

## Exercises

1. Why does a board game's visible state often make spatial judgment more important than hidden-information inference?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Because the key information is on the board where all players can inspect it. Strategy therefore depends more on reading position, routes, control, and timing than on guessing unseen holdings.
</details>

2. What is the structural difference between a grid board and a track board?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A grid emphasizes neighboring spaces and multidirectional relation. A track emphasizes ordered sequence along a route, usually with fewer meaningful directional choices at each step.
</details>
