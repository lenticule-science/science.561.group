A virtual economy is the system of scarcity, production, and exchange inside a persistent game world — and the literature's founding document is a postmortem of one failing. Zachary Booth Simpson's "The In-game Economics of Ultima Online" (1999) reports from inside Origin: UO launched with a closed-loop economy, a fixed stock of resources meant to circulate, and it failed not through inflation but through hoarding — resources flowed into player inventory "and just sat there," the spawn-bank dried up, and "the world became boring and the players began to complain loudly." The fix introduced the vocabulary the field still uses: a faucet (resources created from nothing at a steady rate) and drains or sinks (points where value is destroyed). Simpson is explicit that sink-design is political, not technical — decay, taxes, and maintenance fees work, but "feel too much like real world 'work' and are therefore politically unacceptable." His second finding: NPC shopkeepers running a simulated supply-and-demand economy went broke, because players produce regardless of demand (skills improve by use), so "central planning doesn't work."

Edward Castronova's "Virtual Worlds" (2001) made these economies legible to economists by simply measuring one. Surveying EverQuest's Norrath (3,619 respondents plus scraped currency and avatar auctions), he found a currency trading at $0.0107 — "higher than the Yen and the Lira" — an in-world hourly wage of $3.42, a GNP per capita of $2,266 that would rank Norrath "the 77th richest country in the world, roughly equal to Russia," chronic deflation from items that never decay, and wealth inequality worse than any post-industrial society on Earth. His definition of the object of study turns on persistence: "the program continues to run whether anyone is using it or not." Lehdonvirta and Castronova's later textbook (*Virtual Economies*, 2014) draws the monetary conclusion flatly — "both the US Dollar and World of Warcraft coins are fiat money" — while insisting on the design difference: a virtual economy is not built for efficiency but to "attract users, satisfy them and make the publisher... money"; its scarcities (travel time, cooldowns, drop rates) are the product, not friction.

The mature form of this is the operated economy. CCP hired an economist, Eyjólfur Guðmundsson, in 2007 to instrument EVE Online — publishing quarterly economic reports on the reasoning that "just as entrepreneurs and executives rely on real-world economic indicators, EVE Online players need timely information and analysis of the in-game economy" — and his team's monitoring apparatus doubled as an anomaly detector, discovering that bots comprising 2% of players consumed 30% of server CPU. GTA Online runs the monopoly version: the publisher is the only retailer, prices everything, and sells the currency itself (Shark Cards), with Rockstar's design director candid about how unprepared they were to operate rather than ship a world: "GTA Online was slowly failing — and quickly losing players — right up until the middle of 2014." A virtual economy is thus a standing rebuttal to the sealed [magic circle](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/social-theory-science-services-section/services/ludics-science-services-section/services/game-ludics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/magic-circle.md): its values price through to real money whether the operator permits it or not, which is where [deep play](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/social-theory-science-services-section/services/ludics-science-services-section/services/game-ludics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/deep-play.md) becomes a market condition rather than an anomaly — and managing that porosity is a permanent occupation of the [persistent world](./persistent-world.md)'s operator, not a design decision made once.
