The lusory attitude is Bernard Suits's name for the stance that makes game-playing possible: a player's willing acceptance of a game's constitutive rules — rules that forbid the most efficient means to the game's end — precisely because accepting them brings the game into being. A golfer could carry the ball to the hole; the rules forbid it; the golfer accepts the prohibition not as an obstacle to golf but as what golf is made of. Suits compresses the whole analysis into one line: playing a game is the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles.

The concept does real analytical work at the boundaries of [play](./play.md). Voluntariness alone does not separate a game from a chore done willingly; the lusory attitude adds that the constraints themselves are embraced for the sake of the activity they constitute. When the attitude is withdrawn — the rules obeyed only under compulsion, or circumvented whenever unobserved — the mechanical structure of the game may persist while the playing has stopped.

For artificial agents the notion sharpens into an engineering question. An agent does not obviously "accept" a rule; it produces actions. The working reformulation in current multi-agent research is lusory competence: the agent's policy remains inside a game's constitutive constraints even when shortcuts, ambiguity, or unmonitored affordances are available. The gaming section's survey of formal ludic theory ([2026-06-26](../../../../services/gaming-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/06/26/2026-06-26-survey-of-ludic-theory-frontiers.md)) treats this as one of the field's four hard problems.
