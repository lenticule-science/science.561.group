## Why this is central

The Honinbo house is the institutional root of the professional Japanese
tradition this survey covers. Founded in 1612 by the Buddhist priest
Nikkai (who took the pavilion name "Honinbo" as his own), it operated
through the *iemoto* hereditary-headship system common to Japanese
traditional arts, passing through 21 successive heads over three
centuries and standing as the strongest of the four state-sponsored Go
houses (Honinbo, Hayashi, Inoue, Yasui) of the Edo period. The 21st head,
Honinbo Shusai, transferred the title to the Nihon Ki-in in 1938-1940,
converting a hereditary lineage into an annual tournament — still one of
Japan's most prestigious professional titles today. This is the
institutional continuity [National and Historical Play Styles](../research-articles/national-and-historical-play-styles.md)
§1 names as the source of the territory-first, solidity-favoring
orthodoxy that Shin Fuseki (1933) and the cosmic style (mid-twentieth
century) each in turn pushed against.

## What it does not settle

The article documents institutional history — founding, succession,
dissolution, tournament conversion — not a strategic doctrine. It names
no specific opening theory, shape preference, or move-level style
attributable to "the Honinbo house" as such; the territory-first
orthodoxy this survey attributes to the pre-1933 Japanese tradition is
drawn from [shinfuseki-wikipedia](shinfuseki-wikipedia.md)'s account of
what Shin Fuseki reacted against, not from this article directly.
