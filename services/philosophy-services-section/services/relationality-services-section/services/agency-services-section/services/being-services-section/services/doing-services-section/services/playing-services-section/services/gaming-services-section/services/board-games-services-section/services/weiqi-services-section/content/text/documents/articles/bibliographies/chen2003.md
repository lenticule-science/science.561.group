## Why this is central

This is the founding neuroimaging study of Go: an fMRI companion piece to
the same research group's chess study (`10.1016/S0926-6410(02)00207-0`),
measuring cortical activation while subjects played Go. It found enhanced
activation across dorsal prefrontal, parietal, occipital, posterior
temporal, and primary somatosensory/motor areas, with a modest right-
lateralization in parietal activation — an early empirical anchor for the
now-common claim that Go engages visuospatial and pattern-recognition
circuitry more heavily than the verbal/sequential circuitry associated
with chess. It is the earliest and most-cited entry in the Go-neuroscience
literature that [kim2025](kim2025.md)'s 2025 review synthesizes, and every
later study cited alongside it here ([jung2013](jung2013.md),
[sohn2017](sohn2017.md)) builds on the basic finding that expert Go play
recruits a distinct, measurable cortical network.

This section's own claim that Go "emphasizes global strategy more than
local battle" (see [Weiqi/Go App Landscape](../notes/2026-08-29-note-on-go-app-landscape.md)'s
framing of the game's difficulty for computer programs, and the whole-
board-versus-regional-reading distinction [Obsmetrologia and Go Strategy](../research-articles/obsmetrologia-and-go-strategy.md)
conjectures a formal measure for) has an independent empirical echo here:
the parietal and occipital activation this study reports is consistent
with the visuospatial, whole-board pattern processing that global
strategic reading is thought to require.

## What it does not settle

This is a task-based activation study with a small subject pool, typical
of early-2000s fMRI work — it identifies *where* activity differs during
play, not what that activity computes, and it makes no claim about
executive function, training-induced plasticity, or long-term structural
change (those are the questions [jung2013](jung2013.md),
[sohn2017](sohn2017.md), and [kim2025](kim2025.md) take up).
