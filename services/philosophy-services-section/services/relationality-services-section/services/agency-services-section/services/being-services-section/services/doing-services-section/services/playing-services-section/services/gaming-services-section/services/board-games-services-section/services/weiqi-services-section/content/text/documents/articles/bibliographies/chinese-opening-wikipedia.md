## Why this is central

The Chinese opening is the section's verified source for the specific,
named Chinese fuseki pattern this survey's task scope calls for. Despite
its name, the article records that it was originally used by the
Japanese player Hajime Yasunaga and only later introduced to Chinese Go,
where Chen Zude pioneered it in top-level play (professional adoption
peaking in the 1990s in games such as Nie Weiping's, per this survey's
independent [nie-weiping-wikipedia](nie-weiping-wikipedia.md) verification).
Its defining move — Black at moves one, three, and five, in high or low
variants — is "distinguished by rapid development on the side, rather
than making a corner enclosure, inviting White to start an invasion."
This is a distinct whole-board shape from both pre-1933 territorial
orthodoxy (which takes the corner directly) and Shin Fuseki (which plays
the center point): it develops the sides quickly and deliberately leaves
a corner open to invitation.

## What it does not settle

The article describes a specific joseki-adjacent opening pattern, not a
national playing style as such — "Chinese opening" names one fuseki
choice available to any player of any nationality, and its professional
adoption in Japan "from about 1970 onward" shows it was not
nationally bounded even in practice. This survey's use of it to
characterize a broader "Chinese tradition" (§2) leans on
[nie-weiping-wikipedia](nie-weiping-wikipedia.md) for the fighting-style
component the opening pattern alone does not establish.
