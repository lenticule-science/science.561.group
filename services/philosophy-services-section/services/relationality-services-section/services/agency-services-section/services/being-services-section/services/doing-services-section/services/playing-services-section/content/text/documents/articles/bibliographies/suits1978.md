## Why this is central to the framework

Suits supplies the section's sharpest definitional instrument: playing a
game is "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles." The
analysis decomposes game-playing into prelusory goal, lusory means,
constitutive rules, and the [lusory attitude](../../../glossary/terms/lusory-attitude.md)
— the player's acceptance of less efficient means because that acceptance
is what brings the game into being. This is the only classical account
precise enough to survive translation into agent engineering: "lusory
competence" (a policy that stays inside constitutive constraints even
when shortcuts are available) is Suits's attitude restated as a property
of an artificial agent, and the section's frontier survey treats it as
one of the field's four hard problems.

## What it does not settle

Suits argues, against Wittgenstein's family-resemblance treatment, that
games admit a real definition — but the book's definition covers
game-playing, not play. Free play (paidia without structure) falls
outside it, and Suits says little about the social and cultural life of
games: why these obstacles, for whom, sustained by what institutions.
Those questions belong to Huizinga, Caillois, and the play-anthropology
line carried in social-theory custody.
