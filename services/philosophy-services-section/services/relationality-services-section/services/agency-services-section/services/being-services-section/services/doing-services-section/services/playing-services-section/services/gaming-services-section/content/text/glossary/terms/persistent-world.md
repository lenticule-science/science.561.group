A persistent world is a game environment that continues to exist and develop when nobody is playing it. Richard Bartle's *Designing Virtual Worlds* (2003) makes persistence one of five co-equal necessary conditions for a virtual world — automated physics, a single persistent character per player, real time, shared, and persistent: "the environment continues to exist and develop internally... even when there are no people interacting with it." A chat room fails the physics condition; a first-person shooter match fails persistence. Mark W. Bell's synthesis definition (2008) — "a synchronous, persistent network of people, represented as avatars, facilitated by networked computers" — glosses what persistence does to the player's standing: "No longer is one participant the center of the world but a member of a dynamic community and evolving economy. A participant has a sense the systems in the space... exist with or without a participant's presence." That sentence marks the pivot from a game, which is built around a player, to a world, which merely includes one.

What persistence removes is the exit. An ordinary game has a final position: the match ends, the [magic circle](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/magic-circle.md) dissolves, and whatever was at stake inside it is released. A persistent world has no final position — Raph Koster's law for operators is blunt: "Once you open your online world, expect to keep your team on it indefinitely. Some of these games have never closed." Koster states the categorical claim in its strongest form: "It's a SERVICE. Not a game. It's a WORLD. Not a game. It's a COMMUNITY. Not a game." (The claim is Koster's; Bartle argues the same point obliquely, through a grid that cross-tabulates how much players can change a world against how much of that change survives a reboot, and reads the grid as answering "Whose world is it?") Because nothing resets, populations develop standing dynamics that single-session games cannot host — Bartle's 1996 player-type paper is remembered as a taxonomy (achievers, explorers, socialisers, killers) but its payload is ecological: the types breed, starve, and drive each other off over months, "in a typically Malthusian fashion," which makes balancing a world a matter of population management rather than level design.

Persistence also changes what an ending is, by relocating it. Celia Pearce's ethnography of the Uru diaspora follows a ~350-member community whose world was shut down under them in 2004: members wept as the avatars froze at midnight, then migrated as an intact group — a "fictive ethnicity" — across four other virtual worlds over eighteen months. The world ended; the play did not. That decoupling of the world's death from the community's closure is something no model of games as bounded episodes predicts, and it is one face of a general pattern: in a persistent world, consequences, attachments, and institutions accumulate without a terminal state to discharge them, which is why such worlds grow governance ([god-problem](./god-problem.md)), property regimes ([virtual-property](./virtual-property.md)), and economies ([virtual-economy](./virtual-economy.md)) that a finite game never needs.
