## 0. Why this survey exists

Owner's pressure, 2026-09-03: a claimed lineage — the
[Note on Lakota Weiqi Strategy](../notes/2026-09-04-note-on-lakota-weiqi-strategy.md)'s
hypothesis that traditional Lakota spatial logic and tactical tradition,
mapped onto the 19x19 grid, would produce a measurable, distinctive
signature — can only be measured *against* something. That note's own §3
names two predecessors, Shin Fuseki and Takemiya Masaki's cosmic style,
but leaves them citation-marked "verify before public use." This survey
verifies them, and gathers the rest of what the weiqi world's actual
national and historical play styles are, sourced, so a future signature
computation has real comparison points rather than a hand-picked pair.

Every claim below carries the source that supports it, checked by
[WebSearch](https://en.wikipedia.org)/WebFetch against the live page, not
recalled from training. Where a claim this survey wanted could not be
sourced this way, it is not asserted — §8 lists what was looked for and
not found, rather than silently omitting it.

## 1. The Japanese professional tradition

Japan's professional Go tradition runs through the four state-sponsored
Go houses of the Edo period (Honinbo, Hayashi, Inoue, Yasui), of which
the [Honinbo](../bibliographies/honinbo-wikipedia.md) house — founded
1612 by the priest Nikkai, run through 21 hereditary heads (the *iemoto*
system) until its 1938-1940 transfer to the Nihon Ki-in — was the
strongest and longest-lived, converting from a hereditary lineage into
the annual tournament title still contested today
[honinbo-wikipedia](../bibliographies/honinbo-wikipedia.md). Three
centuries of that lineage established the orthodoxy every later
movement below is defined against: corner first, side second, center
last, valuing the corner because the board's own edges do the enclosing
work for free.

**Shin Fuseki, 1933.** Kitani Minoru and Go Seigen broke this order
directly: playing the center point as an opening move, building
[thickness](../../../glossary/terms/thickness.md) and
[moyo](../../../glossary/terms/moyo.md) in the early game rather than
enclosing territory
[shinfuseki-wikipedia](../bibliographies/shinfuseki-wikipedia.md). Go
Seigen himself called it "a step on the way to the perfecting of go,"
crediting it with freeing players' creativity from fixed *joseki*. The
movement's own intensity was short — "three or four years" of radical
experimentation before its usable ideas were selectively absorbed into
mainstream theory — but it permanently expanded what counted as a
reasonable opening, and named the two players this section's own
[fuseki](../../../glossary/terms/fuseki.md) and
[influence](../../../glossary/terms/influence.md) terms already cite.

**Takemiya Masaki's cosmic style (*uchu-ryu*).** Trained under Kitani
Minoru from 1965, Takemiya built the direct successor to Shin Fuseki:
large, center-facing frameworks (*sanrensei* — three star-point
openings) rather than early territorial enclosure
[takemiya-wikipedia](../bibliographies/takemiya-wikipedia.md). The
source is explicit on a point this survey's §6 table depends on: Takemiya
is known for a "very thick" playing style whose sanrensei openings
"involve plenty of fights" — whole-board and fighting, but **thick**,
not light.

**Otake Hideo's thickness style.** From the same Kitani school as
Takemiya, Otake built a career (44 titles, four Meijin among them) on
what commentators call the "Otake aesthetic": thick, tasteful shapes
favoring aesthetic harmony over aggressive confrontation
[otake-wikipedia](../bibliographies/otake-wikipedia.md). Where Takemiya's
thickness serves a whole-board framework, Otake's serves local solidity
and avoids the fighting Takemiya's sanrensei invites — two different uses
of the same underlying value.

## 2. The Chinese tradition

**The Chinese opening.** Despite the name, this specific fuseki pattern
(Black at moves one, three, and five, developing a side rapidly rather
than enclosing a corner, deliberately inviting White to invade) was
first used by the Japanese player Hajime Yasunaga and only later
pioneered in top-level Chinese play by Chen Zude, with professional
adoption in Japan itself from about 1970 onward
[chinese-opening-wikipedia](../bibliographies/chinese-opening-wikipedia.md).
It is a third whole-board shape distinct from both pre-1933 territorial
orthodoxy (corner directly) and Shin Fuseki (center point): rapid side
development that leaves a corner deliberately open.

**Nie Weiping's fighting style.** China's dominant player of the 1980s
China-Japan Supermatches, Nie Weiping (1952-2026) built his reputation
by repeatedly defeating leading Japanese professionals — including his
own former teacher, Fujisawa Hideyuki — as the last Chinese player
standing, earning the nickname "Steel Goalkeeper"
[nie-weiping-wikipedia](../bibliographies/nie-weiping-wikipedia.md). That
reputation is this survey's direct evidence of a semeai-*engaging*
rather than semeai-*avoiding* signature — the source does not use the
word "fighting" as a stylistic label, but a player whose defining
achievement is repeatedly surviving and winning decisive capturing races
under pressure is not avoiding them.

## 3. The Korean tradition

**Cho Hun-hyun, the founding figure.** Professional at nine in 1962,
first Korean 9-dan in 1982, 150 professional titles — more than any
other player in the game's recorded history — Cho Hun-hyun began
mentoring Lee Chang-ho in 1984, the direct teaching line this survey
traces into the next generation
[cho-hunhyun-wikipedia](../bibliographies/cho-hunhyun-wikipedia.md). A
Go Magic retrospective on his own account of his career names two
distinct style periods, not one
[cho-hunhyun-gomagic](../bibliographies/cho-hunhyun-gomagic.md): an
early **"Swallow" style** — "light, sharp, leaving groups dangling while
seizing territory elsewhere only to return and narrowly escape death" —
later succeeded by the **"God of War,"** "a flame-thrower who balanced
aggression with patience," after Lee Chang-ho's own methodical,
territory-securing play "clipped the Swallow's wings." The same source
frames this as one instance of a national pattern: Korean Go developed
by "breaking one by one the rules and taboos defined by the players in
Japan," treated from the start as "a competitive brain sport" driven by
"a strong instinct for survival and the thirst for victory" rather than
by the accumulated courtesies of the Japanese professional tradition.

**Lee Chang-ho's endgame.** Cho's most famous pupil, world #1 by ELO
rating from 1991 to 2006: "his tendency to prioritize securing his
groups before going on the offensive contributes to his unusually strong
endgame attacks, as they gain power from solid groups"
[lee-changho-wikipedia](../bibliographies/lee-changho-wikipedia.md). This
is the solidity-first pole of the Korean tradition — closer to Honinbo
orthodoxy in temperament than to Cho's own fighting reputation, though
reached by a different route.

**Lee Sedol.** Professional at twelve in 1995, nicknamed "The Strong
Stone," known for a self-described "very weak" opening compensated by
middle-game tactical brilliance, and for a famous 2003 "broken ladder"
formation that defied conventional wisdom and won
[lee-sedol-wikipedia](../bibliographies/lee-sedol-wikipedia.md). His
2016 loss to AlphaGo (4-1, with one win featuring a move widely called
"a divine move") is this section's own anchor for the AI era below.

## 4. The AI era's revision of all three

[silver2016](../bibliographies/silver2016.md) (AlphaGo, 2016) and
[silver2017](../bibliographies/silver2017.md) (AlphaGo Zero, 2017)
established the tree-search-plus-network architecture;
[wu2019](../bibliographies/wu2019.md) (KataGo) is its accessible,
self-play-trained descendant this section already depends on for engine
review. All three are engineering and machine-learning results, silent
on strategy vocabulary by their own account — the strategic *effect* is
documented separately, and this survey adds one specific, sourced
tactical claim to what this corpus already names in general terms.

**The 3-3 invasion and the devaluation of moyo.** Before AlphaGo, an
early 3-3 invasion under a naked 4-4 (*hoshi*) stone was taught as a
beginner's error — the thickness White gains from the standard *joseki*
response was regarded as clearly worth more than Black's small corner.
AlphaGo's own play reversed this within its own games, not merely in
commentary: an early 3-3 invasion appeared in only two of sixty
Master-series games, then in roughly forty of a subsequent fifty-game
series — rare to routine within the same engine's play
[alphago-33invasion-usgo](../bibliographies/alphago-33invasion-usgo.md).
Leading professionals including Park Junghwan (Korea) and Ke Jie (China)
adopted the same early invasion in their own tournament play afterward.
This is a devaluation of moyo *relative to direct invasion*, not a
demonstration that whole-board framework play is worthless — the two are
easy to conflate and this survey states them separately on purpose.

**Ke Jie's own account.** After his 2017 loss to AlphaGo, Ke Jie
"fundamentally reconsidered the game," adopting a more whole-board
perspective — the source names AI's *tenuki* (playing elsewhere at a
locally critical moment) specifically as one adopted tactic — and
incorporating AI-discovered patterns into a hybrid classical/AI-era
approach: "the possibilities of Go are immense," in his own words
[kejie-gomagic](../bibliographies/kejie-gomagic.md). This is evidence
the AI era changed a leading professional's own play, not only
after-the-fact commentary about professional consensus in the abstract.

## 5. Documented regional or club styles

The search for named regional or club-level styles beyond the individual
players above returned an institutional fact but no sourced style claim:
the Kansai Ki-in, Japan's second Go organization, split from the Nihon
Ki-in in 1950 over post-war discrimination against players from the
Kansai region (Osaka) — a real institutional distinction — but no source
found in this pass characterizes Kansai Ki-in play as stylistically
different from Nihon Ki-in play. This claim is not made; it is listed
under §8, not verified.

## 7. How a signature is measured

This survey states expected values qualitatively because no instrument
yet computes them from real games. The instrument this corpus already
has, in proposal form, is
[Whole-Board Reading as a Gluing Problem, and Lineages as Signatures](whole-board-reading-as-gluing-lineages-as-signatures.md)
§1-3: a player's move stream, read against the readout Chu space of
[Readouts as a Chu Space, Coordination as Cohomology](readouts-as-a-chu-space-coordination-as-cohomology.md)
§1, is a distribution over which readouts get settled, held open, or
left contested — a **lineage**, on that reading, is a cluster in that
distribution space, and teaching is moving a student's own signature
toward a lineage's. The same article's "one consequence" (§3) states the
testable form directly: two players confirmed as sharing a teacher
should project to closer distributions, on average, than either projects
to an unrelated player — a within-lineage-versus-between-lineage
separation computable once the instrument exists and real games exist to
run it on.

This survey's table is therefore not itself a signature in that sense —
it is the closest a documentary literature review can get without the
instrument, and its role is to give that instrument, once built, real
comparison points: named historical clusters whose combination of
features a computed Lakota signature could be measured against for
distance, rather than compared only to a hand-picked pair.

## 8. Which styles the Lakota hypothesis is nearest to

Stated plainly, against the sources above, not against memory:

- **Nearest on the whole-board, center-first axis** — Shin Fuseki (1933)
  and Takemiya's cosmic style. Both directly match §1's "Sacred Hoop"
  prediction (center-facing, whole-board framework over corner-first
  enclosure), and both are already named in
  [Note on Lakota Weiqi Strategy](../notes/2026-09-04-note-on-lakota-weiqi-strategy.md)
  §3.
- **Nearest on shape lightness and sacrifice** — young Cho Hun-hyun's
  "Swallow" style, *not* either Japanese predecessor. This is the
  survey's one genuinely new finding: Takemiya and Otake's schools are
  both **documented as thick**, the opposite of the "Mobility, Light
  Shape, and Dispersion" and "Decoys, Feigned Retreats, and Sacrifices"
  paragraphs of §1 of the Lakota note. The Swallow style's "light,
  sharp, leaving groups dangling... to return and narrowly escape death"
  is the closer documented match on exactly those two coordinates.
- **Nearest on forcing and initiative** — mature Cho Hun-hyun's "God of
  War" style and Ke Jie's post-AlphaGo hybrid approach, both described
  as balancing aggression with patience or judgment rather than pure
  force.
- **Furthest — the clearest documented opposite pole** — Nie Weiping and
  Lee Sedol. Both built their reputations specifically on entering and
  winning capturing races and "showdown" fights, the direct inverse of
  §1's "Ethos of Counting Coup" (semeai avoidance in favor of forcing
  moves that constrain without destroying).

**The combination is the unattested part.** No single sourced style
above combines whole-board center-first play *and* light, sacrifice-
favoring shape *and* semeai avoidance at once — the two traditions that
each supply one half (Japanese cosmic-style thickness for the
whole-board axis; Korean early-Cho lightness for the shape/sacrifice
axis) are historically distinct lineages that, on the sources here,
diverge from each other on exactly the thickness/lightness question.
This sharpens, rather than answers, [Note on Lakota Weiqi Strategy](../notes/2026-09-04-note-on-lakota-weiqi-strategy.md)
§3's own conclusion: the hypothesis's claim is a specific *combination*
of features no single verified historical school is shown to hold
together, which is exactly what would make a real Lakota signature,
once computed, distinguishable from any one of its named predecessors
taken alone.

## Not verified

Looked for, and not asserted above because no source was found:

- A quantified encirclement ratio (net/geta captures versus direct
  ladder chases) for any named style or player.
- A quantified sacrifice rate for any style except the qualitative
  "Swallow" description above.
- A quantified whole-board coupling measure (in the readout-pullback
  sense of §7) for any historical style — this requires the instrument
  §7 names, which does not yet exist.
- A stylistic distinction between Kansai Ki-in and Nihon Ki-in play
  (§5) — the institutional split is sourced; a style difference is not.
- A move-level (rather than reputational) account of Nie Weiping's or
  Lee Sedol's actual game statistics.
- Any club-level (as opposed to national or individual-player) style
  beyond the Kansai Ki-in institutional split named in §5.
