An idle game is a game designed around the player's absence. The working definition in the design-research literature (Alharthi et al., 2018, relayed through the CHI PLAY 2019 interview study "'It Started as a Joke': On the Design of Idle Games"): idle games "require players to leave the game and return to it at a later, unspecified point, where the game progresses on its own during that time and opens up different options for further play upon return." The genre's origin is Progress Quest (2002), a game that plays itself entirely — the player only "rolls" a character and watches — and its lineage runs through Cow Clicker (2010), Cookie Clicker (2013), and alarm-games like Dreeps, back to Conway's Game of Life as the ur-case of the zero-player game. The designer of Kittens Game states the phenomenology exactly: "You are, essentially, not a center of the world but a spectator, world goes on with or without you." Ian Bogost locates the novelty in the temporal structure: "It's not just the asynchrony between players... but it's asynchrony with the state, with the game itself. Like, you're there and then you're not and you're there again."

Sonia Fizek's theoretical treatment names the pleasure involved: interpassivity, Robert Pfaller and Slavoj Žižek's term for delegated enjoyment — the VCR that "passively enjoys for me," canned laughter, the prayer wheel. In an idle game "the player assumes a role of a playing subject, but to a large degree escapes the pressure of the actual play, giving up their agency and delegating it to the clicker algorithms." Fizek builds on Alexander Galloway's "ambience acts" — the moments a game plays itself while awaiting the player — but distinguishes idle games proper by consequence: in an ordinary game the idle animation "has no effect," while in an idle game the changes that accrue in the player's absence are permanent and significant. This tests the standard definitional boundary of play itself — Björk and Juul's line that "unless players have some agency to affect the outcome of a game and can intentionally exercise it, they are not really... playing a game" — since the idle game's player exercises agency chiefly by deciding when not to.

The same delegation appears inside persistent multiplayer worlds as automation: Stefano De Paoli's study of MMORPG bots describes a "deskilling of players with the transfer of human skills to automatic-play software" and a converse "enskilling of machines," framed through Marx on industrial automation — the grind continues, executed by software, while the nominal player is elsewhere. And the genre carries a live ethical argument about endings: several idle-game designers deliberately engineer a terminus into an otherwise endless form — Frank Lantz gave Universal Paperclips "a start, a middle and an end... and then, at the end, it's done and it lets you go" — explicitly against the unbounded engagement loop, a designed closure imposed on a form whose default, like the [persistent world](./persistent-world.md)'s, is to never close. Where [paidia](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/social-theory-science-services-section/services/ludics-science-services-section/services/game-ludics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/paidia.md) hardens into [ludus](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/social-theory-science-services-section/services/ludics-science-services-section/services/game-ludics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/ludus.md) by acquiring rules, the idle game does something stranger: it keeps the rules and sheds the player.
