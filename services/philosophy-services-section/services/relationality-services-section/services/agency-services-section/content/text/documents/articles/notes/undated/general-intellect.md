# general-intellect

General intellect is Marx's term — appearing in the famous "Fragment on Machines" in the *Grundrisse* (composed 1857–58, first published 1939–41) — for the socialized knowledge, scientific capacity, technical skill, and accumulated cooperative intelligence of humanity considered as a *direct productive force*.
In a brief but consequential passage, Marx anticipates that as production becomes increasingly machine-mediated, and as machinery comes to embody more and more scientific and technical knowledge, the operative productive force ceases to be the labor time of the individual worker and becomes the general intellect of the social cooperation that produced both the machinery and the knowledge it embodies.

The Fragment makes a connected sequence of claims.
As capitalist production develops, the share of living labor in any given commodity falls and the share of "dead labor" — capital, machinery, scientific knowledge built into the production apparatus — rises; the worker increasingly tends and supervises machinery rather than directly making the product.
The knowledge embodied in that machinery is not the product of any individual worker but of the general intellect, the cumulative socialized knowledge of humanity developed through cooperative scientific, technical, and educational activity that vastly exceeds what any individual capitalist could organize.
As this proceeds, Marx suggests, the measure of value by socially necessary labor time becomes increasingly incongruent with the actual productive process, in which the relevant force is the general intellect rather than the measurable labor time of identifiable workers — and this incongruence prepares conditions in which capitalist measurement of value becomes obsolete and a different organization of social production becomes thinkable.

The passage was largely neglected until the Italian operaismo and post-operaismo traditions seized on it from the 1960s onward as the textual warrant for their analysis of cognitive capitalism.
In their reading, the conditions Marx projected into an indefinite future have arrived: contemporary production depends increasingly on the general intellect — the educational, scientific, communicative, affective, and cooperative capacities of socialized humanity — as a direct productive force.
Capital appropriates value generated by this general intellect without organizing or paying for the activity that produces it: the university, the household, the social network, the language community, the broader cultural life of society develop the capacities the firm subsequently exploits.
The value of contemporary informational and immaterial commodities, on this view, cannot be reduced to the time any identifiable worker spent producing them, and the labor theory of value's individual-time measure is correspondingly in crisis.
Paolo Virno, Hardt and Negri, and Carlo Vercellone are the leading elaborators; Vercellone's periodization (mercantile, industrial, cognitive capitalism) is the most developed, with cognitive capitalism the regime in which the general intellect becomes the dominant productive force.

This appropriation is best understood as a kind of enclosure of a knowledge commons: capital fences off and extracts value from a cooperatively produced, socially held capacity it neither created nor can fully control — the informational counterpart of the enclosure of common land.
That framing also marks where the political stakes lie: it points toward what the tradition calls the *multitude*, the cooperative-cognitive subject that produces the general intellect and could, in principle, reorganize production around its own self-organization rather than capital's appropriation.

The appropriation of the Fragment is contested, and the contest is part of the concept's life.
Critics note that it is a single brief passage in unpublished notebooks, not integrated into the mature *Capital*, so reading it as the foundation of a whole program may overweight it; that the cognitive-capitalism thesis treats the knowledge-worker as paradigmatic when industrial, agricultural, extractive, and care work still occupy most of the global workforce; and that the multitude is too diffuse a political subject.
As a node, the general intellect is to be distinguished from labor-power (the individual worker's capacity, sold through the wage) and from human capital (knowledge treated as an individual investment returning to the individual): it is the *socialized* knowledge that exceeds any individual and accrues, under present conditions, to capital.
It is the substrate on which immaterial labor draws and the ground on which the post-operaismo analysis of contemporary work is built.
