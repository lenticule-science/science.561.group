# culture

Culture is the totality of a people's practices, meanings, institutions, symbols, and ways of being — their language, religion, art, law, kinship, food, architecture, humor, grief, and the unspoken rules that govern all of these.
It is among the most contested words in the language; Raymond Williams, in *Culture and Society* (1958) and later in *Keywords*, called it one of the two or three most complicated words in English, and traced its long migration from "cultivation" — of soil first, then of the mind — to its modern meaning as a whole way of life.
Generated by: community — culture is a community's meaning-making, the sense it makes of itself and its world, carried in everything its members do together.

Two insights about culture matter most for the concepts that depend on it.
The first is that culture is not a thing but a process.
Clifford Geertz, in *The Interpretation of Cultures* (1973), defined culture as the "webs of significance" that human beings themselves have spun and within which they are suspended — not a set of objects (songs, costumes, rituals, artifacts) but the ongoing activity of making and interpreting meaning.
Studying a culture, for Geertz, is "thick description": reading the dense layers of significance that make a wink different from a twitch, a ceremony different from a routine.
Culture lives in the interpreting, not in the inventory of things interpreted.

This matters because the dominant way of misrepresenting a culture is to treat it as a collection of extractable features: a dress, a dance, a tale, a dish.
That operation converts process into product — it freezes a living practice into a consumable image.
The dirndl is not Bavarian culture; a Kurdish bard's performance is not Kurdish culture.
Each is one artifact lifted out of an ongoing process of collective meaning-making, then codified, circulated, and sold until the image comes to stand in for, and finally to replace, the living process it was taken from.
Cultural commodification and the manufacture of "folk" authenticity both work this way: by mistaking — or deliberately presenting — the artifact for the process.

The second insight is that culture is always multi-voiced.
Any living culture contains competing voices, internal disagreements, rival visions of the good life, generational conflicts, and tensions that are never finally resolved.
A culture reduced to a coherent checklist of features — "Kurdish culture is mountain warrior plus tribal honor plus the spring festival" — is no longer a culture but a genre: single-voiced where culture is many-voiced, stable where culture is in motion, consumable where culture is contested.
The reduction from culture (plural, arguing, alive) to genre (singular, fixed, marketable) is the characteristic violence done to the cultures of dominated peoples.

This is why ethnocide — the destruction of a culture while leaving the population physically alive — depends on understanding culture as a living process rather than a stock of objects.
What ethnocide kills is not a set of artifacts, which can sit harmlessly in a museum, but a people's capacity to go on making meaning on their own terms.
Within the reciprocal relations, culture is what community generates and what tradition transmits across generations; collective memory is the means by which it holds its past; and identity is the self-understanding that members form within it.
Each of these is a face of the same living, reciprocal sense-making, and each is endangered by the same operation that converts the living process into a frozen image.
