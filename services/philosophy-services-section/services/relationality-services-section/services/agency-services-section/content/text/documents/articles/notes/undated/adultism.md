# adultism

Adultism is the systematic discrimination against young people by adults.
The term was given its current sense by the psychologist Jack Flasher in a 1978 article, where he defined it as the abuse by adults of the greater power they hold over children.
It names not individual acts of unkindness but a structural condition: the set of behaviors, policies, and assumptions through which adults exercise largely unquestioned authority over young people, and through which the perspectives, capacities, and interests of children are treated as subordinate to those of adults.
Generated by: age-hierarchy — adultism is the concrete practice through which age-based domination operates day to day.

Adultism manifests across institutions.
In families it appears as the assumption that a parent's authority over a child's life needs no justification beyond the parent's age.
In schools it structures the teacher-student relationship as one of command and compliance rather than dialogue.
In legal systems it underwrites the exclusion of young people from voting, contracting, and other forms of political participation.
In the economy it justifies restrictions on youth labor while the unpaid domestic and educational labor of children goes uncounted.
What unifies these is a single operating assumption: that childhood is a deficit of personhood, a not-yet-fully-human condition whose holders need not be consulted about their own lives.

The concept is built on a deliberate comparison to racism, sexism, and other systemic oppressions.
Like those, adultism works through both explicit rules — age-based legal restrictions — and implicit norms, such as the background assumption that a child's opinion is automatically less valid than an adult's.
It is reinforced by a fact peculiar to age: because every adult was once a child and lived through age-based subordination, adults tend to treat that subordination as a normal and inevitable feature of growing up rather than as a political arrangement that could be otherwise.
This naturalization is what makes adultism unusually hard to bring into view as domination at all.

Adultism is best understood alongside its three siblings under age-hierarchy.
Misopedy names the ideological antipathy toward children and childhood; childism, following Elisabeth Young-Bruehl, names the prejudice against children as a group; adult-supremacy names the political arrangement in which adults monopolize power.
Where misopedy names the attitude, childism the prejudice, and adult-supremacy the political structure, adultism names the discriminatory practice — the concrete ways in which adult power is exercised to constrain the lives of the young.
The distinction matters for response: ending a prejudice and dismantling a set of institutionalized practices are different kinds of work.
