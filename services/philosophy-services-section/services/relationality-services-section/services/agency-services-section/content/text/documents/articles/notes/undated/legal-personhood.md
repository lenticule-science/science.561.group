# legal-personhood

Legal personhood is the status of being someone the legal system can see — an entity that can hold rights, bear obligations, sue and be sued, and appear in legal proceedings.
Generated by: legal personhood is the status law confers and withholds, the threshold category that sorts the social world into entities the legal order recognizes and entities it cannot.
The sociological interest lies not in the doctrinal details of who qualifies but in what the sorting *produces*: a division of the world into the visible and the invisible, the standing and the without-standing, drawn by law and enforced through every proceeding that turns on who is allowed to speak.

The category is not natural but constituted, and its boundaries have always been a site of struggle.
Historically, whole classes of human beings were placed outside full legal personhood — the enslaved, treated as property rather than persons; women, whose legal identity was for centuries absorbed into their husbands' under coverture; children and colonized peoples, granted only a diminished standing.
The struggle for emancipation, suffrage, and civil rights has in large part been a struggle over who counts as a full legal person.
At the same time, the law has extended personhood *beyond* human beings: in American law, corporations, trusts, governmental bodies, and other entities are legal persons, able to own property, enter contracts, and litigate in their own name — corporate personhood being the most consequential legal fiction the category contains.

This extension creates a structural asymmetry between institutional and non-institutional forms of social life.
An incorporated organization can sue, be sued, hold property, and assert its interests in its own name; an unincorporated community, an informal network, a neighborhood, or a subcultural scene cannot.
Such communities are not legally *prohibited* — they are legally *invisible*.
The legal system has no category for a mutual-aid network or a local culture as such; it has categories only for the organizations that claim to represent them.
This invisibility is a specific demand for legibility: the system does not so much refuse to see informal communities as require them to become something it can see, and the path to recognition runs through incorporation, formalization, and institutional structuring — the very processes that transform an informal community into a monitorable entity.

The consequences reach into how legal proceedings reshape social life more broadly.
Because only legal persons can appear, contest, and accumulate the credentials that courts treat as evidence of good faith, the incentives created by litigation fall entirely on incorporated, registered, accredited entities; the informal communities that may have originated a practice have no standing to contest its appropriation, because they have no standing at all.
Legal personhood thus operates as the gateway through which governance reaches social life: the state does not govern communities directly, it governs the legal persons that mediate between it and communities, and the pressures of compliance and formatting fall on those mediating institutions and, through them, on the people they claim to serve.

Within this cluster legal personhood is the foundational sorting that law performs before any right can be held or any due process owed: it determines who can hold rights, who can claim the protections of procedure, and who can even enter the legal record.
It is intimately tied to legal fiction (its most striking product, the corporate person) and to legal formatting (the demand that social life take an administrable, recognizable shape), and it marks the quiet boundary at which the legal order decides whose existence it will acknowledge.
