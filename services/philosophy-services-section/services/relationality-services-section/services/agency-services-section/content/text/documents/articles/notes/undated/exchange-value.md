# exchange-value

Exchange-value is the proportion in which use-values trade for one another: one coat for twenty yards of linen, one hour of a lawyer's time for three hours of a cleaner's.
Marx introduces it in the opening chapter of *Capital*, Volume I, distinguishing it from use-value in order to show that the commodity is not a simple useful thing but a social form carrying a definite set of relations within it.
Generated by: **commodity** — exchange-value is the aspect of the commodity that faces the market, the form a product takes once it is made to be sold rather than used.

The central insight is that exchange-value only *appears* to be a property of the commodity itself, as though a coat simply "is worth" twenty yards of linen the way it is warm or grey.
In fact ratios of exchange express relations among producers, not among things.
The question Marx poses, and which organizes his whole analysis, is what makes qualitatively different use-values commensurable at all — what allows a coat and a quantity of corn, which have no physical property in common, to be set equal in exchange.
His answer is abstract labor: labor considered not as the specific skilled activity of weaving or farming but as undifferentiated human effort, measured by time.
Exchange-value is the form in which this abstract labor appears on the market; the magnitude of a commodity's value corresponds to the socially necessary labor time required to produce it.

It is worth being careful about what this does and does not claim.
Marx is not saying that prices are fixed mechanically by labor time — supply, demand, competition, and monopoly all swing prices around their values.
He is making a deeper claim about *form*: that beneath the fluctuating prices of the market lies a system in which the products of many private, independent labors are continually equated and exchanged, and that this equation is how a society of separate producers coordinates its labor without anyone planning it.
The market is not lying about value; it is producing a specific kind of social truth, one that renders the labor and the laborers behind each commodity invisible behind a price.

The dominance of exchange-value over use-value is historically specific, not a universal feature of human economies.
It belongs to commodity production, where goods are made for sale rather than for direct use.
In economies organized through reciprocity or the commons, the meaning of an exchange is not exhausted by a price: a gift carries obligations that outlast the giving, and a shared resource carries responsibilities to those who share it and to those who come after.
The market relation, by contrast, closes at the point of transaction — once the price is paid, the relation ends, leaving no obligation and no trace.

As a node, exchange-value is the bridge between the commodity and the system of value, and the ground on which the commodity's characteristic illusions stand.
Because exchange-value presents a social relation among producers as a quantitative property of a thing, it is the immediate basis of commodity-fetishism, the appearance that value inheres in objects, and through it of reification more broadly.
To analyze exchange-value is to begin pulling apart the seamless surface of price to reveal the human labor and social relations that the price both expresses and conceals.
