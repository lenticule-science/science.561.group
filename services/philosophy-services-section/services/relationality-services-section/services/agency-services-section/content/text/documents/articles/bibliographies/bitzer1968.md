# Bitzer 1968 — "The Rhetorical Situation"

Lloyd F. Bitzer, "The Rhetorical Situation," *Philosophy and Rhetoric* 1, no. 1 (1968): 1–14. The article that introduces the *rhetorical situation* as the unit of analysis in rhetorical theory and reorients the discipline from the rhetor's intentions to the situation's demands.

## What it establishes

Bitzer's central thesis is **discourse-as-response**: rhetorical discourse comes into existence as a response to a situation in the same sense that an answer comes into existence in response to a question. The situation precedes and calls forth the discourse; the discourse is judged by its *fittingness* to the situation rather than by the rhetor's autonomous artistic choices.

The article specifies the situation's structure as a **triple**:

1. **Exigence.** An imperfection marked by urgency — a defect, an obstacle, a thing waiting to be done. An exigence is *rhetorical* iff it is in principle modifiable by discourse (a hurricane is not a rhetorical exigence; the inadequate response to a hurricane is).
2. **Audience.** Not the empirical receivers of the discourse but the persons capable of being influenced by the discourse and capable of mediating change in the exigence. An audience must be both *addressable* and *effectual*.
3. **Constraints.** Persons, events, objects, and relations with the power to influence the audience and to channel the rhetor's available means. Constraints partition into those *given* (audience beliefs, available facts, historical context) and those *contributed* by the rhetor (character, evidence, style).

A discourse is **fitting** to a situation iff it engages the exigence in a way that can move the audience within the constraints. Fittingness is the model's normative notion.

## Bitzer's three theses

The article also commits to three substantive claims that together constitute the model's structural commitment:

1. **Rhetorical discourse comes into existence as a response to a situation** (not as the rhetor's autonomous creation).
2. **A speech is given rhetorical significance by the situation** (not by the speaker's intent).
3. **Situations are real, and rhetorical** (situations exist independently of rhetors' perceptions of them).

The third thesis is the strongest and the most contested. Vatz (1973) attacked it directly, arguing that situations do not exist independently of discourse — they are constituted by the rhetor's selection of what to highlight. The Bitzer-Vatz debate organises subsequent rhetorical theory's treatment of the model.

## Why this corpus cites it

Bitzer 1968 is the founding reference for the [rhetorical-situation](../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/archive/objects/rhetorical-situation.md) object. Without it, the object's exigence/audience/constraints triple has no source and no historical motivation; with it, the triple has its canonical articulation and its connection to the broader rhetorical-theory literature.

The article is also load-bearing for [audience](../../../glossary/concepts/audience.md) (Bitzer's audience-as-addressable-and-effectual is the foundational rhetorical account of audience, distinct from but compatible with the linguistic *audience-design* and conversation-analytic *recipient-design* accounts) and for [genre](../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/archive/references/teaching/explanations/genre.md) (Miller's 1984 "Genre as Social Action" — the foundational reference for genre theory in the corpus — explicitly builds on Bitzer's frame, treating genres as conventional responses to recurrent rhetorical situations).

## The Vatz counter-position

Richard Vatz, "The Myth of the Rhetorical Situation" (*Philosophy and Rhetoric* 6, no. 3, 1973: 154–161), argues the converse causal direction: situations do not exist in nature waiting for discourses to respond; rhetors *construct* situations by selecting which exigencies to highlight, which audiences to address, and which constraints to invoke. The contemporary settlement (after Edbauer 2005) is that both moments are real — the model's triple stays, but its causal structure is two-way rather than one-way. The Vatz paper is sometimes cited alongside Bitzer 1968 as the necessary corrective; for the corpus's current purposes, citing Bitzer 1968 is sufficient and the Vatz response is summarised in the [rhetorical-situation](../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/archive/objects/rhetorical-situation.md) object's "counter-position" section.
