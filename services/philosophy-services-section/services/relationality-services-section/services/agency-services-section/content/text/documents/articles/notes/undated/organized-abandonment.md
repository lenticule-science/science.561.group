# organized-abandonment

Generated by: necropolitics, letting-die by abandonment — premature death produced by neglect rather than by direct killing.

Organized abandonment is Ruth Wilson Gilmore's term for the institutional processes through which specific communities are systematically deprived of resources, services, and protection.
Gilmore develops the concept in *Golden Gulag: Prisons, Surplus, Crisis, and Opposition in Globalizing California* (2007) and extends it in *Abolition Geography: Essays Towards Liberation* (2022).
Its core insight lives in the word "organized": this is not the absence of governance but one of its products.
Abandonment is not the state failing to act; it is the state acting to withdraw, and the withdrawal follows institutional logics that can be traced, mapped, and contested.

Gilmore arrived at the concept by studying California's prison expansion across the 1980s and 1990s.
She showed how surplus state capital, surplus land, surplus labor, and surplus state capacity were reorganized through carceral infrastructure.
Rural communities that had lost their agricultural economies received prisons; urban communities that had lost their industrial economies lost their populations to those prisons.
The result was a geography of organized abandonment in which investment flowed toward cages while schools, hospitals, housing, and transit were defunded — and each withdrawal made the next more plausible.
Gilmore's well-known definition of racism as "group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death" is the engine of this analysis: it locates racism not in individual attitudes but in the political and spatial production of differential mortality.

What distinguishes organized abandonment from looser languages of neglect is its insistence on agency and procedure.
Abandonment of this kind requires zoning decisions, budget allocations, contract negotiations, and inter-agency coordination.
It is built, not merely suffered.
Because it is built, it can be unbuilt: naming the specific contracts and budget lines is, for Gilmore, the first step in an abolitionist politics that treats infrastructure as the terrain of struggle rather than as fixed background.
Abandonment, in this sense, is the negative form of an investment decision — every withdrawal from one place is the condition of an investment somewhere else.

Organized abandonment is best read against its neighbors in this cluster.
It is a species of necropolitics — Mbembe's name for sovereignty exercised through the management of death — but Gilmore's framework sharpens necropolitics where it can become atmospheric, describing conditions of death rather than the agents and mechanisms that produce them.
Where necropolitics can read as a description of a deathly air, organized abandonment names which agencies, which line items, which coordinated decisions.
It overlaps closely with Elizabeth Povinelli's economies of abandonment and with infrastructural abandonment, which describe the same withdrawal from the angles of late-liberal governance and of decaying physical systems respectively, and it supplies the structural cause for which Lauren Berlant's slow death describes the lived, durational effect.
The stakes are practical: if abandonment is organized, it can be reorganized, and the demand shifts from charity for the abandoned to the redirection of the very flows of investment that produced the abandonment.
