# surveillance-capitalism

Surveillance capitalism is Shoshana Zuboff's term, developed in her 2015 paper "Big Other" and her 2019 book *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*, for an accumulation logic she argues is structurally distinct from industrial capitalism.
On her account it originated at Google in the early 2000s, generalized across the major technology platforms through the 2010s, and now operates across consumer products, education, healthcare, finance, and government.
Generated by: **capital** — surveillance capitalism is a historically specific form capital takes, one that finds its raw material not in nature or muscle but in the behavioral residue of digitally instrumented life.

Zuboff describes the logic as a cycle.
First, *behavioral surplus extraction*: the platform observes and records user behavior far in excess of what is needed to deliver the service the user came for — searches, clicks, dwell times, locations, social ties, and increasingly the data streams of home, car, and body sensors.
Second, *computation of prediction products*: that behavioral data is processed into predictions of what users will buy, watch, vote for, or find persuasive, and these predictions, not the ostensible service, are the firm's distinctive output.
Third, *sale in behavioral futures markets*: the predictions are sold to advertisers, insurers, employers, and political campaigns, in transactions between the platform and its clients to which the user — whose behavior is the subject — is not a party and is typically unaware.
Fourth, *behavior modification*: because more accurate predictions command higher prices, the platform has a standing interest in making behavior more predictable, nudging, gamifying, and habituating users toward the patterns its predictions describe.

The fourth step carries Zuboff's claim to novelty.
Earlier observation regimes — statistical sampling, market research, consumer surveys — collected data without altering the behavior observed.
Surveillance capitalism's economics reward modification, so the platform's design, ranking, and notification systems are tuned to produce the behavior they then predict.
Her strongest formulation is that this represents the commodification of a new category of being: where industrial capitalism commodified labor-power and consumer capitalism commodified attention, surveillance capitalism commodifies human experience itself — the trail that lived life unavoidably leaves in an instrumented environment.
The user does not sell this; it is taken and converted into a prediction product the platform owns.
Zuboff names the resulting apparatus *Big Other*, distinguishing it from Orwell's Big Brother precisely because it requires no single sovereign center: it is distributed across many platforms, devices, and firms whose joint operation constitutes the regime.

Zuboff frames the stakes as political-philosophical rather than narrowly economic.
The regime produces an asymmetry of knowledge and power incompatible with the autonomy that self-government presupposes: the platform knows the user better than the user knows themselves, can act on them in ways they cannot perceive, and answers to no one for it.
She therefore rejects the privacy frame as too small — the harm, she argues, is at the level of systemic power, not individual data rights — and calls for constitutional-scale political contestation rather than sector-by-sector reform.

The thesis has been influential and heavily criticized.
Evgeny Morozov and others charge empirical overreach, arguing that Zuboff folds genuinely different business models — Google's advertising, Apple's hardware-and-services, Amazon's retail-and-cloud — into a single "logic."
Marxist critics, drawing on Nick Srnicek's *Platform Capitalism* and the autonomist tradition, argue that she isolates surveillance from the broader political economy — financialization, unpaid labor, the global division of labor, the persistence of industrial production — within which it is one mechanism among several rather than the defining one.
Methodologically, her evidence leans on a handful of firms while the conclusion reaches for "an age," and her diagnosis is paired with a fairly conventional liberal-reformist politics that critics find unequal to the structural change it describes.
Read alongside racial capitalism and the other named variants of capital, surveillance capitalism is best taken as one powerful account of where contemporary accumulation extends its frontier — into the data of everyday life — rather than as the whole story of capital today.
