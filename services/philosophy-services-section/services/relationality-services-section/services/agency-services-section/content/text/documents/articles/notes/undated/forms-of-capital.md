# forms of capital

In Bourdieu's sociology, capital is any resource that confers advantage within a social field, and his decisive move was to show that it comes in several forms, not the economic one alone.
Generated by: capital — these are the distinct currencies the general notion of capital divides into, and the distribution of each is what gives a field its hierarchy.
The argument is laid out in Bourdieu's essay "The Forms of Capital" (1986) and runs throughout *Distinction* (1979).

**Economic capital** is material wealth — money, property, the means of production — the form that classical economics already recognizes and the one most readily converted into the others.
Bourdieu's point is not to deny its importance but to deny that it is the whole story: a purely economic account cannot explain why an impoverished professor and an uncultured millionaire each hold a kind of power the other lacks.

**Cultural capital** is competence, taste, and credential — the dispositions, knowledge, manners, and ways of speaking that a society treats as legitimate and valuable.
Bourdieu distinguishes three states: *embodied* (long-cultivated dispositions, an ear for music, ease in a gallery, the "right" accent, which take time to acquire and cannot simply be bought), *objectified* (cultural goods like books, instruments, and artworks), and *institutionalized* (academic qualifications and titles that certify cultural capital and give it a recognized, comparable value).
Privileged families transmit embodied cultural capital at home, invisibly, over years; the school then rewards it while presenting its judgments as neutral measures of talent — which is the mechanism of social reproduction and symbolic violence.

**Social capital** is the resources accessible through a durable network of relationships — the connections that can be mobilized, the doors that open because of who one knows.
It explains why the same credentials yield different returns for differently-connected people, and why exclusive networks guard their boundaries so carefully.

**Symbolic capital** is capital of any kind once it is *recognized as legitimate* — prestige, reputation, honor, authority.
It is what the other forms become when they are misperceived as merit, distinction, or natural superiority rather than seen as resources unevenly distributed.
Symbolic capital is the basis of symbolic violence precisely because it works through recognition: the dominated grant the dominant's advantages the status of worth.

The forms are **convertible**, and this is central.
Economic capital buys the schooling that yields cultural capital; cultural capital converts back into economic capital through credentialed employment; social capital opens access to both.
But conversion is never frictionless or automatic — the rates are themselves objects of struggle, and much of the labor of privilege goes into disguising economic advantage as cultural worth, so that inherited position can present itself as earned distinction.
The plurality of capitals is what lets Bourdieu analyze domination as running along several currencies at once, rather than reducing all social power to money.
