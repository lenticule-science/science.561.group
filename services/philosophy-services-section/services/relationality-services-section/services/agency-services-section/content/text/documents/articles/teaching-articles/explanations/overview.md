A graduate student in mathematics gives a lecture on a paper she has read carefully. She believes she understands it. She has worked through the proofs. She begins, gets through the setup, comes to the central argument, and stalls. The board fills with notation. She apologizes for the pace and continues. By the end, she has the right theorem on the board with a chain of correct lines underneath. The audience can follow none of it. Several have stopped trying. One who is still tracking will say later that he can confirm each step but has no idea what the proof is actually doing.

The student has produced something correct. She has not communicated.

This is what the framework analyzes: the gap between artifacts that are correct and artifacts that communicate. The two are not the same and they fail in different ways. A communicatively responsible artifact is one whose framed units are recognizable, whose required completions are performable by the audience it claims, whose icons amplify rather than signal, whose sequence carries the structure it depicts, and whose claims transport into the registers its readers actually inhabit. None of this is guaranteed by formal correctness. None of it is inherited from speaking carefully. It is a discipline.

## The central operation

[Closure](../../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/archive/prose/teaching/terms/closure.md) is the unit of communicative work. The artifact frames units; the reader closes between them. Scott McCloud named this operation in *Understanding Comics* (HarperCollins, 1993) for the case of comics, but the operation is general: every sequential communicative practice — proofs, lectures, software documentation, signage routes, presentations, conversation — relies on it. The artifact's responsibility is to make the closures it asks for performable and accountable.

The defect the framework names is [unaccountable closure](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/unaccountable-closure.md): a gap the reader must cross without enough constraint, license, or apparatus to perform the crossing correctly. The reader hits an unaccountable closure and has three options: guess, stop, or reverse-engineer. None of them produce reliable communication. Every one of them transfers cost to the reader and to whoever later has to engage with the artifact, accumulating as [legibility-debt](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/legibility-debt.md).

## The positive criterion

The framework's positive criterion is what [accountable-closure](../../../../glossary/concepts/accountable-closure.md) describes as a parametric pattern and what [convergent-completion](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/convergent-completion.md) names as the operational test. An artifact's closures are accountable when independent qualified readers performing them arrive at compatible completions. The test is procedural and audience-relative — there is no audience-free measure of acceptable gap, and the framework does not pretend otherwise.

The discipline that produces accountable closures is medium-specific. Each register — diagram, prose, code, formal expansion, photograph, recorded gesture — is held to the standard of rigor proper to it. The artifact's transport between registers is acknowledged as transport, with the [transport-loss](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/transport-loss.md) named rather than concealed. This is what [medium-specific-rigor](../../../../glossary/concepts/medium-specific-rigor.md) refuses [medium-imitation](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/medium-imitation.md) for: the policy of forcing every register to imitate one privileged one is a refusal to be honest about what each register can do.

## What the framework synthesizes

The framework draws from several traditions that each describe parts of the same discipline:

- Scott McCloud, Will Eisner (*Comics and Sequential Art*, Poorhouse, 1985), Neil Cohn (*The Visual Language of Comics*, Bloomsbury, 2013), and Thierry Groensteen (*The System of Comics*, University Press of Mississippi, 2007) on comics — the most operational vocabulary available for the units (panels), the gaps (gutters), the completions (closure), the compressions (icons), and the orderings (sequences) of any framed communicative practice.
- Line Edslev Andersen on [acceptable gaps](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/acceptable-gap.md) and [disciplinary gaps](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/disciplinary-gap.md) — the vocabulary for distinguishing licensed omissions from defects, and for naming the apparatus a discipline maintains to license its omissions.
- Yacin Hamami and Rebecca Morris on plan-reconstruction — the vocabulary for the cognitive work the reader actually does, beyond step-by-step verification, in recovering what an argument is doing.
- Jeremy Avigad, Danielle Macbeth, and Zoe Ashton on the philosophy of mathematical practice — the case against [formal-correctness-as-sole-rigor](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/formal-correctness-as-sole-rigor.md) and the development of medium-specific accounts of mathematical reliability.

The contribution of the framework is to take this vocabulary seriously as portable. The disciplined production of communicative artifacts has been studied carefully in mathematics and in comics; the same vocabulary operates in software documentation, technical writing, signage, lecturing, design review, policy writing, and any other practice that frames units and asks readers to do controlled work between them.
