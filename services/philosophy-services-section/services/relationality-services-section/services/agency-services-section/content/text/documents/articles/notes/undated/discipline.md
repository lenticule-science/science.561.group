# discipline

Generated by: power-knowledge — the modality that trains bodies.

Discipline is Michel Foucault's name for the modality of power that produces docile and useful bodies through the detailed organization of space, time, and activity.
He analyzes it in *Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison* (1975), tracing its emergence across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries in the barracks, the school, the workshop, the hospital, and the prison.
Disciplinary power does not work mainly by prohibiting acts or threatening death, as sovereign power did.
It works by training: it takes hold of the body and shapes its movements, habits, and capacities so that the body becomes at once more obedient and more productive.
The aim is not to crush a force but to optimize it — to increase the body's utility while decreasing its power to resist.

Foucault identifies a small set of techniques through which discipline operates.
There is enclosure and partitioning — the assignment of each body to its own place within a bounded institutional space, so that everyone can be located and no one is anonymous.
There is the timetable, which breaks activity into precisely scheduled and measured units, eliminating idleness and synchronizing bodies to a rhythm set from above.
There is hierarchical observation, an architecture and chain of supervision arranged so that supervisors can see those below them.
And there is normalizing judgment, in which every gesture and performance is continually measured against a standard, rewarded or corrected, and ranked.
Binding these together is the examination, which combines observation and judgment into a single act that both watches the subject and produces a documentary record of them — the case file, the grade, the chart.
The examination is where discipline meets power/knowledge: to discipline a body is also to know it.

The crucial argument of *Discipline and Punish* is that these techniques are not confined to the prison but circulate across the whole of modern society.
The same diagram that arranges inmates arranges schoolchildren at their desks, soldiers in formation, workers on the line, and patients in their beds.
Foucault speaks of a "disciplinary society" precisely because the carceral form has leaked outward: the institutions that train, sort, and correct bodies share a common logic, and an individual passing through them is shaped again and again by the same procedures.
Discipline individualizes — it isolates each body, fixes it in a grid, and builds up a knowledge of it as a distinct case.

Discipline is one half of Foucault's account of modern power; biopower is the other.
Discipline acts on the individual body, training it into docility; the regulatory side of biopower acts on the population as a biological mass.
The two are complementary poles of a power over life, and the disciplines feed the population-level knowledge that biopolitics manages.
Discipline generates its own further mechanisms: panopticism, the architectural and perceptual principle by which the mere possibility of being seen induces self-policing; and normalization, the operation by which individuals are measured against a norm and so sorted into the normal and the deviant.
Together these make discipline a power that is most effective when it is least visible — when the trained body governs itself.
