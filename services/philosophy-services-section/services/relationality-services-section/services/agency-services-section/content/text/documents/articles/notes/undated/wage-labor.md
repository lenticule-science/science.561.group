# wage-labor

Wage labor is the social relation in which a person sells their capacity to work — their labor-power — to an employer in exchange for a wage.
It is not simply "working for money" but a specific arrangement: the worker, lacking independent access to the means of production (land, tools, materials, premises), must sell their labor-power as a commodity on the market, and the employer who buys it directs its use and keeps what it produces.
Generated by: **exploitation** in its distinctively capitalist form — wage-labor is the relation through which surplus is extracted under capitalism, the contractual surface beneath which the taking of unpaid labor occurs.

The relation is historically specific, and seeing this is the first thing Marx wants his readers to grasp.
Wage labor required the prior dispossession of people from land and other means of subsistence — the process he called primitive accumulation, which included the English enclosures, colonial land theft, and the destruction of Indigenous and peasant economies.
This is why the "free" labor contract is unfree at its root: the worker is legally free to sell labor-power to whichever employer they choose, but not free to decline to sell it at all, because the alternative — having been separated from any independent means of living — is destitution.
The freedom of the wage contract is real at the surface and hollow underneath; the compulsion has been moved from the visible relation of master and servant into the prior, invisible fact of dispossession.

It helps to set wage labor against other forms of compelled labor, because the contrast shows what is distinctive about it.
Under slavery, the person is owned outright and their whole life appropriated.
Under debt peonage, labor is owed against a debt that may be impossible to discharge.
Under corvée, labor is owed directly to a lord or a state.
Under indenture, labor is contracted for a fixed term.
Each involves a different configuration of ownership, coercion, and extraction.
Wage labor's peculiarity is that coercion is displaced out of the relation itself: the worker appears as a free contracting equal, and the unfreedom resides in the structure of property that surrounds the contract.

Wage labor produces two of the central phenomena the tradition analyzes.
It generates alienation, because the worker controls neither the labor process nor its products: what is made belongs to the employer, is directed by the employer, and confronts the worker as something alien.
And it generates surplus-value, because the employer pays for labor-power — the capacity to work, valued at roughly the cost of the worker's reproduction — while extracting labor, the actual work performed, whose value exceeds what was paid.
The gap between the two is the source of profit, and the whole relation is organized to keep that gap open and to widen it.

As a node, wage labor sits at the center of the labor side of the cluster.
It presupposes primitive accumulation behind it, commodifies labor-power within it, and yields surplus-value and alienation from it; its internal organization is the labor process, contested and reshaped through scientific management, deskilling, and real subsumption.
It is also worth remembering what wage analysis tends to leave out: the unwaged labor of social reproduction — feeding, clothing, housing, and emotionally sustaining workers — performed disproportionately by women and colonized peoples outside the wage relation, on which the entire wage system silently depends.
