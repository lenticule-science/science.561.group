# intersectionality

Intersectionality is the term Kimberlé Crenshaw introduced in two foundational law-review essays — "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" (*University of Chicago Legal Forum*, 1989) and "Mapping the Margins: Intersectionality, Identity Politics, and Violence Against Women of Color" (*Stanford Law Review*, 1991) — for the analytic that the experience and situation of Black women cannot be understood as the sum of "race oppression" plus "gender oppression," because the categories themselves are produced through their intersection.
Generated by: interlocking-systems — intersectionality is the translation of the Combahee Collective's interlocking framing into the vocabulary of law and legal theory.

The 1989 essay's central case is *DeGraffenreid v.
General Motors* (1976), in which five Black women sued GM, alleging that its seniority-based layoff policy disproportionately harmed Black women because the company had not hired Black women into the relevant positions until the 1970s.
The court refused the claim.
White women had been hired, so there was no sex discrimination; Black men had been hired, so there was no race discrimination; and the court held that the plaintiffs could not combine the two statutory remedies into a new "super-remedy."
Crenshaw's analysis is that the legal categories of "race discrimination" and "sex discrimination" had each been modeled on a paradigm case of one axis at a time — white women for sex, Black men for race — so that the intersection the plaintiffs actually occupied fell through the categorical seams.
The law was structurally incapable of describing the structure that produced their situation.

The analytic Crenshaw named has roots that precede the term.
The Combahee River Collective's 1977 statement on interlocking systems of oppression analyzes the same position a decade earlier in political terms; Patricia Hill Collins's matrix of domination systematizes it at the sociological level; Audre Lorde's insistence on the analytical primacy of difference and bell hooks's analysis of "imperialist white-supremacist capitalist patriarchy" do the same work in the essay register.
Crenshaw's contribution was to carry the analytic into legal theory and, through it, into a much wider political vocabulary.
Collins's later work — *Intersectionality* with Sirma Bilge (2016) and *Intersectionality as Critical Social Theory* (2019) — develops it into a systematic critical social theory.

A recurring danger in the term's reception is its dilution into a demographic checklist — the additive sum the analytic was designed to refuse, as though one simply tallies up a person's identity categories.
Used rigorously, intersectionality names a structural claim: the categories of race, class, gender, and sexuality are produced together by interlocking systems of domination, so any analysis that treats them as separable axes to be summed misidentifies what the structure is and how it operates.
Within this library intersectionality stands beside the matrix of domination as one of the two formal tools derived from the interlocking-systems insight — the legal-theoretical tool to the matrix's sociological one — both refusing the additive and the single-axis readings alike.
