# skill

A skill is compiled procedural knowledge — a named, repeatable unit of method that an agent can invoke reliably, without deliberating about *how* to do it. The person with a skill does not reason out each step; they have internalized a way of acting so that, given the right conditions, they simply do it well. Generated by: **method** — a skill is method compiled into a reliable capacity, the point at which an explicit way of working has been absorbed into a practitioner so deeply that it runs as second nature.

The concept recurs across several fields with a stable core. In cognitive science, a skill is procedural memory that executes without deliberation: the learner who once thought through every step of driving or reading now does it automatically, the knowledge having been compiled from slow, attentive performance into fast, fluent action. In the crafts and professions, skill is the trained judgment of the experienced practitioner — the carpenter who reads the grain, the clinician who recognizes the presentation, the editor who hears the wrong word — a capacity built only through long practice and not fully reducible to any written rule. What unites these is that a skill is a discrete, named, deployable unit of know-how: not a goal, not a plan, not a whole task, but the compiled capacity to do one specific kind of thing well.

Skill is one of the most important and most contested concepts in the sociology of work, because skill is simultaneously a real capacity and a social valuation, and the two do not always coincide. Whether a given kind of work is recognized as "skilled" — and therefore commands higher pay, more autonomy, and more respect — is partly a matter of the genuine difficulty and judgment it requires and partly a matter of who performs it and how well they are organized to defend its status. Feminist scholars have shown that work coded as women's work (much care work, much service and clerical work) has often been classified as low-skill regardless of the real competence it demands, the classification tracking the gender of the worker more than the difficulty of the task. Skill, in other words, is not only in the worker's hands; it is also a label fought over in the labor market and the workplace.

This is the point at which skill connects directly to the critical analysis of work, and the connection is its sharpest edge. Skill is exactly what deskilling expropriates. Harry Braverman's thesis is that capital, through scientific management, separates conception from execution: it captures the worker's compiled know-how, formalizes it, and recomposes it elsewhere — in the machine, the procedure, the planning department, and now the algorithm — leaving the worker only the simplified executory motions. Because skill held by the worker is a source of bargaining power and autonomy (a skilled worker is hard to replace and hard to fully command), its expropriation is not merely a technical efficiency but a shift of power. The history of the labor process can be read, in part, as a history of the struggle over who holds the skill.

As a node, skill is the smallest fully formed unit of the practice cluster: method compiled into a usable capacity, the building block from which larger structures of practice are assembled. It is held by a practitioner and exercised in concrete situated practice; it is what training builds, what craft traditions transmit, and what deskilling takes away. To analyze the skills a kind of work requires, who possesses them, and who controls them is to analyze one of the central stakes in the organization of work.
