# scientific-management

Scientific management is Frederick Winslow Taylor's program for the systematic study and reorganization of industrial work, developed in his consulting practice with U.S. machine shops and steel mills from the 1880s on and codified most influentially in *The Principles of Scientific Management* (1911) and the earlier *Shop Management* (1903).
It combined an empirical methodology (time-and-motion study), an organizational prescription (the separation of planning from execution), a compensation structure (differential piece rates), and an explicit ideology (the claim that properly organized work is cooperative rather than conflictual).
Generated by: **real-subsumption** — scientific management is the originating technical-managerial method through which capital came to reorganize the labor process from within.

Taylor summarized the program in four principles: develop a science for each element of work, replacing rule-of-thumb craft methods with systematic study; scientifically select and train the worker, rather than leaving workers to train themselves; cooperate with workers to ensure the scientifically developed methods are followed; and divide work and responsibility between management, which plans, and workers, who execute — "the managers assume new burdens, new duties, and responsibilities never dreamed of in the past."
The fourth principle, the separation of conception from execution, is the one later critics identify as the program's defining political move.
Taylor presented it as cooperation; Braverman analyzed it as the engine of deskilling.

The empirical core was time-and-motion study.
A trained observer decomposed the worker's activity into elementary motions, timed each with a stopwatch under varying conditions, identified the "one best way" to perform each, and reassembled the optimized motions into a procedure the worker was then required to follow.
Taylor and his disciples — Frank and Lillian Gilbreth, Henry Gantt, Carl Barth — reported dramatic reductions in cycle times.
Whether those reductions reflected genuine technical improvement or simply an intensification of labor, whether they were sustainable, and whether the workers who executed them gained anything beyond higher piece rates were contested then and remain contested.
Robert Kanigel's biography *The One Best Way* (1997) documents both Taylor's real influence and a substantial gap between his self-presentation and his practice, including, on the analysis of several historians, the exaggeration or fabrication of his most-cited results, such as the famous pig-iron loading study.

Taylor's influence was global and rapid.
By the 1920s scientific management — sometimes under other names, the Bedaux system, German *Rationalisierung*, the Soviet *NOT* movement — was being applied across U.S. industry (Ford's River Rouge being the canonical case, though Fordism extended Taylor through the mechanically paced assembly line and the high-wage, mass-consumption coupling Taylor never theorized), across Soviet planning, where Lenin endorsed Taylor's methods while separating them from their capitalist application, and across mid-century clerical and service work.
Its descendants run straight to the present: industrial engineering, predetermined motion-time systems, the *kaizen* methodology of lean production, and the granular work-measurement of call centers and warehouse logistics.

The reputation of scientific management collapsed across the twentieth century — by the 1970s "Taylorism" was a term of abuse, associated with mechanistic, dehumanizing work design that the human-relations and lean-production schools claimed to have transcended — even as its *substantive practice* continued and intensified.
Contemporary algorithmic management implements the Taylorist program with vastly more granular measurement and vastly less worker recourse than the stopwatch-and-instruction-card era allowed: the same separation of conception from execution, the same one-best-way logic, now embedded in software that paces, monitors, and scores.
As a node, scientific management is the method-side of real subsumption, the empirical-historical referent for the deskilling thesis, and the direct ancestor of algorithmic control over work.
