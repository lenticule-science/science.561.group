# precarious-life

Generated by: grievability — the shared bodily vulnerability that grievability sorts unequally.

Precarious life is Judith Butler's name, developed in *Precarious Life: The Powers of Mourning and Violence* (2004), for the condition of exposure to violence, deprivation, and abandonment — a condition that is at once universal and unequally distributed.
Butler begins from an ontological claim: all human life is precarious.
Every body is vulnerable to injury, illness, dependency, and death, and every life is constituted through relations to others it did not choose and cannot fully control.
To be a body is to be exposed — handed over, from the start, to a social world that can sustain or destroy it.
This vulnerability is not a defect to be overcome but the basic condition of embodied, relational existence.

The political force of the concept comes from the gap between that shared vulnerability and its actual distribution.
Precariousness in the ontological sense is common to all; precarity in the political sense is produced.
Specific populations are made precarious by the withdrawal of legal protection, economic security, and social recognition, while other populations are shielded by the very same structures.
The exposure is not natural fact but political arrangement.
Butler insists that this makes precariousness a thoroughly biopolitical matter: the governance of who is exposed to harm is the governance of who counts as fully alive in the first place.

Butler wrote *Precarious Life* in the aftermath of September 11, 2001, asking why certain deaths — American, Western, civilian — produced massive collective grief while other deaths — Afghan, Iraqi, Palestinian, detained — were absorbed into silence or actively justified.
Her answer is not that some people care and others do not.
It is that the frames through which lives appear — visual media, state rhetoric, the legal categories of citizen and combatant — determine in advance which lives register as lost.
A life that was never apprehended as living cannot be mourned when it is destroyed.
The mechanism, Butler stresses, is not only material (who has resources) but perceptual (whose suffering is visible).
A population can be materially deprived and perceptually invisible at the same time, and the invisibility is precisely what makes the deprivation politically sustainable.

The demand that follows is not the elimination of precariousness, which is impossible because vulnerability is constitutive of embodied life, but its equal distribution and the recognition that it obligates.
If every life is precarious, then the structures that expose some while protecting others are not neutral; they are forms of violence.
Precarious life is the ground concept of Butler's cluster: grievability sorts this shared vulnerability into lives that count and lives that do not; precarity names the resulting uneven distribution; frames of war name the machinery that does the sorting.
Together they relocate the ethics of mourning into politics, arguing that to recognize a common precariousness is already to be obligated to those whose precariousness has been made invisible.
