**Harm governance** is the institutional-affective regime in which care has been collapsed into risk management, and subjects who have lost faith in the state police each other using the state's own administrative logic.
Under harm governance, political action must be administered — assessed, contextualized, pre-empted, and corrected — in order to count as legitimate.
This produces a characteristic inversion: individual action is treated as inherently risky (because it lacks institutional validation), while mass action feels safe (because institutional form makes it feel legitimate), regardless of the actual risks either poses.

## Foucault and Povinelli as joint foundation

Foucault showed that modern governance operates through the production of self-governing subjects: the well-governed subject has internalized the state's categories so thoroughly that external enforcement becomes unnecessary.
They monitor, assess, and correct — not because they are ordered to, but because they have come to understand this as what responsibility means.
The crucial feature is that this self-governance persists even when the state loses credibility: people do not stop being governed when they stop trusting the government.
They become the government of each other.

Povinelli's late liberalism adds the tolerance dimension: the formation does not deny harm but tolerates it, as long as the actor can be shown to have been constrained.
If behavior can be understood as economically or emotionally rational — if the choice can be framed as non-choice — then harm becomes acceptable.
The question shifts from "what happened?" to "could the person who caused it have reasonably done otherwise?"
Harm is absorbed into a calculus of constraint, and the conversation shifts from epidemiology and solidarity to the governance of judgment itself: who has standing to evaluate someone else's behavior, and under what conditions.

Harm governance is where these two operations meet in the contemporary political field: Foucault's self-governing subject encounters Povinelli's tolerance regime, and the result is a left-liberal political culture that administers its own political life through the same risk frameworks the state uses — including when those frameworks are being applied to resist the state.

## The inversion

The defining asymmetry of harm governance is the inversion of apparent risk.
A small direct action — unpermitted aid, banner-material acquisition, organized refusal — is treated as requiring intensive prior scrutiny and contextual disclosure because it lacks institutional validation.
A general strike or mass demonstration is treated as inherently safer because its scale gives it institutional-seeming legitimacy.
This is not a strategic assessment: the general strike requires no existing infrastructure and carries real repression risk; the small direct action may be comparatively lower-risk and immediately materially useful.
The assessment is not strategic; it is genre-governed.
The small action violates the administrative form; the mass action fits it.

The inversion reveals harm governance's operative logic: the framework cannot recognize that institutional form itself introduces risks — of co-optation, of dependency on the very structures being challenged, of slowness relative to material need.
Risk is assessed within the administrative genre; within that genre, institutional form is definitionally safer.

## Migration into alternative spaces

What makes harm governance durable is its migration.
When people lose faith in the state, the administrative logic migrates into the spaces that were supposed to be its alternatives.
Community organizations adopt grant metrics; mutual aid groups develop intake forms; care networks build hierarchies of competence and authorization.
The administrative apparatus of the state migrates into its putative alternatives — not because anyone chose this, but because harm governance has become the only available grammar for legitimating action in the late-liberal formation.
The formation has been so thoroughly installed through [ontogenring](../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/ontogenring.md) that subjects operating without it cannot generate the social conditions needed to sustain collective action.
The alternative spaces absorb the logic and reproduce it.

## Care is not governance

The term is critical, not defeatist.
Care does not wait for consensus; care does not require everyone to understand; care is not afraid of smallness, inconsistency, or discomfort.
Care is practice.
Harm governance is the operation that converts care into its administrative simulacrum.
The distinction between care and harm governance is the distinction between an action oriented toward the other's wellbeing and an action oriented toward one's own defensibility within the administrative frame.
Both may look similar from outside; they operate on different settlement logics. ---
