# nationalism

Nationalism is the doctrine that humanity is naturally divided into nations, that the nation is the primary unit of political loyalty, and that each nation is entitled to its own state.
Generated by: **the-state** — nationalizing is the work a state does to manufacture a single people in whose name it can claim to rule.
What looks like the nation speaking through the state is, on closer inspection, the state producing the nation it then claims merely to represent.

The decisive twentieth-century reversal of common sense comes from Ernest Gellner, who argued that "nationalism is not the awakening of nations to self-consciousness: it invents nations where they do not exist."
For Gellner the nation is not an ancient sleeping essence that modernity rouses; it is a requirement of modern industrial society, which needs a mobile, literate, interchangeable population sharing one standardized "high culture" transmitted by mass schooling.
The state builds that culture and calls it the nation.
Benedict Anderson, in *Imagined Communities* (1983), gave the companion formulation: a nation is "an imagined political community" — imagined because its members will never meet most of their fellows yet hold a vivid image of their communion; limited because it has finite boundaries beyond which lie other nations; sovereign because it claims its own state.
Anderson traced how print-capitalism, the novel and the newspaper, let dispersed strangers picture themselves moving through the same homogeneous time as one people.
Anthony Smith dissented partway, insisting that modern nations are often built from older *ethnies* — pre-existing ethnic symbols, memories, and myths — so the raw material is not conjured from nothing even if the nation-form is modern.

The stakes are that nationalism naturalizes a contingent arrangement.
It presents the nation as primordial, organic, and self-evident, when the historical record shows borders, national languages, and "the people" being assembled by states, schools, censuses, maps, and armies within living memory.
Concrete cases make the manufacture visible: Eugen Weber's *Peasants into Frenchmen* documents how, deep into the nineteenth century, much of the population of France did not speak French or think of itself as French until conscription, railways, and compulsory schooling produced French citizens.
The standardization of a single official language out of many spoken dialects, the writing of a national history curriculum, the designation of national heroes and holidays — each is a state operation, presented to citizens as the discovery of who they already were.

Nationalism's relation to its neighbours runs along this axis of manufacture.
Its method is the reduction of a living, plural population to a single recognizable type, which the **romantic-nationalism** branch elaborates aesthetically through **volksgeist** and **the-folk**.
It works on shared memory and inherited practice, refashioning them into **political-myth** and **invention-of-tradition** — narratives and customs that feel immemorial but serve present political needs.
Its most dangerous intensification is **palingenetic-ultranationalism**, the fascist myth of national rebirth after decline.
What nationalism suppresses is the heteroglossic reality it claims to speak for: actual communities and diasporas are internally various, multilingual, and bound by chosen reciprocal ties rather than by membership in a homogeneous type.
Nationalism's accomplishment, and its harm, is to overwrite that plurality with a single image and to hand the state a population it can govern as one.
