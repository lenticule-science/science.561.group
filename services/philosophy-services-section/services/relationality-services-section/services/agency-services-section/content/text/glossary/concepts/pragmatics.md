# Pragmatics

**Pragmatics** is the study of how meaning is constructed in use — beyond what the literal semantics of words and sentences encodes. It includes how speakers and hearers exploit shared context, conversational expectations, indirect speech acts, deixis, presupposition, implicature, politeness, and the cooperative norms governing discourse.

## What pragmatics studies

A pragmatic account of meaning addresses questions semantics alone cannot:

1. **Reference resolution in context.** "She left an hour ago" requires knowing who *she* is, when *now* is, and what counts as *leaving* in the situation. Deixis and anaphora are pragmatic phenomena.
2. **Implicature.** What a speaker conveys beyond what they literally say. Grice (1975) gave the canonical analysis: conversational implicatures arise when the speaker is presumed to be observing the cooperative principle and its maxims (quality, quantity, relation, manner). "Some students passed" implicates "not all", though logically it does not.
3. **Speech acts.** Saying is doing: assertions, promises, requests, declarations, apologies. Austin (1962) and Searle (1969) developed the canonical taxonomy. The same form can perform different acts ("Can you pass the salt?" is a request, not a question about ability).
4. **Politeness and face.** Brown & Levinson (1987) on face-threatening acts and the strategies speakers use to mitigate them; politeness as a pragmatic universal with culturally-varying realizations.
5. **Common ground.** Clark (1996, *Using Language*) on the accumulating shared knowledge interlocutors track and exploit; common ground is what makes elliptical and indirect language work.
6. **Relevance.** Sperber & Wilson (1986/1995) on relevance theory: hearers seek the interpretation that maximises cognitive effect for processing effort.

## Pragmatics vs. semantics vs. discourse

- **Semantics** studies literal meaning: what sentences entail, what truth-conditions they have, what their compositional structure is.
- **Pragmatics** studies use-meaning: what speakers convey by uttering sentences in context, including conveyed content beyond the literal.
- **Discourse analysis** studies sequences of utterances: turn-taking, conversation structure, narrative organisation. Discourse analysis builds on both semantics and pragmatics.

The boundaries are contested (the *semantics-pragmatics interface* is a live research area), but the broad division is stable: semantics gives literal meaning, pragmatics gives meaning-in-use.
