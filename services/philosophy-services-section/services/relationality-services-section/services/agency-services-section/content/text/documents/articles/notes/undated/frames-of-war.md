# frames-of-war

Generated by: grievability, framing — the operation that sorts lives before any particular death.

Frames of war is Judith Butler's name for the visual, discursive, and institutional frames through which war is presented to publics — and through which the lives destroyed in war are rendered either grievable or invisible.
Butler develops the concept in *Frames of War: When Is Life Grievable?* (2009).
The frame, in her account, is never a neutral window onto events.
It is a structure that determines what appears within it and what is excluded, what registers as a life and what does not, what counts as violence and what counts as security.
Because the frame works in advance of any particular death, it decides ahead of time which lives are available for destruction.

Butler draws on Erving Goffman's frame analysis and extends it into the domain of war and political violence.
A photograph of a dead civilian can produce outrage or indifference depending on the frame within which it circulates.
The embedded journalist's footage of "precision strikes" frames destruction as surgical and necessary; cell-phone footage of the same strike, circulated outside institutional media, frames it as massacre.
The content can be identical.
The frame determines the response.
This is why, for Butler, contesting war is partly a struggle over frames rather than only over facts.

The frame operates at several levels at once.
At the level of visual representation, it governs what is shown and what is cropped out, what is captioned and what is left unnarrated.
At the level of state rhetoric, it governs which populations are framed as threats whose destruction secures the public and which are framed as protected, their lives treated as sacred.
At the level of legal category, it governs who counts as a combatant (killable) and who counts as a civilian (protected) — and who falls into the gap between, where killing is neither murder nor lawful war but something the legal framework refuses to name.

Crucially, the frame is not all-powerful.
Butler argues that frames can break — that the reality they exclude can force its way back in through images, testimony, and acts of solidarity that refuse the official framing.
But the breaking of a frame is itself a political act, not a neutral disclosure of the truth, because what replaces a broken frame is always another frame with its own inclusions and exclusions.
The political task is therefore not to achieve some impossible framelessness but to contest the frames that render specific lives ungrievable.
Frames of war is best read as the operational companion to grievability: grievability names the condition (whether a life counts as a life), and frames of war names the machinery — visual, rhetorical, legal — that distributes that condition unevenly across populations before the first shot is fired.
