# color-line

The color-line is W.E.B. Du Bois's name for the racial division that structures the modern world.
His most famous statement of it opens *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903): "the problem of the twentieth century is the problem of the color-line."
The library reads this not as a prediction but as an analytical claim — that the global organization of the modern world is structured by a racial division, and that any social theory which treats race as a derivative or secondary phenomenon has failed to describe its object.
Generated by: race — the color-line is the line racial domination draws through the world-system, separating those it elevates from those it subordinates.

Du Bois meant the phrase at several scales at once.
At the scale of a life, the color-line is the daily, physical fact of segregation — the places one may not enter, the work one may not do, the company one may not keep, the partner one may not marry.
At the scale of a nation, it is the structuring division of American society after slavery, the line along which Reconstruction was defeated and Jim Crow built.
At the global scale — a register Du Bois increasingly emphasized through his Pan-Africanist work — it is the line between the colonizing and the colonized, between the imperial powers and the darker peoples of Asia, Africa, and the Americas whose labor and land underwrote European wealth.
"The relation of the darker to the lighter races of men in Asia and Africa, in America and the islands of the sea" is how he put the global version.
The single image holds the local and the planetary together.

The analytical force of the color-line is that it names race as a line of power rather than a spectrum of difference.
A line divides; it assigns sides; it determines on which side one is born and what that birth will cost or grant.
To say the modern world is organized by a color-line is to say that race is not one social fact among many but a principle of global order — that the wealth of nations, the routes of trade, the distribution of violence, and the boundaries of citizenship were drawn along it.
This is the claim that the Black radical tradition takes from Du Bois and extends: Cedric Robinson's racial capitalism, in which the modern economy is built through racial differentiation, is a development of the insight that the color-line runs through the foundations of the modern world rather than across its surface.

Within this library the color-line is the spatial and global face of race, and it stands beside double-consciousness as the other great concept this library draws from Du Bois.
If the color-line names the division as it cuts through the world, double-consciousness names what it does to the inner life of those it places on the subordinated side — the experience of seeing oneself through the eyes of a world arrayed across the line.
The two concepts are the outer and inner aspects of a single condition: the world split by the line, and the self divided by living on the wrong side of it.
