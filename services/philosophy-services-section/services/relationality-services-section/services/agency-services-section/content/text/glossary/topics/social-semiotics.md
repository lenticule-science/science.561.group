Social semiotics: the study of sign-making as social practice, drawing on Halliday's social linguistics and critiquing structuralist semiology's emphasis on system over use.

Social semiotics is a tradition that treats sign-making as a social practice rather than a system of fixed codes. It was developed by Robert Hodge and Gunther Kress in *Social Semiotics* (1988), drawing on M. A. K. Halliday&rsquo;s systemic functional linguistics and critiquing the structuralist emphasis on *langue* (the abstract system) over *parole* (the situated act of meaning-making).

## Methods and approach

Social semiotics shifts the focus from the [sign](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/language/terms/sign.md) as a pre-existing unit in a code to sign-making as a motivated, interest-driven process. Where [Saussurean semiology](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/semiotics-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/saussurean-semiology.md) asks &ldquo;what does this sign mean within the system?&rdquo;, social semiotics asks &ldquo;who made this sign, for whom, with what resources, under what conditions, and to what effect?&rdquo;

### Key contributions

- **The motivated sign** — social semiotics rejects the [Saussurean](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/semiotics-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/saussurean-semiology.md) principle that the relation between [signifier](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/communication-science-services-section/services/linguistics-services-section/services/semiotics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/signifier.md) and [signified](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/communication-science-services-section/services/linguistics-services-section/services/semiotics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/signified.md) is arbitrary. Kress and Hodge argue that sign-makers choose their [signifiers](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/communication-science-services-section/services/linguistics-services-section/services/semiotics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/signifier.md) based on their interests, selecting aspects of the object that are criterial for them at the moment of representation. A child drawing a car emphasizes wheels because wheels are what matters to the child. The sign is motivated by the sign-maker&rsquo;s interest, not arbitrary.

- **Multimodality** — Kress and Theo van Leeuwen extended semiotic analysis to visual communication in *Reading Images: The Grammar of Visual Design* (1996), arguing that images, like language, have a grammar — systems of compositional choice that carry meaning. This opened semiotic analysis to a full range of communicative modes: writing, image, gesture, layout, color, sound, movement. Meaning-making is always multimodal; language is one resource among many.

- **Semiotic resources** — social semiotics replaces the concept of &ldquo;code&rdquo; (a fixed set of signs and rules) with &ldquo;semiotic resources&rdquo; — the actions, materials, and artifacts available for communication in a given social context. Resources are shaped by their history of use but are not deterministic: sign-makers can use them in new ways, and new resources can be created.

- **Social power and access** — social semiotics foregrounds the politics of sign-making. Not everyone has equal access to semiotic resources. The ability to make meaning — to produce signs that others attend to — is distributed by social power. Institutions regulate what can be said, in what mode, by whom, and to what audience.

- **Design and transformation** — social semiotics treats meaning-making as design: the intentional shaping of semiotic resources to achieve communicative purposes. Every act of representation transforms its object — the sign is not a copy but a new entity shaped by the sign-maker&rsquo;s interests, the available resources, and the social context.

## Relation to other traditions

Social semiotics shares the [Bakhtin Circle](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/semiotics-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/bakhtin-circle.md)&rsquo;s emphasis on the situated, socially contested character of meaning-making, and its rejection of structuralist abstraction. It shares [French semiology](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/semiotics-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/french-semiology.md)&rsquo;s concern with ideology and power but rejects the semiological method of reading culture as a system of codes, preferring to analyze the social processes through which signs are produced and interpreted.

## Key texts

- Hodge, Robert, and Gunther Kress. *Social Semiotics*. Ithaca, NY: Cornell University Press, 1988.

- Kress, Gunther, and Theo van Leeuwen. *Reading Images: The Grammar of Visual Design*. London: Routledge, 1996.

- Halliday, M. A. K. *Language as Social Semiotic: The Social Interpretation of Language and Meaning*. London: Edward Arnold, 1978.

- van Leeuwen, Theo. *Introducing Social Semiotics*. London: Routledge, 2005.

- Chandler, Daniel. *Semiotics: The Basics*. 2nd ed. London: Routledge, 2007.

## Related schools

- [Saussurean Semiology](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/semiotics-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/saussurean-semiology.md) — the tradition social semiotics explicitly critiques for privileging system over use

- [Bakhtin Circle](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/semiotics-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/bakhtin-circle.md) — shares the emphasis on the social, situated, and contested character of meaning
