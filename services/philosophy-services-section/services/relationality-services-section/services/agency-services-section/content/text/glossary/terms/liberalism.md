# Liberalism

Liberalism is the political-economic formation premised on the self-owning individual, formal equality before the law, private property, and the market — the order that takes the free, separate, rights-bearing person as both its starting point and its end.
It is at once a political philosophy, an economic order, and a way of picturing what a human being fundamentally is.
Generated by: **possessive-individual** — liberalism is what obtains when the self-owning individual is treated as ontologically prior to relations, and society is re-presented as an aggregate of such individuals.

Its foundations were laid in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.
Hobbes (*Leviathan*, 1651) imagined individuals in a state of nature prior to society, so that political order arises *because* there are individuals first; Locke (*Two Treatises*, 1690) made property a fact about persons that political society exists to defend.
C.B. Macpherson named the form of selfhood this lineage produces — *possessive individualism*: the person owns themselves and their capacities the way one owns property, and political life flows from that ownership.
On this foundation liberalism builds its characteristic commitments: the individual as the unit of analysis; rights as properties belonging to individuals; the state as a neutral guarantor of fair procedures rather than a promoter of any particular vision of the good life; and the market as the proper arena in which self-owning agents pursue their ends.
John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971) gives the procedural version its fullest modern statement: justice is what rational agents would choose from behind a "veil of ignorance," a fairness located in the structure of institutions rather than in any substantive account of human flourishing.

Liberalism is internally tensioned in ways that have driven its history and its critique.
The first tension is between its universal claims and its constitutive exclusions: it proclaims the equal freedom of all while building itself, historically, on the property and labor of those it did not count as full persons — the enslaved, the colonized, the propertyless, women.
Sylvia Wynter has shown how this works as a racial-colonial operation, a particular Western bourgeois figure of *Man* installed as the overrepresented stand-in for the human, against which others register as defective instances.
The second tension is between freedom and propertylessness: liberalism promises self-determination to everyone but distributes the material conditions of self-determination through a market that produces propertylessness as its other face, so that formal equality coexists with substantive domination.
The third is the one communitarian critics (Sandel, MacIntyre, Taylor) press: liberalism's claim to neutrality among ways of life is itself a substantive commitment — its unencumbered, choosing self is not a default but a particular and contestable metaphysics.

These tensions have generated a rich critical tradition.
Marx read liberal freedom and equality as the ideological surface of a class order, the "free" exchange between worker and capitalist resting on the prior expropriation that makes labor power something the worker must sell.
Karl Polanyi (*The Great Transformation*, 1944) traced liberalism's recurrent crises as the social fabric repeatedly tore under the attempt to organize society around a self-regulating market.
Foucault's lectures on biopolitics traced liberalism's shift from a doctrine of rights toward the administration of populations.
And a contemporary literature on the *legitimacy crisis* of liberalism (Habermas, Brown, Streeck, Fisher) argues that its constitutive contradictions have become experientially insoluble for its subjects rather than locally manageable, so that the order increasingly shifts from promising to *resolve* contradiction toward merely *regulating* its symptoms.

It is at this late phase that the analysis in this branch concentrates.
The cybernetic-postliberalism school reads the present as a stage in which liberal governance has migrated away from ideological persuasion and overt coercion toward the management of feeling at scale — toward *affect governance*, conducted through the *platform* as its medium, holding subjects in *cruel optimism* toward a present it can no longer make good on.
That account is developed in the concepts gathered beneath this one.
The point of the present entry is the formation itself: the order of the self-owning individual, formal equality, private property, and the market — universal in its claims, exclusionary in its history, and persistently tensioned between the freedom it promises and the domination it organizes.
