# tradition

Tradition is the transmission of practices, knowledge, and meanings across generations.
The word descends from the Latin *traditio*, "a handing over," and the handing over is the whole of the matter: tradition is not a fixed deposit but an active process.
Each generation receives what was passed down, interprets it in the light of present circumstances, transforms what needs transforming, and passes the result forward.
A tradition is alive while this process continues and dead once it has frozen into mere repetition.
Generated by: culture — tradition is culture's continuity across time, the means by which a community's living meaning-making is carried from one generation to the next.

Hans-Georg Gadamer, in *Truth and Method* (1960), argued that all understanding takes place within tradition.
We are always already embedded in inherited frameworks of meaning, and interpretation is a dialogue between our present horizon and the horizon of what was handed down to us.
On this account tradition is not the opposite of reason but its condition: we think *from* a tradition, never from nowhere, and even the act of criticizing an inheritance draws on the resources that inheritance gave us.
Edward Shils, in *Tradition* (1981), developed the point sociologically, showing that every society depends on the transmission of beliefs, practices, and institutions — and that even revolutionary movements, which define themselves against the past, build and hand down traditions of their own.

The political trouble begins when tradition is opposed to modernity as a strict binary.
Under that binary, "traditional" comes to mean static, pre-modern, unchanged, while "modern" means dynamic, progressive, transformed.
This opposition is the engine of two distinct forms of domination.
It powers colonial rule, which classifies Indigenous peoples as "traditional" and therefore as relics — available for displacement, assimilation, or museum preservation, but not as living agents in the present.
And it powers Romantic nationalism, which worships tradition as the authentic life that modernity has corrupted, and seeks to restore a frozen past that never existed in the form it imagines.

Both poles falsify what tradition actually is.
A living tradition is neither static nor abandoned; it occupies the middle position of inheriting, continuing, and transforming all at once.
A Lakota ceremony adapted to contemporary conditions is traditional.
A Kurdish bard's songs performed over satellite television are traditional.
What makes them traditional is not that they are unchanged but that they are continuous — that the thread of transmission has not been cut, even as it has been stretched, knotted, and rewoven.
To demand that a tradition stay identical to be authentic is to mistake death for fidelity.

Two operations destroy tradition by counterfeiting it.
The first is the freezing already described, which converts a living practice into a fixed image and then measures living people against it.
The second is the invention of tradition, Eric Hobsbawm's concept (1983) for practices that present themselves as ancient and organically continuous but are in fact recent, deliberately constructed, and serving present political needs — the Highland kilt, the codified national folk costume, the manufactured "ancient" ceremony.
Invented traditions are not simple lies; they draw on real materials, real songs and garments and stories, but curate and codify them until the result confirms what a nation or regime needs to believe about itself, and then claim a continuity with the past that the material never had.
Tradition, properly understood, is thus the reciprocal counterpart to both: against the frozen image and the fabricated continuity stands the actual living process of handing down — receiving, transforming, and passing on what a community carries through time.
