**Research snapshot:** 2026-06-11  
**Scope:** ActivityPub-conformant Web, including Mastodon, Pixelfed, PeerTube, Lemmy/Mbin/Kbin, Misskey/Sharkey/Iceshrimp, Pleroma/Akkoma, WriteFreely, BookWyrm, Owncast, WordPress ActivityPub, Ghost ActivityPub, Threads federation, Bridgy Fed, Bonfire, GoToSocial, and adjacent infrastructure.

## Abstract

The Fediverse is not a capital-free zone. It is a weakly monetized but intensely capitalized social-technical system. Its wealth does not primarily appear as ad revenue, stock price, or tokenized reward, but as reputation, trust, moderation legitimacy, domain prestige, software credibility, infrastructure capacity, long-lived account history, bridge position, archive visibility, and the ability to make other people’s posts, servers, projects, or identities legible across the network.

The core unit of Fediverse capital is not the individual account alone. It is the relation among account, instance, software, moderation regime, domain, federation graph, and public memory. A popular account may have followers; an instance may have trust; an admin may have discretion; a blocklist maintainer may have boundary-setting power; a software project may have adoption and compatibility authority; a bridge may have translation power between publics. These forms can convert into one another, but conversion is uneven, fragile, and morally policed.

The Fediverse partially resists platform capitalism by refusing a single owner, a single graph, a single algorithmic attention market, and a single monetization pipeline. But it does not abolish hierarchy. Federation redistributes hierarchy into admin discretion, moderation labor, software defaults, domain control, bridge chokepoints, donor relations, blocklists, searchability, and informal prestige economies. The important question is not whether the Fediverse is liberatory, but where its capital accumulates, who can convert it, and who bears the cost of maintaining it. [S1–S12]

## Core thesis

The Fediverse’s capital is **federated social trust made operational**.

That trust becomes valuable when it can do something: get a new account followed, get an instance accepted, get a server blocked, get a developer listened to, get a fundraiser funded, get a post boosted, get an archive indexed, get an invite granted, get a bridge tolerated, get a moderation decision believed, or get a project treated as part of “the Fediverse” rather than an invader.

This means Fediverse capital is always double-sided:

| Capital form | Commons face | Power face |
|---|---|---|
| Instance reputation | A safer, more legible home | Gatekeeping over membership and federation |
| Admin reputation | Trusted stewardship | Personal authority over infrastructure |
| Moderator legitimacy | Care work, safety, repair | Boundary power, opaque discipline |
| Follower graph | Portable audience, mutual recognition | Clout, harassment reach, asymmetric visibility |
| Blocklist authority | Shared defense against abuse | Delegated exclusion, false positives |
| Software credibility | Interoperability and maintainability | De facto standards, maintainer bottlenecks |
| Domain prestige | Durable identity and public memory | Ownership concentration, DNS dependence |
| Donor relations | Mutual aid and sustainability | Patron capture, popularity funding |
| Bridge capital | Cross-public accessibility | Translation chokepoints, corporate ingress |
| Archive/search visibility | Public memory and discoverability | Scraping, surveillance, context collapse |

The Fediverse is therefore best understood as a **distributed economy of social capital under infrastructural constraint**.

## 1. What counts as “wealth” in a low-monetization network?

In the Fediverse, wealth is the capacity to produce durable social effects without needing a centralized platform owner’s permission.

This includes:

| Wealth form | What it looks like | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Reach | Followers, boosts, replies, mentions, hashtags, cross-instance circulation | Lets actors affect publics beyond their home server |
| Trust | Being believed, followed, invited, cited, mirrored, or unblocked | Reduces friction in a suspicious network |
| Safety legitimacy | Being seen as someone who protects others well | Converts moral authority into moderation influence |
| Instance standing | A domain treated as safe, stable, well-run, and worth joining | Makes local membership valuable |
| Admin standing | A person or team treated as competent and fair | Makes discretionary power tolerable |
| Technical credibility | Contributions, docs, bug reports, compatibility work, FEP knowledge | Lets maintainers shape affordances |
| Infrastructure capacity | Uptime, storage, bandwidth, moderation tooling, backups, legal preparedness | Turns social demand into durable service |
| Memory | Old posts, account age, known handles, public archives, backlinks, search presence | Makes identity and contribution legible over time |
| Scarcity | Invite-only instances, closed registrations, selective communities | Converts belonging into status |
| Patronage | Donations, grants, mutual aid, hosting sponsorships | Converts reputation into operating capacity |
| Interoperability position | Bridges, relays, crawlers, publishers, plugins | Lets an actor route attention between publics |

Money exists, but mostly as a secondary conversion: reputation becomes donations; donations become infrastructure; infrastructure becomes instance reputation; instance reputation attracts users; users produce more reputation and moderation burden.

## 2. The capital of an instance

An instance is not just a server. It is a named jurisdiction, a neighborhood, a moderation boundary, a technical dependency, a public identity provider, and sometimes an archive.

The capital of an instance accumulates through:

| Instance asset | Accumulation mechanism | Conversion path | Loss condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Domain name | Memorable URL, niche identity, institutional credibility | Trust, searchability, user recruitment | Domain loss, sale, takeover, stigma |
| Local culture | Shared norms, posting style, humor, politics, etiquette | Member loyalty, invite demand | Culture war, growth shock, admin abuse |
| Moderation reputation | Clear rules, fair enforcement, responsive reports | Federation trust, fewer blocks | Opaque decisions, tolerated abuse, burnout |
| Federation graph | Who it talks to, who blocks it, who follows it | Reach, legitimacy, discovery | Defederation, spam, bad neighbors |
| Admin capacity | Competent operators, backups, upgrades, security | Reliability, donor confidence | Downtime, breaches, abandonment |
| Local timeline | A curated ambient public | Belonging, identity, discovery | Overgrowth, spam, topic drift |
| Invite scarcity | Closed or selective registration | Status and safety | Cliques, exclusion, stagnation |
| Policy clarity | Rules, privacy policy, blocklist rationale | Lower uncertainty for users/admins | Ambiguity, arbitrary enforcement |
| Historical continuity | Long-lived accounts and archives | Institutional memory | Server shutdown, unimportable posts |

Instance capital is fragile because it is both social and infrastructural. A beloved instance can lose capital through a security incident, unpaid hosting bill, admin conflict, defederation cascade, or growth spike. A small instance can be culturally rich but structurally poor: high trust, low uptime, low moderation bandwidth.

## 3. The capital of an admin

The admin is the ambiguous sovereign of the Fediverse. They may be a volunteer caretaker, sysadmin, founder, moderator, donor, legal contact, policy writer, and emergency responder at once.

Admin capital has several layers:

| Admin capital | Source | Converts into | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technical competence | Keeping the service running | Trust, donations, federation confidence | Invisible labor until failure |
| Moral legitimacy | Fairness, anti-abuse practice, care for vulnerable users | Compliance, deference, public support | Paternalism, clique rule |
| Network position | Relationships with other admins/mods | Defederation coordination, incident response | Backchannel governance |
| Public communication | Transparent announcements and appeals | Reduced panic, higher trust | Overexposure, harassment |
| Scarcity control | Opening/closing registrations, invites | Cultural shaping, safety | Gatekeeping, status hoarding |
| Interpretive authority | Deciding what rules mean | Governance power | Arbitrary enforcement |
| Fundraising trust | Ability to ask for money credibly | Infrastructure capacity | Donor dependence |

Admin reputation is valuable because federation is not automatic social belonging. Other servers must decide whether an instance is safe enough to interact with. Admins become the human face of that decision.

## 4. Moderator legitimacy as capital

Moderation in the Fediverse is not merely content removal. It is network boundary maintenance. It determines whether an instance remains connected, trusted, and livable.

Moderator legitimacy accumulates through:

- responsiveness to reports;
- transparent norms;
- consistency across similar cases;
- willingness to explain without turning every decision into a public trial;
- sensitivity to harassment, racism, transmisogyny, ableism, antisemitism, casteism, colonialism, and other harms;
- refusal to outsource all judgment to imported blocklists;
- refusal to treat every conflict as equivalent.

This legitimacy converts into real power. A trusted moderator can recommend a domain block and be believed. A trusted moderation team can ask other admins to investigate an instance. A trusted safety project can shape federation boundaries across thousands of users.

But moderation legitimacy is also one of the easiest forms of Fediverse capital to lose. A false positive, concealed conflict of interest, public pile-on, uneven enforcement, or affiliation with abusive actors can rapidly convert accumulated trust into suspicion.

## 5. Personal account capital

A popular Fediverse account has capital, but less cleanly than on Twitter/X, Bluesky, Substack, or Hive. There is no single global algorithm that reliably converts engagement into impressions, no built-in ad-revenue share, no universal quote-dunk machine across all software, and no single platform-wide verification regime.

Personal capital still accumulates through:

| Signal | Capital type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Follows | Durable relational capital | Stronger than likes; partially portable in Mastodon migration if software supports Move |
| Boosts / repeats / reposts | Distribution capital | A public endorsement that moves posts into other home feeds |
| Replies | Relational and conversational capital | Builds familiarity, but also conflict and obligation |
| Likes / favorites | Weak attention capital | Often less public and less distributive than boosts |
| Mentions | Invocation capital | Calls a person into a thread; can be care or harassment |
| Hashtags | Discovery capital | More important where algorithmic discovery is weaker |
| Lists | Curatorial capital | Often private or semi-private infrastructure of attention |
| Account age | Historical capital | Signals durability, context, and recognizability |
| Handle/domain | Identity capital | A stable domain or known instance can become prestige |
| Cross-platform identity | Bridge capital | Lets followers recognize the same actor across protocols |

Fediverse clout is the ability to make people care without a platform ranking system doing the caring for them. It is policed by anti-virality norms, suspicion of influencer behavior, hostility to engagement bait, and norms around consent, content warnings, alt text, credit, and not importing Twitter-style dunking.

## 6. Replies, boosts, follows, mentions, and endorsements as distinct capital forms

The Fediverse has multiple attention currencies, and they do not convert evenly.

| Action | What it gives | What it risks | Capital reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Follow | Ongoing audience relation | Social obligation, graph exposure | Durable social capital |
| Boost | Distribution and endorsement | Amplifying harm, context collapse | Circulation capital |
| Like/favorite | Recognition, weak signal | Misread endorsement | Low-cost attention capital |
| Reply | Conversation, presence, intimacy | Derailment, harassment, conflict | Relational capital |
| Mention | Summoning attention | Dogpiling, boundary violation | Invocation capital |
| Hashtag use | Discoverability | Spam, audience collision | Indexing capital |
| Recommendation | Trust transfer | Reputation risk | Endorsement capital |
| Instance invite | Membership and status | Gatekeeping | Belonging capital |
| Block/report | Boundary claim | Abuse of process | Negative capital / defensive capital |

A boost is not just “engagement.” It is a voluntary routing decision. A follow is not just audience capture. It is a durable subscription relation mediated by instance policy. A reply is not just visibility. It is a claim on attention and social context. This makes Fediverse capital more relational and less directly financialized than on ad platforms.

## 7. Cross-instance trust

Cross-instance trust is the Fediverse’s most important infrastructural social capital.

It answers questions like:

- Can our users safely see posts from that server?
- Will their admin respond to abuse reports?
- Are their moderators reachable?
- Do they allow harassment campaigns?
- Are their accounts mostly real users, bots, spam, or bad-faith actors?
- Does their software federate correctly?
- Do they honor privacy expectations?
- Are they likely to disappear?

Cross-instance trust is built through ordinary federation, but also through crisis. During spam waves, harassment campaigns, security incidents, or corporate integration events, admins and moderators make rapid judgments. Those judgments accumulate into a memory of which domains are safe, risky, chaotic, abandoned, or hostile.

## 8. Blocklist authority

A blocklist maintainer accumulates capital by transforming distributed reports, screenshots, moderator experience, and public incidents into a portable boundary object.

That object can be imported, forked, disputed, cited, or treated as a starting point for server governance. Its authority depends on:

| Criterion | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Receipts | Allows others to evaluate claims |
| Clear inclusion criteria | Prevents “bad vibes” from becoming infrastructure |
| Categories or severity levels | Avoids binary all-or-nothing blocking |
| Update discipline | Keeps stale incidents from becoming permanent exile |
| Appeals or correction paths | Reduces irreversible harm |
| Maintainer accountability | Makes delegated exclusion governable |
| Community trust | Determines whether admins import it |

Blocklist capital is powerful because it compresses labor. An admin who cannot investigate every bad server may trust someone else’s curation. But this also makes blocklists a site of delegated sovereignty. The maintainer can become a hidden governor.

## 9. Software project capital

Software projects accumulate Fediverse capital when they become dependable ways of inhabiting ActivityPub.

Mastodon has disproportionate de facto standard-setting power because many other projects must decide whether to interoperate with Mastodon-style expectations. But the Fediverse is not Mastodon. Different software produces different capital regimes:

| Software family | Primary capital | Distinctive issue |
|---|---|---|
| Mastodon | Microblogging reach, admin tooling, default expectations | De facto standard pressure |
| Pixelfed | Visual/art/photo identity and Instagram-alternative legitimacy | Media hosting and mobile discovery |
| PeerTube | Video hosting autonomy and creator archive control | Bandwidth/storage costs, audience fragmentation |
| Lemmy/Mbin/Kbin | Community/forum/link aggregation capital | Reddit-like mod power plus federation conflict |
| Misskey/Sharkey/Iceshrimp | Expressive sociality, reactions, Japanese/alt microblog cultures | Compatibility variation |
| Pleroma/Akkoma | Lightweight/customizable microblogging | Reputation unevenness across instances |
| GoToSocial | Small-server/self-host capital | Fewer built-in features, client dependence |
| WriteFreely | Longform/blogging capital | Weaker social affordances |
| BookWyrm | Reading/review/community catalog capital | Specialized object types |
| Owncast | Live video/community event capital | Partial federation expectations |
| WordPress ActivityPub | Domain-based publishing capital | Publisher/comment interop complexity |
| Ghost ActivityPub | Newsletter/publication capital | Beta maturity and hosted-product dynamics |
| Bonfire | Boundary/privacy/experimental governance capital | Emerging adoption |
| Bridgy Fed | Cross-protocol translation capital | Bridge trust, consent, failure modes |
| Threads federation | Corporate-scale audience and ingress capital | Moderation, asymmetry, enclosure risk |

Software capital converts into grants, contributors, hosted services, conference invitations, user trust, and protocol influence. It is lost through security failures, maintainer burnout, poor interop, abandoned documentation, hostile governance, or incompatibility with dominant expectations.

## 10. Bridge and publisher capital

Bridges and publishers are increasingly important because the Fediverse is becoming less purely “instance social media” and more a social layer for websites, newsletters, blogs, video hosts, forums, and corporate platforms.

Bridge capital is the ability to translate identity and interaction across publics. Bridgy Fed, for example, can make a Bluesky or website actor visible from ActivityPub contexts. Threads federation lets public Threads users opt into some ActivityPub visibility. Ghost and WordPress ActivityPub turn publications into followable social actors. [S6]

This creates new wealth forms:

| Actor | Capital gained | Capital risk |
|---|---|---|
| Independent publisher | Direct followers outside email/RSS | Comments, harassment, moderation burden |
| Newsletter platform | Social discovery and retention | Platformization of the open social layer |
| Corporate platform | Legitimacy from openness | Asymmetric scale and policy mismatch |
| Bridge maintainer | Translation authority | Being blamed by every side |
| Website owner | Domain identity and archive capital | Technical complexity |
| Reader/follower | Cross-platform access | Context collapse and privacy confusion |

Bridge capital is potentially commons-producing, but it is also the clearest path for enclosure. A bridge can help users escape silos, or it can become the chokepoint through which capitalized platforms absorb open-network legitimacy.

## 11. Conversion paths

The Fediverse is full of conversions, even where money is absent.

| Starting capital | Converts into | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Personal followers | Donations, consulting, invitations, job leads, publication reach | Trust and attention |
| Admin reputation | Federation trust, instance growth, donations | Operational credibility |
| Moderator legitimacy | Defederation influence, blocklist authority | Safety expertise |
| Instance reputation | Invite demand, user loyalty, local culture | Belonging |
| Blocklist authority | Network boundary power | Labor compression |
| Software credibility | Grants, adoption, contributors | Technical trust |
| Domain prestige | Durable identity and search value | Naming and memory |
| Archive visibility | Citation, journalism, research attention | Public memory |
| Bridge position | Cross-network influence | Translation |
| Ideological authority | Community leadership, exclusion power | Shared values |
| Infrastructure capacity | Growth, reliability, donor confidence | Uptime and scale |
| Early adopter status | Interpretive authority | Historical continuity |

The crucial pattern: **social capital often converts into moderation power before it converts into money.** In the Fediverse, the ability to decide who is safe, who is real, who belongs, and who should be blocked may be more immediately valuable than the ability to monetize posts.

## 12. How capital is lost

Fediverse capital is reversible. It can disappear quickly when trust fails.

| Capital holder | Loss event |
|---|---|
| Popular account | Harassment scandal, plagiarism, excessive clout-seeking, bad migration, instance shutdown |
| Admin | Downtime, arbitrary bans, poor incident response, hidden conflicts, donation opacity |
| Moderator | Inconsistent decisions, public cruelty, capture by cliques, refusal to correct errors |
| Instance | Defederation, spam, poor rules, abandoned maintenance, bad neighbors |
| Blocklist maintainer | False positives, opaque criteria, stale entries, personal vendettas |
| Software project | Security failures, abandoned roadmap, poor interop, hostile maintainers |
| Bridge | Consent failures, broken translation, corporate capture |
| Publisher | Spammy posting, off-platform extraction, comment neglect |
| Donor-funded project | Funding cliff, patron influence, failure to communicate |

The Fediverse remembers, but unevenly. Memory may persist in posts, screenshots, blocklists, admin backchannels, search indexes, and folklore. This makes reputational repair possible but difficult.

## 13. Comparison with other platforms

| Platform | Dominant capital form | Portability | Monetization | Governance locus | Fediverse contrast |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Twitter/X | Algorithmic reach, paid verification, monetized impressions | Low | Ads, subscriptions, creator revenue | Corporate platform | Fediverse weakens central algorithmic capital but strengthens instance/admin capital |
| Bluesky | Handle/DID identity, follows, custom feeds, starter packs, ATProto portability | Medium-high by design | Emerging/indirect | Bluesky company plus protocol ecosystem | Fediverse has broader software diversity but weaker account portability |
| Discord | Server membership, roles, boosts, private chat archives | Low | Nitro, boosts, subscriptions | Server owners plus Discord | Fediverse is more public and web-addressable; Discord capital is more enclosed |
| Reddit | Subreddit mod power, karma, votes, community scale | Low | Ads, awards/contributor program | Reddit plus volunteer mods | Lemmy-like Fediverse spaces distribute Reddit-like moderation across instances |
| Hive | Tokenized stake, rewards, curation, governance | Blockchain-native | Direct token rewards | Stake-weighted protocol | Fediverse attention is not natively financialized |
| Substack | Email list, paid subscribers, writer brand | Medium via email export, but network effects remain | Paid subscriptions/platform cut | Platform plus publication owner | Fediverse publishing capital is less directly monetized but more interoperable |
| Forums | Domain/community memory, admin/mod legitimacy | Usually low | Donations, ads, memberships | Site owner/community | Fediverse resembles forums plus cross-site subscription/federation |

The Fediverse is closest to forums, email, RSS, and self-hosted blogs, but with social graph delivery. It is least like Hive because social attention does not automatically become token reward. It is unlike Twitter/X because there is no single engagement market. It is unlike Discord because much of the network is public and addressable. It is unlike Bluesky because ActivityPub has a larger multi-software ecology but a weaker portability model.

## 14. Worked examples

### Example A: A small queer/trans/disabled tech instance

A small invite-only instance accumulates safety capital through moderation, shared norms, alt-text expectations, consent norms, anti-harassment practice, and admin availability. Its wealth is not size. Its wealth is credible refuge.

Conversion paths:

1. Safety reputation attracts users.
2. Invite scarcity converts belonging into status.
3. Member loyalty converts into donations.
4. Donations convert into hosting and moderation capacity.
5. Good incident response converts into cross-instance trust.

Risk: the same safety capital can become gatekeeping. Admins may become unaccountable, conflicts may become personality politics, and invite scarcity may reproduce exclusion.

### Example B: A blocklist maintainer

A blocklist maintainer collects reports of abusive or poorly moderated servers and publishes a list. Small admins import it because they lack time to investigate every server.

Conversion paths:

1. Moderation labor becomes a dataset.
2. Dataset becomes trusted infrastructure.
3. Trust becomes boundary-setting authority.
4. Boundary-setting authority becomes political capital.

Risk: if the list lacks receipts, categories, or correction paths, moral judgment becomes infrastructural exile. A bad entry can isolate an instance without due process.

### Example C: A popular account migrates instances

A user with a large Mastodon following moves from a large general instance to a self-hosted GoToSocial or small Mastodon instance. Some follower capital moves if the old and new accounts use supported migration flows. Old posts and media do not necessarily move, and follows/blocks/bookmarks may need separate export/import. [S2]

Conversion paths:

1. Follower graph partially ports.
2. Domain identity may improve if self-hosted.
3. Local timeline capital changes completely.
4. Search/archive continuity may fracture.
5. Old instance reputation may no longer protect the user.

Conclusion: federation makes some social capital portable, but traps other capital in server-local archives, software affordances, and community memory.

### Example D: Threads federation

Threads brings corporate-scale audience capital to ActivityPub. Public Threads users who opt into federation can be followed and interacted with from other servers, depending on current feature limits. [S6]

Conversion paths:

1. Corporate user base becomes potential Fediverse reach.
2. ActivityPub compatibility becomes Meta legitimacy.
3. Fediverse actors gain access to larger publics.
4. Large-scale moderation problems enter smaller networks.

Risk: scale asymmetry. Threads can gain open-web legitimacy while remaining the dominant holder of user attention, design control, moderation policy, and product analytics.

### Example E: Ghost and WordPress ActivityPub

A publication using Ghost or WordPress ActivityPub turns a website into a followable actor. Its capital is not just followers; it is domain-owned publishing plus social delivery.

Conversion paths:

1. Domain archive becomes feed content.
2. Fediverse follows become readership.
3. Replies become comments or social feedback.
4. Publication credibility becomes social graph growth.

Risk: publishers may treat the Fediverse as another outbound channel rather than a governed social space. Comment moderation and reader safety become part of publishing infrastructure.

### Example F: Lemmy community federation

A Lemmy community accumulates Reddit-like capital: subscribers, moderators, posting norms, topic authority, and archive value. But federation means posts and moderation actions may be interpreted through multiple instances.

Conversion paths:

1. Community subscribers become topic authority.
2. Moderator legitimacy becomes content ordering and removal power.
3. Instance reputation affects who can interact.
4. Cross-instance conflict can split the public.

Risk: community capital may be trapped by instance politics. A community can be popular but isolated if its home instance becomes distrusted.

## 15. Extraction risks

The Fediverse’s low monetization is not immunity. It creates particular extraction patterns.

| Risk | Description | Who benefits | Who pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| Care-labor extraction | Moderators/admins provide safety for free | Users, instances, broader network | Mods/admins |
| Donor dependency | Projects rely on patrons or major gifts | Users, maintainers | Maintainers, donors with influence |
| Blocklist capture | Informal authorities set network boundaries | Overloaded admins | Listed communities, edge cases |
| Corporate ingress | Large platforms federate selectively | Corporate platforms | Smaller instances handling spillover |
| Search/archive extraction | Public posts become datasets | Researchers, AI firms, journalists | Users and admins |
| Compatibility capture | One implementation becomes de facto standard | Dominant software | Smaller projects |
| Prestige hoarding | Early adopters define “real fedi” norms | Established users | Newcomers, marginalized latecomers |
| Invite gatekeeping | Scarcity becomes status | Insiders | Excluded users |
| Bridge chokepoints | Cross-network access depends on few maintainers | Bridge operators, users | Everyone if bridge fails |
| Anti-monetization moralism | Suspicion of money prevents sustainable funding | Purist status-holders | Care workers and infra maintainers |

The Fediverse often treats monetization as contamination, but unpaid labor is not automatically non-extractive. A serious capital analysis must distinguish anti-capitalist practice from hidden subsidy by exhausted volunteers.

## 16. Strategic framework

To evaluate a Fediverse project, instance, or intervention, ask:

| Question | Good sign | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns the domain? | Durable, accountable ownership | Personal domain held by one absent admin |
| Who pays the bills? | Transparent funding | Secret patron or unpaid crisis labor |
| Who moderates? | Named process, shared workload | One exhausted person |
| How are blocks decided? | Criteria, receipts, review | Imported list with no review |
| Can users leave? | Migration docs, exports, domain strategy | Social pressure or technical lock-in |
| What is archived? | Clear retention/search policy | Surprise indexing or scraping |
| What software governs affordances? | Maintained, documented, interoperable | Fork drift with no support |
| How does it handle growth? | Registration policy and capacity planning | Viral growth plus no moderation plan |
| What capital is being accumulated? | Trust, safety, public knowledge | Clout, dependence, opacity |
| Who can convert the capital? | Community and maintainers | Only admins, founders, or platforms |

## 17. Open questions

1. Can the Fediverse develop portable reputation without recreating centralized verification?
2. Can account migration become content migration without breaking trust, signatures, and context?
3. Can blocklists become accountable institutions without becoming bureaucratic police?
4. How should public posts be treated in research, archiving, AI training, and search indexing?
5. What funding models support moderation and infrastructure without importing platform capitalism?
6. How should small instances negotiate with Threads-scale actors?
7. Can bridge infrastructure be governed as a commons?
8. What metrics measure Fediverse health besides user count and monthly activity?
9. How do racialized, queer, trans, disabled, Indigenous, furry, academic, art, journalist, and tech publics accumulate different forms of ideological capital?
10. When does “anti-clout” become its own clout?
11. How do software defaults create class positions among admins?
12. Can instance governance scale without reproducing Reddit-like moderator oligarchies?
13. What should be monetized, what should be funded, and what should remain deliberately non-market?

## 18. Annotated bibliography

**ActivityPub specification and protocol base.** Establishes actors, inboxes, outboxes, client-server API, and server-server federation. Necessary for distinguishing protocol facts from Mastodon-specific culture. [S1]

**Mastodon documentation: moderation and migration.** Useful because Mastodon remains the de facto reference implementation for many Fediverse expectations. Especially important for domain blocks, local-only moderation, imported blocklists, and the limits of account migration. [S2]

**Fediverse network statistics.** FediDB, Fediverse Observer, and Mastodon’s own annual reports give approximate scale, but all counts should be treated as unstable and method-dependent. [S3]

**Implementation documentation.** Pixelfed, PeerTube, Lemmy, WriteFreely, BookWyrm, GoToSocial, Akkoma, Bonfire, WordPress ActivityPub, Ghost ActivityPub, and Bridgy Fed show that the Fediverse is not one product but a multi-software ecology. [S4–S6]

**Moderation and governance research.** Studies of admin burden, rule scaling, defederation, and decentralized moderation show that governance labor is central rather than incidental. [S7]

**Blocklist research.** Recent work treats blocklists as community-level moderation infrastructure and as moral/administrative lists with risks around opacity, binary judgment, and growth. [S8]

**Migration and social influence studies.** Research on Twitter-to-Mastodon and academic Twitter migration shows that existing social graphs help migration but do not guarantee sustained engagement. [S9]

**Funding and political economy.** Mastodon’s annual reports and failed monetization experiments like sub.club show the tension between donations, grants, major gifts, paid services, and creator monetization. [S10]

**Comparative platform sources.** X, Discord, Reddit, Substack, Bluesky, and Hive clarify how Fediverse capital differs from monetized reach, server boosts, karma/gold, paid subscriptions, account portability, and tokenized curation. [S11–S12]

## Bottom line

The capital of the Fediverse is not “followers.” It is the capacity to make a relation durable across servers.

The richest Fediverse actors are not always the biggest accounts. They are the actors whose trust can be operationalized: admins who can make a domain legible, moderators who can make safety credible, maintainers who can make software interoperable, blocklist curators who can make danger portable, bridge operators who can make publics visible to one another, and communities whose norms make people want to stay.

Federation does not abolish capital. It changes its topology.

`[S4]` Implementation docs establish the multi-software scope: Pixelfed uses ActivityPub for Pixelfed and non-Pixelfed federation; PeerTube implements ActivityPub for video federation; Lemmy documents ActivityPub federation around instances and site bans; WriteFreely supports ActivityPub when enabled; BookWyrm uses ActivityPub with extended book-related types. <https://pixelfed.github.io/docs-next/spec/ActivityPub.html>

`[S5]` GoToSocial, Akkoma, and Bonfire show smaller/emerging governance and software shapes: GoToSocial describes itself as lightweight and safety-focused, Akkoma documents admin message-rewrite/moderation hooks, and Bonfire documents instance-level blocks and boundary controls. <https://gotosocial.org/>

`[S6]` Threads, Ghost, WordPress ActivityPub, and Bridgy Fed are key bridge/publisher sources: Meta describes Threads fediverse sharing as opt-in for public profiles; Ghost’s social web beta is ActivityPub-based but notes rough edges; the WordPress ActivityPub plugin broadcasts blogs and receives comments; Bridgy Fed connects websites, the Fediverse, and Bluesky. <https://help.instagram.com/169559812696339>

`[S7]` Moderation/governance research: Anaobi et al. study admin burden; Bono et al. study blocklisting as decentralized moderation; Muralidharan, Ahn, and Truong’s 2026 paper analyzes how Mastodon rules scale; Colglazier, TeBlunthuis, and Shaw find defederation reduces activity on blocked servers but not blocking servers, with no evidence of toxicity reduction. <https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05915>

`[S8]` Blocklist sources: Zhang et al. analyze community-level blocklists and note variation in goals, criteria, and transparency; Gehl frames lists as moral/administrative acts; Garden Fence and Oliphant are public blocklist examples. <https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05522v1>

`[S9]` Migration studies: La Cava et al. study social influence in Twitter-to-Mastodon migration; Wang, Koneru, and Rajtmajer’s “Failed Migration of Academic Twitter” finds strong internal connectivity did not sustain academic migration; broader migration-pattern work compares user behavior across Twitter and Mastodon. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10703911/>

`[S10]` Funding and political economy: Mastodon’s 2024 annual report reports multiple revenue streams, major gifts, grants, commercial contracts, recurring small donors, technical hosting costs, moderation workload, and nonprofit restructuring; The Verge reported sub.club’s shutdown after failing to get sufficient traction/funding. <https://joinmastodon.org/reports/Mastodon%20Annual%20Report%202024.pdf>

`[S11]` Comparative platform references: X requires Premium/Premium Business/Premium Organizations, Stripe, and identity verification for creator monetization; Discord Nitro includes server boosts; Reddit’s Contributor Program ties awards/gold and karma to eligibility; Substack describes paid subscriptions as its core revenue model. <https://help.x.com/en/rules-and-policies/content-monetization-standards>
