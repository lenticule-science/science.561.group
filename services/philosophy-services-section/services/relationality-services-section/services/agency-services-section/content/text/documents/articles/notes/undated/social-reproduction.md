# social-reproduction

Social reproduction is the process by which a social order perpetuates itself across time — reproducing not only its members but its positions, hierarchies, and relations, so that the same structure reappears in the next generation even though every particular person and event passes.
Generated by: **structure** (reproducing) — a sedimented pattern of relations persists by continually remaking the conditions of its own existence, and where it fails to remake them, it transforms.

The classic sociological statement is Bourdieu and Passeron's *Reproduction in Education, Society and Culture* (1970).
Their question is how class structure survives across generations in a society that no longer transmits position by formal inheritance or law, but claims to allocate it by merit through open schooling.
Their answer is that the school does the work of reproduction precisely by appearing not to.
It rewards the cultural capital — the dispositions, language, tastes, and ease that privileged families transmit at home — while presenting its judgments as neutral measures of ability.
Children arrive unequally equipped; the school treats them as equally placed; the unequal outcome is then experienced by all parties as a verdict on talent rather than an effect of inheritance.
The hierarchy is reproduced *and* legitimated in a single motion, which is what makes school more effective at reproduction than open privilege would be.
This is symbolic violence at the scale of a whole social order.

Reproduction is not automatic and it is not free.
An order has to keep producing the conditions of its own continuation: it has to form people who will occupy its positions, instill the dispositions those positions require, regenerate the resources it consumes, and renew its claim to legitimacy with each cohort.
Sociology after Durkheim treats this as the central puzzle of social continuity — how arrangements that exist only in being enacted manage to be enacted the same way again and again.
Giddens's structuration and Bourdieu's habitus are both answers: structure persists only because action reproduces it, and action reproduces it because the actors have been formed by the structure they go on to reproduce.
The order is carried in the bodies and dispositions of the people it shapes.

There is a second, materialist sense of social reproduction, developed in the Marxist-feminist tradition (Federici, the wages-for-housework debates, more recently social-reproduction theory).
Here reproduction names the labor — overwhelmingly gendered, largely unwaged, performed in the household and community — that produces and replenishes labor power itself: feeding, raising, nursing, and emotionally sustaining the workers that capitalist production consumes and assumes.
The argument is that production cannot account for its own conditions: capital depends on a sphere of life-making labor it neither pays for nor acknowledges, and the systematic devaluation of that labor is one of the structuring facts of the social order.
This sense and the Bourdieusian sense are complementary — one names how positions and legitimacy are reproduced, the other how the people and capacities that fill those positions are.
The life-making labor itself is treated under **care-work**; the cross-reference matters because the two analyses meet exactly at the point where reproducing an order and reproducing its people turn out to be the same problem.

What makes reproduction sociologically interesting is that it can fail.
An order reproduces itself only so long as it can keep absorbing the strains its own operation generates — and where the strains accumulate faster than they can be absorbed, the same machinery that reproduced the order begins to transform it.
Reproduction and transformation are therefore not opposites but two outcomes of one process; the difference between a society that perpetuates itself and one that changes lies in whether its contradictions can still be metabolized.
