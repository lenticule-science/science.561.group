# Halliday 1985 — *An Introduction to Functional Grammar*

M. A. K. Halliday, *An Introduction to Functional Grammar*, 1st ed. (London: Edward Arnold, 1985). The systemic-functional grammar (SFG) tradition's canonical entry point.

## What it establishes

Halliday's grammar is *functional* in two senses: it explains grammatical choices by reference to the social and communicative functions they serve, and it organises the description around three **metafunctions** that every clause simultaneously enacts:

- **Ideational.** What is the clause *about*? — the experiential and logical relations the clause encodes (transitivity, causation, taxonomy).
- **Interpersonal.** What is the speaker *doing* to the addressee? — mood, modality, evaluation, the social relationship enacted.
- **Textual.** How does the clause *connect* to what comes before and after? — theme/rheme, given/new, cohesion.

Every clause encodes all three metafunctions simultaneously; grammatical choices in one metafunction can be made independently of choices in the others. This is the load-bearing structural claim.

The metafunctions are realised through systems — networks of choices a speaker makes, with each choice opening up further choices. Halliday's grammar is a *systemic* grammar in the sense that it specifies the choice systems and their realisations; the description of any text is the trace through the systems that the text's clauses enact.

The framework also formalises **register** as the configuration of language a speaker selects to fit a situation, characterised along three dimensions:

- **Field.** What is happening? — the social activity the language is part of.
- **Tenor.** Who is taking part? — the relationship between participants.
- **Mode.** What role does the language play? — spoken/written, monologic/dialogic, composing/repeating.

A register is the joint specification of field, tenor, and mode. Different registers select different metafunctional resources; the systemic grammar makes the selection patterns describable.

## Why this corpus cites it

Halliday's register framework is the foundational reference for the [register](../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/archive/references/teaching/explanations/register.md) concept and load-bears on [genre](../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/archive/references/teaching/explanations/genre.md) (genres are conventional register patterns) and [pragmatics](../../../glossary/concepts/pragmatics.md) (much pragmatic content is interpersonal-metafunction content). The field/tenor/mode decomposition is what the predicated-mvks subtheory's [pragmatic-register](../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/information/theories/knowledge-systems/theories/markdown-vault-knowledge-system/theories/predicated-markdown-vault-knowledge-system/concepts/pragmatic-register.md) concept bottoms out in when it asks "what register does this note type write in" — the answer is a triple of field (the note's subject), tenor (its author–reader relationship), and mode (markdown-as-written-medium).

The metafunction analysis is also the cleanest available account of why the same proposition can be expressed in registers that *do different things* despite carrying the same ideational content: the interpersonal and textual metafunctions are doing the differentiating work. A theorem stated as a `definition` and a theorem stated as a `narrative` differ in interpersonal stance (assertive vs. expository) and textual organisation (atomic vs. through-line) but can encode the same ideational content.
