# identity

Identity is the ongoing process by which a person or group comes to understand itself as itself — as continuous across time, as distinct from others, as bearing certain commitments and characteristics.
The concept is at once indispensable and treacherous.
It is indispensable because people really do experience themselves as having identities, and much of political life is organized around identity claims.
It is treacherous because the concept tends to reify: to treat a fluid, contested, relational process as if it were a fixed thing that a person or a group simply possesses.
Generated by: community — identity is the self-understanding formed in relation, the sense of self a person or people works out within the shared life of a community.

Stuart Hall, in "Who Needs 'Identity'?"
(1996), proposed understanding identity not as what someone *is* but as what someone is *becoming* — a process of identification rather than a settled state.
Identity, on Hall's account, is constituted through narrative: the stories a person or group tells about where they came from, what they have been through, and where they are going.
These narratives are always partial, always contested, always under revision; there is no moment at which an identity is finally complete.
Identity is something one is perpetually doing, not something one has finished being.
This reframing matters because it locates identity in relation and in time rather than in essence — one identifies *with* others, *against* others, *through* a history, and all of these can shift.

Rogers Brubaker and Frederick Cooper, in "Beyond 'Identity'" (2000), pressed a more skeptical argument: that "identity" has become analytically useless precisely because it has come to mean everything at once.
They proposed unbundling it into more specific concepts that do the work the word only gestures at — identification (the act of classifying oneself or others), self-understanding (one's lived sense of who one is), social location (one's position in social structure), commonality (sharing attributes with others), and connectedness (relational ties).
Each of these can be examined without the metaphysical baggage of a single fixed "identity" that one either has or lacks.
Their critique is a tool against reification: it lets us describe what identity-talk points to without smuggling in the assumption that there is a stable essence underneath.

The danger the concept guards against is the conversion of identity-as-process into identity-as-type.
When a fluid process of identification is frozen into a fixed category, that category becomes a standard against which living people are measured.
The frozen figure — the authorized image of what a people supposedly is — does exactly this: those who match the image are recognized as authentic, while those who do not are judged to have lost, betrayed, or never truly possessed the identity.
The person whose sense of self is genuinely in motion, inheriting and transforming, has no place in such a scheme, because identity-as-process is invisible to a framework that recognizes only identity-as-type.
Even well-meant recognition can do this, fixing a people as a category in order to grant it standing, and fixing it is also confining it.

Within the reciprocal relations, identity is the self-understanding that community generates in its members, the counterpart to culture (the community's meaning-making) and collective memory (its remembering): identity is how a person or group locates itself within those shared processes.
Its reciprocal, living form is identification-in-process — relational, contested, historically produced, never finished — and its captured form is the excluding type, the single fixed image that recognition and cultural commodification produce when they convert a people's living self-understanding into a category that admits some and casts out the rest.
