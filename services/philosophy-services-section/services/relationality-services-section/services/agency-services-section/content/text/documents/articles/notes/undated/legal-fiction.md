# legal-fiction

A legal fiction is a statement accepted as true by a legal system despite being known to be false, or at least unverifiable — adopted not because it describes reality but because it produces useful legal results.
Generated by: legal fictions are law's way of accommodating new realities without explicitly rewriting its rules.
Corporate personhood is the paradigm: the law treats a corporation as a "person" who can own property, enter contracts, and sue and be sued.
No one believes a corporation is literally a person; the fiction exists because it solves a practical problem — how to assign rights and obligations to a collective entity — and the pretense is maintained because it works.

Legal fictions are pervasive, and most are invisible until pointed out.
The doctrine of constructive notice holds that a person "knows" information recorded in a public registry even if they never looked at it.
The attractive-nuisance doctrine holds that a landowner "invited" the trespassing children whom a dangerous condition on the property lured in.
Adoption law treats an adopted child as the biological child of the adoptive parents for purposes of inheritance.
In each case the law deliberately attributes a fact that everyone knows to be untrue, because doing so lets it reach an outcome it considers just or administratively workable.
The fiction is not a lie in the ordinary sense; it is an openly acknowledged "as if," a stipulation that for legal purposes the world shall be treated as though something were so.

Lon Fuller, in *Legal Fictions* (1930), argued that fictions are characteristically *transitional* devices.
They appear when a legal system confronts a new social reality that its existing rules cannot straightforwardly accommodate, and they let the system absorb that reality without the disruption of formally amending its doctrine.
Rather than rewrite the law of persons to include collective entities, the law fictionalizes the corporation as a person; rather than rewrite property law to track communal land use, it fictionalizes constructive notice.
The fiction preserves the formal integrity of the existing doctrine — everything still fits the old categories — while quietly allowing the system to reach results those categories alone would not support.
Over time a successful fiction often hardens into settled doctrine, its fictional origin forgotten.

The sociological interest is that legal fictions expose, more clearly than almost anything else, the gap between legal categories and social reality — and the legal system's preferred method of managing that gap.
Faced with a mismatch between its categories and the world, the system does not, as a rule, adapt its categories to the world; it adapts the world to its categories, declaring that for legal purposes the world will count as being whatever the doctrine requires.
This is legal formatting in its most explicit and self-aware form: the law openly announcing that it describes not what is, but what, for its own purposes, shall be taken to be.

Within this cluster the legal fiction is the most candid instrument of legal formatting and the engine behind the most consequential reach of legal personhood — the corporate person.
It reveals the constructed character of the categories law treats as natural, and it shows that the legal order's authority extends not only to commanding conduct but to stipulating, within its own domain, what shall count as true.
