# law

Law is the binding declaration a polity issues and enforces — the standing, general rule that an authority puts in force and backs with the threat of sanction.
Generated by: law is authority in its declaring form, the act by which a norm is not merely held but officially pronounced and made enforceable.
It joins three things: a norm (the rule itself), authority (the recognized right to declare it), and coercion (the force that stands behind it).
Law is what a command becomes when it is generalized — addressed to everyone rather than a single person, standing rather than momentary — and in the modern world it is characteristically issued by the state.

What law *is* has been the central dispute of legal philosophy.
The command theory of John Austin held that law is simply the general command of a sovereign backed by sanction.
Legal positivism, in its mature form, treats law as a matter of social fact: a rule is law because it has been enacted through the recognized sources and procedures of a given system, regardless of its moral merit.
H.L.A. Hart (*The Concept of Law*, 1961) refined this into the view that a legal system is the union of *primary* rules (which impose duties) and *secondary* rules (which confer powers and tell us how to identify, change, and apply the primary ones), all resting on a *rule of recognition* that officials accept from an internal point of view.
Against the positivists, the natural-law tradition insists that an unjust enactment may fail to be law at all — *lex iniusta non est lex* — while interpretivists like Ronald Dworkin argue that law includes the principles that best justify the community's legal practice as a coherent whole, so that hard cases have right answers reached by interpretation rather than by the exercise of bare discretion.

Sociology approaches law from a different angle, asking not what law *is* but what it *does*. Émile Durkheim read a society's law as an index of its moral structure, distinguishing repressive law (which punishes, expressing the shared conscience of a close-knit community) from restitutive law (which restores and compensates, expressing the interdependence of a differentiated society).
Max Weber traced the rationalization of law — its movement toward a system of general, formal, calculable rules administered by trained specialists — as part of the broader rationalization of modern life, and tied it to the legal-rational form of legitimate authority.
Karl Marx and the traditions following him read law as bound up with class power, the rules in which the interests of a dominant class are encoded and enforced as if they were the neutral interest of all.

The stakes of these debates are practical, because how we understand law shapes what we can ask of it.
If law is just the sovereign's command, then there is nothing to appeal to against an unjust statute but force; if law contains principle, then even valid enactments can be challenged in law's own name.
And whatever one's theory, law's distinctive feature remains its claim to bind generally and impersonally — to apply the same rule to all who fall within its scope — a claim that is at once the source of its legitimating power and the standing measure by which its failures of equal application are judged.

Within this cluster law is the great declaring act of authority, issued by the state and standing at the head of an entire family of legal forms.
From it descend the rule of law (the demand that governance run through such rules rather than through whim), jurisdiction (the bounding of legal authority), sanction (the force that makes a rule binding), and the whole apparatus of rights, adjudication, precedent, and procedure through which law operates on social life.
