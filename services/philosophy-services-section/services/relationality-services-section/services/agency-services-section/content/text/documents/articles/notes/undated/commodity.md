# commodity

A commodity is a good produced not for the direct use of its maker but for exchange — for sale on a market.
Marx opens *Capital*, Volume I, by analyzing the commodity as the "cell-form" of capitalist wealth, the elementary unit from which the whole system can be unfolded, much as a biologist begins from the cell.
The wealth of capitalist societies, he writes, presents itself as "an immense accumulation of commodities," and so the analysis of capital begins with the analysis of this single, deceptively simple thing.
Generated by: **capital** — the commodity is the form into which capital casts social wealth, the shape all products must take where things are made to be sold rather than used.

Marx's decisive move is to show that the commodity has a double character.
Every commodity is at once a *use-value* — a thing with concrete qualities that satisfy some human need, a coat that warms or bread that feeds — and an *exchange-value*, a thing that trades in some ratio against other commodities, so much linen for so much corn.
These two aspects pull in different directions.
As use-values, commodities are qualitatively distinct and incommensurable: a coat and a quantity of wheat have nothing physically in common.
Yet on the market they are routinely equated, exchanged in definite proportions, as if they shared a common substance.
The puzzle that drives the opening chapter of *Capital* is what that common substance could be.

Marx's answer is abstract labor.
What makes qualitatively different use-values commensurable is that all are products of human labor considered not as specific skilled activity — weaving, baking, building — but as undifferentiated expenditure of human effort, measured in time.
The commodity is therefore not a simple useful object but a *social form*: it carries within it a definite set of relations among the producers whose labor it congeals.
To produce for exchange is to enter, through one's products, into a web of social relations mediated by the market rather than by direct cooperation, kinship, or command.

This makes the commodity historically specific rather than natural.
The dominance of production-for-exchange over production-for-use is a feature of a particular kind of economy, not of human economic life as such.
In economies organized through reciprocity or the commons, objects carry the history of their making and giving as part of their social meaning: a gift carries obligation, a shared resource carries responsibility, and the relation does not end at the moment of transfer.
The market commodity, by contrast, closes at the point of sale — once the price is paid, the relation is over, and the object's social history is wiped clean.

As a node, the commodity is the source from which a whole sequence of concepts unfolds.
From its exchange aspect comes exchange-value; from the way the commodity form presents social relations as relations between things come its characteristic illusions, commodity-fetishism and, generalized to all of social thought, reification.
To begin social analysis from the commodity, as Marx did, is to insist that the most ordinary object of everyday life — the thing on the shelf with a price on it — already contains, folded up within it, the entire structure of the society that produced it.
