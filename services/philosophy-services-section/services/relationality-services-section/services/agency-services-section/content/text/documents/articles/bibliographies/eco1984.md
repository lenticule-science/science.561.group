## Annotation

Eco's 1984 follow-on to [eco1976](eco1976.md) develops three structural arguments load-bearing for this theory's framing:

1. **The encyclopedia made structural.** Where 1976 introduced the encyclopedia as a working bound on the interpretant chain, 1984 argues for the encyclopedia as a *labyrinth* (a structurally open network admitting multiple paths through the same content) rather than a tree (a hierarchically closed taxonomy). The labyrinth structure is what makes the bound *encyclopedic* rather than *dictionary*-shaped, and grounds the substrate-level argument that the encyclopedia bound corresponds to a Scott-continuous *but not strict* regime.
2. **Metaphor as encyclopedia operation.** Eco analyses metaphor as a structural operation on the encyclopedia: a metaphor invokes a path through the labyrinth that the dictionary would not license. This is structurally a *connotative-tower* iteration in the Hjelmslevian sense, and grounds the structural reading of metaphor as iterated [denotation](../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/archive/library/linguistics/library/semiotics/references/teaching/explanations/denotation.md) → [connotation](../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/archive/library/linguistics/library/semiotics/references/teaching/explanations/connotation.md).
3. **Symbol vs. sign.** The book distinguishes *symbol* (with its open referential ambiguity) from *sign* (which is structurally constrained); the distinction informs the discipline's commitment that not every interpretant chain has a determinate fixpoint at $\omega$.
