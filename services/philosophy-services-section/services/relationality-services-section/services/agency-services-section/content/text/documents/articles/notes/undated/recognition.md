# recognition

Recognition is the political framework in which a subordinate group seeks acknowledgment of its distinct identity, rights, and existence from the dominant power.
In its affirmative form, associated with Charles Taylor's "The Politics of Recognition" (1994), recognition is treated as a vital human need: misrecognition — being seen as inferior, invisible, or distorted — is itself a kind of harm, and justice requires the dominant society to acknowledge the equal worth of different cultural groups.
Recognition in this sense underwrites multiculturalism, minority rights, and the politics of identity-based inclusion.
Generated by: domination, in the mode of acknowledging-on-terms — recognition is not a prior primitive of mutual respect but an offer the dominant power extends, on conditions it sets.

The critical theorists this library follows turned the framework inside out.
Drawing on Frantz Fanon's analysis in *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952), Glen Coulthard (Yellowknives Dene), in *Red Skin, White Masks* (2014), argues that the politics of recognition is a colonial trap.
Fanon had shown that the colonized subject who seeks recognition from the colonizer enters a relation whose terms are set by the colonizer: what is recognized is not the subject as they are but a version of them the dominant framework can accommodate.
Recognition does not end domination; it reformats it, and renders the recognized subject legible — and legibility is the precondition of administration.
To be recognized is to be made processable by the very power one sought acknowledgment from.

In the settler-colonial context Coulthard analyzes, the state offers recognition — legal status, cultural rights, self-government agreements, land-claim settlements — but always on terms that presuppose the legitimacy of the state itself and the sovereignty of the Crown.
To be recognized, Indigenous governance must translate itself into the language of rights, constitutionalism, and institutional procedure; what cannot survive that translation — authority grounded in specific relationships to land, in ceremony, in oral tradition and kinship obligation — is either excluded or reduced to cultural decoration.
The offer of recognition is therefore an offer to accept the dominant power's template in exchange for the dominant power's acknowledgment.

This analysis is what positions recognition as a form of domination rather than its remedy, and it sets up the concepts that respond to it.
Its direct counterpart in this library is recognition-refusal: the rejection of the demand to make oneself legible to the dominant power as the price of legitimacy.
The contrast is between inclusion on the dominant's terms and self-determination on one's own — a difference Audra Simpson documents in *Mohawk Interruptus* (2014), where the Kahnawake Mohawks decline to seek recognition from Canada and instead exercise a sovereignty they do not ask anyone's permission to hold.
Recognition, in short, is the soft face of domination: it grants the subordinate a place inside the order rather than dismantling the order itself.
