# political-myth

A political myth is a narrative that constitutes a political community by telling it what it is, where it came from, and what it is destined to become.
It is not a falsehood in the simple sense — calling a myth "false" misunderstands its function.
A political myth produces the reality it claims to describe.
The story of the American founding produces the America that believes in it; the *Shahnameh* produces the Iran that claims it; a national origin legend produces the people who gather to celebrate it.
Generated by: **nationalism** — the political myth is nationalism's mobilizing narrative, the story that turns a population into a people with a shared past and a common destiny.

Chiara Bottici's *A Philosophy of Political Myth* (2007) gives the working definition: a political myth is a narrative that provides significance to the political conditions of a social group, is shared and believed by that group, and addresses the specifically political conditions of its existence.
Two features matter for the grad student.
First, the work of a myth is mobilization, not description — its test is not accuracy but whether it moves people to act, belong, sacrifice, and obey.
Second, political myths are neither simply imposed from above (like propaganda) nor simply welling up from below (like spontaneous folk memory); they are constituted through ongoing collective work, told and retold, revised, contested, and sedimented through institutions until they feel like common sense.

The **volksgeist** is a political myth in exactly this sense.
Herder's claim that each people has a unique spirit is not a testable hypothesis but a narrative frame that, once adopted, generates the evidence of its own truth: the tales get collected, the costumes codified, the festivals organized, and the *Volk* duly becomes visible.
The myth does not report a pre-existing reality; it produces the reality it needs.
The same holds for founding stories, liberation narratives, and myths of a chosen people or a promised land — each binds a community by giving it a story it can recognize itself in, and each thereby furnishes a standard for who counts as truly of the community and who does not.

Political myth is the mechanism by which **collective-memory** is turned to political work.
Shared memory is the raw material; the myth is the shaped narrative that organizes that memory around a present purpose and a projected future.
Roger Griffin's **palingenetic-ultranationalism** — the fascist myth of national rebirth after decline — is the most dangerous specialization of the form: it requires a cast of characters representing what the nation was, what it lost, and what it will become, and the figures of **the-folk** are recruited to fill those roles.
The myth makes the folk figures feel necessary, and the folk figures make the myth feel true.
Read against its neighbours, political-myth is the narrative engine of **nationalism**, fed by collective memory, dressed by the **invention-of-tradition**, and weaponized at its limit by palingenetic ultranationalism — a story whose power lies precisely in its capacity to make itself come true.
