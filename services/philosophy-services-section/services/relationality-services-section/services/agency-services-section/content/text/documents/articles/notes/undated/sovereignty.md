# sovereignty

Sovereignty is the claim to supreme authority within a defined territory — the power to make, enforce, and adjudicate law without answering to any higher authority.
Generated by: sovereignty is authority claiming to be foundational and unanswerable, the point at which the chain of "by whose authority?" is declared to stop.
In its classical formulation, given by Jean Bodin in the sixteenth century and Thomas Hobbes in the seventeenth, sovereignty is indivisible and absolute: there must be some final decision-maker whose word is not subject to review, on pain of the war of all against all.
In actual political life sovereignty is rarely so tidy — it is distributed across institutions, layered between levels of government, and perpetually contested.

The Treaty of Westphalia (1648) is conventionally credited with establishing *territorial* sovereignty as the organizing principle of the international order: each state holds exclusive authority within its borders, and other states are obliged to respect that exclusion.
This Westphalian model still furnishes the formal grammar of international law, but its adequacy has been challenged from every side — by international human rights law, which asserts claims that pierce the border; by supranational bodies like the European Union, which pool and redistribute sovereign powers; by transnational corporations whose practical reach exceeds that of many states; and by Indigenous peoples who assert a sovereignty that predates and stands independent of the settler states claiming to contain them.

Carl Schmitt shifted the entire analysis with a single formula: "Sovereign is he who decides on the exception" (*Political Theology*, 1922).
For Schmitt the essence of sovereignty is revealed not in the normal operation of law but in the power to *suspend* it — to declare the state of exception, the emergency in which the ordinary legal order is set aside.
The sovereign is the one who stands both inside the legal order (as its highest authority) and outside it (as the power that can switch it off), and it is the borderline case, not the routine one, that exposes where sovereign power actually resides.
Giorgio Agamben (*Homo Sacer*, 1995; *State of Exception*, 2005) extended this claim to argue that the exception has become the rule: modern states increasingly govern through emergency powers, producing zones — the camp, the detention facility, the border — where law is suspended indefinitely and persons are abandoned to a "bare life" stripped of the protections of citizenship.

For Indigenous peoples sovereignty has an altogether different genealogy.
Indigenous sovereignty does not derive from Westphalian recognition or constitutional grant but from the ongoing relationship between a people and their territory, governance systems, and lifeways — a relationship that endures regardless of whether any state acknowledges it.
From this standpoint the settler state's claim of sovereignty over Indigenous land is itself an assertion of power lacking the very legitimacy it announces, and "recognition" by the state can become a trap that subordinates Indigenous authority to the settler order it was meant to escape.

Within this cluster sovereignty is the most exalted form of authority — authority that claims to answer to nothing above it — and the property the state asserts when it claims a monopoly on rightful force within its borders.
Its dark twin is the state of exception, the moment when the sovereign proves its supremacy precisely by suspending the law it is supposed to uphold; and its outer edge is the friend-enemy distinction, Schmitt's claim that the sovereign decision that founds the political is the decision over who counts as the community and who as its existential enemy.
