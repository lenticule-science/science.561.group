The philosophy of mathematical practice is a recent and active subfield (roughly post-2000) that takes the *actual* practice of mathematics — its informal proofs, its diagrams, its narrative arguments, its institutional review — as the object of philosophical study, rather than the formal-derivation idealization. For a framework studying communicative responsibility, it is the most directly useful philosophical literature available, because it asks exactly the questions the framework needs to answer about mathematical communication. This text is a survey of the contributions the framework draws on.

## Avigad on reliability

Jeremy Avigad's "Reliability of mathematical inference" (*Synthese*, 2020) and surrounding papers ask why mathematicians are entitled to confidence in their results. The naïve answer (the proof can be expanded into a formal derivation) does not account for what working mathematicians actually do, none of whom produce the formal expansions. The richer answer Avigad develops is institutional: peer review, replication, integration with surrounding theory, computational verification, application to problems whose solutions can be checked independently. Mathematical reliability is a practice's achievement, not an individual proof's property.

The framework borrows this directly. The criterion for [accountable-closure](../../../../../../glossary/concepts/accountable-closure.md) is not formalizability but practice-confirmable correctness — the closure is acceptable when the practice's apparatus and audience can perform and audit it. This is Avigad's reliability picture transposed from the level of theorems to the level of individual closures.

## Macbeth on conceptual content

Danielle Macbeth's *Realizing Reason: A Narrative of Truth and Knowing* (Oxford, 2014) and earlier papers argue that mathematical writing accumulates *conceptual content*: each definition, theorem, and proof develops a structure of concepts that subsequent work builds on. The artifact is not just a chain of derivations; it is the construction of a vocabulary that subsequent mathematical thought operates within. A proof is a composition of conceptual moves, not a sequence of step-replacements.

This account is what makes [plan-reconstruction](../../../../../../glossary/concepts/plan-reconstruction.md) intelligible as a target. The plan a reader recovers is, in Macbeth's terms, the conceptual structure the proof is constructing. A proof that fails to support plan reconstruction is one whose conceptual content the reader cannot extract; the reader is left with verifiable steps and no growing vocabulary.

## Hamami and Morris on the shape of mathematical reasoning

Yacin Hamami's "Mathematical Rigor and Proof" (*Review of Symbolic Logic*, 2022) and his work with Rebecca Morris on diagrammatic and routine reasoning give the most operational treatment of what mathematical reading actually involves. Their position: a proof is rigorous when its constituent steps are *high-level inferences* — patterns the practice has institutionally accredited — and the reader can recover the *plan* of the argument. Formal expansion exists in principle; what readers do is reason at the level of plans and patterns.

This framework, more than any other in the list, is the direct ancestor of the framework's central operations. The framework's [acceptable-gap](../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/acceptable-gap.md) and [disciplinary-gap](../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/disciplinary-gap.md) draw on Andersen, who is in dialogue with Hamami and Morris, and the [plan-reconstruction](../../../../../../glossary/concepts/plan-reconstruction.md) concept formalizes their account directly. The framework owes its operationalism to this strand. See [the-hamami-morris-test-for-plan-reconstruction](../../../../teaching-articles/explanations/the-hamami-morris-test-for-plan-reconstruction.md) for the focused treatment.

## Ashton on the social construction of proof

Zoe Ashton's work on the audience structure of mathematical proof argues that a proof is, irreducibly, an artifact addressed to an audience — that the rigor of a proof cannot be evaluated independently of the audience whose capacities it presupposes. Different audiences support different gap-discipline; what looks like sloppy proof to one audience is appropriately calibrated proof to another.

This is what makes [audience](../../../../../../glossary/concepts/audience.md) a load-bearing parameter in the framework rather than a piece of cosmetic framing. The framework's insistence that gap-acceptability is audience-relative is downstream of Ashton's audience-relative account of mathematical rigor.

## Andersen on acceptable gaps

Andersen's "Acceptable gaps in mathematical proofs" (*Synthese*, 2020) is the framework's principal source for the term [acceptable-gap](../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/acceptable-gap.md) and the distinction from [disciplinary-gap](../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/disciplinary-gap.md), each treated in dedicated entries. See [the-andersen-test-for-acceptable-gaps](../../../../teaching-articles/explanations/the-andersen-test-for-acceptable-gaps.md) for a fuller synthesis.

## What this literature does not provide

The philosophy of mathematical practice is concerned with mathematics. It does not generalize its vocabulary to non-mathematical communicative practices. The framework does the generalization — claiming that the operations these authors describe in mathematics also operate in software documentation, signage, lecturing, design, and other framed-unit communicative practices — but the generalization is the framework's contribution and not the literature's.

The companion non-mathematical literature is the comics-theory tradition (McCloud, Eisner, Cohn, Groensteen) treated in [introduction-to-mccloud-for-non-comics](../../../../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/archive/prose/learning/surveys/introduction-to-mccloud-for-non-comics.md). Together the two traditions provide most of the vocabulary the framework operates with: the comics tradition for the panel/gutter/closure/icon/sequence operations, the philosophy-of-mathematical-practice tradition for the audience-relative, plan-reconstruction-oriented account of acceptability and rigor.
