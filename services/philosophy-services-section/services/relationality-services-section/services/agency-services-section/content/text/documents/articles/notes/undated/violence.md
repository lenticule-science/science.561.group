# violence

Violence is the exercise of physical force to damage, restrain, dispossess, or kill.
Nearly every political tradition claims to oppose it; the question this library, following the anarchist analysis, presses is a different one — whose force gets counted as violence, and whose gets counted as order.
The central claim is that violence is asymmetric: the state and capital exercise systematic, ongoing, lethal force that is rendered all but invisible by its legality and normality, while resistance to that force is immediately classified as the origin of violence.
Generated by: domination — violence is the harmful force domination exercises and then naturalizes, the point at which a relation of command becomes a wound.

The state, in Weber's famous formulation, defines itself as the holder of the monopoly on legitimate violence.
Police force, military force, incarceration, deportation, the eviction that enforces a property claim, the border regime that lets people drown rather than rescue them — these are not ordinarily called violence.
They are called law enforcement, national defense, the maintenance of order.
The force is entirely real: people are beaten, caged, and killed.
But its classification as legitimate removes it from the category of violence and turns it into background — the way things are, the price of civilization.
Against that background, any physical resistance is instantly visible as violence and condemned as such.
A broken window at a protest is violence; the eviction that emptied the building was not.
The question "do you support violence?" is asked of demonstrators and never of the police.

The concept of structural violence, named by Johan Galtung in 1969, extends the analysis to the domination that kills without anyone pulling a trigger.
When people die because they cannot afford care, when life expectancy differs by decades between neighborhoods, when workers' bodies break down under conditions set by someone else's profit, that is violence — produced by identifiable structures, maintained by identifiable institutions, for identifiable ends.
The anarchist insistence is that this be called violence literally and not metaphorically: the refusal to name structural lethality as force is itself ideological, a way of categorizing domination's killing as something other than killing.
Capitalism, the state, colonialism, and patriarchy produce structural violence not as their failure but as their ordinary function.

This reframing dissolves the usual violence-versus-nonviolence debate as it is normally posed.
To ask whether a movement should "use violence" is already to accept the state's monopoly on the definition, treating its structural violence as a neutral baseline and resistance as the thing that would introduce force into a peaceful scene.
The anarchist reply is that violence is already happening; the only question is whether to resist force already being exercised.
This is not an endorsement of unlimited force — figures like Malatesta argued that liberatory force should be proportional and targeted because means shape ends — but a refusal of the binary.
Within this library violence is the sharp edge of domination, the moment coercion combines with harm; its reciprocal counterpart is the building of relations in which no party can damage another without consequence, and its analysis runs alongside coercion, obedience, and the structural forms domination takes across capital, state, colony, and gender.
