# necropolitics

Generated by: biopower, abandoning, composed with colonialism — the administration of life for some is the administration of death for others.

Necropolitics is Achille Mbembe's term for the form of sovereignty that operates through the management of death rather than the management of life.
Where Michel Foucault's biopolitics analyzes how modern states govern by "making live and letting die," Mbembe asks after the conditions — colonial occupation, plantation slavery, contemporary zones of abandonment — in which sovereignty works the other way, by making die and letting live.
The governing question shifts from how states optimize the life of a population to how states decide which populations are exposed to death.
Mbembe introduced the concept in the 2003 essay "Necropolitics" and expanded it in the 2019 book of the same name.

Mbembe's central claim is that Foucault's framework, drawn largely from European history, cannot account for the forms of power exercised in colonial and postcolonial settings.
In the colony, on the plantation, and in the occupation zone, sovereignty does not govern through the optimization of life but through the subjugation of life to the power of death.
Crucially, Mbembe treats this not as an aberration at the edge of modern politics but as its constitutive outside — the condition that makes metropolitan biopolitics possible.
The fostering of life within the metropole depends on the production of death-worlds elsewhere.
He describes these death-worlds as places where vast populations are consigned to the status of the living dead, kept in conditions that confer on them the status of the perpetually expendable.

The concept names arrangements that persist in the present, not only the colonial past.
Mbembe points to differential exposure to environmental toxicity, the withdrawal of infrastructure from specific communities, the management of migration through deliberate exposure to danger at borders and in deserts and seas, and the calculus by which some deaths register as tragedy while others register as statistics or do not register at all.
He also analyzes the rise of figures of the suicide bomber and of "war machines" that no longer correspond to the territorial state, where the body itself becomes a weapon and death becomes both means and end.

Necropolitics sits in deliberate tension with its neighbors, and the tension is instructive.
It extends biopower by naming its death-facing side, but it accuses Foucauldian biopolitics of being too European to see how central death has always been to modern power.
Ruth Wilson Gilmore's definition of racism as "group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death" is, in effect, a necropolitical formulation that locates racism not in attitudes but in the political production of differential mortality.
And Gilmore's organized abandonment offers a friendly correction from the other direction: where necropolitics can become atmospheric — a description of conditions of death rather than of the agents and mechanisms that produce them — organized abandonment insists on the specific contracts, agencies, and budget lines through which disposability is built.
Read together, necropolitics names the stakes (who is made to die) and the abandonment concepts name the machinery.
