# bare-life

Generated by: biopower, excepting — the sovereign exception that suspends law and produces a life that can be killed but not sacrificed.

Bare life (*nuda vita*) is Giorgio Agamben's term for human existence stripped to its biological minimum — life that is included in the political order only by being excluded from it.
Agamben develops the concept in *Homo Sacer: Sovereign Power and Bare Life* (1995; English translation 1998), the opening volume of a long series, drawing together Foucault's biopolitics and Carl Schmitt's theory of sovereignty.
The figure that gives the book its name is the *homo sacer*, drawn from archaic Roman law: a person who could be killed by anyone without the killing counting as murder, yet could not be sacrificed in religious ritual.
This double exclusion — from human law and from divine law alike — produces a life with no standing in either order.

The homo sacer exists in what Agamben calls a zone of indistinction: neither fully inside political life, where killing is regulated by law, nor fully outside it, where killing would have no political meaning.
Agamben argues that this figure is not an archaic curiosity but the hidden foundation of modern sovereignty.
The sovereign is the one who decides which lives count as political — citizens bearing rights — and which are reduced to bare life: the refugee, the stateless person, the detainee, the person on life support, the inmate of the camp.
Bare life is not natural life.
It is life that has been produced as bare by a political operation, the sovereign exception that simultaneously includes and excludes.

The state of exception is the engine of this production.
Every sovereignty, Agamben argues, claims the power to declare an exception — to suspend the law while remaining within its authority.
It is in this suspension that bare life appears: a person held within the reach of law's force precisely by being stripped of law's protection.
The exception, on this reading, is not an aberration that interrupts normal politics but the structure through which sovereignty constitutes itself in the first place.
In *State of Exception* (2003), Agamben traces how the exception has tended to become the rule in modern governance, normalizing the very condition that was supposed to be temporary and extraordinary.

The concentration camp is, for Agamben, the paradigmatic space where bare life is produced.
In the camp, law is suspended and inhabitants exist as pure biological life under total administrative control — not outside the political order but inside it in the specific mode of having been stripped of everything except bodily existence.
The camp, in this analysis, is not an exception to modern politics but its concealed truth, the place where the production of bare life becomes the ordinary operation of power.
The concept is powerful and contested: critics, including Achille Mbembe and Judith Butler, argue that Agamben's emphasis on the sovereign decision and the figure of bare life can flatten the differences among those exposed to abandonment, and can obscure the racial and colonial specificity of who gets reduced to bare life and who does not.
Read alongside necropolitics and grievability, bare life names one limit-figure of biopower: the human being whom power can reach only as a body, and reach without restraint.
