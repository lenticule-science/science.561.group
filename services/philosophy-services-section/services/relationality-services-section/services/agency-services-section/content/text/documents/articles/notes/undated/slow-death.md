# slow-death

Generated by: biopower, letting-wear — life administered toward slow attrition rather than spectacular death.

Slow death is Lauren Berlant's term for the condition of populations whose physical wearing-out is a defining feature of their ordinary experience of the present — not dying in a dramatic event but being worn down across the span of everyday life by conditions that erode health, agency, and any sense of a future.
Berlant develops the concept in *Cruel Optimism* (2011), most directly in the essay "Slow Death (Sovereignty, Obesity, Lateral Agency)."
It names a way of dying that has no single moment, no scene of catastrophe, and therefore no obvious before-and-after to mourn or to litigate.

The concept turns on a problem of time.
Crisis implies an event — a rupture with a clear beginning and end, a thing that happens to people.
Slow death is what remains when crisis stops being an event and becomes the ordinary atmosphere of life.
Obesity, environmental toxicity, chronic illness, and economic precarity are, in Berlant's reading, not catastrophes that befall individuals but conditions that grind down whole populations across unremarkable duration.
The damage is real but dispersed; no single instant qualifies as the disaster.
This is why slow death resists the usual languages of harm and redress, which are built to recognize events.

Berlant connects slow death directly to sovereignty and to biopower.
Classical political theory locates sovereignty in the dramatic power over life and death — the exceptional decision.
Slow death reveals power operating at a far lower register: not the decision to kill but the management of populations under conditions that quietly shorten and diminish life.
This is biopower at its most ordinary — not the camp but the food desert, not the execution but the denied claim, not the spectacle of dying but the slow administration of attrition.
Berlant also reworks the idea of agency here through what she calls "lateral agency": in conditions of slow death, acts like eating, numbing, or zoning out are not failures of self-care or self-destruction so much as small forms of self-suspension, ways of getting a break from the demand to keep maintaining oneself.

Slow death sits close to several neighboring concepts and sharpens each by contrast.
It is kin to Ruth Wilson Gilmore's organized abandonment and Elizabeth Povinelli's economies of abandonment, but where those name the administrative processes of withdrawal, slow death names the lived, temporal texture of being worn out under them.
It overlaps with Butler's precarity, but precarity foregrounds vulnerability and grievability while slow death foregrounds duration — the experience of persistence as entropy.
And it offers a corrective to readings of biopower that fixate on the spectacular: the camp, the strike, the exception.
Most people governed by biopower are not killed; they are slowly let to wear.
The stakes of the concept are that it makes that wearing legible as a political condition rather than a private misfortune, and refuses to let the absence of a dramatic event stand in for the absence of harm.
