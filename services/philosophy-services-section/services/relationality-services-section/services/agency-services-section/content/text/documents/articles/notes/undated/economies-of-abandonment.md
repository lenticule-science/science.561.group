# economies-of-abandonment

Generated by: necropolitics — the quiet, dispersed sorting of who is sustained and who is let go.

Economies of abandonment is Elizabeth Povinelli's term, developed in *Economies of Abandonment: Social Belonging and Endurance in Late Liberalism* (2011), for the mode of governance in which late liberalism manages populations and places not primarily through direct oppression but through withdrawal — the removal of resources, infrastructure, and recognition from those it deems unviable.
The phrase is deliberately economic: abandonment is treated as a distribution, a sorting of social belonging, in which sustaining some lives and places requires letting others go.
The violence is real but rarely takes the form of a blow; it takes the form of a budget that does not include you.

The crucial claim is that abandonment is not neglect but a governed process.
The state does not simply forget communities; it assesses them, classifies them, and determines their viability through administrative, economic, and demographic metrics.
When a community is found unviable — too small, too remote, too costly to service — the withdrawal of resources follows as a rational, procedural outcome.
The community is not attacked; it is abandoned.
The distinction matters because abandonment operates through the very frameworks that late liberalism presents as neutral: cost-benefit analysis, demographic modeling, infrastructure planning.
The harm is laundered through proceduralism, which is what makes it deniable.

Povinelli emphasizes that abandonment produces its own evidence, in a self-confirming loop.
The community deprived of infrastructure comes to appear as if it lacks the capacity for self-governance; the population denied resources comes to appear as if it failed to thrive.
The managed exhaustion of a place becomes, retroactively, the proof that the place was unviable all along.
This circularity is the concept's sharpest point: the withdrawal manufactures the conditions that justify the withdrawal, so that abandonment looks, from inside the procedure, like a sober response to facts rather than the production of those facts.

Against this, Povinelli foregrounds *endurance* — the ongoing, unglamorous work of persisting under conditions of abandonment — as the central political act.
Not resistance in the heroic, event-shaped sense, but the refusal to disappear on the schedule that abandonment has set.
This connects to her larger argument that late liberalism governs through the quasi-event: disturbances and harms that never quite rise to the status of a crisis, and so never trigger the response a crisis would demand.
Economies of abandonment is closely allied with Gilmore's organized abandonment — both name governed withdrawal rather than passive neglect — but Povinelli's version foregrounds the late-liberal calculus of viability and the temporality of endurance, where Gilmore foregrounds the concrete carceral and budgetary mechanics.
Read alongside necropolitics and slow death, it describes the administrative face of letting-die: the meeting, the metric, the deferral that decide, without ever deciding outright, who is kept and who is released.
