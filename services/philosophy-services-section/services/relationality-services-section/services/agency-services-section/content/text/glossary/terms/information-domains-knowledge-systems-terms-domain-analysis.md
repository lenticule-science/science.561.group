Birger Hjorland's framework for studying knowledge domains as discourse communities with shared epistemological commitments.

Domain analysis is a framework in information science, developed by Birger Hjorland and Hanne Albrechtsen (1995), that treats a **domain** as &ldquo;a body of knowledge, defined socially and theoretically as the knowledge of a group sharing ontological and epistemological commitments.&rdquo; Domains are not natural kinds waiting to be discovered. They are discourse communities held together by shared assumptions about what exists, what counts as evidence, and how knowledge is produced.

Hjorland (2002) identified eleven approaches to domain analysis: producing literature guides, constructing special classifications and thesauri, indexing and retrieving specialties, empirical user studies, bibliometric studies, historical studies, document and genre studies, epistemological and critical studies, terminological studies, studies of structures and institutions in scientific communication, and domain analysis in scientific cognition (including artificial intelligence and database semantics). Each approach reveals different aspects of a domain&rsquo;s structure. No single approach is sufficient.

The framework&rsquo;s key move is sociological. Traditional classification assumes subjects exist independently and the classifier&rsquo;s job is to mirror them. Domain analysis says the opposite: what counts as a subject depends on who is asking and why. A domain&rsquo;s boundaries, internal divisions, and relevance criteria all reflect the commitments of the community that produces and uses the knowledge. This makes domain analysis inherently comparative &mdash; understanding one domain means understanding how its commitments differ from those of neighboring domains.

Domain analysis connects to [epistemic culture](../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/epistemic-culture.md) (Knorr-Cetina&rsquo;s parallel account of how knowledge communities differ in their &ldquo;machineries of knowing&rdquo;) and to the [demarcation problem](../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/demarcation-problem.md) in philosophy of science. It provides information science with a theoretical foundation for why different domains need different classification schemes, different indexing languages, and different retrieval strategies.

## Key texts

- Hjorland, B. & Albrechtsen, H., &ldquo;Toward a New Horizon in Information Science: Domain-Analysis&rdquo; (1995)

- Hjorland, B., &ldquo;Domain Analysis in Information Science: Eleven Approaches &mdash; Traditional as Well as Innovative&rdquo; (2002)
