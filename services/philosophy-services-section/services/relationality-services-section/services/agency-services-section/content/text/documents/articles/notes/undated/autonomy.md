# autonomy

Autonomy means self-governance: the capacity of a person, group, or community to make decisions about its own life without being directed by an external authority.
The word joins the Greek *autos* (self) and *nomos* (law) — to be autonomous is to live under a law you give yourself rather than one imposed from outside.
For the anarchist tradition, autonomy is not a privilege to be earned or a right granted by the state; it is treated as the default condition of human beings, which hierarchy suppresses and which collective self-organization restores.
Generated by: reciprocating — autonomy is the reciprocal relation turned inward, a collective relating to itself, governing its own affairs rather than being governed from above; it is the positive counterpart to domination's external direction, and the ground on which the other forms of self-determination stand.

Autonomy operates at more than one scale, and the scales depend on each other.
Individual autonomy is the capacity to determine your own actions, work, relationships, and beliefs without coercion.
Collective autonomy is a group's or community's capacity to govern its own affairs — to decide together, without being directed by an external government, corporation, or institution.
The two are not opposed but mutually conditioning.
You cannot be autonomous in isolation if the surrounding society compels your obedience, so individual autonomy depends on collective conditions; and a group in which some members dominate others is not genuinely self-governing, so collective autonomy depends on the autonomy of its members.
Self-rule at one level is hollow without self-rule at the other.

This is what separates anarchist autonomy from liberal individualism.
Liberalism treats autonomy as an individual attribute — the rational adult making free choices in a marketplace — but that "freedom" quietly presupposes a whole apparatus of authority: the state that enforces contracts, the property regime that determines which choices are even available, the class structure that decides who has meaningful options and who does not.
Anarchist autonomy insists instead that real self-governance has material preconditions.
To govern your own life you need access to food, shelter, knowledge, and community, and securing those conditions for everyone — not just for those who can already buy them — requires collective action, mutual aid, and the dismantling of the hierarchies that concentrate resources and decision-making power.
Autonomy in this sense is not the freedom of the isolated chooser but the shared capacity of people who provide for one another to set the terms of their common life.

Autonomy has specific dimensions that the surrounding concepts draw out.
Temporal autonomy is the capacity of a community to determine its own relationship to time, against the clock, the shift, the deadline, and the fiscal year.
Knowledge sovereignty is a community's authority over how its knowledge is held and transmitted.
And autonomy connects directly to Indigenous self-determination: many Indigenous nations practiced self-governance through consensus, council, and distributed authority long before European anarchism gave the practice a name, and colonialism was, among its other violences, the destruction of that autonomy and its replacement with dependence on colonial institutions.
The branches that grow from autonomy — confederacy as autonomous groups coordinating without a center that commands, grounded normativity as place-rooted self-governance, indigenous sovereignty and indigenous resurgence as the reassertion of self-determination against the settler state — are all forms of autonomy worked out at different scales and in different registers.
