# legal-formatting

Legal formatting is the process by which a cultural practice, social relation, or community dynamic is translated into a legal category that the state's apparatus can process.
Generated by: legal formatting is law's intake operation — the step at which the legal system, which cannot deal with social reality as it actually is, reformats that reality into administrable units it can recognize, classify, and act upon.
The courtroom does not observe a dispute as the people living it experience it; it receives the dispute only after it has been broken down into pleadings, charges, claims, and admissible facts.
What enters as a tangled, situated, relational mess emerges as a tidy, attributable legal artifact.

The mechanism works through the combined demands of legal procedure.
Adversarial process requires that everything brought before the court serve an argumentative purpose, so that lived experience is admitted only insofar as it helps one side win.
Rules of evidence determine what the court is even permitted to receive, screening out whole kinds of knowledge as hearsay, irrelevant, or improper.
And legal personhood determines whose perspective can enter the record at all.
The combined result is a one-way translation: social realities flow into the legal system and come out as legal artifacts, and the reverse — legal artifacts flowing back out and resuming their original social form — does not happen.
The formatting is irreversible; the type the law assigns sticks.

Legal formatting is a specific instance of *legibility* in the sense James C.
Scott develops in *Seeing Like a State* (1998).
Scott analyzes the modern state's standing demand for simplified, standardized, administrable categories across domains as varied as forestry, cadastral mapping, city planning, and agriculture — the state can only manage what it can see, and it can only see what has been flattened into a uniform grid, so it remakes the world to match its categories rather than the reverse.
Legal formatting names this same demand as it operates through legal proceedings.
The courtroom is a legibility machine: it takes complex, situated, relational realities and produces standardized, attributable, categorizable facts that the rest of the state apparatus can then process.

The stakes of legal formatting are that the flattening is never neutral.
To translate a living relation into a legal type is to decide what about it counts and what is discarded, whose account is heard and whose is inadmissible, which features become legally salient and which vanish.
A communal land-use practice formatted into the categories of individual property law loses exactly the communal dimension that defined it; a culture of mutual obligation formatted into contract loses the obligation that was not a contract.
The format is administrable precisely because it has been stripped of the texture that made the original a living thing, and that stripping has winners and losers.

Within this cluster legal formatting is the legal kin of enclosure (which makes land administrable by converting commons into property) and of genring (which makes a people administrable by flattening them into a type).
It depends on legal personhood to decide whose perspective enters, it propagates through precedent, and it is the underlying mechanism by which ambient governance does its work — shaping social life not by command but by quietly converting it into the forms the legal order can recognize and reward.
