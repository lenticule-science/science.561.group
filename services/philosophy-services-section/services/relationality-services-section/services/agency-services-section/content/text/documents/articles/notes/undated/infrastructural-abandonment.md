# infrastructural-abandonment

Generated by: necropolitics — abandonment carried out through the infrastructures a population depends on.

Infrastructural abandonment is the withdrawal of institutional maintenance from communities, spaces, or systems that remain inhabited.
It is not demolition — nothing is destroyed.
It is the cessation of upkeep: the road is not repaired, the water treatment plant is not upgraded, the levee inspection is deferred, the school is not staffed, the clinic loses its funding.
The infrastructure remains physically present but degrades on a timeline set by neglect rather than by catastrophe.
People go on living amid systems that are quietly being allowed to fail around them.

The distinction from destruction is what gives the concept its force.
Destruction is legible — a hurricane, an airstrike, an eviction produces a clear before-and-after that can be named, mourned, and responded to.
Abandonment is slow, diffuse, and deniable.
The pipe did not burst because someone decided to harm you; the pipe burst because no one decided to replace it.
Responsibility dissolves into institutional absence: the harm is real but the agent is nowhere to be found, because the agent acted by not acting.
This deniability is not incidental; it is the political utility of abandonment as a technique.

Abandonment is not random.
It follows the contours of race, class, and political disposability.
Indigenous communities on reservations, Black neighborhoods in deindustrialized cities, rural communities whose extractive industries have departed — these are not accidentally neglected.
They are deprioritized through institutional logics that route maintenance toward populations and places that generate revenue, political support, or strategic value.
Eric Klinenberg's *Heat Wave* (2002) documents the mechanism precisely: in the 1995 Chicago heat wave, the neighborhoods with the highest death tolls were those where parks had closed, senior centers had lost funding, commercial strips had emptied, and the social infrastructure that would have connected isolated elders to help had been withdrawn over decades.
The heat was the proximate cause; the abandonment was the structural one.

Infrastructural abandonment is the most material and concrete member of the abandonment cluster.
Where Gilmore's organized abandonment names the budgetary and carceral reorganization that drives withdrawal, and Povinelli's economies of abandonment names the late-liberal calculus of viability that justifies it, infrastructural abandonment names the physical substrate where the withdrawal lands — the pipes, grids, clinics, and transit that wear out when upkeep stops.
It is the structural counterpart to Berlant's slow death: the deteriorating system on one side, the worn-out body on the other.
For the communities living it, the practical consequence is that the institutional scaffolding many political frameworks take for granted — functioning government, responsive emergency services, maintained infrastructure — simply is not there.
What persists in its place is relational: kinship networks, place-based knowledge, and informal mutual support, the very capacities that abandonment strands and that the abandoned must rebuild on their own terms.
