# Austin 1962 — *How to Do Things with Words*

J. L. Austin, *How to Do Things with Words* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), edited by J. O. Urmson from Austin's 1955 William James Lectures at Harvard.

## What it establishes

Austin's central move is the rejection of the **descriptive fallacy** — the philosophical assumption that the primary function of language is to describe the world and that utterances are to be evaluated as true or false. Austin observes that many utterances are evaluated not for truth but for whether they *succeed* at what they do: "I promise to call" is not true or false, it is *felicitous* or *infelicitous*, depending on whether the speaker can call, intends to call, takes on the obligation to call, and so on.

The book proceeds in three movements. First, Austin distinguishes **performatives** (utterances that *do* what they say — promises, declarations, christenings) from **constatives** (utterances that *describe* what is the case). Performatives are evaluated by **felicity conditions**; constatives are evaluated by truth.

Second, Austin notices that the distinction is harder to draw than it first appears — many constatives carry implicit performative force ("I assert that …"), and many performatives can be reformulated as constatives. The performative/constative distinction is replaced by a more general framework.

Third, the general framework: every utterance performs three acts simultaneously — a **locutionary** act (uttering a meaningful sentence with a sense and reference), an **illocutionary** act (the act performed *in* uttering — asserting, promising, requesting, declaring), and a **perlocutionary** act (the effect produced *by* uttering — convincing, persuading, frightening, amusing). The locutionary/illocutionary/perlocutionary triple is what survives as the structural commitment of speech act theory.

## Why this corpus cites it

Austin's book is the founding reference for the [speech-act](../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/archive/objects/speech-act.md) object — the formal model the science-side [pragmatics](../../../glossary/concepts/pragmatics.md) concept is built around. Without Austin, the model has no source and no historical motivation; with him, the locutionary/illocutionary/perlocutionary triple has its canonical articulation.

The book is also load-bearing for the corpus's broader pragmatic vocabulary: the distinction between *what is said* and *what is done in saying* (later sharpened by Grice 1975 as *what is said* vs. *what is implicated*) is implicit in Austin's distinction between locution and illocution. The Searle 1969 development of the felicity-condition framework into a full taxonomy of illocutionary types is the immediate elaboration of Austin's work that downstream pragmatics rests on.

## Editorial note

The book is famously unfinished — Austin died in 1960, before he could complete the lectures into a book. The published text is reconstructed from his lecture notes by J. O. Urmson; the second edition (1975, with Marina Sbisà as co-editor) restores material from the 1955 lectures more fully. The 1962 first edition is the canonical citation for the framework's introduction; the 1975 edition is the canonical citation for textual scholarship.
