# biopower

Generated by: the state, governing — the modern state's positive administration of life, beyond the old sovereign right to take life.

Biopower is Michel Foucault's name for the form of political power that takes the biological life of populations as its object.
Where the older model of sovereign power operated through the right to "take life or let live" — the king could execute or pardon — biopower inverts the formula: it operates through the right to "make live and let die."
The state no longer primarily threatens its subjects with death; it manages their lives.
Their health, their reproduction, their longevity, the conditions under which they flourish or deteriorate, become the substance that power works on.
Foucault introduced the concept in the final chapter of *The History of Sexuality, Volume 1* (1976) and developed it through his 1975–76 Collège de France lectures, published as *"Society Must Be Defended."*

The shift from sovereignty to biopower is not a clean replacement.
Sovereign power persists — states still execute, wage war, and imprison — but it comes to operate inside a biopolitical frame in which killing is justified as the protection of life.
Wars are waged to defend a population; executions are justified by the danger the condemned poses to public safety; the right to kill is exercised in the name of the right to make live.
Foucault argues that what makes this reversal possible is racism in its modern, state form: the introduction of a break within the biological continuum of a population, a line dividing those whose life must be optimized from those whose death is held to secure the health of the whole.
State racism is, in this account, the precondition under which a power devoted to fostering life can still take it.

Biopower works at two levels at once.
At the level of the individual body, disciplinary techniques — surveillance, the examination, the timetable, the spatial ordering of bodies — produce what Foucault calls "docile bodies," subjects trained to behave in predictable, productive ways: the soldier, the student, the factory worker, the patient, the prisoner.
At the level of the population, regulatory techniques — demography, epidemiology, public health campaigns, insurance, actuarial calculation — manage aggregate biological processes: birth rates, mortality curves, the spread of endemic disease, rates of reproduction.
Discipline individualizes; regulation totalizes.
The two poles are not rivals but the joined faces of a single power over life.

The concept anchors a wide field of later work, much of which reads as a quarrel with what biopower leaves out.
Giorgio Agamben locates beneath it the sovereign production of *bare life*; Achille Mbembe argues that in colonial and postcolonial settings sovereignty works primarily through death rather than the optimization of life, and renames this *necropolitics*; Judith Butler reframes the differential management of life as a question of which lives are *grievable*; Lauren Berlant traces biopower at its most ordinary as *slow death*, the wearing-out of populations across unremarkable time.
Governmentality, Foucault's later concept, names the specific rationality — liberal, welfarist, neoliberal — that organizes these techniques into a coherent art of governing.
If biopower names *that* modern power takes life as its object, governmentality names *how* a given regime does so.

The stakes of the concept are that it relocates politics.
Domination is no longer only a matter of who may command and who must obey; it becomes a matter of whose life is fostered and whose is left to wither, decided through the apparently neutral instruments of statistics, medicine, and administration.
To read power this way is to read a budget line, a vaccination schedule, or an insurance category as a political act with consequences for who lives and for how long.
