## Annotation

The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on process philosophy. Surveys the field across three axes: (i) *historical* — Heraclitus, Buddhist philosophy, Spinoza, Leibniz, Bergson, James, Whitehead, Hartshorne, Rescher, contemporary developments; (ii) *systematic* — the central theses of process metaphysics (process-primacy, internal relatedness, novelty, temporal directionality), with a careful analysis of how each thesis can be weakened or strengthened to yield distinct *kinds* of process philosophy; (iii) *applications* — process accounts of causation, persistence, agency, mind, and natural science.

For the predicated framing, Seibt's article is the single most useful source for understanding *which* process commitments the framing's substrate inherits and which it deliberately weakens. Specifically:
- The framing inherits *internal relatedness* (subject identity by [aboutness](../../../glossary/concepts/aboutness.md)) and *temporal directionality* (the [knowledge-order](../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/humanities/theories/math/theories/categories/theories/predication-categories/concepts/knowledge-order.md) on the bilattice) without modification.
- The framing weakens *process-primacy*: rather than insisting process is foundational and substance derivative, it treats predicates and cells as *coequally primary* — a cell is at once a process (a settling-event) and a relation between substances. This is the framing's analogue of Seibt's *general process theory* (GPT), which similarly admits both processual and substantial entities while treating them as types within a single ontology.
- The framing remains agnostic on *novelty/emergence*: whether the closure regime can produce predicates not derivable from the asserted vocabulary is a regime-choice question, not a metaphysical commitment.

Seibt's article is the recommended starting point for any reader wanting to understand the framing's metaphysical bearings without reading the entire process-philosophy primary literature.
