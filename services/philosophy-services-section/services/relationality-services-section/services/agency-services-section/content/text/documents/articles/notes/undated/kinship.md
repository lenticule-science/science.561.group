# kinship

Kinship names the systems of relation that organize social, economic, and political life: who counts as kin, what obligations flow between kin, and how kinship is established.
Every society organizes itself through kinship in some form, but what kinship includes and what it demands varies enormously — kinship is not a single fact of biology but a structure that each culture builds and rebuilds.
Generated by: reciprocating — kinship is the clearest case of relating constituting its terms, the relations through which people become who they are rather than connections drawn between people who already exist.

The deepest claim the concept makes is that in many Indigenous contexts kinship is not metaphorical or sentimental but constitutive.
It is not a warm feeling laid over the real business of governance and economy; it is the form that governance and economy take.
Kinship relations structure who has obligations to whom, who can make decisions about what, how resources move through a community, and how the land is stewarded.
The Lakota *tiospaye* — the extended family group — is a political and economic unit, not merely a domestic one.
To know one's kinship relations is to know one's responsibilities, one's standing, and one's place in the distribution of work and care.
These are not customs added on top of pre-existing individuals; they are the relations through which social persons are produced in the first place.

This is what makes kinship the social-practice expression of relational thinking: that things are constituted through their relations rather than existing independently and only afterward entering into relation.
Kinship does not link two finished people.
It produces the very persons who stand in it — the grandmother becomes a grandmother through the grandchild, the relative becomes responsible through the web of obligation that names them.
Kim TallBear, in *Native American DNA* (2013), presses this point against the geneticization of Indigenous identity: kinship is specific, contextual, made and remade through practice and obligation, and cannot be reduced to a measurable share of ancestry.
Kinship is something one does, not something one possesses in the blood.

Western anthropology long treated kinship the opposite way — as a classificatory system, a grid of terms for labeling biological relationships, the special province of "family studies."
That reduction strips kinship of its political, economic, and spiritual dimensions and domesticates it into a private matter of who is related to whom.
Gayle Rubin's analysis of the "sex/gender system" showed how that domesticated kinship organizes the exchange of women and enforces compulsory heterosexuality; queer-kinship scholarship has shown, in turn, that kin can be chosen, that families are made by care and not only by descent.
Restoring the full meaning of kinship means attending to the traditions — Indigenous above all — that never reduced it to genealogy in the first place.

Because kinship in this constitutive sense already governs land, decision-making, and the movement of resources, it is the bridge from the reciprocal relations to Indigenous self-determination.
The kinship that structures a people's life on its land is the ground from which autonomy and indigenous sovereignty grow; co-equal kinship is the form kinship takes when it refuses hierarchy, holding each relation as bearing equal worth while carrying unequal and differently directed obligations.
