# power

Power is the capacity, borne in a relation, to shape another's conduct.
It is not a substance that some people own and others lack but a feature of relations themselves — exercised, not possessed.
Generated by: **social-relation** (it is the name for the relation's *asymmetric* form, the relation in which what one party does, has, or can become is directed or appropriated by another).

The classical statement is Weber's: power (*Macht*) is "the probability that one actor within a social relationship will be in a position to carry out his own will despite resistance."
Weber immediately found this too broad to be useful and narrowed it to *Herrschaft* — domination, or authority: the probability that a *command* will be obeyed, which is power made durable, patterned, and (usually) believed-in.
Most of this theory's power branch is the anatomy of that move from raw capacity to settled, legitimated, institutionalized domination: hierarchy, authority, the state, the law.

Foucault reworked the concept in two ways this theory keeps.
First, power is *relational and capillary*: it does not radiate from a single sovereign center but runs through every relation, "comes from everywhere," and is exercised from innumerable points.
Second, and against the intuition that power is essentially a brake on freedom, power is *productive*.
It does not only forbid and repress; it *makes* things — it produces subjects (the delinquent, the patient, the homosexual as kinds of person), produces knowledge (the examination, the census, the case file), produces institutions and pleasures and norms.
Power and knowledge are not opposites: the knowing that classifies and measures a population is itself an exercise of power over it.
For this reason power is not branded "bad" here.
The point of placing it as one branch of social relation is analytic, not moral.

What that placement marks is its *form*: power is the relation of asymmetric direction and appropriation, and it is therefore distinct from — and historically often parasitic upon — the reciprocal relations of mutual aid, solidarity, and the commons that make up the other main branch.
The two are co-equal kinds of social relation, not trunk and leaf.
The error this arrangement guards against is the one critical theory most often makes: mistaking domination for the whole of social life, so that cooperation looks like an exception and hierarchy like the human baseline.

Two consequences run through everything below it.
Because power is productive, the most important forms of modern rule do not work mainly by force but by *forming* people — disciplining bodies, administering populations, cultivating the subject who governs herself (this is the line through discipline, biopower, and the late-liberal school).
And because power is a relation, it is never one-sided: "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault).
Every settled relation of domination is read differently, and refused, from below — which is why the refusals (recognition-refusal, decolonization, the refusal of work) and the reciprocal forms they reach toward belong to the same map as the power they answer.
