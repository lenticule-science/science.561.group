# knowledge-sovereignty

Knowledge sovereignty is the right of a community to control how its knowledge is represented, stored, transmitted, and used.
It applies to Indigenous knowledge systems, community archives, traditional ecological knowledge, and any body of knowledge whose meaning depends on the context of its production — knowledge that cannot be lifted out of the relationships and the place that give it sense without being distorted or destroyed.
Generated by: autonomy — knowledge sovereignty is collective self-governance extended to a community's own knowledge, the principle that a people governs not only its land and its affairs but what is known of it and how that knowing circulates.

The concept is best understood through the extractive practices it names and refuses.
Colonial knowledge practices extract knowledge from communities in the same way colonial economies extract resources: the raw material is taken, processed elsewhere, and returned — if it is returned at all — in a form that serves the extractor's purposes rather than the community's.
Ethnography, linguistics, and natural history have all operated, at various times, as extractive knowledge industries: the anthropologist who records a ceremony and publishes it for a foreign academy, the linguist who documents a language as data, the naturalist who catalogues plant uses learned from people who are then credited with nothing.
Knowledge sovereignty is the refusal of this extraction and the assertion of community authority over what is known and how — including the authority to keep some knowledge from circulating at all, since some knowledge is meant for particular people, particular times, or particular ceremonial contexts, and is harmed rather than honored by being made universally available.

For Indigenous communities, knowledge sovereignty is inseparable from indigenous sovereignty more broadly.
Control over land, over governance, and over knowledge are not three separate struggles but aspects of one self-determination.
Traditional ecological knowledge, ceremonial practice, and language are not cultural artifacts to be preserved in a museum or an archive; they are living systems that function within, and only within, the communities that hold them.
A song removed from the people who sing it, a plant-knowledge removed from the land it concerns, a story removed from the relations it was meant to teach — each survives as a specimen while ceasing to be what it was.
This is why preservation by outside institutions is not a substitute for sovereignty and can be its opposite: to hold a people's knowledge on their behalf, on terms they did not set, is one more form of the absorption that colonialism performs.

Knowledge sovereignty extends into the digital domain, where the same struggle recurs in new infrastructure.
Questions of data governance, of how communities are represented in databases and algorithms, and of who controls the platforms through which community knowledge circulates are contemporary versions of the old extractive problem.
The principle of data sovereignty — that Indigenous data should be governed by Indigenous nations — applies the same claim to the archive, the dataset, and the model trained on a people's words and images.

Within the reciprocal relations, knowledge sovereignty is a specific dimension of autonomy, parallel to temporal autonomy: where temporal autonomy claims a community's authority over its own time, knowledge sovereignty claims its authority over its own knowing.
Both are answers to a domination that works by taking — taking time through the clock and the wage, taking knowledge through extraction and enclosure — and both reassert, against that taking, a people's standing to govern what is properly its own.
It is the reciprocal, grounded counter-form to the enclosure of knowledge that extractive industries and the commodification of culture perform.
