# class-struggle

Class struggle is the conflict between social classes whose interests are structurally opposed within a given mode of production.
Marx and Engels placed it at the center of historical analysis: the history of all hitherto existing society, in the opening line of the *Communist Manifesto*, is the history of class struggles — freeman and slave, patrician and plebeian, lord and serf, guild-master and journeyman, and in the modern world bourgeoisie and proletariat.
Each pairing names a relation of exploitation in which one class appropriates the labor of another.
Generated by: **exploitation** — class struggle is the conflict that exploitation's opposed positions necessarily generate, the antagonism built into a relation where one side's gain is the other's loss.

In capitalist society Marx identified the primary antagonism as that between the bourgeoisie, who own the means of production, and the proletariat, who own nothing but their labor-power and must sell it for wages.
The bourgeoisie extracts surplus-value from workers — the difference between the value workers produce and the value they receive as wages — and this extraction is not incidental but structural, since it is the very mechanism by which capital accumulates.
Class struggle in this framework is therefore not a matter of individual grievances or bad employers but of the objective contradiction between those who own and those who work.
It is fought concretely over the variables surplus-value depends on: the length of the working day, the intensity of labor, and the level of wages, each of which sets the two classes against one another by the structure of the relation rather than by anyone's ill will.

The concept has been refigured, and in some respects challenged, by the very traditions that build on Marx.
Racial capitalism, beginning with Cedric Robinson, argues that capitalism was racial from its origins — that class formation was always entangled with racial differentiation, and that treating race as secondary to class misidentifies how exploitation actually operates.
Social-reproduction theory demonstrates that the class relation presupposes unwaged reproductive labor, overwhelmingly performed by women, that produces laborers as laborers; the worker who arrives ready to work was made ready by care work the wage never touches.
And operaismo inverted the orthodox framing entirely, insisting that working-class struggle *drives* capital's development rather than the reverse — that capital reorganizes itself in response to the resistance it provokes.

Indigenous and decolonial critiques raise a different problem.
Class analysis, even in its most sophisticated forms, tends to center the wage relation and the factory as the primary site of exploitation, and this framing can erase the constitutive role of land dispossession — primitive accumulation and ongoing settler colonialism — in producing the conditions under which wage labor exists at all.
For peoples whose primary relation to land is not ownership but kinship, class analysis as conventionally formulated does not describe the relevant antagonism, and the demand at stake is not a fairer wage but the return of land.

As a node, class struggle is the dynamic dimension of exploitation: where exploitation names the static structure of extraction, class struggle names the living conflict it sets in motion.
It is the trunk from which the operaismo tendency branches — workerism being class struggle read from labor's own side — and from which the politics of refusal grows.
To call a conflict class struggle is to claim that it is not a clash of personalities or values but the surfacing of an antagonism that the structure of production itself produces and reproduces.
