This is an operational guide for distinguishing the icons in a candidate artifact that are doing structural work from those that are doing prestige work. The procedure is shorter than the gutter audit because icons are usually visually distinguishable and finite in number.

## What you need

- The artifact.
- A working understanding of which icons in the artifact's discipline have an *operational* use (commutative diagrams, circuit symbols, pictograms, named technical notation, code idioms) and which are primarily badges of community membership.

## The procedure

**Step 1: Enumerate the icons.** Walk through the artifact and list every icon: every diagram, every piece of distinctive notation, every standardized visual element, every named idiom that is doing the work an icon does (compressing structure into recognizable form). Some of these will be obvious; others will be implicit (a particular phrasing convention, a particular structural template).

**Step 2: For each icon, ask what work it does.** The diagnostic is replacement: write out, in plain register, what the icon stands for. Compare the result to the icon. Three possibilities:

- **The expansion is much larger and harder to read.** The icon is performing [icon-amplification](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/icon-amplification.md): real compression of real structure. Keep the icon. (Most operative icons fall here.)
- **The expansion is comparable in size and clarity.** The icon is borderline. It may still be earning its place if it supports an operation (chase, paste into a larger diagram, manipulate algebraically) that the expansion does not, but it is not paying for itself purely on compression. Annotate.
- **The expansion is shorter or clearer than the icon.** The icon is a [prestige-icon](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/prestige-icon.md). It is in the artifact for what its presence signals about the writer's affiliation, not for what it accomplishes for the reader. Mark for removal or replacement.

**Step 3: For each icon that survives Step 2, check that it is recognizable to the declared audience.** An icon that compresses real structure but that the declared audience cannot read is not amplifying for that audience; it is creating an [unaccountable closure](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/unaccountable-closure.md). Either expand the icon, or narrow the audience claim, or transport the artifact's load-bearing operations into a register the audience can engage with.

**Step 4: Check for icon overload.** Even operative icons exhaust the reader's attention if there are too many of them in too short a span. A page with twelve commutative diagrams may be doing real compression at each diagram but is exceeding the reader's capacity to chase them. The repair is to thin the icons (carry the structure in prose where the icon would be redundant) or to space them across more of the artifact.

## What the output is for

The audit produces:

1. **A removal list.** Prestige icons identified for removal or replacement.
2. **An audience-fit report.** Operative icons that exceed the declared audience's training, with notes on whether to expand them or to narrow the audience.
3. **A density map.** Locations in the artifact where icon density exceeds reasonable cognitive load, with notes on rebalancing.

The audit is the icon-side counterpart to the [gutter audit](guides-how-to-classify-the-gutters-of-an-artifact.md). Together they cover most of the diagnostic work the framework supports.
