# racial-formation

Racial formation is Michael Omi and Howard Winant's name, developed in *Racial Formation in the United States* (first edition 1986; third edition 2014), for "the sociohistorical process by which racial categories are created, inhabited, transformed, and destroyed."
It is the theory that race is neither a biological fact nor a mere illusion to be debunked, but a permanently contested social structure whose meaning is made and remade through political struggle.
The categories shift as the politics shift.
Generated by: race — racial-formation is the historical process through which the system of racial categories acquires and loses its content.

Omi and Winant developed the concept against two earlier views they found inadequate.
One treated race as essentially biological — a natural kind that social arrangements merely reflect.
The other treated race as an illusion, an epiphenomenon of something supposedly more real, usually class, that would dissolve once the underlying economic relations were addressed.
Racial formation rejects both.
Race is real, but it is real as a social structure with its own history and its own logic, not reducible to economics and not grounded in biology.
It is built up out of countless "racial projects" — efforts by states, movements, institutions, and individuals to give racial categories particular meanings and to organize resources and bodies accordingly.

A racial project, in their account, is any effort that simultaneously interprets racial dynamics and redistributes resources along racial lines.
A discriminatory mortgage policy is a racial project; so is a civil-rights statute; so is a census reclassification or a political slogan.
Some projects entrench racial hierarchy and some contest it, but all of them participate in the ongoing formation of race.
This is why the categories are historically mobile: who counts as "white," what "Black" means politically, whether a group is treated as a race at all — these are settled and unsettled repeatedly, through conflict, over time.
The shift of the Irish into whiteness, the racialization and partial deracialization of Jews, the changing legal definitions of who was eligible for naturalized citizenship in the United States, all illustrate categories being formed and reformed by political force.

Racial formation is the macro-historical companion to the other concepts in this cluster.
Racialization names the local act of producing a group as racial; racial formation names the encompassing process within which such acts accumulate and the categories themselves are transformed.
Where the Fieldses' racecraft emphasizes the sleight that makes race appear natural, Omi and Winant emphasize the visible, contested, institutional labor that keeps race in motion.
Both insist that race is political all the way down — that because it was made through struggle, it can be remade, which is the analytical hope built into the theory.
