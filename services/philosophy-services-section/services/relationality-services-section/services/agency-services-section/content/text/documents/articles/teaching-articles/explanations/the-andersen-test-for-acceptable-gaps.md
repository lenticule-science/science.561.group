Line Edslev Andersen's "Acceptable gaps in mathematical proofs" (*Synthese*, 2020) gives the cleanest available distinction between gaps that are defects and gaps that are not. The paper is set in mathematics, but its analytical move — and its method — generalize to any practice with framed units and trained audiences. This text is a synthesis of the framework Andersen develops and a translation of its diagnostic into general communicative use.

## The problem Andersen addresses

A common implicit assumption in writing about mathematical proof is that any unstated step is a problem. On this view, a fully rigorous proof would in principle fill every gap, and the proofs we actually publish are imperfect approximations of the gap-free ideal. The implicit standard is the formal derivation, expanded line by line.

Andersen, drawing on interviews with practicing mathematicians, observes that this picture is empirically wrong. Real proofs are full of gaps. Mathematicians know they are full of gaps. They have detailed, mostly tacit, mostly shared norms about which gaps are fine and which are not. The interesting question is not "how do we get rid of gaps?" but "what makes a gap acceptable?"

## Andersen's distinction

A gap is *acceptable* when:

- The audience the proof is addressed to can perform the omitted step from their training.
- The step would be uninstructive to spell out — its inclusion would slow the argument without illuminating anything.
- The discipline's conventions license the omission.

A gap is *not acceptable* when:

- The omitted step exceeds what the addressed audience has been trained to perform.
- The step's omission conceals something the writer themselves did not check.
- The discipline's conventions do not license the omission and no replacement license is offered.

The distinction is *audience-relative*: a gap acceptable for one audience is not acceptable for another. There is no audience-free criterion of acceptable gap, and Andersen does not propose one. The gap discipline of a practice is calibrated to the practice's audiences.

Within the acceptable cases, Andersen distinguishes a special subkind: the [disciplinary gap](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/disciplinary-gap.md), closed by appeal to specific apparatus the discipline maintains (named theorems, standard arguments, normal forms). These are particularly well-licensed because the apparatus is *visible* — the gap is closed by something the reader can be sent to look up if needed.

## Why the test matters

The framework borrows Andersen's vocabulary because it solves a problem the framework otherwise has no good way to solve. The framework's central defect — [unaccountable closure](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/unaccountable-closure.md) — needs a way to distinguish gaps that are defects from gaps that are simply how disciplined communication works. Andersen's distinction provides exactly this. A gap is unaccountable iff it fails her test: it is unacceptable for the audience the artifact addresses. A gap that meets her test is acceptable, and the artifact has not erred in leaving it.

## Translation outside mathematics

The same test runs in any practice with trained audiences. A signage system can omit "turn here" because the arrow is shared vocabulary among everyone who has ever read signs; the omission is acceptable. A software tutorial can omit "open a terminal" for an audience of developers and not for an audience of designers; whether the omission is acceptable depends on which audience the tutorial declares. A surgical checklist can omit "confirm the patient's identity" if the institution's norms include it as a separate compulsory step; if those norms erode, the omission stops being acceptable even though the document has not changed.

In every case, the test is the same: who is the document for, what have they been trained to close, and is this gap one of those? When the answer is yes, the gap is acceptable. When the answer is no, the artifact has work to do — license the gap (cite the apparatus, expand the omitted step, narrow the audience claim) or leave the gap and accept that the artifact is not what it claims to be.

## Companion reading

Andersen's "Acceptable gaps in mathematical proofs" (*Synthese*, 2020) is the key paper. Her later work with Henrik Kragh Sørensen develops the empirical methodology. The companion to Andersen on the *plan*-side of proof reading is Hamami and Morris — they ask what the reader is recovering when they recover the proof, and Andersen asks what the writer can leave out without preventing the recovery.
