# labor-power

Labor-power is Marx's term for the worker's *capacity* to work — the aggregate of physical and mental capabilities a person can set in motion to produce something — as distinct from labor, the actual exercise of that capacity.
The distinction looks small and is in fact the hinge on which Marx's whole account of exploitation turns.
What the worker sells to the employer is not labor but labor-power: the right to use their working capacity for a stretch of time.
Generated by: **wage-labor** — labor-power is the thing the wage relation commodifies, the peculiar commodity whose sale defines the worker's position under capitalism.

Marx's key move is to ask what kind of commodity labor-power is, and to find that it is unique.
Like any commodity, it has a value, and that value is determined the way the value of any commodity is determined: by the labor time socially necessary to produce and reproduce it — in this case, the means of subsistence needed to keep the worker alive, able to return to work the next day, and able to raise the next generation of workers.
The wage, in the long run, tends toward this value.
But labor-power has a property no other commodity has: its *use* — putting it to work — creates value, and can create more value than the labor-power itself costs.
A worker whose subsistence is worth the product of, say, four hours of work can be set to work for eight, ten, or twelve.
The capacity costs one thing; its exercise yields another and greater thing.

This gap is the secret Marx claims to have uncovered, the thing that distinguishes his theory of profit from the idea that capitalists simply "buy cheap and sell dear."
If everything exchanged at its value — if no one were cheated in any market — profit would still arise, because the one commodity the capitalist buys at its value, labor-power, produces *more* value in use than its value in purchase.
Surplus-value is the name for that excess.
Exploitation, in Marx's precise sense, is not unequal exchange or fraud; it is the appropriation of the difference between the value of labor-power and the value that the use of labor-power creates.
The wage is "fair" by the market's standards and the extraction happens anyway, structurally, through the ordinary operation of buying and selling.

The concept also clarifies what the employer is actually purchasing and why the workplace is organized as it is.
Having bought labor-power, the capitalist owns its use for the contracted period but still faces the problem of converting that capacity into actual labor — of getting the worker to work, and to work as much and as intensively as possible.
This is why the wage contract does not settle the matter but opens it: the difference between the labor-power bought and the labor extracted is precisely what the organization of the labor process, supervision, discipline, and incentive systems are designed to maximize.
The distinction between capacity and exercise thus reappears as the everyday conflict of the workplace.

As a node, labor-power is the pivot between the wage relation and surplus-value.
Wage-labor is the relation in which labor-power is sold; surplus-value is what its use yields over its cost.
To grasp the difference between labor-power and labor is to grasp how a system of free and equal exchanges can nonetheless rest on, and continually reproduce, the extraction of unpaid labor.
