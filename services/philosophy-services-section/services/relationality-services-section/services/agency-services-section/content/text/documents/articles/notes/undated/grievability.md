# grievability

Generated by: biopower, distributing — biopower distributes grievability unequally, so that some lives register as losses and others are ungrievable.

Grievability is Judith Butler's term for the condition under which a life is recognized as worth mourning — and therefore worth protecting in the first place.
Butler develops it across *Precarious Life: The Powers of Mourning and Violence* (2004) and *Frames of War: When Is Life Grievable?* (2009).
The central and counterintuitive claim is that grievability is not a response to a death but a precondition for recognizing a life as a life at all.
A life that is not apprehended as grievable is not fully apprehended as living.
The question of who can be grieved is therefore prior to, and helps decide, the question of who can be killed.

Political and media structures frame certain lives as ungrievable before any violence is done to them.
For Butler the framing is not a distortion laid over an otherwise neutral perception; it is the condition of perception itself.
No life appears to us outside of a frame.
Some frames present a life as fully human — with a name, a history, a community that will mourn its loss.
Other frames present lives as threats to be neutralized, as statistical abstractions, or as collateral damage whose destruction is regrettable but necessary.
The frame that renders a life ungrievable does not merely fail to recognize it; it actively produces the conditions under which that life's destruction becomes acceptable, or simply invisible.

Butler traces how this framing operates through several channels at once.
Through visual media: which images of death circulate, with what captions, in what contexts.
Through state rhetoric: who is named a victim and who is named a combatant or an enemy.
And through the differential distribution of precarity: whose vulnerability is addressed by institutions and whose is treated as a fact of nature.
Some deaths produce national mourning, memorials, and demands for justice; others produce silence, are absorbed into a statistic, or are recast as the inevitable cost of security.
Butler wrote much of this work in the aftermath of September 11, 2001, asking why certain deaths — American, Western, named — generated overwhelming public grief while others — Afghan, Iraqi, Palestinian, detained — did not register as losses at all.

The concept carries a normative charge.
Butler argues that the recognition of a shared precariousness — the fact that every embodied life is vulnerable and dependent on others — obligates a political response.
If all life is precarious, then the structures that render some lives ungrievable are not merely unjust; they are themselves a form of violence, doing harm before any blow is struck.
To insist on the grievability of lives that have been framed as ungrievable is thus a political act: a refusal of the frame that made their destruction thinkable.
Grievability gives a precise name to one mechanism of biopower's distribution of life and death — the sorting of populations into those whose loss counts and those whose loss does not — and it opens directly onto its companion concepts: the frames of war that do the sorting, the precarious life that is its object, and the precarity that names its uneven distribution.
