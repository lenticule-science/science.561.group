# Entrainment and groove — coordination through a shared pulse

This text gathers the literature that lets `music` be read as an **agentic** phenomenon in the repository's specific sense: agents coordinating **through a shared field, not point-to-point** (compare the stigmergic reading of `working` at `theories/existence/becoming/doing/working/docs/working.md`).
The shared field music supplies is a **common pulse** — and the literature on how independent bodies fall into that pulse is the literature of entrainment and groove.

## Entrainment: independent oscillators falling into phase

The phenomenon was first recorded by **Christiaan Huygens** (1665), who noticed that two pendulum clocks hung on the same beam drifted into anti-phase synchrony — coupled oscillators settling into a shared rhythm through the medium that connects them.
The transfer of this idea into the study of music is made explicitly by **Martin Clayton, Rebecca Sager, and Udo Will**, "In time with the music: The concept of entrainment and its significance for ethnomusicology" (*ESEM CounterPoint*, 2005), which defines musical entrainment as the process by which independent rhythmic processes — performers, dancers, listeners — adjust to and lock with a shared periodicity.
Entrainment is the mechanism by which many separate agents become **one field in time** without any of them commanding the others: each reads and contributes to the common pulse.

## Dynamic attending: the pulse pulls perception

**Mari Riess Jones and Edward Large** (dynamic attending theory; Large & Jones, "The dynamics of attending," *Psychological Review*, 1999) model the listener's attention as itself an oscillator that **entrains** to the music's periodicities, so that expectation, accuracy, and pleasure track how well internal and external rhythms align.
The shared field is not passively heard; it actively organizes when and how sharply each participant attends.
This is the cognitive face of "coordination through the field."

## Groove and participatory discrepancy

**Charles Keil**'s "Participatory Discrepancies and the Power of Music" (*Cultural Anthropology*, 1987) argues that the compelling, body-moving quality of music — its **groove** — arises not from mechanical perfection but from the small, living timing and tuning discrepancies *between* participants.
Groove is relational: it lives in the gaps between players, not in any one line. **Vijay Iyer**, "Embodied Mind, Situated Cognition, and Expressive Microtiming in African-American Music" (*Music Perception*, 2002), grounds this in the moving body, showing that microtiming is heard and produced against a felt bodily pulse — music as situated, embodied cognition rather than abstract pattern.
Empirical work on the **sensation of groove** (e.g. Janata, Tomic, & Haran, "Sensorimotor coupling in music and the psychology of the groove," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 2012) ties the experience directly to the urge to move — closing the loop back to the dancing body the field came from.

## What the literature licenses

Read together: a shared periodicity is the medium through which independent agents coordinate (Huygens, Clayton/Sager/Will); attention itself entrains to it (Large & Jones); and the field's life and pull come from the discrepancies *between* participants reading and feeding it (Keil, Iyer, Janata).
That is enough to read **music as the agentic turn**: not one agent acting on another but many agents constituting and reading a common pulse — the existential shared field, sensed as sound and time, out of which bounded rule-structured play (the game) can crystallize.
