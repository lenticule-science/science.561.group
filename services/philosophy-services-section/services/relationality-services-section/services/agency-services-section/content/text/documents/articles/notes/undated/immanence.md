# immanence

Immanence is the principle that a social form is to be explained from within the relations that constitute it, rather than by appeal to a transcendent essence or an outside cause.
To explain immanently is to stay inside the process: to show how a practice, an institution, or an identity is produced and sustained by the relations among its participants, instead of grounding it in something standing above or behind those relations — a human nature, a divine plan, a national spirit, a market logic treated as a law of nature.
Generated by: **relating** — immanence is the methodological commitment that explanation is sought within relating itself, not in any term imported from outside the web of relations.

The contrast is with transcendence, and the contrast is old.
A transcendent explanation appeals to a principle that lies outside the world it explains and is supposed to be exempt from it: society is the way it is because of an eternal essence, a sacred order, a fixed human type.
An immanent explanation refuses that exemption.
It insists that whatever organizes social life is itself produced within social life and is therefore answerable to it.
In the dialectical tradition this takes the form of *immanent critique*: rather than judging a society against an external ideal smuggled in from nowhere, the critic holds the society to its own professed commitments and exposes the gap between what it claims and what it does — showing that a liberal order proclaiming equality produces durable hierarchy, and letting the contradiction, internal to the order, do the critical work.

In sociology the immanent commitment runs through the discipline's strongest relational thinkers.
Durkheim's rule that social facts must be explained by other social facts, not by psychology or biology, is a statement of immanence: the explanatory ground of a social phenomenon lies at the social level itself.
Bourdieu's refusal to explain conduct either by free individual choice or by mechanical external determination, locating it instead in the relation between *habitus* and *field*, is immanent in the same way — the dispositions and the structured space that shape action are both produced within the social game, not imposed from beyond it.
The shared move is to bring the explanatory ground down into the relations themselves and to deny that any essence outside the relations is doing the real work.

The stakes are simultaneously methodological and political, which is why this entry sits at the heart of the branch.
An essence placed outside relations is, by construction, beyond challenge: if society is grounded in a fixed nature, then its arrangements are as unalterable as that nature.
Immanence pulls the ground back inside, where it can be examined, contested, and changed.
This is the same gesture that **contingency** makes about necessity and that **relation** makes about the parties it constitutes: everything explanatory is produced within the relational fabric and could, in principle, be produced otherwise.
To commit to immanence is to refuse the comfort and the closure of a transcendent cause and to take responsibility for explaining the social by the social — the foundational stance of a relational, anti-essentialist account of social life.
