# adjudication

Adjudication is the formal process by which a legal body resolves a dispute or determines a question of law.
Generated by: adjudication is law applied to a case — the moment the general rule meets a particular controversy and a judge hears arguments, weighs evidence, applies the relevant rules, and issues a decision that binds the parties.
The decision then enters the legal record and, in common-law systems, may bind future courts through precedent.
Adjudication is thus where law becomes concrete: where the abstract norm is brought to bear on real people and real facts, and where the legal order discharges its promise to settle conflicts authoritatively.

What distinguishes adjudication from other ways of resolving disputes is its institutional character and its backing by the state.
Negotiation is bilateral: the parties work things out between themselves.
Mediation introduces a third party who facilitates an agreement but cannot impose one.
Arbitration introduces a third party who can impose a decision, but only because the parties have agreed in advance to be bound by it.
Adjudication alone is backed by the state's coercive apparatus — the court's judgment is enforceable through fines, imprisonment, and the seizure of assets, whether or not the losing party consents.
It is this state backing that makes a court's word something more than an opinion.

That backing is also what gives adjudication its formatting power.
Because court decisions are enforceable, they do more than resolve the immediate dispute: they establish facts that other institutions treat as authoritative.
When a court rules that a particular institutional practice constitutes evidence of good faith, that ruling reshapes the behavior of institutions far beyond the parties to the case, as actors everywhere adjust to what the courts will now recognize.
Adjudication therefore produces effects on the whole field of social practice as a structural by-product, not merely a resolution of the case at hand — it is one of the principal sites at which legal formatting and the propagation of precedent actually happen.

The most striking fact about adjudication, sociologically, is how rarely it occurs.
In American criminal law roughly ninety-five percent of cases are resolved through plea bargaining — negotiated agreements between prosecutors and defendants that bypass trial entirely — and in civil law settlement is the norm.
The full adjudicatory process, formally central to the legal system, is statistically marginal.
Its influence operates chiefly through the *shadow* it casts: parties negotiate in light of what they expect a court would decide if the case went to trial, so the threat of judgment governs the vast majority of outcomes that judgment never actually reaches.
The adjudicative process is, in this sense, most powerful precisely where it does not run its course.

Within this cluster adjudication is the application of law to the individual case and the event that due process is meant to make fair.
It presupposes jurisdiction, since a body must have authority before it can decide; it generates precedent, which carries its rulings forward into future cases; and it is backed by sanction, the coercive enforcement without which its judgments would be mere words.
It is the hinge between the law as written rule and the law as it is lived.
