# Imagination rollout

An **imagination rollout** is a synthetic trajectory generated by
repeatedly applying a learned world model to its own predictions:

$$
s_0,\quad s_1 = T(s_0, \pi(s_0)),\quad s_2 = T(s_1, \pi(s_1)),\quad
\ldots,\quad s_H
$$

with rewards $r_t = R(s_t, a_t)$ accumulated along the way. The
rollout exists entirely *inside* the world model — no real
environment interaction is consumed.

## What rollouts are used for

**Policy improvement.** Train a policy $\pi$ to maximize total
reward over imagined trajectories. The policy never touches the
real environment during this update; the cost is $H$ forward
passes through the model, not $H$ environment steps. The Dreamer
family is built on this. See
[model-based-policy-improvement](model-based-policy-improvement.md).

**Value learning.** Compute $\lambda$-returns or $n$-step returns
along the imagined trajectory and use them as targets for a value
function. Reduces the bootstrap depth required and accelerates
value propagation.

**Exploration.** Roll out under different exploratory policies
to estimate which states would be reached and their reward; pick
the policy with maximal expected information gain.

**Counterfactual analysis.** Roll out the same initial state
under different policies to attribute outcomes.

## The horizon-error tradeoff

Longer rollouts:
- Provide more reward signal per training sample (good).
- Better propagate value information (good).
- Accumulate more model error
  ([model-error-compounds](model-error-compounds.md))
  (bad).

Empirically, the sweet spot for Dreamer-style training is
$H \approx 15$–$50$ steps. Shorter rollouts under-use the model;
longer rollouts diverge into model-fantasy territory the policy
overfits to.

## Re-grounding

To bound accumulated error, imagination rollouts are typically
**re-grounded**: instead of one long rollout from a single seed
state, many short rollouts from many real states sampled from a
replay buffer. This trades horizon for breadth and ensures the
imagined-state distribution stays close to the real-state
distribution.
