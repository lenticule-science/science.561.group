# social-relation

A social relation is a connection between two or more social beings that has consequences for how they act — a standing orientation of one party's conduct toward another's.
It is the primitive material of sociology: everything else the discipline studies — practices, norms, roles, positions, institutions, communities, cultures — is a configuration or *precipitate* of social relations, a pattern that forms when relations settle into something durable.
Generated by: **social-being** (a social being just is a being capable of entering such relations; the relation is what one being's action, oriented toward another's, brings into being).

The concept is Weber's *soziale Beziehung*: "the behaviour of a plurality of actors insofar as, in its meaningful content, the action of each takes account of that of the others and is oriented in these terms."
What makes a relation *social* is not physical proximity but mutual orientation — two strangers ignoring each other in an elevator are barely in a social relation; two enemies who have never met but each of whom shapes their conduct around the other are deeply in one.
Simmel turned this into the study of the *forms* of association (*Vergesellschaftung*): the same form — subordination, competition, exchange, the secret, the gift — recurs across utterly different contents (a state, a firm, a friendship), and it is the form, the shape of the relation itself, that sociology can generalize about.

This theory takes the further, relational-sociological step (Emirbayer's "Manifesto for a Relational Sociology"; behind it Marx and Simmel, and further back Nāgārjuna's dependent origination and Whitehead's internal relations): relations are not links *between* pre-existing individuals but are *prior to* their terms.
A person is not a substance who later enters relations; a person is the relatively stable pattern that a web of relations sustains — kin, worker, citizen, neighbour.
Change the relations and you change what the person is.
The methodological commitment that follows is to explain a social form by the relations that constitute it, rather than by appeal to a fixed human nature or a pre-given essence.

## The major forms a social relation takes

Social relations are not all of one kind, and that difference is the central fact of social and political life.
Three forms organize the rest of this theory — and are kept as the three main branches of relations below.
The division follows Graeber's three "moral principles" of economic life, and behind them Polanyi's forms of integration (reciprocity, redistribution, exchange) and Kropotkin's *Mutual Aid*:

- **Reciprocity and mutual aid** (under `reciprocating`).
  Relations among people who treat each other as, in the relevant respect, equals: solidarity, the gift, kinship, the commons, cooperation, collective self-governance.
  Help given here is not a debt that subordinates the helped; capacity is pooled rather than appropriated, and the relation tends to leave both parties more able rather than one richer at the other's expense.
  Kropotkin argued that this, not competition, is the older and more pervasive substrate of social life — it does not need domination in order to exist, and in most times and places precedes it.

- **Domination** (under `power`).
  Asymmetric relations in which one party's conduct, labour, land, or attention is directed or appropriated by another: Weber's *Herrschaft* (the probability that a command will be obeyed), Marx's exploitation, the rule of states, owners, and masters.
  This is the branch most of critical sociology examines, and it is large — but it is *one* branch, not the trunk.
  To treat domination as the whole of social relation is itself an ideological move: it writes mutual aid out of the picture and makes hierarchy look like the natural condition of human beings.

- **The common and its enclosure**.
  Some of what social relations circulate — knowledge, language, culture, a shared field or fishery — can in principle be held and used in common, shared without being used up.
  Much of modern history is the story of *enclosure*: the forcible conversion of such commons into private property — from the literal enclosure of English fields that Marx called primitive accumulation, through settler land title, to the capture of attention and behaviour by platforms.
  This runs below through **the-commons** (in the reciprocal branch) and **enclosure** (under capital).

## Relations are contradictory, and contested from below

Real relations rarely fall cleanly into one form.
The same relation usually carries mutuality and domination *at once*, and holding that contradiction together is much of what social life consists of.
The family is care and patriarchy in one bond.
The wage is a voluntary agreement and a coerced extraction.
Recognition — being granted a place in the social order — is at once inclusion and subjection (Coulthard: the offer of recognition is the offer to be defined on the dominant's terms).
Berlant's *cruel optimism* names the sharpest case: an attachment to the very thing that wears you down.
Societies can be told apart by how they manage these contradictions — whether they resolve them quickly by suppressing one side (the rigid order that answers every dispute with force or denial) or hold them open indefinitely so no resolution ever forms (the late-liberal "coherent confusion" examined in the school below).
How fast, and by what means, a society metabolizes its own contradictions is in effect the rate of its reproduction and the reachability of its transformation.

And every relation of domination looks different from each side, and is never simply accepted by the party it subordinates.
What the master calls order the slave calls bondage; what the settler calls property the dispossessed calls theft.
This is the ground of standpoint theory — Du Bois's double-consciousness, Collins's standpoint epistemology: the subordinated party knows the relation in a way the dominant cannot afford to.
It is also why every form of domination has its answering refusal — recognition has recognition-refusal, colonization has decolonization, the wage has the refusal of work — and why the reciprocal forms are not sentimental extras but the positive content those refusals reach toward.
