# habitus

Habitus is Bourdieu's name for the durable, transposable dispositions a person acquires through their history — the internalized structure of perception, taste, and action that generates conduct without conscious rule-following.
Generated by: social-position — a position in the social world, inhabited over time, writes itself into the body as a set of dispositions, so that people come to perceive, want, and do what their position has made familiar, available, and likely.

Bourdieu's compressed formula is that habitus is a "structured structure predisposed to function as a structuring structure": it is shaped *by* one's conditions of existence (a class position, a trajectory, a milieu) and in turn *shapes* one's practice.
It is acquired through prolonged exposure rather than taught as rules — above all in childhood, in the family and the early encounter with one's class conditions — and once laid down it operates beneath deliberation, as a "feel for the game" or practical sense.
The skilled practitioner, the at-ease guest, the native speaker all act correctly without consulting any rule; that fluency is habitus.

The concept is Bourdieu's resolution of the old opposition between structure and agency, between seeing action as determined by society and seeing it as free choice.
Habitus is neither.
It is a generative principle of *regulated improvisation*: people act inventively, responding to endless particular situations, but their inventions are statistically patterned by the dispositions their formation installed.
This is how an order can reproduce itself without coercion and without anyone following orders — the dispositions people acquire under given conditions lead them, freely, to act in ways that tend to reproduce those very conditions.
The working-class student who "doesn't see the point" of university and the bourgeois child wholly at ease in it are each enacting a habitus that quietly routes them toward the destiny their origin made probable; necessity is experienced as preference (what Bourdieu, after Nietzsche, calls *amor fati*, the love of one's fate).

Because habitus is embodied — carried in posture, gait, accent, table manners, the management of the body — class and trajectory are legible at a glance, and this is part of why symbolic violence works: domination is recognized and misrecognized through bodies that have been shaped to fit, or to feel out of place.
That mismatch has its own name, *hysteresis*: a habitus formed in one world and then displaced into another, where its dispositions no longer fit — the migrant, the scholarship student, the newly rich or newly poor, whose acquired sense of the game has come unstuck from the game now being played.
Habitus is durable but not eternal; it lags, and in the gap between an old habitus and new conditions some of the most acute experiences of social dislocation, and some of the openings for change, are found.
