# Silver et al. 2018

**A general reinforcement learning algorithm that masters chess, shogi, and Go through self-play.** Science 362(6419), pp.
1140–1144.

## What the paper does

Reports AlphaZero: a single algorithm that achieves superhuman performance in chess, shogi, and Go through self-play alone, with no domain-specific knowledge beyond the rules.
AlphaZero combines Monte Carlo tree search with a deep neural network that provides both a value estimate and a policy prior for the search.

## Key contributions

1. **The MCTS-with-learned-priors recipe.** Use a neural network to provide both the rollout value (replacing random rollouts in classical MCTS) and the tree-policy prior (biasing UCB1 selection).
  This reduces the search-budget required for strong play by orders of magnitude relative to vanilla MCTS.
2. **Self-play training**.
  The network is trained on data generated by playing itself; no human-game data is required.
  The training signal is the agreement between the MCTS-improved policy and the network's output.
3. **Architectural unity across games.** The same algorithm and architecture work for chess, shogi, and Go with no game-specific tuning beyond the input representation.
  Demonstrates that the MCTS-plus-network template is *general-purpose* rather than domain-specific.
4. **Demonstration of inference-time-compute scaling.** AlphaZero's playing strength scales monotonically with the per-move MCTS simulation budget, demonstrating the [inference-time-compute-tradeoff](../notes/undated/inference-time-compute-tradeoff.md) in a high-stakes setting.

## Why this paper matters

AlphaZero is the canonical demonstration that explicit search and deep learning *combine multiplicatively*: each is dramatically strengthened by the other.
Search structure provides correctness and verifiability; learning provides heuristic strength.

This recipe — *learned function approximation inside an explicit search procedure* — has become the template for high-performance planning across many domains (theorem proving, drug discovery, robotics, compiler optimization, and most contemporary high-performance LLM-reasoning systems).

The paper also empirically validates the inference-vs-training compute substitution: AlphaZero's strength can be increased either by training a better network (training compute) or by giving each move more MCTS simulations (inference compute).
The substitution rate is non-trivial in both directions.
