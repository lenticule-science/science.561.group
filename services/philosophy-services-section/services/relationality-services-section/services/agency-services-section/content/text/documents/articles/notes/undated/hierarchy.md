# hierarchy

Hierarchy is a fixed arrangement of social positions in which some hold structural power over others — the capacity to command, punish, allocate resources, or define meaning in ways those below cannot refuse without consequence.
It is the organizational form of ranked, vertical relations: positions stacked into a chain in which the higher direct and the lower comply.
Generated by: **power** read as ranking — hierarchy is what power becomes when it settles into stable, asymmetric positions rather than circulating among equals.

The decisive analytic move, sharpest in the anarchist tradition (Bakunin, and later Bookchin), is to distinguish hierarchy from mere difference.
People differ in knowledge, skill, and experience, and those differences are real.
A person who knows how to build a boat knows more about boatbuilding than someone who does not.
Hierarchy is not that difference; it is the *conversion* of a situated difference into a fixed structural position — the claim that because one person knows more, owns more, or arrived first, the others owe them obedience.
Authority names that claim to a right of command, and coercion enforces it.
Bakunin's critique of "the principle of authority" turned on exactly this point: he was willing to defer to the bootmaker on boots, but refused to grant any person or institution a standing right to direct others across the whole of life.

Hierarchy tends to reproduce itself, and this is one of its most important features.
Hierarchical organizations produce hierarchical outcomes, which are then cited as justification for the hierarchy that produced them.
The employer extracts surplus from the worker, accumulates wealth, and points to that wealth as evidence of superior capacity.
The colonial power destroys Indigenous governance, imposes dependency, and points to that dependency as evidence that the colonized cannot govern themselves.
Bakunin identified this self-reinforcing quality as the reason hierarchical means cannot produce non-hierarchical ends — the insight that grounds prefigurative politics, the commitment to building the future in the structures of present struggle rather than postponing equality to an indefinite "after."

It matters that hierarchy is not the same as structure.
Coordination, roles, division of labor, and durable agreements are all forms of structure, and a society without them is not freedom but paralysis.
Horizontal organizations have structure — they have roles, processes, and rules — but they distribute power rather than concentrating it; federation coordinates autonomous groups without subordinating them.
The objection a critic of hierarchy raises is not to organization as such but to its *vertical* form: the stacking of positions into a chain of command from which the subordinate cannot exit or refuse without penalty.
A relationship one can leave without punishment must sustain itself through mutual benefit; a relationship one cannot leave can sustain itself through domination.

Hierarchy is therefore the hinge between the broad concept of power and the specific harms that follow from it.
Power is everywhere in social life — in influence, persuasion, care, and coordination — and is not in itself an evil.
Domination is the subset of power that runs through fixed hierarchy: where positions are arranged vertically, where the relation is not mutual, and where the subordinated party bears consequences for refusal.
Almost every concept downstream of this one — domination, exploitation, the wage relation, the disciplinary apparatus of the state — presupposes a prior verticalization of social position.
To name a relation as hierarchical is already to raise the question the whole tradition presses: whether the ranking is answerable to those it ranks, or whether it has hardened into a structure that commands them.
