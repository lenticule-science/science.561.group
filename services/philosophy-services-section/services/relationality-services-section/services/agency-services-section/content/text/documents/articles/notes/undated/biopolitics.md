# biopolitics

Generated by: biopower — the population-level administration of life.

Biopolitics is the politics of populations: the concrete, policy-facing exercise of biopower over life understood as a biological mass to be measured, managed, and optimized.
Where biopower names the general fact that modern power takes living beings as its object, biopolitics names the specific domain in which that power operates — the census, the public health campaign, the management of birth and death rates, the actuarial governance of risk.
Michel Foucault developed the concept in *The History of Sexuality, Volume 1* (1976) and across his Collège de France lectures of the late 1970s, including *Security, Territory, Population* and *The Birth of Biopolitics*.

The defining move of biopolitics is the constitution of "the population" as a new kind of object.
The population is not the same as the people of sovereign theory, nor the legal subject of rights.
It is a biological collective with its own regularities — a birth rate, a mortality curve, a distribution of disease and longevity — that can be tracked statistically and acted on in the aggregate.
Once the population exists as an object of knowledge, it becomes governable in a new way: not by commanding each subject but by adjusting the conditions under which the population as a whole lives, reproduces, and dies.
Demography, epidemiology, political economy, and the "science of the state" (Statistik) are the knowledges that make this object visible and the levers that act on it.

Biopolitics supplements rather than replaces sovereign power.
The modern state keeps its capacity for spectacular violence but exercises it within a biopolitical frame: war is waged to defend the life of the population, not the honor of the sovereign.
Foucault argues that racism is the mechanism that lets a biopolitical regime still kill — it introduces a break within the biological field, marking some lives for optimization and others for exposure to death in the name of the health of the whole.
The fragment of the population that must be made to die is recast as a biological threat to the fragment that must be made to live.

The concept has been pulled in directions Foucault did not take.
Achille Mbembe argues that biopolitics, drawn largely from European history, cannot account for colony, plantation, and occupation, where sovereignty governs primarily through death; he develops *necropolitics* as the corrective.
Elizabeth Povinelli argues that biopolitics stays trapped inside the distinction between Life and Nonlife and cannot capture how late liberalism governs through the slow management of what never rises to the level of an event, developing her notion of the quasi-event and of geontopower.
Roberto Esposito rereads biopolitics through the paired logics of immunization and community — the protection of life that can tip into its negation.
In plain terms, biopolitics is the working machinery of biopower: the food-safety inspection, the screening program, the migration quota, the line on a budget that decides which clinic stays open.
It is where the abstract claim that modern power governs life becomes a set of concrete decisions about whose life, on what terms.
