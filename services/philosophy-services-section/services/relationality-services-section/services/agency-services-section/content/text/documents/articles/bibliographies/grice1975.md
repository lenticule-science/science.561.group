# Grice 1975 — "Logic and Conversation"

H. Paul Grice, "Logic and Conversation," in Peter Cole and Jerry L. Morgan (eds.), *Syntax and Semantics, Vol. 3: Speech Acts* (New York: Academic Press, 1975), pp. 41–58. Originally delivered as part of Grice's 1967 William James Lectures at Harvard.

## What it establishes

Grice introduces the distinction between *what is said* (the conventional, truth-conditional content of an utterance) and *what is implicated* (the inferences a hearer draws from the fact that the speaker said *this*, in *this way*, in *this context*). The central theoretical move is the **Cooperative Principle**: "Make your conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged."

The Cooperative Principle is decomposed into four maxims, each with sub-maxims:

- **Quantity.** Make your contribution as informative as required, but not more so.
- **Quality.** Try to make your contribution one that is true: do not say what you believe to be false; do not say that for which you lack adequate evidence.
- **Relation.** Be relevant.
- **Manner.** Be perspicuous: avoid obscurity, avoid ambiguity, be brief, be orderly.

When a speaker appears to violate a maxim while still being cooperative, the hearer infers a *conversational implicature* — additional content the speaker is communicating without saying. Grice's mechanism explains why "Some of the students passed" implicates that not all of them did, why "He drank a bottle of gin and got into his car" implicates a temporal order, and why irony, metaphor, and indirection work as they do.

## Why this corpus cites it

Grice's framework is the foundational reference for the [pragmatics](../../../glossary/concepts/pragmatics.md) concept — the study of how meaning depends on context, intention, and conversational expectations beyond what is conventionally encoded in the linguistic form. The maxims are not the last word on pragmatics (relevance theory, neo-Gricean accounts, and politeness theory all develop or contest the framework), but they remain the canonical entry point.

Grice's distinction between *what is said* and *what is implicated* also load-bears on the corpus's [register](../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/archive/references/teaching/explanations/register.md) concept: register-shifts are often the surface form of implicature-generating moves. A note's [pragmatic-register](../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/information/theories/knowledge-systems/theories/markdown-vault-knowledge-system/theories/predicated-markdown-vault-knowledge-system/concepts/pragmatic-register.md) (in the predicated-mvks subtheory) is partly a Gricean object: the register an author selects communicates implicatures about audience, expected effort, and admissible inference moves.
