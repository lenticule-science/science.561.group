# network

A network is the pattern of ties among a set of actors — people, organizations, states — treated as the primary unit of analysis rather than as a derivative of the actors' individual attributes.
Generated by: **relational-field** — the network is that field read as nodes and ties, the relational fabric made into an explicit object of study.
Its core methodological commitment is that explanation runs from relations to outcomes, not from individual traits to outcomes: whether you find a job, whether a firm survives a shock, whether an idea spreads depends less on who the actors are than on where they sit in the web of connections.

The intellectual move belongs above all to Harrison White and the structural-sociology tradition he led at Harvard in the 1960s and 70s.
White's program — developed with students including Mark Granovetter, Barry Wellman, and Ronald Breiger — treats social structure as a concrete pattern of relations, with actors' positions defined by *structural equivalence*: two actors occupy the same position when they have the same pattern of ties to others, regardless of their personal characteristics.
The unit of analysis is the configuration of ties itself.
White's later *Identity and Control* (1992) pushed this further, arguing that identities and social meanings themselves emerge from positions in networks rather than preceding them — a strong statement of the relational commitment this branch shares.

Granovetter's "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973) is the canonical demonstration that the network, not the individual, carries the explanation.
Strong ties — close friends and family — tend to know one another and to share the same information, forming dense, redundant clusters.
Weak ties — acquaintances — bridge between clusters and so carry novel information across the social structure.
Granovetter found that people more often heard about jobs through weak ties than strong ones, precisely because acquaintances reach into regions of the network one's close circle cannot.
The counterintuitive lesson — that weak connections can matter more than strong ones for certain outcomes — only becomes visible once you look at the pattern of ties rather than the qualities of the people.
Ronald Burt's work on "structural holes" extended this: actors who bridge otherwise disconnected groups gain advantage from controlling the flow between them.

The stakes, and the cautions, are political.
Reading social life as a network makes visible forms of advantage and disadvantage that attribute-based explanation misses — why opportunity clusters, how influence propagates, why some positions confer power independent of merit.
But networks are not innocent: who is included, who is excluded, and which network properties get measured and optimized are themselves questions of power, sharpened today as platform companies reorganize social life into ties legible to corporate measurement and surveillance.
Read against its neighbours, the network is the **relational-field** made analyzable, an extended application of the principle that the **relation** is the unit and that social outcomes are explained from within the pattern of relations rather than from the essences of the parties — the methodological face of this branch's **immanence**.
