# confederacy

A confederacy is a union of autonomous groups that share a common purpose and bind themselves to shared directives while retaining independent governance.
Each member group governs its own internal affairs, sets its own decision-making processes, and is free to withdraw.
The confederacy coordinates across the groups but does not command them: authority rests with the constituent groups, and the confederacy holds only what they choose to delegate upward.
Generated by: autonomy — a confederacy is collective autonomy scaled outward by coordination rather than subordination, the way self-governing groups act together without any one center gaining the power to direct them.
The word itself records this: from the Latin *confoederatio*, *con* (together) plus *foedus* (covenant, treaty) — a covenant between equals, not a government over subjects.

The structural question that defines a confederacy is where final authority sits, and it is useful to set it beside the federation.
In a federation, base assemblies of individuals send delegates who carry revocable mandates upward, and authority flows up through those delegates while the federation itself has no standing to override the assemblies.
A confederacy is looser still: its members are not assemblies of individuals but entire self-governing entities — whole peoples, nations, or communities — that choose to cooperate under shared terms.
A federation coordinates people through delegates; a confederacy coordinates groups through directives.
The clearest historical example is the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy, which bound together sovereign nations — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, and later Tuscarora — through the Great Law of Peace.
Each nation conducted its own affairs, governed its own people, and waged its own campaigns; the Great Law bound them all in shared principle, but no nation's internal governance was dictated by the confederacy.

This is also what separates a confederacy from an institution like a company.
A company has staff who bear ongoing roles, a governance mechanism that produces decisions attributable to the company as a whole, and standing as a single entity — so that "the company decided" is a coherent thing to say.
A confederacy has none of these.
It has member groups rather than staff, coordination rather than unified governance, and no single entity-status, so that "the confederacy decided" does not work the same way.
The confederacy is the agreement itself, not an actor that stands above its members.

The deep interest of the form is how it produces coordinated action without ever becoming a hierarchy.
It can commission specific undertakings — charter a venture with its own purpose, crew, and command structure — and delegate authority to whoever leads that venture for its duration, all without surrendering its own autonomy.
A chartered undertaking may run hierarchically under its commission, with someone empowered to give orders, while the confederacy that issued the commission remains a covenant between equals.
The commissioning body retains the powers that matter — to amend or revoke the charter, to relieve the leader, to dissolve the undertaking — even though it has handed over operational command.
The leader commands the venture; the confederacy commands whether there is a venture at all.
This is how autonomous groups generate concerted action at scale: not by centralizing power, but by commissioning it temporarily and keeping the standing to withdraw it.

A confederacy is thus one of the central answers to the question that runs through all the reciprocal relations — how do equals act together without one of them coming to rule the rest?
Where autonomy names a single collective governing itself, confederacy names many self-governing collectives binding into common action while each keeps its own authority intact.
It is consensus and covenant scaled up to the level of whole communities.
