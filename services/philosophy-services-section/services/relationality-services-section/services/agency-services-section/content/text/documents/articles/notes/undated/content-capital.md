# capital

Capital, in Marx's analysis, is not a thing but a *social relation* that takes the form of self-expanding value: wealth thrown into circulation in order to come back enlarged.
A pile of money or a stock of machines is not yet capital; it becomes capital only when it is deployed to command labor and return more value than was advanced.
Marx captured this in the formula of capital's movement — money is laid out to buy commodities (means of production and labor-power) which are set to work and yield commodities sold for more money than was started with — and the whole point of the arrangement is that final sum exceeding the first.
Generated by: **domination** in its accumulating mode — capital is the form social wealth takes when it is organized to grow by extracting surplus from labor.

The decisive insight is that capital presupposes a class relation.
For money to become self-expanding, there must exist a class of people who own the means of production and a class of people who own nothing but their capacity to work and must therefore sell it.
The growth of capital is the appropriation of the surplus-value those workers produce.
This is why Marx insisted that capital is not wealth in general, nor a neutral "factor of production," but a historically specific relation of domination: it exists only where labor has been separated from the means of subsistence, and it accumulates only by continuing to extract unpaid labor.
Capital that did not exploit would not be capital; it would simply be hoarded money.

It is worth distinguishing this from a second, sociological sense of the word, developed by Pierre Bourdieu, who spoke of economic, social, cultural, and symbolic *capitals* as field-specific accumulated resources that confer advantage and can be converted into one another — a credential, a network, a reputation.
Bourdieu's usage usefully widens the analysis of how advantage is stored and transmitted across generations, but it should not be confused with Marx's.
For Marx, capital is defined by the *valorization* relation — its self-expansion through the exploitation of labor — not merely by being an accumulable asset.
The two senses can be held together (economic capital in Bourdieu's sense often *is* capital in Marx's), but the critical edge of the term lives in the Marxian relation.

Capital is restless by nature, and this restlessness drives much of the rest of the cluster.
Because each unit of capital must grow or be outcompeted by units that do, capital is under constant pressure to raise the rate of surplus extraction: lengthening the working day, intensifying labor, and above all revolutionizing the labor process through machinery, the division of labor, and the reorganization of work — what Marx called the production of relative surplus-value and the real subsumption of labor.
It is equally driven to expand its frontier, converting what lies outside it — common land, public goods, knowledge, ecosystems, unwaged care — into new sites of accumulation, the ongoing dispossession that primitive-accumulation names.

As a node, capital is the form-giving center of this whole region of social theory.
It casts social wealth into the commodity form, from which exchange-value and its concealing appearances (commodity-fetishism, reification) follow; it organizes labor through the wage relation, surplus-value, and the labor process; and it generates the historically specific variants the tradition has named — industrial capital, racial capitalism, surveillance capitalism, and the rest.
To call something capital is to claim that it is not inert wealth but a relation under the imperative to grow, and that its growth comes from somewhere and someone.
