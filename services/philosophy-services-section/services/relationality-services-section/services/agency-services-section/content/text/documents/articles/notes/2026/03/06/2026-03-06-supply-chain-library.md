A supply chain is the sequence of entities, processes, and relationships through which raw materials become finished products and reach the end customer.
It includes suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, and retailers — each link adding transformation, transportation, or transaction costs.

For a small business, supply chain management means choosing suppliers, negotiating terms, managing inventory, and ensuring that the inputs needed for daily operations arrive reliably, at acceptable quality, and at a cost that preserves margins.
A restaurant's supply chain includes food distributors, local farms, beverage suppliers, and equipment vendors.
Disruptions at any link — a distributor's warehouse floods, a crop fails, a shipping route closes — cascade into operational problems: menu items become unavailable, costs spike, or quality drops.

In [American business practice](../../../../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/content/individual/content/agent/endeavoring/content/endeavor/organizing/content/organization/resourcing/content/resource/recording/content/record/supplying/content/supply-chain/schools/american-business-practices/index.md), supply chain management is increasingly treated as a discipline of optimization — minimizing inventory holding costs through just-in-time ordering, diversifying suppliers to reduce dependency, and using [POS](../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/business-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/point-of-sale.md) data to forecast demand and calibrate purchasing.
This optimization-oriented framing treats suppliers as interchangeable inputs to be managed for cost and reliability.
It contrasts with relational approaches to provisioning — where long-term relationships with specific producers carry obligations of loyalty, fair pricing, and mutual support that may not optimize for cost but provide resilience and trust.

[Risk mitigation](../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/sociology-science-services-section/services/work-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/risk-mitigation.md) in supply chain management addresses the fragility that optimization introduces: just-in-time systems save on inventory costs but leave no buffer when disruptions occur.
The tension between efficiency and resilience is a recurring structural problem in supply chain design.
