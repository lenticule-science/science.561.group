# racial-capitalism

Racial capitalism is the argument that capitalism did not emerge as a racially neutral economic system that only later incorporated racial hierarchy; capital accumulation was, from its origins, structured through racial differentiation — the production of categorical distinctions among populations in order to cheapen labor, justify dispossession, and sort people into hierarchies of human value.
Generated by: **capital** — racial capitalism is not a variant of capital but a claim about what capital has always been, that accumulation works by differentiating and devaluing populations.

The term and its developed argument come from Cedric Robinson's *Black Marxism* (1983).
Robinson traced racialism back into feudal Europe, where intra-European racial logics — the racialization of the Irish, the Slavs, the Roma, the Jews — long predated the colonial encounter.
Capitalism, on his account, inherited and extended these logics rather than inventing race from scratch or grafting it on afterward.
The Atlantic slave trade did not introduce race into an otherwise neutral economy; it scaled up a logic of categorical differentiation already present in European social organization.
From this Robinson drew his most consequential challenge to orthodox Marxism: its treatment of race as superstructural — as ideology layered over a class base — misdescribes the system.
Race was not a distortion of capitalism but a condition of its possibility, woven into the production of the very class relations Marxism analyzes.

Ruth Wilson Gilmore extended the concept toward the geography of life and death.
In *Golden Gulag* (2007) and later work she shows racial capitalism operating through what she calls *organized abandonment*: the uneven distribution of life chances across racialized geographies, the withdrawal of investment and protection from some places and people while others are secured.
Her widely cited definition of racism — the state-sanctioned or extralegal production and exploitation of group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death — is in effect a definition of racial capitalism's output.
It relocates the harm from attitudes and individual prejudice to the political and economic organization of differential mortality: who lives shorter lives, in which neighborhoods, under what conditions, and to whose benefit.

A concrete way to see the argument is to follow how capital has repeatedly relied on populations marked as available for super-exploitation or disposal.
Enslaved labor built the plantation wealth that financed industrialization; colonized labor and land were extracted under regimes that defined the colonized as less than fully human; "guest" and migrant workers are admitted precisely because their racialized and legal status lets employers pay them less and discard them more easily; and the carceral system Gilmore studies converts surplus populations and surplus land into prisons.
In each case the differentiation is not incidental to the accumulation — it is the means by which labor is cheapened and dispossession is legitimated.

The stakes of the concept are methodological as much as moral.
Racial capitalism refuses the separation of economic analysis from racial analysis: any account of accumulation that treats race as incidental will misidentify how value is produced, distributed, and destroyed.
It sits alongside the tradition's other extensions of Marx — social-reproduction theory's recovery of unwaged gendered labor, decolonial analysis of ongoing dispossession — as an insistence that the wage relation between a generic capitalist and a generic worker is an abstraction that hides the racial and colonial structuring through which real capital has always operated.
To analyze capital without race, on this view, is to analyze a system that has never existed.
