# coercion

Coercion is the use or credible threat of force, deprivation, or punishment to compel behavior — power exercised as compulsion rather than as recognized right.
Generated by: coercion is power in its forcing mode, the modality that secures compliance by making the alternative unbearable.
It is the line that separates domination from mere disagreement and compulsory association from voluntary: a relationship sustained by coercion is one you cannot leave or refuse without suffering consequences deliberately imposed by the other party.
Coercion is what authority is held *against* — the form power takes when obedience flows from fear of harm rather than from any belief that the command is rightful.

Its most visible forms are direct: police violence, imprisonment, military force, the gun held to the head.
But theorists of coercion have long stressed that its most effective operation is structural rather than spectacular.
Wage labour is coercive not because the employer threatens violence but because the worker's access to food, shelter, and survival depends on selling labour to someone who controls the means of production.
The worker is formally "free" to quit — and free to starve.
This structural coercion is what allows a system to compel without ever raising a hand: the threat is built into the arrangement of material life, so that the costly alternative to compliance is destitution rather than a beating.

The state combines structural and direct coercion in a distinctive way.
Its laws are backed by police, courts, and prisons — the credible promise that disobedience will be met with force — and Weber's famous definition makes the *monopoly* of legitimate physical force within a territory the defining feature of the state itself.
But the state also organizes everyday existence so that operating outside its framework grows progressively harder: one needs its documents to work, its currency to transact, its infrastructure to move.
Settler colonialism made this layering explicit.
Indigenous peoples were subjected not only to direct violence but to the destruction of their subsistence systems, the delegitimation of their governance, and the enclosure of their land — producing a dependence on colonial institutions that functions as ongoing coercion long after the initial conquest.

A crucial distinction, central to anarchist and broader critical thought, separates coercion from mere consequence.
If you refuse to cooperate with your neighbours and they decline to cooperate with you in return, that is a consequence — the ordinary result of your choices within a relation of equals.
If you refuse to obey your employer and they cut off your wages and so your means of life, that is coercion — the exercise of structurally unequal power to compel compliance.
The difference lies in whether the harm is imposed from a hierarchical position or arises naturally from the relationship itself, and the difficulty of drawing this line in particular cases is exactly where arguments about exploitation, consent, and freedom are fought.

Within this cluster coercion is the raw force that the state claims to monopolize and that law mobilizes and channels.
Law without coercion is advice; sanction is coercion administered through legal form; and the whole apparatus of authority and legitimacy can be read as an attempt to convert naked coercion into something obeyed willingly — to get compliance without the constant, costly, destabilizing application of force.
