The **rhetorical situation** is the foundational formal model of the conditions that call a discourse into being and constrain what counts as a fitting response, due to Lloyd Bitzer (1968, "The Rhetorical Situation," *Philosophy and Rhetoric*). The model formalises the situation as a triple of *exigence*, *audience*, and *constraints* that jointly determine which discourses are *fitting* — possible, appropriate, and effective — in the situation.

## Definition

A **rhetorical situation** is a tuple $S = (X, A, C)$ where:

- $X$ is an **exigence**: an imperfection marked by urgency — a defect, an obstacle, an absence, a misunderstanding — that calls for a response and is in principle modifiable by discourse. An exigence is *rhetorical* (rather than purely material) iff a discourse could change it.
- $A$ is an **audience**: not the empirical receivers of the discourse, but those persons who are capable of being influenced by the discourse and who can mediate the change the exigence calls for. An audience must be both addressable by the discourse and effectual on the exigence.
- $C$ is a set of **constraints**: persons, events, objects, and relations that have the power to influence both the audience's response and the rhetor's available means. Constraints partition into those *given* (the audience's beliefs, the available facts, the historical context) and those *contributed* by the rhetor (the rhetor's character, evidence, style).

A discourse $D$ is **fitting** to $S$ iff $D$ engages the exigence $X$ in a way that can move the audience $A$ within the constraints $C$. Fittingness is the model's normative notion: a fitting discourse is what the situation calls for; an unfitting discourse misreads at least one of the three components.

## Bitzer's central thesis

Bitzer's claim is that **rhetorical discourse is response, not invention**: the situation precedes and calls forth the discourse, rather than the rhetor freely choosing what to say. The model thus treats discourse as situated action whose appropriateness is judged against external conditions, not against the rhetor's internal aims.

This is the load-bearing structural commitment: the model is *situational* rather than *intentional*. A discourse can be technically excellent (well-argued, beautifully phrased) yet *unfitting* if it misreads the exigence or addresses the wrong audience or violates the constraints; conversely, a humble discourse can be *fitting* if it accurately responds to a real exigence in terms its audience can use.

## The Vatz counter-position

Richard Vatz (1973, "The Myth of the Rhetorical Situation") argued the converse: situations do not call discourses into being; rather, rhetors *create* situations by selecting which exigencies to highlight and which audiences to address. On Vatz's view the model's causal direction is reversed.

The contemporary settlement (after Edbauer 2005 and the *rhetorical ecologies* literature) is that both the Bitzerian and Vatzian moments are real: situations partly constrain discourses (Bitzer), and discourses partly constitute the situations they respond to (Vatz). The triple $S = (X, A, C)$ remains the unit of analysis; what is contested is who fixes the triple.

## Why this object load-bears

Rhetorical situation is the foundational formal model the [audience](../concepts/audience.md) concept is built around in the rhetorical tradition. Without it, [audience](../concepts/audience.md) cites Bitzer but has no co-located triple-structure for downstream theories to load on. With it, theories that need a more structured account of *which* audience is being addressed (rather than just *that* an audience exists) can cite the model directly.

The model also load-bears on [genre](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/archive/code/structures/teaching/explanations/genre.md) (genres are conventionalised responses to recurrent rhetorical situations — Miller 1984's "genre as social action" rests on the Bitzerian frame) and on [register](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/communicating/content/communication/archive/code/structures/teaching/explanations/register.md) (a register is in part a constraint $c \in C$ inherited from the situation).
