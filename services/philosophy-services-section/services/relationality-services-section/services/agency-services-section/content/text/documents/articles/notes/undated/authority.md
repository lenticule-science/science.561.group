# authority

Authority is the recognized right to command — power that is obeyed not merely because it is feared but because those who obey hold the command to be rightful.
Generated by: authority arises when power is composed with legitimacy; it is the form domination takes once it is acknowledged rather than simply imposed.
The classic statement is Max Weber's: authority (*Herrschaft*) is the probability that a command will be obeyed by a given group of people, where obedience flows from a belief in the validity of the order itself.
A person with a gun can compel; an authority commands, and the difference lies not in the means of enforcement but in the standing of the one who issues the order.

Weber's enduring contribution was to sort the grounds on which such validity is believed into three pure types. *Traditional* authority is obeyed because "it has always been done this way" — its warrant is custom, inheritance, sanctified precedent, and the ruler may not invent commands at will without forfeiting the claim. *Charismatic* authority is obeyed because the figure is held to possess extraordinary gifts — holiness, heroism, vision — and is therefore inherently unstable, since it collapses when the gift fails or the bearer dies, and must be "routinized" into one of the other forms to endure. *Rational-legal* authority is obeyed because the command issues from an officeholder acting within written, impersonal rules; here authority is vested not in a person but in the office, and acting outside the rules is not a strong command but a usurpation.
These three, Weber argued, exhaust the ways legitimacy is sustained, and most durable regimes braid all three together.

Other traditions sharpen what Weber leaves sociological.
Hannah Arendt (*Between Past and Future*, 1961) insists authority is neither persuasion nor coercion: persuasion treats the other as an equal and works through their judgment, coercion overrides their will by force, but authority is obeyed *without* argument and *without* force, on the strength of a recognized standing that is prior to any assessment of whether the command is wise.
On this view the modern "crisis of authority" is precisely that authority increasingly has to either explain itself (collapsing into persuasion) or enforce itself (collapsing into coercion) — and the need to do either is already a sign that genuine authority has been lost.
Joseph Raz (*The Morality of Freedom*, 1986) offers the opposite emphasis: authority is justified only when following its directives actually serves the reasons the subject already has better than their own judgment would — a doctor over medical questions, say — which makes legitimate authority conditional and revocable rather than absolute.

The stakes of the concept lie in this gap between obedience-from-belief and obedience-from-fear.
A regime can command vast power and still lack authority if subjects comply only to avoid punishment; conversely a court may wield little direct force yet hold great authority through the standing to issue binding interpretations.
Authority is always scope-bound: a teacher's runs in the classroom, a judge's over questions of law, a council's within its territory.
No authority is unlimited, and the bounds are not natural facts but creations of the very normative order that grants the standing.

Authority sits at the centre of this cluster of concepts.
It is the impersonal slot of the office that outlasts any holder, the systematised machinery of bureaucracy, the binding declaration of law, and the foundational, unanswerable claim of sovereignty.
It is held apart from coercion (force without recognised right), from influence (which works through a free judgment that may reject it), and from mere competence (which must persuade rather than command).
And it underwrites the state, whose claim to a monopoly on rightful force is nothing other than a claim to ultimate authority over a territory and its people.
