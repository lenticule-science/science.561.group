# subject-formation

Subject-formation is the process by which persons are made into subjects — not born as fully formed individuals who then enter society, but produced *as* the kind of person their social order requires, through the practices, institutions, and discourses that act on them.
The point is that the individual who feels most spontaneously themselves is, on this analysis, the most thoroughly made.
Generated by: **social-reproduction** — an order reproduces itself above all by forming the subjects who will go on to enact it, so subject-formation is reproduction working at the level of the person.

Louis Althusser gave the concept its sharpest early statement with *interpellation*.
In his essay on ideological state apparatuses, ideology works by "hailing" individuals into subject-positions — his image is a policeman calling "Hey, you there!" in the street, and the person who turns around, recognizing themselves as the one addressed, becoming a subject in the act of turning.
The hail does not impose a role from outside onto a pre-existing self; it constitutes the self in the recognition.
Ideology, for Althusser, is therefore not false belief held by an already-formed mind but a *lived relation* to real conditions, installed through material institutions — school, family, church, media — that produce people who recognize themselves in the positions the social order needs filled.
We do not believe ideology despite our experience; we have the experience we do because we have been formed as the subjects who would have it.

Michel Foucault developed a parallel but distinct account under the heading of *subjectification*.
For Foucault the subject is produced through the workings of power and knowledge together — through classification, examination, and normalization.
The school does not receive a student and then teach them; it produces "the student" through enrollment, testing, grading, and routine, and the student's self-understanding ("I'm good at math," "I'm a troublemaker") is shaped by the categories the institution applies.
The prison produces "the delinquent," the clinic "the patient," each a type of person with a profile, a record, a predicted trajectory.
Crucially, Foucault insists this operates on two axes at once: categorization by others, and self-formation — the ways people come to understand and work on themselves through the categories institutions supply.
The person who lives through a psychiatric diagnosis, a credit score, or an identity category is participating in their own subjectification.
He summarized his work's concern as "not power, but the subject."

Both thinkers refuse the language of false consciousness, and this is the conceptual core they share.
The subject produced through these processes is real, not a mask over a true self underneath.
There is no undistorted person waiting to be liberated from formation, because the person is *what formation produces*.
Where Althusser stresses recognition and the moment of being hailed, Foucault stresses the long disciplinary production of a documented, self-monitoring individual; but for both, subjectivity is an effect, contingent and historical, not a given.
This is also why subject-formation is not the same as oppression: one can be formed as "capable," "healthy," or "normal," and those are subject-positions too.
The claim is about the *origin* of the categories through which people are known and know themselves — that they are products of social practice, not descriptions of a natural human kind.

The completion of subject-formation is what makes it matter for reproduction: a fully formed subject is one who reproduces the order without being compelled to.
Having internalized the categories and norms of their position, they go on to enact, transmit, and police those same categories — answering the hail, keeping the record, applying the standard to themselves and others.
This is the link Foucault drives at when he says subjects are not merely subjected but produced as productive participants in their own subjection: power that has to coerce is weak, while power that has formed the subject who acts on its behalf needs no further coercion.
It is also where subject-formation opens onto its limit — for if the subject is made rather than given, then the categories that made them are historical, contestable, and in principle remakeable, which is the small opening every account of social change has to work through.
