# Audience

The **audience** of a communicative artifact is the receiver (or class of receivers) the artifact is designed for, and whose interpretive resources the artifact's encoding presupposes. Audience is *constitutive* of the artifact's communicative meaning, not merely a downstream pragmatic concern: a message that is correct for one audience is unintelligible or misleading for another, and the discipline of audience-design is what determines which is which.

## What "designing for an audience" commits to

A communicatively responsible artifact commits to (and a contributor must be able to name):

1. **Who the audience is.** The recipient class — by field, by expertise level, by role, by epistemic situation. Often plural, often stratified: a paper has primary, secondary, and tertiary audiences with different licensed interpretations.
2. **What the audience is presumed to know.** The shared common ground (Clark 1996) — definitions, prior results, conventions, terminology — the artifact does not redefine.
3. **What interpretive work the audience is licensed to do.** The closures the artifact requires the audience to perform (after the [humanities-side audience](audience.md) framing) and the recoveries the audience is presumed competent at (rederiving omitted steps, recognising standard moves, locating cited sources).
4. **What pragmatic conventions hold.** The Gricean and politeness defaults the audience reads under (cooperation, relevance, manner, quality, quantity).

## Why audience is its own anchor

Audience cannot be derived from the message: the same string of symbols is one artifact for graduate students in algebraic geometry and a different artifact for first-year undergraduates. The discipline of communication insists that audience be a *first-class commitment* of every artifact, declared rather than implicit, because confusion about audience is the largest source of artifact-level miscommunication.

In linguistics this is *audience design* (Bell 1984): speakers shift register and lexis based on whom they are addressing, including absent third parties they imagine as overhearers. In rhetoric it is part of the *rhetorical situation* (Bitzer 1968): the audience together with the exigence and the constraints determines what counts as a fitting response. In conversation analysis it is *recipient design* (Sacks–Schegloff–Jefferson): turn construction is calibrated to the addressed recipient.
