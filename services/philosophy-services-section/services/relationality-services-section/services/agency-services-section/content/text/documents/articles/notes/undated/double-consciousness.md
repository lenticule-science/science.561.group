# double-consciousness

Double-consciousness is W.E.B. Du Bois's name for the divided self-awareness imposed on Black Americans by life under the color-line.
In the famous passage from *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), he describes it as "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity."
It is the condition, he writes, of "two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals in one dark body" — of feeling oneself to be both an American and a Black person in a society that treats the two as incompatible.
Generated by: race — double-consciousness is the standpoint that racial domination forces upon those it subordinates.

The concept has both a psychological and a structural meaning, and this library emphasizes the structural one.
Psychologically, double-consciousness is the painful experience of always perceiving oneself partly through the hostile gaze of the dominant society, of never being able to take one's own selfhood as simply given because it is constantly being defined from outside.
Structurally — and this is the reading the library takes — double-consciousness names the epistemic condition of those whose social existence is constituted from the outside by a hostile gaze, and the distinctive knowledge that condition produces.
To be forced to see oneself through another's eyes is also to see that other's eyes, and the whole apparatus behind them, with a clarity unavailable to those who have never had to.
The subordinated standpoint is a wounding, but it is also a vantage.

This second, structural reading is what later traditions develop.
Du Bois paired double-consciousness with the image of the veil — the partition that hangs between Black and white America, through which the Black American can see out but the white American does not bother to see in.
Living behind the veil produces what Du Bois called "second sight": the ability to see the dominant society both as it presents itself and as it actually operates, an angle of vision the dominant cannot achieve about themselves.
Black feminist standpoint epistemology, in Patricia Hill Collins's work, generalizes this insight: those positioned at the bottom of intersecting hierarchies are forced to understand both their own situation and the workings of the system that subordinates them, and that double knowledge is a resource for analysis, not merely a burden.

Within this library double-consciousness is the inner counterpart to the color-line.
Where the color-line names the division as it cuts through the world, double-consciousness names what that division does to the self of the person living on its subordinated side.
It is one of the two foundational concepts the library draws from Du Bois, and it grounds a larger claim that runs through the analysis of domination: that the standpoint of the dominated is not a deficient or partial view to be corrected by the dominant's, but often the more complete one — the place from which the structure of domination becomes most fully visible.
