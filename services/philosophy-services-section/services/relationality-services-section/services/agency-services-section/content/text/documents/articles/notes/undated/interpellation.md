# interpellation

Interpellation is Louis Althusser's name for the process by which ideology recruits individuals as subjects — the "hailing" through which a person is called into a social position and, in answering the call, becomes the kind of subject the social order requires.
Althusser introduces it in his 1970 essay "Ideology and Ideological State Apparatuses," and it has become one of the foundational concepts of twentieth-century theories of the subject.
Generated by: **subject-formation** — interpellation is the operation by which a subject-position is installed in a person, the act through which an individual is constituted as a subject by being addressed.

Althusser's illustration is famous.
He imagines a police officer calling out in the street, "Hey, you there!"
The person who turns around at the call has, in that very turn, become the one who was addressed — has recognized themselves in the hailing and thereby taken up the position offered.
The turn is the recognition; the recognition is the subjection.
Althusser's point is that there is no neutral individual who exists first and is then addressed; the address is what constitutes the subject as a subject.
Ideology, on this account, "hails" or "interpellates" individuals continually, and the individual is constituted in the moment of response.
This is why Althusser says ideology has no outside for those caught within it: we are always already subjects, always already turning around, because the hailing is the medium in which social existence is conducted.

Two features of the concept are essential.
First, interpellation operates *materially*, through what Althusser calls ideological state apparatuses — schools, families, churches, media, legal and political institutions — rather than as a free-floating set of ideas.
These apparatuses do the hailing: the school addresses the child as a pupil, the law addresses the person as a citizen, the market addresses them as a consumer, and in answering each address the individual takes up the corresponding subject-position together with its norms and obligations.
Ideology, for Althusser, is therefore not "false consciousness" but a lived relation — people do not hold ideology as a set of mistaken beliefs *despite* their experience; they live it *through* their experience, recognizing themselves in the positions the social order offers.
Second, interpellation can *fail*.
A person may be hailed and not turn — may not recognize themselves in the address, may refuse the position, or may take it up only ambivalently.
Misrecognition, refusal, and partial identification are all possible, and the gap they open is where the politics of the concept lives.

Interpellation does not work in a single decisive event.
Subject-positions are sedimented through repetition: the individual is hailed again and again, and the position settles into place through accumulated address rather than one founding act.
Judith Butler's account of *performativity* develops exactly this point — the subject is constituted through the iterated citation of norms, not by a single moment of recognition, which is also why the repetition that installs a position carries the possibility of its subversion, since each citation can be turned.
The subject, once formed, no longer needs to be hailed anew; they occupy the position and reproduce its norms on their own, which is precisely what makes subject-formation a mechanism of social reproduction.

For a graduate student, interpellation is the hinge between ideology and the self.
It explains how a social order reproduces itself not only through institutions and force but by producing people who recognize themselves in the very roles the order needs filled, and who then sustain those roles as their own identity.
In contemporary conditions the concept extends to the *platform*, which hails individuals not through a single dramatic encounter but through the continuous, ambient, algorithmic delivery of content that models inhabitable positions — the wellness consumer, the engaged citizen, the growth-minded worker — so that recognition drifts into place through accumulated exposure rather than arriving in one decisive turn.
The mechanism is Althusser's; the scale and the medium are new.
