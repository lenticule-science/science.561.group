# possessive-individual

Generated by: subject — the liberal specification of the subject: a subject that owns itself as property.

The possessive individual is the subject-form at the heart of liberal political theory: the individual imagined as the sole proprietor of their own person and capacities, owing nothing to society for them.
The phrase comes from C.B. Macpherson's *The Political Theory of Possessive Individualism: Hobbes to Locke* (1962), which traced how seventeenth-century English political thought came to conceive the human being as essentially an owner — and, first of all, an owner of themselves.
On this conception a person's faculties, labor, and capacities are not gifts of a community or obligations within a web of relations but private property, held by the individual the way one holds a thing, to be used, withheld, or sold at will.

Macpherson's argument is that this self-conception is not a neutral description of human nature but a historically specific assumption that liberal theory smuggles in as its starting point.
He reads Hobbes and Locke as the decisive figures.
Hobbes (1651) imagines individuals in a state of nature prior to the social contract, so that political society arises *because* there were separate individuals first; the relations come after the parties to them.
Locke (1690) treats property as a fact about persons that political society exists to defend, grounding it in the claim that a person owns their own body and therefore the labor of that body and whatever that labor mixes itself with.
In both, the individual is made ontologically prior to the relations they participate in.
Macpherson names the form of selfhood this lineage produces possessive individualism: the person owns themselves and their powers as property, and political life is built outward from that ownership.

The political stakes are considerable, because so much of liberal order rests on this premise.
If the individual is a self-owner whose capacities are private property, then the labor market appears natural — selling one's capacity to work is simply disposing of what one owns — and the wage relation looks like a free exchange between proprietors rather than a structured inequality.
Rights are conceived as protections of what the individual already possesses against the encroachments of others and of the state.
Society becomes, on this picture, an association of proprietors who enter relations only to secure holdings that predate the relations.
Macpherson's critique is that this naturalizes a particular market society by reading its assumptions back into the human being as such, and that it obscures the dependence and obligation that actually constitute persons.

The possessive individual is the liberal specification of the Foucauldian subject: a subject produced by power and discourse, here produced in the specific mode of imagining itself an unencumbered self-owner.
It is the premise from which liberalism builds, and for that reason a frequent target of critique.
Theorists of relational and dependent personhood — including Judith Butler's account of the body as exposed and constituted through others, the abandonment and precarity literatures that show how thoroughly survival depends on social infrastructure, and broader critiques of substance ontology that deny the self-contained individual any priority over its relations — all press against the possessive individual's founding assumption.
To name it as a subject-form, rather than as a fact about human beings, is already to open the question of what politics would look like if persons were understood as constituted by their relations rather than as owners who enter relations from outside.
