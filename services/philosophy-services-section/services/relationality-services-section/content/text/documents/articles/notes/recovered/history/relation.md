# relation

A relation, in the social sense this entry develops, is a connection that does not merely link
two pre-existing parties but *constitutes* them — makes them, in part, what they are. To stand
as parent is to stand in relation to a child; to be an employer is to be in relation to
employees; to be a citizen is to be in relation to a state and to fellow citizens. Remove the
relation and you do not have the same party minus a link; you have something that is no longer
that party at all. Generated by: **relating** — a relation is what the ongoing activity of
relating settles into when it holds as a standing connection.

The sociological tradition that takes this seriously begins with Georg Simmel, who insisted
that society *is* the web of ongoing interactions among people — "sociation" — rather than a
substance lying behind them. For Simmel the proper object of sociology is not individuals and
not a reified "society" above them, but the forms of relation themselves: exchange, conflict,
domination, the dyad and the triad, the stranger. A dyad and a triad are not the same group
plus one person; adding a third party transforms the relation entirely, creating the
possibility of the mediator, the coalition, and the divide-and-rule. The relation is the unit,
and its form changes what the parties can be and do.

The philosophical backing for treating relations as constitutive rather than incidental comes
from the doctrine of *internal relations*, most fully developed by Alfred North Whitehead. On
this view an entity is constituted by its relations to other entities — Whitehead's term is
"prehension," the entity's grasp of and by everything it is related to. A person is not an
independently existing being who *happens* to be related to parents, language, and community;
the person is constituted through those relations, and severing them does not leave a
self-sufficient core but an abstraction. The same intuition appears in the Buddhist philosopher
Nāgārjuna's dependent origination: nothing has intrinsic self-nature; things exist only through
their relations to other things. "When this is, that is." A subject exists only in relation to
objects, a part only in relation to a whole.

In contemporary sociology this commitment is sharpened by Charles Tilly's *relational realism*:
the claim that the basic stuff of social life is transactions and ties, not individuals with
fixed attributes and not free-floating social wholes. Tilly explained durable inequality not by
the traits of the disadvantaged but by relational mechanisms — exploitation, opportunity
hoarding — that operate *between* categories of people. The methodological payoff, which the
neighbouring **network** entry develops, is that explanation runs from relations to outcomes
rather than from individual attributes to outcomes. The stakes are large: if relations are
constitutive, then identities, capacities, and inequalities are not properties people carry
into their interactions but products of the interactions themselves — which means they are
**contingent**, alterable, and political. To take relation as primary is to commit, with
**immanence**, to explaining social forms from within the relations that make them up rather
than by appeal to fixed essences standing behind the parties.
