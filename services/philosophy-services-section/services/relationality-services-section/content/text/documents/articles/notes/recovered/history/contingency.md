# contingency

Contingency is the condition of existing without necessity: something exists, but it could have
been otherwise. A contingent arrangement is not random — it has specific, traceable causes — but
those causes were not inevitable, and different causes would have produced a different
arrangement. The English language is contingent; the borders of a nation are contingent; the way
schools, prisons, hospitals, and gender are organized is contingent. Each came about through a
particular history of events, decisions, and struggles that could have gone differently.
Generated by: **relating** — social arrangements are settlements reached within a web of
relations, and a settlement reached one way could have settled another.

The concept is the most powerful tool that critical social thought possesses, because most of
what people treat as natural and inevitable is in fact contingent. The decisive distinction is
between "this is how things are" and "this is how things happen to be, given what happened." The
first closes inquiry; the second opens it. Arrangements that present themselves as necessary —
as rooted in human nature, biology, or the order of things — are far harder to question than
arrangements understood as outcomes of a particular, alterable history. To demonstrate the
contingency of an arrangement is therefore to reopen the possibility that it could be otherwise,
which is the space in which political imagination operates. This is why contingency is described
here as load-bearing: the move from "necessary" to "could-have-been-otherwise" is the hinge on
which the possibility of change turns.

The exemplary method for making contingency visible is Michel Foucault's *genealogy*. A
genealogical analysis takes something that presents itself as timeless and necessary — the
prison, sexuality, madness, the clinical gaze — and traces the specific, identifiable history
that produced it: the institutions, techniques, and power relations through which it was
assembled. The point is not principally that the arrangement is bad (though it may be) but that
it is not the only possible arrangement, because the causes that produced it were themselves
contingent. Genealogy thereby *denaturalizes* its object, stripping away the appearance of
inevitability and showing the seams. The same denaturalizing impulse runs through the
sociological study of how categories like race, the family, or the normal body were historically
constructed rather than discovered.

The stakes are sharply political, and they define contingency's relation to its neighbours. The
opposite operation — call it ontologization or naturalization — takes a contingent arrangement
and hardens it into a claim about what *must* exist, closing the space that contingency keeps
open. **Volksgeist**, in the nationalism branch, is precisely this move: a historically produced
people presented as a timeless essence. To insist on contingency is to refuse that hardening and
to keep arrangements answerable to the histories that made them. It pairs directly with
**immanence** (explaining forms from within their relations rather than by a transcendent
necessity) and with **relation** (parties are constituted in relations that could have been
configured differently): if the social is made in relations, then it is made, and what is made
can be remade.
