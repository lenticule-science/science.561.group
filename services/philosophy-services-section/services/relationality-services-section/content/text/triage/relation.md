**Note on grounding (2026-04-05):** The 561 Group's relationality is rooted in Lakota epistemology, not in the Western tradition surveyed below. See [Lakota philosophy](./_index.md) and the [relationality domain](./_index.md). In Lakota thought (*mitákuye oyás'iŋ* — "all my relatives"), relating is prior to things: what exists are acts of relating sustained in kinship with land, ancestors, other-than-human beings, and ceremony. The Western tradition below — Aristotle, Russell, Bradley, Whitehead, James, Peirce — is recorded as **contrast material**, useful for triangulating how European metaphysics arrived at neighbouring but distinct positions. It is not the foundation. The foundation is relational kinship epistemology; the Western survey is how that foundation looks from outside.

A relation is a connection between [things](./thing.md). Parent-of, taller-than, inside, before, causes — all relations. A relation is what holds between two or more things. It is not a [thing](./thing.md) in the same way a rock is a thing. A relation depends on the things it connects. Remove one of the things, and the relation dissolves. But relations are real. "Parent of" is a real connection between a parent and a child. "Taller than" is a real connection between two objects of different heights.

Aristotle called these relatives (*pros ti*, "said of something in relation to something else"). In *Categories* Chapter 7, he writes: "A relative is that which is said to be what it is of or than something else." A parent is a parent OF a child. A master is a master OF a slave. A mountain is taller THAN a hill. The defining feature is that you cannot understand what the relative IS without reference to something else. A parent cannot be conceived except in relation to a child. This makes relations depend on their terms — but it does not make them unreal.

Aristotle's relatives raise a question that philosophers have grappled with ever since: are relations reducible to properties of the things they connect, or are they fundamental features of reality in their own right? If you say "A is taller than B," are you describing a property of A (A's height) and a property of B (B's height), and inferring the relation? Or is "taller than" itself a real fact — a fact about A-and-B together, not about either one separately?

Bertrand Russell (1903) argued that relations are irreducible. You cannot explain "A is taller than B" by listing A's properties and B's properties separately. "Taller than" is not something you can derive from A alone or from B alone. It is a fact about A and B *together*. Russell wrote: "Relations are not adjectives, and therefore cannot be subsumed under the category of qualities." A relation is a kind of thing — but not a substance or an individual. It is something that connects individuals.

This creates a problem: if a relation connects A and B, what connects the relation to A and B? Must there be a further relation doing the binding? F. H. Bradley (1893) argued that if relations are things, this leads to infinite regress. To avoid the regress, Bradley concluded that relations are not real — they are appearances, projections of how our minds organize experience. Russell rejected this. Relations are real and primitive. They do not require further relations to bind them to their terms. The relation just *is* the connection; that binding is constitutive.

Not all relations are the same. Some relations depend essentially on the nature of their terms. "A is larger than B" depends on A's size and B's size — the relation is grounded in the intrinsic properties of what it connects. Other relations seem more arbitrary. "A is 10 miles from B" depends on where A and B happen to be located, and the location could change without changing what A or B fundamentally are. Russell distinguished internal relations (dependent on the nature of their terms) from external relations (contingent on accidents of position or circumstance). But even this distinction is unclear. It is difficult to say which relations are essential and which are accidental.

There is another way to think about relations: not as connections between pre-existing things, but as constitutive — as what *makes* the things what they are. Alfred North Whitehead (1929) argued in *Process and Reality* that all relations are internal in this deeper sense. Nothing exists in isolation. Each actual entity — each genuine individual — is constituted by its relations to other entities. He called these relations "prehensions" (grasps). An entity *is* its network of prehensions. A person is not an independently existing being who *happens to be* related to parents, community, and the world. The person *is* constituted by her prehensions — her grasp of her parents' presence, her appropriation of cultural meanings, her responsive engagement with others. Sever these relations, and you do not have a person in isolation. You have an abstraction, a remainder that lacks the reality of a living individual.

William James (1912) reached a similar conclusion from a different angle. In *Essays in Radical Empiricism*, he argued that relations are directly experienced, not inferred. When you see one person stand next to another, you do not first observe two isolated humans and then infer the relation "beside." You perceive the relation directly. The "and" between two things is as real as the things themselves. Relations are immediate facts about the world.

Charles Sanders Peirce distinguished dyadic relations (relations between two things) from triadic relations (relations among three things). "A is taller than B" is dyadic — reducible to comparing heights. But "A is between B and C" is triadic — A's position as the middle term cannot be reduced to dyadic relations. You cannot decompose "A is between B and C" into pairs without losing what makes the relation what it is. Peirce called this irreducible three-part structure "Thirdness" — mediation, continuity. It appears wherever meaning or interpretation is involved. In predication, "Socrates is wise" is not a dyadic fact. It involves a subject (Socrates), a predicate (wise), and what the predicate interprets in relation to the subject. Remove any of the three parts, and you lose the meaning.

The Buddhist philosopher Nāgārjuna (c. 150 CE) argued in the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way) that nothing has intrinsic self-nature (*svabhāva*, literally "own-being"). Everything exists only through dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) — through relations to other things. "When this is, that is; when this is not, that is not." A subject only exists in relation to objects, just as objects only exist in relation to subjects. A part is only a part in relation to a whole. The distinction between any two things is not given intrinsically but emerges through dependent origination. This is not mysticism. It is clarity about the structure of how things depend on each other. Nothing stands alone. Everything that is stands in a network of relations, and it is through that network that anything becomes what it is.

A **relation** in the [relationality](../_index.md) framework is the structural form a [relating](relating.md) produces when it stabilizes. Where relating is the activity, the relation is what relating *leaves behind*: a directed triadic shape with [subject](subject.md), predicate, and [object](object.md), recording that the relating has happened and is now part of the system of relations.

A relation is not a connection between pre-existing entities. It is the structural shape that constitutes the entities-as-related, present in the system once the relating has gone through. Before the relating: no entities, no relation. After the relating: a directed triple in which the entities are the relata and the predicate is what holds between them.

## Relation and relating

The distinction between a relation and relating is grammatical first and structural second. *Relating* is the verb form — the activity. *Relation* is the noun form — the standing structural fact that the activity produces. They name the same primitive at two different aspects:

- **Relating** is the active aspect: the directed act constituting its terms, ongoing or completed.
- **A relation** is the structural aspect: the directed triple that stands once the relating has occurred, with definite subject, predicate, and object positions.

In ordinary speech, *relation* sometimes means a connection between independently existing things — a friendship between two people who already exist, a contract between two parties already constituted. The relationality framework's reading is more austere: a relation is what the relating produces, not a link added to pre-existing relata. The relata are products of the relating, not its inputs.

## Relations are directed

Every relation in the framework is *directed*: its subject coordinate and object coordinate are structurally distinct, even when the predicate is symmetric in its content. A relation in which $A$ is related to $B$ is structurally different from one in which $B$ is related to $A$, related by the *converse*. Symmetric relations — where the converse equals the original — are special cases, not the general structure.

This directedness is what lets relations encode hierarchies, processes, asymmetric dependencies, and irreversible changes. A symmetric primitive could not represent these without imposing additional structure; the framework's directed primitive accommodates them by default.

## Relations are not links

A common misreading: that a relation is a *link* between two terms — an edge in a graph, a wire between nodes. The framework's relation is more than a link. A link presupposes the terms it connects; a relation in this framework constitutes its terms. There is no node-then-edge progression; the directed triple is the unit, and the entities are extracted from it as positions, not added to it as inputs.

This is the structural content of the relationality commitment: relations are ontologically prior to entities. Saying *there is a relation between $A$ and $B$* in this framework is not naming a connection that runs between $A$-as-pre-existing and $B$-as-pre-existing; it is naming the directed triadic shape from which $A$ and $B$ emerge as the relation's relata.

## Relations and predication

The framework's mathematical layer formalizes the relation as a [predication cell](../../../../math/theories/voidmath/terms/predication-cell.md): the triple $(s, p, o)$ with subject, predicate, object as its three coordinates. The cell is the formal articulation of what a relation *is* once the philosophy is written down with mathematical precision. Relations and predication cells are co-extensive; the difference is voice.

## Relations versus the broader philosophical concept

The [philosophy/relation](../../../terms/relation.md) entry surveys the broader philosophical tradition of *relation* in Aristotle, Bradley, Russell, Whitehead, Peirce, and others. That entry is the contrast material — how Western metaphysics has conceived relations across its history. The current entry is the relationality-specific reading: relation as the structural face of the framework's primitive, distinct from the surveyed traditions that the framework draws from but does not reduce to.

# Relation

## What this is

A **Relation** between objects A and B is a subobject of A × B in the ambient category — equivalently, a morphism A × B → Ω into the subobject classifier Ω.

This is the standard mathematical definition. In Set: a subset R ⊆ A × B, with (a, b) ∈ R meaning "a stands in relation R to b." In a Heyting algebra H_t: a relation on propositions is an element of H_t × H_t → H_t, the internal hom. In a sheaf topos Sh(T, J): a relation is a natural transformation between the represented functors, or a subobject of the product sheaf.

A relation is not a label, a tag, or a category membership declaration. It is a mathematical object with a domain, a codomain, and an extension (the set of pairs that satisfy it).

## Why this file exists

Every named relation in the system — `grounding:`, `extends:`, `component-of:`, etc. — is a specific instance of this primitive. The DAG of entity dependencies bottoms out here. Without a primitive Relation entity, every relation-defining entity has a dangling reference: it `extends: Relation` but there is no entity that grounds what Relation means at the base level.

This file is that base. It carries no FARS-specific content. It states the mathematical primitive that the FARS Relation concept ([flatfile-agential-resource-system-relation](flatfile-agential-resource-system-relation.md)) is built on top of.

## Open questions

The fiberwise reduction is expected from the local nature of sheaf conditions but has not been derived in the math locale.
