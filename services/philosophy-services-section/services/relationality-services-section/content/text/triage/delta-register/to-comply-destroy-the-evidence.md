# **To Comply, You Will Have to Destroy the Evidence**

_Subject: Ambiguity as Mechanism in the Trump Administration’s K–12 DEI Directive_

---

On April 4, 2025, the U.S. Department of Education, under the direction of the Trump administration, issued a memo to state education agencies. It requires that all school districts receiving federal funds certify that they are not participating in “prohibited” diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) activities. Certification is due by April 24. The memo is not a law. It does not define DEI. It does not enumerate violations. It does not describe enforcement mechanisms. It does state that failure to comply may result in the loss of funding.

Because the directive offers no operational definition of “prohibited DEI activity,” districts are not being asked to follow policy. They are being asked to anticipate it. The only way to certify compliance is to eliminate any material that could plausibly be read as a violation under a future, retroactive interpretation.

This process requires the removal or revision of content, staff practices, policy language, and archived documentation that reference or could be associated with DEI frameworks. It requires that all evidence of prior DEI participation be reclassified, obscured, or destroyed. It also requires that these actions be taken quickly and without confirmation that they are sufficient.

The directive does not generate policy. It generates panic labor.

---

## Institutional Response Under Ambiguity

School districts are not responding based on ideological alignment. They are responding based on structural risk. Without a clear definition of what the memo prohibits, administrators must assume the broadest possible scope. This includes:

- Professional development materials referencing bias, race, or gender

- Curriculum units covering systemic inequality

- Board resolutions adopting DEI language

- Grant applications mentioning inclusion or restorative practices

- Job descriptions for equity roles

- Internal reports and audits related to demographic disparities

- External communications naming race, gender, or oppression


The short timeline ensures that actions taken will not be deliberative. They will be defensive. The absence of criteria makes over-compliance the rational choice. The risk of funding loss means there is no time to challenge the directive legally or administratively.

This pattern is not accidental.
It is a mechanism.

---

## Compliance as Document Destruction

In practice, certification requires not only current conformity but the removal of evidence that nonconformity may have existed. This includes instructional materials, staff communications, and public statements previously considered part of lawful or encouraged professional conduct.

To comply, districts are:

- Deleting internal training videos and slide decks

- Replacing inclusive policy language with neutral phrasing

- Archiving or eliminating documentation of past DEI programming

- Removing references to DEI from web infrastructure

- Scrubbing digital records of grant-funded equity initiatives

- Halting or postponing programming without formal review


This destruction is not mandated in writing. It is the logical outcome of a directive that imposes a compliance deadline without defining noncompliance.

Sara Ahmed, in _On Being Included_, outlines how diversity infrastructure is often designed to be symbolic rather than transformative. In this case, the infrastructure is not being transformed — it is being deleted. The district must not only show that it is no longer using certain frameworks. It must also show that it never relied on them in the first place.

The memo demands disavowal. The only way to certify that disavowal is to eliminate the trace.

---

## Ambiguity as Enforcement Strategy

A directive that defines its terms can be challenged. A vague memo with real consequences cannot. By refusing to state what counts as a violation, the Department of Education forces each institution to determine its own boundaries — and to assume those boundaries may be reviewed at any time.

The ambiguity is structurally productive. It:

- Offloads the cost of interpretation to the lowest administrative level

- Shields the federal agency from responsibility for individual outcomes

- Prevents organized resistance by keeping institutions isolated

- Forces action without making action contestable


Michel Foucault, in _Security, Territory, Population_, describes a mode of power that functions not by issuing direct orders but by shaping the environment so that desired behavior becomes the default. This directive does not prohibit DEI. It conditions institutions to act as if DEI has already been prohibited.

The ambiguity is not a flaw.
It is the enforcement architecture.

---

## Memory Removal as Compliance Practice

In addition to current materials, the timeline forces districts to review past documentation. Staff must determine whether archived documents could be interpreted as evidence of a DEI violation. If so, those documents must be removed, hidden, or rewritten. There is no procedure for this. There is no guarantee that these actions will be sufficient.

This includes:

- Equity statements published in response to social movements

- Legacy curriculum files referencing racial justice

- Hiring and retention data disaggregated by race or gender

- Archived community engagement materials

- Student group sponsorship records

- Notes from advisory councils and task forces


The result is the collapse of institutional memory.
What a district previously considered progress becomes a liability.
What was once mandated may now be grounds for funding denial.

Jacques Derrida, in _Archive Fever_, writes that the archive is always haunted by its own erasure. The memo transforms this into procedure. Forgetting becomes a strategic requirement. What remains must appear as if it was always there. What is gone must appear as if it never existed.

---

## The Normalization of Anticipatory Fear

Compliance under these conditions requires a shift in organizational behavior. Districts that respond defensively now will adjust future practice accordingly. The cost of reassessing every message, lesson, training, and policy under threat will make institutions less likely to engage with politically adjacent content going forward.

This will include:

- Self-censorship in professional development

- Preemptive exclusion of race and gender from curricular framing

- Revision of hiring materials to remove inclusive language

- Hesitation to respond to future crises involving identity or equity

- Avoidance of terms that have not yet been prohibited


Lauren Berlant, in _Cruel Optimism_, describes systems in which the desire to maintain functioning conditions becomes a barrier to meaningful action. The desire to avoid disruption results in a pattern of compliance that eventually erases the conditions of agency.

This memo introduces a pattern. It does not need to be repeated. It only needs to be remembered. Once remembered, it will shape decision-making long after it is no longer current.

The fear will not be visible.
It will be procedural.

---

## The System Is Changing Without Writing New Rules

After April 24, most schools will remain open. Staff will continue instruction. Students will receive grades. There will be no visible mark of the memo in daily operation. What will change is the decision logic.

Teachers will avoid language without being told to.
Administrators will exclude content without confirming that it is prohibited.
Districts will perform compliance with policies that do not exist in writing.

Fred Moten and Stefano Harney, in _The Undercommons_, argue that education is not just content but control over the conditions of thought. This memo shifts those conditions — not by imposing ideology, but by delegitimizing the tools used to describe its removal.

There will be no formal curriculum stating that race and gender must be omitted.
There will only be documents that no longer contain them.

What began as a certification condition now becomes a design principle for classroom instruction. The shift is not policy-driven. It is behavioral. The institution no longer changes rules. It changes what remains sayable.

---

## Omission as an Instructional Condition

After compliance, most instructional formats remain unchanged. Classes are held. Units proceed. Materials are distributed. Evaluations continue.

What has changed is the internal logic of curriculum.

Instruction is now structured by deliberate omission. Foundational concepts have been removed without replacement. Teachers are expected to deliver instruction that appears whole, using documents and pacing guides that no longer contain all relevant content.

There is no formal list of exclusions. There is no visible acknowledgment of revision scope. Instructional staff are not briefed on what was removed, only that materials have been “updated.”

The result is a condition in which teachers are expected to navigate absence without recognition. Content is missing, but not marked as missing. Topics are present in outline but absent in substance. Instruction proceeds under the assumption of continuity, but that continuity is no longer operational.

Omission is not an error. It is the format. The system has retained its procedures while altering its content base. Instruction now functions as if the erased material was never necessary.

---

## Disalignment Between Classroom and Experience

The structural omissions do not neutralize subject matter. They create sustained misalignment between instructional content and student experience.

Students continue to encounter race, gender, and power outside of school. The removal of those categories from classroom discourse does not eliminate the questions they generate. It only ensures that the institution no longer addresses them.

Educators are now required to teach around conditions they are not permitted to name. In subjects ranging from history to literature to health, relevant frameworks have been removed. Instruction becomes a pattern of redirection and substitution. Terms that previously organized complexity are replaced by neutral placeholders or omitted entirely.

Students recognize the gap. They are aware that certain subjects are avoided. This awareness does not generate clarity—it generates mistrust. What is missing cannot be asked about directly. What is present cannot be explained fully. The classroom becomes a space in which recognition is structurally disincentivized.

The disalignment is systemic. It does not depend on individual teaching choices. It is the result of directive-driven revision processes that removed content without modifying the external conditions that make it relevant.

---

## Disrupted Instructional Progressions

Instruction depends on progression. Topics build on prior knowledge, establish context, and generate conceptual continuity across units and grade levels. The removal of key frameworks breaks these sequences.

For example:

- A government unit may refer to voting access without referencing race

- A literature unit may analyze character development without naming identity

- A biology unit may cover reproduction without addressing gender


Teachers are now required to deliver outcomes that presume prior instruction in concepts no longer formally present. The coherence of instruction is undermined at the level of design. Students are assessed on their understanding of ideas that were not named. Teachers are evaluated on their delivery of standards they were not permitted to fully address.

These disruptions are not isolated. They compound across subjects and years. By removing foundational categories, the directive severs connections between early and advanced instruction. The system continues to assign content-based tasks without supporting content-based learning.

Over time, this produces gaps in comprehension that are not visible in planning documents or pacing guides. They are visible only in the classroom—where questions cannot be answered using the tools that remain.

## Institutional Denial of Instructional Change

The removal of content is not acknowledged in official communications. Revisions are framed as realignments, not reductions. Updated materials are presented without explanation of what was removed. No statement has been issued describing how certification requirements altered instruction.

Educators are asked to proceed as if instruction is continuous. They are expected to assign, assess, and report using tools that no longer align with the previous instructional logic. Students are expected to learn from materials that reference topics only in passing or not at all.

This creates operational dissonance. Teachers understand that materials have changed, but are not authorized to describe those changes. Students notice that certain questions are redirected or ignored. Administrators maintain that all standards are being met.

There is no institutional language for acknowledging partial instruction. Educators who attempt to clarify omissions risk being interpreted as noncompliant. As a result, the system enforces coherence through omission, not explanation.

Instruction continues, but alignment between content, method, and outcome has been displaced. The public record presents an image of stability. Internally, instructional continuity is no longer assured.

The system does not resolve contradiction. It removes its trace.

---

## Assessment Without Content

Standardized assessments remain in place. They are calibrated to prior curriculum frameworks that included now-removed concepts. Testing schedules have not changed. Evaluation rubrics have not been revised to reflect content reduction.

Teachers are expected to administer assessments based on content that may no longer be taught in full. Students are expected to demonstrate mastery of ideas that were not explained using previously accepted terms or frameworks. Administrators are expected to report results as if the underlying curriculum is intact.

Assessment continues not despite content deletion, but because deletion no longer affects the system’s performance indicators.

This arrangement generates data that supports the appearance of stability. Test scores that remain steady are used to validate the revisions. Test scores that decline are attributed to teacher performance or student engagement, not to curricular deletion.

The assessment cycle masks instructional loss. It produces quantifiable outputs that suggest instructional continuity, even when alignment is no longer present. As long as testing structures persist, the system will continue to claim that instruction is complete.

This is not error—it is a feature. The system has retained the appearance of assessment while shifting the content of instruction. The result is an evaluative structure that reinforces compliance by making omissions invisible in outcome data.

---

## Isolation and Internalization of Risk

Compliance interpretation is distributed. Educators are not given detailed guidelines on what to remove or retain. Risk assessment is localized to schools, departments, or individual classrooms. Teachers are left to make instructional decisions without formal institutional support.

In this environment, collaboration decreases. Shared planning time is reduced or restructured. Professional development focuses on generic instructional strategies, not content. Informal communication becomes cautious.

Teachers begin to internalize risk thresholds. They preemptively adjust content to avoid potential conflict, even in the absence of formal prohibition. Decisions about what to teach are shaped by perceptions of visibility, auditability, and administrative scrutiny.

No directive specified what to remove. But removal became the condition of safety.

This internalization does not produce uniform outcomes. It produces inconsistency. Students in adjacent classrooms may receive materially different instruction based on their teacher’s interpretation of compliance boundaries.

What appears externally as professional discretion is, in practice, individualized liability management. Teachers are not empowered—they are unsupported. The institution delegates uncertainty downward, then evaluates outcomes as if certainty were present.

---

## Instruction Under Recursive Ambiguity

As the system continues, ambiguity becomes routine.

Teachers develop instructional strategies that are not documented. They introduce concepts verbally but not in writing. They remove references from slides but retain them in discussion. They segment student engagement based on perceived risk of disclosure.

Students also adapt. They learn which questions will be redirected. They adjust language. They avoid topics that have previously gone unanswered. Inquiry narrows to what is legible within the modified environment.

The classroom becomes a recursive environment in which ambiguity is maintained by every participant. Teachers cannot confirm what they know has been changed. Students cannot clarify what they sense is missing. Instruction persists as a process of calibration rather than exploration.

This condition does not result in silence. It results in repetition. Lessons repeat content stripped of prior complexity. Assignments repeat objectives that no longer produce learning. Outcomes repeat claims of proficiency that no longer map to full content.

The ambiguity is not temporary. It is structural. It does not depend on the memo’s persistence. It depends on the system’s continued willingness to present omission as neutrality.

---

## Trace Loss in Future Teaching Generations

As documentation disappears, so does institutional memory. Teachers entering the profession after the purge encounter curricular materials that have already been revised. Instructors are not provided with comparative frameworks. They are not told what used to be included. They are trained to reproduce current structures without reference to prior configurations.

Supervisors and mentors may be unable or unwilling to describe changes. Lesson plan review focuses on alignment with current standards, not continuity with past frameworks. Certification programs emphasize procedural compliance over historical understanding of instructional design.

What is missing becomes unremarkable. Not because it is unimportant, but because it is undocumented.

This is not a policy outcome. It is the final stage of a modulation cycle that began with an undefined directive and ends with unrecognized absence.

This is not knowledge suppression through censorship. It is knowledge loss through administrative transformation. Content that was once central is now omitted without notation. The next generation of educators will teach within a system that has no record of what it has removed.

The system no longer remembers what was removed. It produces instruction that no longer requires the memory.

The result is compression. Not of scope, but of trace.
There is no visible deletion.
There is only absence that no longer requires explanation.
