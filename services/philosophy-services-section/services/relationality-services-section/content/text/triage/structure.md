# Structure

*The role concept for the patterned arrangements that organize and constrain social life — the relations, rules, distributions, and positions that persist across particular interactions and shape what individuals can do.*

Structure names what is regular about social life: that institutions repeat their forms across many enactments, that classes and statuses carry stable distributions of resources, that interactions follow recognizable scripts even when the participants change. The concept is sociology's answer to the question of what survives a particular event.

Schools differ on what structure *is*. For Marx, structure is the mode of production — the ownership of means of production and the relations of labor it produces. For Durkheim, structure is the moral order: the [social facts](social-fact.md) that exist external to and constraining of any individual. For Lévi-Straussian and structuralist sociology, structure is a system of differences between elements, with the elements themselves derivative. For network sociology, structure is the pattern of relations between nodes — see [network](network.md). For Bourdieu, structure is the distribution of capital across [fields](field.md), embodied in [habitus](habitus.md).

The pairing with [action](action.md) — what people do versus what constrains and enables what they do — is one of sociology's organizing oppositions. Giddens's *structuration* and Bourdieu's *habitus* are major attempts to refuse the dichotomy: structure is real but only persists because action reproduces it; action is creative but only intelligible inside the structures it enacts.

# Relational Structure

There exists a notion of *relational structure* in which all mathematical
objects we speak about live.

Formally, a relational structure `S` consists of:

- A nonempty underlying set `|S|` of points.
- For each index `i` in some fixed indexing set:
  - an arity `n_i ≥ 1`, and
  - an `n_i`‑ary relation `R_i ⊆ |S|^{n_i}`.

We regard operations as special cases of relations: an `n`‑ary operation is an
`(n+1)`‑ary relation that is single‑valued and total in the last coordinate.

Relational structures are the carriers for all subsequent constructions; later
internal theorems introduce particular choices of:

- underlying sets (e.g. recognitions, terms, contexts, sheaf sections),
- distinguished relations and operations on those sets,
- and additional laws they satisfy.
