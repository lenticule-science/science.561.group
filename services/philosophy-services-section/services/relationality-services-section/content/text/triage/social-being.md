---
title: Social Being
description: "A being capable of entering into social relations — of acting in ways that are perceptible to, and meaningful for, other beings of the same kind. The presupposition of all sociology."
---

A social being is a being capable of entering into social relations: of acting in ways that are perceptible to other beings, of having those actions matter — as recognition, as obligation, as affront, as gift. The concept is the founding presupposition of sociology. Before there can be any practice, norm, role, institution, community, or culture, there must be beings capable of social existence.

What makes a being social is not a fixed essence but a relational capacity. A being is social insofar as its actions can be oriented toward others and others' actions can be oriented back. This mutual orientability is what Mead called the social act and Weber called social action: action whose subjective meaning takes account of the behavior of others.

The category of social being is broader than person. Persons are social beings with full normative standing. Collectives — families, firms, states — can also be social beings in the sense that their actions enter into social relations, produce normative consequences, and are held accountable by others. The question of which entities count as social beings in a given normative order is itself a social question, settled by institution.
