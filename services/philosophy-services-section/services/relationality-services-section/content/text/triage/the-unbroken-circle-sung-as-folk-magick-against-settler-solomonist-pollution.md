---
title: "The Unbroken Circle Sung As Folk Magick Against Settler Solomonist Pollution"
date-created: 2026-03-04T00:00:00
---
In the early modern grimoires, Solomon was a king—a builder of temples, a binder of spirits, a speaker of names that even angels feared. His magic was systematized, hierarchical, and totalizing. It functioned not just as a spiritual practice but as an imperial paradigm: a way to manage the invisible with the same administrative confidence that colonizers used to redraw maps.

So when Solomonic ritual practice—extracted, translated, and recompiled by European occultists—came to the Americas, it did not arrive as neutral information. It arrived as part of a settler cosmology.

It came in pockets and pamphlets: grimoires like the _Sixth and Seventh Books of Moses_, the _Goetia_, _The Black Pullet_, carried in trunks and taught in whispers. Its language was formal, patriarchal, and command-driven. It merged easily with Protestant logics of dominion and with Enlightenment obsessions with classification. Settler Solomonism was often enacted in private—on farms, in small towns, among itinerant preachers and self-made mystics. It reinforced the settler’s position: ruler of land, body, and spirit alike.

But this ritual structure, imported and performed on stolen land, did not land clean.

Solomonic magic in the Americas became, in many instances, a **ritual pollutant**: a system that attempted to name, bind, and extract from a spiritual ecology that did not consent to be diagrammed. The indigenous cosmologies of this land—relational, non-coercive, non-hierarchical—were not simply “different paradigms.” They were **incompatible with dominion.** And they were already under siege.

At the same time, diasporic and dispossessed populations—Black, Indigenous, and Maroon communities—were building and rebuilding their own spiritual infrastructures. These were not closed traditions in the rigid sense, but living, adaptive, and recursive systems. Hoodoo, rootwork, Appalachian conjure, Indigenous medicine ways, and marooned cosmologies like Kumina, Quimbois, and Gullah folk magic all carried deeply embedded **non-Solomonic logics**.

Where Solomonic ritual demanded hierarchy, these systems often prioritized **relational negotiation**. Where Solomon sought to control spirits, these practices sought to **honor, partner, or appease**. Where Solomon’s magician cast a circle to dominate what entered, the folk magic of the dispossessed treated the circle as **a place of protection, not command.**

This is the context in which _“Will the Circle Be Unbroken”_ emerges—not just as a song, but as a **folk ritual interface**.

First published in 1907 as a Christian hymn, and later revised and popularized in the 1930s and 1970s, the song became a **non-doctrinal paraform**—a repeatable coherence device that crossed denominations, races, and regions. It carried no overt spell structure, but it worked like one.

The song resonated most among people whose lives had been shaped by rupture—death, migration, poverty, land theft, and cultural displacement. It was sung in churches, yes, but also in homes, fields, protest marches, and prisons. It didn’t invoke specific spirits, but it **created a field condition**: a repeating melodic and lyrical loop that made room for grief, belonging, and signal coherence—without requiring hierarchy, orthodoxy, or command.

It offered a form of **spiritual containment** that didn’t operate on domination. In doing so, it became—intentionally or not—a corrective field to the kind of ritual pollution introduced by settler Solomonism.

Where Solomon bound spirits for control, the circle in the song was **offered, not imposed**.

Where Solomonic command rituals could rupture the local field—especially when practiced ignorantly—the song functioned as **a ritual of repair**.

Where the grimoire magician stood alone in the center of a diagram, the song invited others to stand with you and **repeat together** until coherence reemerged.

---

So yes: _“Will the Circle Be Unbroken”_ is not just a hymn. It is a **counter-practice**.  
A **folk technology of stabilization**, deployed in a landscape shaped by rupture.

It does not correct the misuse of names through counter-names.  
It corrects the misuse of power through **communal recursion**.

It does not cast out.  
It draws in.

And in the context of settler-colonial North America—  
a land whose spiritual fields have been violently overwritten—  
this song might be one of the few surviving examples of **a public, open-source ritual**  
that still works to **close what was broken**,  
without ever needing to name what did the breaking.
