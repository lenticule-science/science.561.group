### Conjecture — Relational Viscosity: Inertia from Trace Depth {#relational-inertia}

*A system that has accumulated a long relational history — a deep history — carries
more settled content in its fixed fiber $H_t^*$. Every counit operation that moves
this system must drag that settled content through restriction maps, which cost more
in high-Fischer-Rao-curvature regions of the moduli space. The result is relational
inertia: resistance to state change that is separate from mass-energy and proportional
to the depth and settledness of the system's relational history. Two objects with the
same mass can require different force to stop.*

---

#### The Mathematical Structures {#ri-math}

- **Fixed fiber** (§fixed-fiber): $H_t^* = \mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_t) \cap \mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t)$,
  the doubly-quiescent layer at history $t$. Its cardinality $|H_t^*|$ measures how much
  settled relational content the history carries. Longer histories accumulate more settled
  content: the sub-Heyting-algebra $H_t^*$ grows (weakly) with history depth $|t|$,
  because each extension step can only add propositions to the fixed layer, not remove
  them.

- **Invariant mode fiber** (§spectral-decomposition): $V_t = K^{H_t^*}$, the
  $K$-module generated by the settled propositions. Its dimension $\dim_K V_t = |H_t^*|$
  is the relational dimension of the settled content.

- **Counit as restriction** (§directed-comonad): the counit $\epsilon_H(t): H_{s \star t}
  \to H_t$ is the restriction from the extended history back to $t$. For the fixed fiber,
  this restriction is an isomorphism on the stable subalgebra: $\epsilon|_{H_{s \star t}^*}$
  maps onto $H_t^*$ faithfully. Any operation that changes a system's history context must
  route all settled content through the restriction map — it cannot discard $H_t^*$.

- **Fischer-Rao curvature at settled states** (§fischer-rao-metric, §riemannian-moduli): the
  Fischer-Rao scalar curvature $K(\mathcal{M}_n, p)$ at a point $p \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}_n)$
  concentrates at distributions that are highly concentrated on a small number of nucleus
  families — i.e., near the fixed point where the depth filtration has converged. A history
  $t$ with large $|H_t^*|$ corresponds to a point $p$ near this fixed-point locus, where
  curvature is high. The Riemannian depth tower (§riemannian-moduli) connects levels by
  Riemannian submersions; horizontal geodesics through high-curvature regions require more
  "force" in the natural-gradient sense.

#### Restriction Maps as Drag {#ri-drag}

**The counit law for process carriers.** A process carrier $(X, \gamma)$ is a $G_s$-coalgebra:
a sheaf $X$ with stepping maps $\gamma_t: X(t) \to X(s \star t)$, satisfying the counit law
$\epsilon_X \circ \gamma = \mathrm{id}$. The counit law says the stepping map $\gamma$ is a
section of the restriction $\epsilon_X$: stepping forward then restricting back is the identity.
The system is stable under this round trip.

**Drag from the fixed fiber.** For the fiber algebra sheaf $H$ itself: the restriction map
$\epsilon_H(t): H_{s \star t} \to H_t$ must map the fixed fiber $H_{s \star t}^*$ onto
$H_t^*$ faithfully (by the fixed-fiber stability theorem). Any stepping operation on $H$
must be accompanied by the transport of the entire fixed-point content — there is no way
to step forward without "dragging" all of $H_t^*$ along.

The **relational drag** of a history $t$ is the content that must be carried in any state
transition: $|H_t^*|$ elements of the fixed fiber, each of which must be consistently
transported through the restriction map. The larger $|H_t^*|$, the more content must be
coherently maintained during any counit operation. This is the viscosity: the resistance
of the settled layer to any change in the system's history context.

**Geodesic cost in the Fischer-Rao geometry.** On the Riemannian depth tower
(§riemannian-moduli), a state transition from the current history $t$ to a new context $t'$
corresponds to moving along a path in $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}_n)$ from the distribution
$p$ (concentrated near the nucleus family of $H_t$) to the distribution $p'$ (concentrated
near the nucleus family of $H_{t'}$). The geodesic cost of this transition is measured by
the Fischer-Rao distance:
$$
d_{\mathrm{FR}}(p, p') \;=\; \arccos\!\left(\sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}_n} \sqrt{p(m)\,p'(m)}\right),
$$
the Bhattacharyya angle between the two distributions.

When $t$ has a large fixed fiber ($|H_t^*|$ large), the distribution $p$ is concentrated
near the fixed-point locus — a high-curvature region of $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}_n)$. Moving
from a high-curvature point requires traversing a larger geodesic arc per unit displacement
than moving from a low-curvature point. The **effective inertia** of the system at history $t$
is proportional to the Fischer-Rao curvature $K(\mathcal{M}_n, p_t)$ at its current moduli
space position.

#### Definition — Relational Inertia {#ri-definition}

**Definition.** The **relational inertia** of a system at history $t$ at depth $n$ is:
$$
I_{\mathrm{rel}}(t,n) \;:=\; \dim_K V_t^{(n)} \;=\; |H_t^{*(n)}|,
$$
the number of settled propositions in the fixed fiber at depth $n$. The **effective inertia**
is the sum of the mass-energy contribution and the relational contribution:
$$
I_{\mathrm{eff}}(t) \;=\; m \;+\; c_{\mathrm{rel}} \cdot I_{\mathrm{rel}}(t,n),
$$
where $m$ is the standard mass-energy inertia and $c_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is a coupling constant
(the relational analog of $G/c^2$ — a constant relating relational units to physical force
units) that is not yet determined.

**The depth dependence.** As the tower descends and more modes settle to $E_{t,0}$, the
fixed fiber grows: $|H_t^{*(n+1)}| \geq |H_t^{*(n)}|$. A system that has been "running
longer" — that has processed more depth levels of $U_G$ — has a larger fixed fiber and
therefore higher relational inertia. Relational inertia is an increasing function of the
depth $n$ at which the system lives.

#### The Bridge — Historical Depth as Inertial Contribution {#ri-bridge}

**Classical mechanics** identifies inertia with mass: $F = ma$, where $m$ is invariant
and determined by the particle species. Every electron has the same mass; every proton
has the same mass. The idea that two particles of the same species could have different
inertia is foreign to the standard model.

**The relational reading** proposes that $m$ is not the only contributor to effective
inertia. A system embedded in a relational universe at history $t$ carries $|H_t^*|$
settled propositions — the accumulated fixed-point content of its entire relational
history. Each settled proposition is a "committed" piece of relational content that
must be coherently maintained in any state transition. The more such commitments, the
more "drag" any state change encounters.

**The viscosity analogy.** In fluid dynamics, viscosity is resistance to deformation:
a viscous fluid requires more force to shear because its internal structure resists
reorganization. Relational viscosity is the same phenomenon at the level of propositional
content: the settled layer $H_t^*$ is the "internal structure" that resists counit
operations. Just as a highly viscous fluid becomes stiffer at lower temperatures (as
thermal fluctuations decrease and molecular order increases), a relational system becomes
more inert as more propositions settle into the fixed fiber.

**The depth tower and increasing viscosity.** As the depth tower descends ($n$ increases),
more modes settle and $|H_t^*|$ grows. This means:

- A system at depth $n = 0$ (the ground relational universe) has minimal relational inertia:
  few propositions have settled, the fixed fiber is small.

- A system at depth $n \gg 1$ (deep in the tower, close to the fixed point $R = U_G(R)$)
  has maximal relational inertia: most propositions have settled, $|H_t^*|$ is large.

The physical universe, if it corresponds to a specific depth $n$, would have a characteristic
effective inertia contribution $c_{\mathrm{rel}} \cdot |H_t^*|$. If this contribution is
small compared to $m$ (because $c_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is small), it would be undetectable in
current experiments. If it is not small, it would show up as an anomalous mass-like
contribution to inertia for historically complex systems.

**Distinction from Nucleus Drag.** The present correspondence is about the accumulation
of settled content over history depth — a historical, integrated quantity. A closely related
conjecture (§nucleus-drag, see below) concerns the instantaneous drag from the nucleus
itself: the rate of settling per step, rather than the total accumulated settling. Relational
viscosity is the integral; nucleus drag is the derivative.

#### Prediction and Falsification {#ri-prediction}

**Prediction (qualitative).**

1. **Inertia of complex systems exceeds mass prediction**: systems with a richer
   relational interaction history — more histories converging on their current state —
   should exhibit higher effective inertia than systems of the same mass with simpler
   histories. In high-energy physics: a long-lived, multiply-interacting composite
   system should resist acceleration more than a freshly-produced particle of equal mass.

2. **Inertia grows with history depth**: if the universe is deepening over cosmic time
   (the depth tower advancing), then the relational inertia of all systems should
   increase with cosmic time. This is not the same as mass increasing (mass is
   conserved); it is a subtler effect in which the coupling between force and acceleration
   — the effective inertia — slowly drifts.

3. **Relational inertia is not screened by distance**: unlike gravity, which depends on
   position, relational inertia depends on history. Two objects at the same
   location but with different historical complexities can have different inertia.
   This would show up as an anomalous equivalence-principle violation for
   historically-complex systems.

**Falsification.** The correspondence fails if:

- All precision tests of the equivalence principle (that all objects of the same mass
  fall identically) hold to arbitrary precision for systems of arbitrarily different
  historical complexity — ruling out any $c_{\mathrm{rel}} |H_t^*|$ contribution.

- The Fischer-Rao curvature does not increase with $|H_t^*|$ in explicit computations
  on the Fibonacci history model (§quasicrystal-hypertensor):
  if high-settledness does not produce high curvature, the drag mechanism fails.

- The counit law $\epsilon_X \circ \gamma = \mathrm{id}$ holds even when $H_t^*$ is large,
  with no additional geodesic cost: if the fixed content is transported freely without
  any additional curvature cost, the drag argument is wrong.

---

#### What the Correspondence Requires {#ri-requires}

- **A proof that $K(\mathcal{M}_n, p_t) \propto |H_t^*|$**: the claim that Fischer-Rao
  curvature grows with fixed-fiber size requires a concrete computation in the moduli
  space. The Riemannian depth tower (§riemannian-moduli) provides the framework; the
  explicit curvature formula for the submersion $K_n$ must be worked out.

- **A value for $c_{\mathrm{rel}}$**: the coupling between relational inertia units
  ($|H_t^*|$, a count of propositions) and physical inertia units (kg) is not
  determined by the framework. This requires an additional identification — perhaps
  via the Landauer bound (§landauer-limit), which provides a unit
  conversion between spectral energy and physical energy.

- **The dictionary between history depth and physical time**: relational depth $|t|$ is
  not the same as cosmological time. Identifying which physical systems correspond to
  which history depths requires the physical dictionary common to all these correspondences.

**Status.** Stub. The drag mechanism — restriction maps must carry the fixed fiber,
and more fixed content means more drag — is structurally immediate from the counit law.
The Fischer-Rao curvature connection is the bridge conjecture, requiring explicit
computation. The identification with physical inertia is the far end of the
correspondence and requires both $c_{\mathrm{rel}}$ and the physical dictionary.
