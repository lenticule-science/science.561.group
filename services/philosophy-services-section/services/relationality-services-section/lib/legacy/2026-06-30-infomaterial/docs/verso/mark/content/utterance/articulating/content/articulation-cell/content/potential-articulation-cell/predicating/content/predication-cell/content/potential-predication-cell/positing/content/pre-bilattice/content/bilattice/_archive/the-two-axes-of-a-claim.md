This is an explanation.
It does not tell you how to do anything; it tries to change the shape of a thing you already half-know, so that a confusion you keep running into stops recurring.
The confusion is this: we are trained to ask of a claim *"is it true or false?"* — a single question with two answers — when in fact a claim's standing always answers **two** independent questions at once.
Collapsing them into one is the source of a surprising amount of avoidable trouble, and pulling them apart is the first move of a way of thinking that this archive returns to again and again.

## The single axis, and where it fails

Picture the familiar model first.
A claim — *"the bridge is safe"*, *"the patient has the disease"*, *"the defendant was present"* — is a switch.
It is up (true) or down (false).
Reasoning is the business of flipping switches to their correct positions.

This model is not wrong so much as it is *flat*.
It has one dimension, and a single dimension can record only one thing.
So when reality hands you a situation that two things are true of at once, the flat model has to throw one of them away.

Two situations break it immediately:

- **Ignorance.** No one has inspected the bridge.
  There is no evidence either way.
  On the flat model you must still call it true or false — and whichever you pick, you have manufactured a commitment out of nothing.
  "Unknown" is not a position on a one-dimensional switch.
- **Conflict.** One engineer's report says the bridge is sound; another's says it is failing.
  Now there is evidence *both* ways.
  The flat model cannot hold this.
  It forces you to discard a report, or to average two things that are not numbers, or — most often — to declare the whole situation an *error* and stop.

Ignorance and conflict are not exotic.
They are the ordinary weather of any inquiry that draws on more than one source: law, medicine, intelligence, science, history, governance, an honest argument between two people.
A model that treats them as malfunctions is a model that malfunctions exactly when the stakes are highest.

## Two questions, not one

The repair is to notice that "what is the standing of this claim?" was never one question.
It is two, and they are independent:

1. **Which way does it lean?**
   Given whatever evidence there is, does it point toward *yes* or toward *no*?
   Call this the **leaning** of the claim — its truth-direction.
2. **How much is there to lean on?**
   Setting aside *which* way, how much is known at all?
   Nothing? One side only? Both sides at once?
   Call this the **knowing** of the claim — how informed its standing is.

These are genuinely separate dials.
You can turn one without touching the other.
"Strong evidence, pointing firmly toward yes" and "strong evidence, pointing firmly toward no" differ in *leaning* but not in *knowing*.
"Nothing is known" and "everything contradicts itself" differ in *knowing* but are both, in their leaning, perfectly balanced — neither tips.

The moment you hold the two dials apart, the two situations that broke the flat model become ordinary readings:

- **Ignorance** is *low knowing, no leaning* — the dials at their quiet corner.
- **Conflict** is *high knowing, no leaning* — you have been told a great deal, and it cancels.

They are opposites on the *knowing* axis (nothing said versus everything said) while sitting together on the *leaning* axis (neither tips).
The flat model could not tell them apart because it had thrown the entire *knowing* axis away.

## The four landmarks

Two independent axes give a plane, and a plane has corners.
Four landmark states organize everything else:

- **Open** — nothing is known; the claim has not been touched by evidence.
  This is where every claim starts.
- **Leaning yes** — the evidence speaks, and it speaks for the claim.
- **Leaning no** — the evidence speaks, and it speaks against.
- **Conflicted** — the evidence speaks on *both* sides at once.

The flat model kept only the two middle ones.
The two it discarded — *open* and *conflicted* — are precisely the ones that carry information about the *process* of inquiry rather than its verdict: how far it has gotten, and whether its sources agree.

It helps to see that the four corners sit at the ends of the two axes, but *crosswise*:

- Along **knowing**, the extremes are **open** (told nothing) and **conflicted** (told both); *leaning yes* and *leaning no* sit in the middle, equally informed.
- Along **leaning**, the extremes are **leaning no** and **leaning yes**; *open* and *conflicted* sit in the middle, equally balanced.

Each axis treats the other axis's extremes as its own midpoints.
That crosswise relationship is the whole geometry, and it is why a single dial could never have captured it: the two dials are transverse, not aligned.

## Contradiction becomes a value

Here is the consequence that does the most work downstream.

On the flat model, a contradiction is a catastrophe.
Classical reasoning has a rule — *from a contradiction, anything follows* — which means that the instant your evidence conflicts, every claim whatsoever becomes "provable", and reasoning is destroyed.
This is why the flat model must treat conflict as an error and halt: one local disagreement, untreated, would burn down the entire structure.

But "conflicted" is just the fourth corner of the plane.
It is a *value* a claim can have, exactly as legitimate as the other three.
A reasoner who can represent it can also *contain* it: the bridge claim is conflicted, and that fact stays local to the bridge claim.
It does not leak outward and make the moon claim or the tax claim suddenly conflicted too.
Reasoning that has this property — that tolerates a local contradiction without collapsing globally — is called **paraconsistent**, and it falls out of the two-axis picture for free.
Once "told both" is a place on the map, you no longer have to flee the map when you arrive there.

This is not a license to be sloppy about contradictions.
It is the opposite: it lets you *keep* a contradiction in view, labelled and quarantined, long enough to investigate it — instead of being forced to suppress it the moment it appears.
A conflict you can name is a conflict you can resolve.
A conflict that detonates your whole system is one you will be tempted to hide.

## Why two orders of comparison

Because there are two axes, there are two different ways for one claim's standing to be "more" than another's, and keeping them distinct matters.

- One claim is **more known** than another if it rests on more evidence — if moving from the first to the second only ever *adds* to what is on the table, never retracts it.
  Inquiry, at its best, climbs this order: it accumulates.
  *Open* is the bottom (nothing yet); *conflicted* is the top (everything, including the disagreements).
- One claim is **more affirmed** than another if it leans further toward yes.
  *Leaning no* is the bottom of this order; *leaning yes* is the top.

These two orders genuinely disagree about which states are "extreme" and which are "middling", because they run crosswise.
Confusing them — treating "we now know more" as if it meant "we now believe more firmly" — is one of the most common errors in practical reasoning.
Learning that two trustworthy sources flatly contradict each other is a large *gain in knowledge*; it is not a gain in *confidence*, and on the two-axis picture you can say so precisely: knowing went up, leaning stayed balanced.

## What this buys you

The point of separating the two axes is not tidiness.
It is that the separation keeps showing up as the right move in domain after domain:

- A diagnosis is not "disease / no disease" but a position in the plane: how much has been tested, and which way the results lean — with *untested* and *contradictory* as first-class readings a clinician must act on differently.
- An intelligence picture is not "threat / no threat" but, again, two dials: corroboration and direction, kept apart so that *no reporting* and *conflicting reporting* are never confused.
- A deliberating group does not reach "agreement / disagreement"; it occupies a standing whose *knowing* records how much has been surfaced and whose *leaning* records where the room tips — and a room that has surfaced everything and split evenly is in a very different state from a room that has said nothing, though both "fail to agree."

In each case the same structure is doing the work: a claim's standing lives in a plane, not on a line; contradiction and ignorance are positions in that plane, not failures of it; and the two ways of being "more" must be told apart.

This two-axis picture is the floor.
Everything else this archive builds — how evidence settles toward a stable verdict, how that settling can be fast or endless, how disagreement between observers shows up as a measurable obstruction — stands on the simple refusal to flatten a claim's standing into a single yes-or-no.
Once you have seen the plane, the line looks like what it is: a shadow, useful for the cases where nothing is ever unknown and nothing ever conflicts, and misleading everywhere else.
