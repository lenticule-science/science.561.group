# Research memo: frontiers related to aperiodic quasicrystals

**Date:** June 25, 2026 **Prepared for:** emsenn **Scope:** frontiers around aperiodic quasicrystals and adjacent quasiperiodic systems, excluding the earlier memo’s narrow focus on coloring aperiodic tiles.

## Executive synthesis

Quasicrystal research is no longer mainly about proving that nonperiodic long-range order exists.
That fight was settled experimentally in the 1980s and mathematically through model sets, substitutions, diffraction theory, and symbolic dynamics.
The frontier is now about **control**: controlling formation, detecting hidden order, designing interactions that assemble target quasiperiodic structures, building quantum and photonic devices on quasiperiodic substrates, and deciding which aperiodic systems are genuinely quasicrystalline rather than merely nonperiodic.

A compact way to phrase the whole research program is:

\[ \text{nonperiodic order} \; + \; \text{finite/local rules or interactions} \; \longrightarrow \; \text{spectral, mechanical, electronic, optical, or topological function}. \]

The hottest current zones are:

1. **Thermodynamic stability and nucleation.** New first-principles methods are attacking whether quasicrystals can be true ground states rather than metastable approximant-adjacent curiosities [@baek2025stability].
2. **Phasons and defects as functional degrees of freedom.** Phasons are no longer just internal-space bookkeeping; they appear in growth, defect healing, modulation, and historical memory [@wang2025defectfree; @takakura2025icosahedrite].
3. **Data-driven discovery.** Open quasicrystal/approximant datasets and machine-learning discovery are beginning to turn the field from artisanal alloy hunting into computationally guided search [@fujita2024datasets; @liu2023mlqc].
4. **Soft-matter and colloidal assembly.** The field is learning to design particles and protocols that assemble quasicrystals, including cyclic shear, DNA-origami-inspired patchy particles, and inverse-design algorithms [@pinto2025blueprints; @maire2026cyclicshear].
5. **Quantum, superconducting, and magnetic quasicrystals.** Quasicrystals are becoming platforms for antiferromagnetism, superconductivity, bosonic phases, and strongly correlated systems [@tamura2025antiferromagnetic; @tokumoto2024superconductivity; @grossklags2025quantum].
6. **Moiré quasicrystals.** Twisted multilayers provide tunable quasiperiodic electronic systems with superconductivity, strong interactions, and flat-band physics [@uri2023moire; @hao2024flatbands].
7. **Topology and photonics.** Quasicrystals can host topological phases, charges, corner states, photonic vortex lasers, and higher-dimensional topological information unavailable in ordinary crystals [@varjas2019topological; @arjas2024lasing; @tsesses2025fourdim].
8. **Mathematical diffraction and inverse problems.** Hat/Spectre monotiles, model sets, renormalization, pure point diffraction, and spectral classification are pushing the boundary between “aperiodic tiling” and “mathematical quasicrystal” [@baake2025hatdiffraction; @baake2026renormalization; @popov2026modelsets].

The unifying question is not merely “what structures exist?” It is:

> Which kinds of aperiodic order can be forced, predicted, assembled, measured, inverted, or exploited?

---

## 1. Working definitions: quasicrystal, aperiodic order, approximant

A **periodic crystal** has translational periodicity: there is a lattice of translations preserving the structure.
A **quasicrystal** has long-range order without translational periodicity, often with rotational symmetries forbidden to periodic crystals, such as 5-fold, 8-fold, 10-fold, or 12-fold order.
The canonical experimental breakthrough was Shechtman, Blech, Gratias, and Cahn’s observation of a metallic phase with long-range orientational order and no translational symmetry [@shechtman1984metallic].

An **aperiodic tiling** is any tiling that lacks translational periodicity.
This is broader than quasicrystallinity.
A random tiling, a substitution tiling with mixed spectrum, or a nonperiodic but disordered structure may be aperiodic without being a quasicrystal in the stricter diffraction sense.

A **mathematical quasicrystal** is often modeled as a Delone set with strong diffraction or almost-periodic structure, especially a cut-and-project or model set.
In the cleanest case, the diffraction is **pure point**: sharp Bragg peaks rather than continuous diffuse scattering.
Pure-point diffraction in cut-and-project sets remains one of the central mathematical paradigms [@richard2017guide].

An **approximant** is a periodic crystal whose local atomic motifs resemble those of a nearby quasicrystal.
Approximants are experimentally crucial because they are easier to model with ordinary periodic computational tools.
They are also conceptually dangerous: if one models a quasicrystal only by approximants, one risks washing out the genuinely quasiperiodic degrees of freedom.

---

## 2. Frontier: thermodynamic stability, nucleation, and growth

### Core issue

A longstanding question is whether quasicrystals are true thermodynamic phases, metastable states, entropy-stabilized structures, or kinetic accidents.
The answer appears to depend on the system.
Some metallic quasicrystals are stable; others are close to approximants; soft-matter systems may be entropy- or protocol-stabilized; and natural quasicrystals can require violent pressure-temperature histories.

### What changed recently

Baek and collaborators used first-principles calculations on increasing-size quasicrystal nanoparticles to extrapolate bulk and surface energies, giving a route around the usual obstacle that quasicrystals do not have small periodic unit cells.
Their reported conclusion that ScZn\(_{7.33}\) and YbCd\(_{5.7}\) can be ground-state phases is a major conceptual advance: it directly challenges the idea that translational symmetry is needed for zero-temperature stability in inorganic solids [@baek2025stability].

In situ growth studies are also making quasicrystal formation less mysterious.
Wang and collaborators studied decagonal quasicrystals growing around obstacles and reported defect-free growth around pores, combining synchrotron X-ray tomography with molecular dynamics [@wang2025defectfree].
The significance is that quasicrystalline growth is not merely “fragile nonperiodicity”; it can reorganize through internal degrees of freedom and heal around obstructions.

### Key frontier questions

**Can first-principles methods predict stable quasicrystals without relying on periodic approximants?** This requires finite-size extrapolations, cluster expansions, internal-space methods, or other nonperiodic analogues of ab initio solid-state computation.

**Which quasicrystals are energy-stabilized, entropy-stabilized, or kinetics-stabilized?** The field needs a taxonomy.
Metallic alloys, soft matter, quantum fluids, and surface oxides may stabilize quasiperiodicity through different mechanisms.

**Can nucleation barriers be computed for true quasiperiodic phases?** For periodic crystals, classical nucleation theory has a well-developed language.
For quasicrystals, nucleus shape, surface energy, phason strain, approximant competition, and local cluster motifs complicate the theory.

**Can growth be controlled through phason elasticity?** If defect healing and growth around obstacles depend on phason-like rearrangements, then phason elasticity may become a design parameter rather than a post hoc explanation.

## 3. Frontier: phasons, defects, elasticity, and memory

### Core issue

In cut-and-project language, a quasicrystal is described using both physical space and an internal or perpendicular space.
Ordinary phonons are displacements in physical space. **Phasons** are rearrangements associated with internal-space degrees of freedom.
They can appear as flips, strain, diffuse scattering, satellite peaks, growth modes, or relaxation channels.

### Why this is hot

The old temptation was to treat phasons as a mathematical artifact of higher-dimensional descriptions.
Current experimental and computational work instead treats them as real mechanisms.

Takakura and collaborators performed a high-resolution synchrotron X-ray study of natural icosahedrite from the Khatyrka meteorite and described a modulated icosahedral phase by six cosine phason waves propagating along six fivefold axes, with polarization in perpendicular phason space [@takakura2025icosahedrite].
This is important because phason modulation becomes a **historical tracer**: the structure can preserve information about high-pressure/high-temperature formation and cooling.

Likewise, defect-free growth around obstacles in decagonal quasicrystals suggests that phason-mediated rearrangements can allow ordered growth to recover after geometric obstruction [@wang2025defectfree].
In that framing, defects are not merely failures of order.
They are probes of how order propagates.

### Key frontier questions

**When are defects destructive, and when are they stabilizing?** Soft-matter quasicrystals can gain entropy from rearrangements; metallic quasicrystals can relax phason strain; natural quasicrystals can preserve modulated histories.
The same word “defect” covers many phenomena.

**Can phason waves be engineered?** In photonic, acoustic, or mechanical quasicrystals, one might deliberately tune phason strain to steer wave propagation or localization.

**Can phason data reconstruct formation history?** Natural quasicrystals may function as shock, pressure, or cooling-rate archives, especially for meteorites and lightning- or impact-formed materials [@stagno2023extreme; @takakura2025icosahedrite].

**What is the correct continuum mechanics of quasicrystals?** The frontier is a coupled phonon-phason elasticity theory that works quantitatively across metallic, soft, and synthetic systems.

## 4. Frontier: data-driven discovery and computational materials search

### Core issue

Quasicrystal discovery has historically been slow because the composition space is huge, stable windows are narrow, and the structures are difficult to index.
Data-driven methods are changing the workflow.

### What changed recently

Fujita and collaborators released comprehensive experimental datasets of quasicrystals and approximants, including compositions, structure types, phase diagrams, fabrication processes, and physical properties [@fujita2024datasets].
This matters because machine learning needs curated negative and positive examples: not only “known quasicrystals,” but approximants and ordinary phases.

Liu and collaborators showed that machine learning can predict stable quasicrystalline phases and used it to discover new stable decagonal quasicrystals, including Al\(_{65}\)Ni\(_{20}\)Os\(_{15}\), Al\(_{78}\)Ir\(_{17}\)Mn\(_5\), and Al\(_{78}\)Ir\(_{17}\)Fe\(_5\) [@liu2023mlqc].

### Key frontier questions

**Can ML learn quasicrystal stability mechanisms rather than just composition correlations?** A chemical-composition classifier is useful, but the next level is a model that understands electron concentration, cluster chemistry, approximant energetics, phason entropy, and phase-diagram context.

**Can datasets encode failed syntheses?** Negative data are especially valuable.
A list of alloys that did *not* form quasicrystals under specific processing conditions may be as important as a list that did.

**Can AI combine diffraction recognition and composition design?** The ideal closed loop would propose a composition, suggest a processing route, predict candidate diffraction signatures, classify experimental patterns, and update the model.

**Can approximants serve as a bridge rather than a trap?** Approximants are computationally tractable, but they can mislead if treated as substitutes for true quasiperiodic order.
Data-driven approaches need to learn both the approximant relation and the distinction.

## 5. Frontier: soft matter, colloids, DNA origami, and inverse assembly

### Core issue

Soft matter lets researchers design building blocks and interactions directly.
Instead of discovering quasicrystals in alloy phase diagrams, one can try to **program** particles to assemble into a target quasiperiodic structure.

### What changed recently

Pinto, Šulc, Sciortino, and Russo introduced automated design protocols for colloidal quasicrystal clusters, including experimentally motivated DNA-origami-style patchy building blocks [@pinto2025blueprints].
The conceptual leap is that quasicrystal assembly becomes an inverse problem: choose patch colors, interaction matrices, and geometric constraints so that the target quasiperiodic arrangement is selected.

Maire, Plati, Smallenburg, and Foffi showed that cyclic shear can drive particles into two-dimensional dodecagonal quasicrystalline states, effectively replacing thermal fluctuations with plastic rearrangements [@maire2026cyclicshear].
This makes the assembly protocol itself a design variable.

Jiang and collaborators reported a mechanical route to dodecagonal quasicrystal motifs and approximants through perturbations of hard-disk configurations, suggesting that mechanical preparation can help find or improve quasicrystalline order [@jiang2025mechanical].

### Key frontier questions

**Can quasicrystals be assembled on demand?** This requires controlling not only the target structure but also kinetic traps, competing crystals, finite-size effects, and defect annealing.

**Can process replace potential?** Cyclic shear and mechanical perturbation suggest that a desired quasicrystal may not need to be the equilibrium ground state if the driving protocol reliably assembles it.

**Can inverse design scale from clusters to bulk quasicrystals?** Many inverse-designed structures work beautifully at finite cluster scale.
The harder problem is macroscopic assembly with long-range order and manageable defect density.

**Can physical patch colors implement mathematical matching rules?** DNA sticky ends, ligand chemistry, magnetic patches, and shape-complementary binding sites can be viewed as physical edge colors or local matching rules.

## 6. Frontier: quantum quasicrystals and superfluid/quasicrystalline phases

### Core issue

A quantum quasicrystal is not merely a classical quasiperiodic lattice with particles placed on it.
The frontier is to make quantum matter whose coherence, interactions, excitations, and order parameters coexist with quasiperiodicity.

### What changed recently

Grossklags and collaborators studied interaction potentials that stabilize quantum quasicrystal phases in two-dimensional bosonic systems [@grossklags2025quantum].
Their results distinguish octagonal, decagonal, and dodecagonal phases by the number of tuned length scales required for stabilization, and they connect the proposal to laser-painted interactions in cavity QED settings.

Zhu, Yao, and Sanchez-Palencia studied the thermodynamic phase diagram of interacting bosons in a two-dimensional quasicrystal potential, highlighting phases such as Bose glass behavior in a disorder-free quasiperiodic setting [@zhu2023bosons].

### Key frontier questions

**Can superfluidity coexist robustly with quasicrystalline density order?** This is the “superquasicrystal” or quasicrystalline supersolid problem.

**What are the low-energy modes?** A self-organized quantum quasicrystal should have ordinary phase modes, phonon-like modes, phason-like modes, and possibly coupled collective excitations.

**Can optical/cavity platforms engineer the necessary length scales?** Classical soft quasicrystals often need multiple length scales.
The quantum version turns length-scale engineering into an interaction-design problem.

**How does quasiperiodicity change quantum criticality?** Quasiperiodic systems sit between periodic and random systems.
This affects localization, coherence, finite-temperature order, and universality.

## 7. Frontier: magnetic quasicrystals

### Core issue

Magnetism usually relies on lattice regularity, sublattices, and symmetry.
Quasicrystals have long-range order but no translational unit cell, so magnetic order becomes conceptually strange.

### What changed recently

Tamura and collaborators reported experimental evidence for antiferromagnetic order in the icosahedral quasicrystal Au\(_{56}\)In\(_{28.5}\)Eu\(_{15.5}\), including a Néel transition and magnetic Bragg reflections [@tamura2025antiferromagnetic].
This is significant because antiferromagnetic order is much more sensitive to spatial organization than ferromagnetism.

### Key frontier questions

**What replaces sublattice order?** In periodic antiferromagnets, sublattices are central.
In a quasicrystal, the natural language may involve clusters, inflation shells, local environments, or higher-dimensional reciprocal modules.

**What do spin waves look like without Bloch momentum?** Magnons in a quasicrystal may be critical, hierarchical, or cluster-localized rather than Bloch-like.

**Can quasiperiodicity tune frustration?** Quasicrystal tilings can have many inequivalent local environments and loop structures, which may generate frustrated magnetic phases not available in periodic lattices.

**Can magnetic order reveal hidden structural hierarchy?** Magnetic Bragg peaks, diffuse scattering, and local susceptibility may act as probes of quasiperiodic order.

## 8. Frontier: superconducting quasicrystals

### Core issue

Superconductivity in quasicrystals is difficult because the usual crystalline toolkit—Bloch bands, simple Fermi surfaces, periodic pairing channels—is weakened or unavailable.
But that is exactly why the field is interesting.

### What changed recently

Tokumoto and collaborators reported bulk superconductivity at roughly 1 K in the van der Waals layered quasicrystal Ta\(_{1.6}\)Te [@tokumoto2024superconductivity].
This opened a route to studying superconductivity in thermodynamically stable layered quasicrystalline materials, including quasi-two-dimensional settings.
Terashima and collaborators then studied anomalous upper critical field behavior in this superconducting quasicrystal [@terashima2024criticalfield].

On the theory side, Jeon and Lee proposed “pattern-selective superconductivity” in a dodecagonal quasicrystal, where local quasiperiodic patterns control charge distribution, kinetic energy, gap structure, and local transition behavior [@jeon2025pattern].

### Key frontier questions

**Does quasiperiodicity suppress, enhance, or pattern superconductivity?** The answer may depend on whether quasiperiodicity produces localization, criticality, high density of states, spectral gaps, or favorable local environments.

**Can superconducting order be local-pattern-selective?** In periodic crystals, every unit cell is equivalent.
In a quasicrystal, many local patches recur with well-defined frequencies.
Pairing may track those patches.

**What is the correct pairing taxonomy without crystal momentum?** Classifying superconducting order parameters by lattice irreducible representations becomes subtler in quasicrystals.

**Can quasicrystalline superconductors host topological superconductivity?** This is a natural intersection of superconductivity, quasiperiodicity, and higher-order topology.

## 9. Frontier: moiré quasicrystals and twisted multilayers

### Core issue

Moiré systems give experimentalists knobs: twist angle, layer number, displacement field, pressure, and carrier density.
If multiple moiré patterns are mutually incommensurate, a tunable quasicrystal emerges from periodic layers.

### What changed recently

Uri and collaborators introduced a tunable moiré quasicrystal by twisting three graphene layers with two different twist angles, forming mutually incommensurate moiré patterns; they observed superconductivity and strong interactions [@uri2023moire].
Hao and collaborators used STM/STS to characterize flat bands in twisted trilayer graphene moiré quasicrystals [@hao2024flatbands].
Ghadimi and collaborators studied quasiperiodic pairing in graphene quasicrystals [@ghadimi2025pairing].

### Key frontier questions

**Can quasiperiodicity be an independent control knob for correlated phases?** The magic-angle story shows that moiré periodicity can create flat bands.
The quasicrystal story asks whether incommensurate moiré patterns create broader “magic continua,” critical states, or robust high-density-of-states structures.

**How do electronic states interpolate between periodic-like and quasiperiodic regimes?** Moiré quasicrystals can be tuned by energy and density.
Some regimes may look periodic-like; others may be strongly quasiperiodic.

**Can quasicrystalline moiré systems host superconductivity without fine-tuned magic angles?** If quasiperiodicity produces networks or filaments of enhanced density of states, superconductivity may be more robust than in sharply tuned periodic moiré systems.

**What replaces band theory?** For large or incommensurate moiré systems, millions of atoms and lack of exact periodicity force new numerical and theoretical approaches.

## 10. Frontier: topological quasicrystals

### Core issue

Topological phases were first understood through crystalline band theory and Brillouin zones.
Quasicrystals lack ordinary Bloch momentum, but they can still host topological invariants, boundary modes, pumps, and higher-dimensional topological structures.

### What changed recently

Varjas and collaborators theoretically constructed topological phases protected by quasicrystalline rotational symmetries, including phases without crystalline counterparts [@varjas2019topological].
A 2025 preprint reported experimental exploration of an acoustic Ammann-Beenker quasicrystal model with eight zero-energy corner modes, presenting it as an observation of topological phases without crystalline counterparts [@observation2025topological].

Tsesses and collaborators reported four-dimensional conserved topological charge vectors governing real-space topology in two-dimensional quasicrystals, emphasizing that quasicrystals naturally encode higher-dimensional information [@tsesses2025fourdim].
A 2026 preprint proposes Berry-phase-based topological charges in two-dimensional quasicrystals, including charges not accessible in conventional periodic systems [@liu2026berry].

### Key frontier questions

**What is topology without Bloch momentum?** The answers include real-space invariants, noncommutative geometry, higher-dimensional ancestors, rotational-symmetry invariants, and topological pumps.

**Which topological phases are intrinsically quasicrystalline?** A phase is especially interesting if no periodic crystal with comparable symmetry can realize it.

**Can phasons pump topology?** In many quasicrystal models, phason shifts behave like synthetic dimensions.
Varying them can produce topological pumping.

**Can topological protection survive structural disorder and finite patches?** Real quasicrystalline devices are finite and imperfect.
Edge, corner, and defect modes must be robust under realistic fabrication constraints.

## 11. Frontier: photonic, acoustic, mechanical, and polaritonic quasicrystals

### Core issue

Quasicrystals are powerful wave-control media.
Their forbidden rotational symmetries and dense reciprocal modules can produce optical, acoustic, and mechanical effects unavailable in periodic lattices.

### What changed recently

Arjas, Taskinen, and Törmä demonstrated high topological charge lasing in quasicrystals, including charges −5, +7, −17, and +19 [@arjas2024lasing].
The essential point is that two-dimensional Bravais lattices cannot support the same high rotational symmetries, while quasicrystalline designs can.

Alyatkin and collaborators demonstrated quantum fluids of light in a two-dimensional artificial reconfigurable aperiodic crystal based on a Penrose tiling, with exciton-polariton condensation and long-range phase synchronization [@alyatkin2025polariton].

### Key frontier questions

**Can photonic quasicrystals outperform periodic photonic crystals?** Promising areas include isotropic bandgaps, vortex lasers, flat-band-like lasing, robust cavity modes, and multidirectional wave control.

**Can reconfigurable optical quasicrystals emulate quantum matter?** Polaritonic and cold-atom platforms let researchers tune coupling, gain/loss, interactions, and nonlinearity.

**Can quasicrystals realize higher-dimensional topological photonics?** Because quasicrystals are often projections of higher-dimensional periodic structures, they naturally suggest synthetic dimensions.

**Can acoustic and mechanical quasicrystals produce robust topological modes?** These platforms are experimentally accessible and allow large, directly measurable finite quasicrystal samples.

## 12. Frontier: localization, critical states, gaps, and transport

### Core issue

Quasiperiodic systems live between periodic and random systems.
Wavefunctions can be extended, localized, or critical/multifractal.
This makes quasicrystals ideal for studying transport regimes that ordinary Bloch theory and ordinary Anderson localization do not capture.

### What changed recently

Gottlob and collaborators studied the origin of energy gaps in quasicrystalline potentials, showing how true gaps can arise in an eightfold optical quasicrystal through resonant hybridization and configuration-space structure [@gottlob2026gaps].

Padhan, Colbois, Alet, and Laflorencie studied long-range resonances in quasiperiodic many-body localization, identifying anomalous eigenstates with long-range correlations even where standard diagnostics suggest stable localization [@padhan2026mbl].

### Key frontier questions

**What is the taxonomy of states?** Periodic systems have Bloch waves; random systems have Anderson-localized states; quasicrystals can have critical states with fractal scaling.

**Can true spectral gaps be predicted analytically?** This matters for cold atoms, photonics, phononics, and electronic quasicrystals.

**Does quasiperiodic many-body localization really avoid Griffiths effects?** Quasiperiodic potentials lack random rare regions, but long-range resonances may create different kinds of instability.

**How do transport coefficients depend on local environment distributions?** Conductivity, thermal transport, and wave transmission may depend on recurring local patches rather than periodic unit cells.

## 13. Frontier: hyperuniformity, gyromorphs, and competitors to quasicrystalline order

### Core issue

Quasicrystals are not the only useful form of aperiodic order.
Hyperuniform disordered materials suppress long-wavelength density fluctuations.
Gyromorphs and other correlated disordered systems can mimic or outperform quasicrystals for certain wave-control tasks.

### What changed recently

Jeon and collaborators studied delocalization induced by enhanced hyperuniformity in one-dimensional disordered systems, showing that strong hyperuniformity can produce delocalization transitions and mobility edges [@jeon2026hyperuniform].
Casiulis, Shih, and Martiniani introduced gyromorphs as correlated disordered materials with liquid-like translational disorder but quasi-long-range rotational order, designed to open isotropic bandgaps [@casiulis2025gyromorphs].

### Key frontier questions

**When is a quasicrystal better than hyperuniform disorder?** For diffraction purity, quasicrystals may win.
For isotropic bandgaps or fabrication tolerance, correlated disorder may sometimes win.

**Can we classify aperiodic order by function rather than by geometry?** For materials design, the relevant axis may be “wave-control performance” rather than “quasicrystal versus not quasicrystal.”

**Are gyromorphs Fourier-dual relatives of quasicrystals?** This is a provocative structural question: both involve sharp features in reciprocal space, but arranged differently.

**Can hybrid systems combine quasiperiodicity and hyperuniformity?** A design could use quasiperiodic long-range order with controlled disorder to broaden robustness.

## 14. Frontier: mathematical diffraction, model sets, and inverse problems

### Core issue

Mathematical quasicrystal theory asks which Delone sets, tilings, substitutions, or dynamical systems produce diffraction like physical quasicrystals.
The canonical case is pure point diffraction from regular model sets, but the frontier is broader: mixed spectra, random substitutions, singular continuous components, non-FLC systems, and inverse diffraction.

### What changed recently

Richard and Strungaru’s guide remains a useful baseline for pure point diffraction in cut-and-project sets [@richard2017guide].
Recent work pushes beyond standard examples.
Baake, Gähler, Mazáč, and Mitchell computed diffraction for Hat and Spectre tilings and relatives using model-set representatives and exact renormalization in internal space [@baake2025hatdiffraction].
Baake, Gähler, Klick, Mañibo, and Mazáč developed renormalization techniques for inflation systems and applications to diffraction [@baake2026renormalization].
A 2026 preprint on quasicrystal model sets from overlapping self-similar iterated function systems gives a computational way to combine cut-and-project methods with self-similarity [@popov2026modelsets].

### Key frontier questions

**What exactly does diffraction forget?** Different structures can be homometric: same diffraction, different arrangement.
The inverse problem is underdetermined unless additional data are used.

**Can renormalization compute diffraction for broad classes of substitutions?** This is important for monotiles, inflation tilings, and systems with fractal windows.

**How do defects and randomization alter pure point spectra?** Physical samples are not perfect.
A realistic theory needs Bragg peaks, diffuse scattering, phason disorder, and finite-size effects together.

**Can new invariants go beyond cohomology and diffraction?** Tiling cohomology, dynamical spectrum, orbit separation dimension, model-set windows, and factor maps all capture different information.

## 15. Frontier: Hat, Spectre, monotiles, and the boundary of quasicrystallinity

### Core issue

The Hat and Spectre monotiles changed aperiodic tiling theory.
They are not simply classical Penrose quasicrystals, but recent work shows they have unexpectedly strong quasiperiodic and diffraction structure.

### What changed recently

Smith, Myers, Kaplan, and Goodman-Strauss introduced the Hat monotile family [@smith2023hat].
Socolar argued that the structure enforced by the Hat constraints is quasiperiodic with hexagonal symmetry [@socolar2023hat].
Baake and collaborators analyzed long-range order and diffraction of Hat/Spectre systems, including pure-point spectra and model-set representatives [@baake2025spectre; @baake2025hatdiffraction].

### Key frontier questions

**Which monotile systems are quasicrystalline?** A monotile can force aperiodicity without obviously being a model set.
The question is whether its tiling hull has pure point spectrum, cut-and-project descriptions, or only weaker order.

**Can shape alone encode matching rules and internal space?** Hat/Spectre tilings raise the possibility that geometric constraints can enforce the same information that older constructions encoded with colors, arrows, or decorations.

**Are Hat/Spectre systems exceptional or the beginning of a large class?** If the latter, monotile discovery becomes a route to new quasicrystalline families.

**How should “quasicrystal” be defined for monotile tilings?** Possible criteria include pure point diffraction, model-set conjugacy, Meyer property, quasiperiodic coordinate systems, inflation hierarchy, and spectral purity.

## 16. Frontier: natural, planetary, shock, and lightning quasicrystals

### Core issue

Natural quasicrystals are rare but scientifically rich because they encode extreme formation conditions.
The Khatyrka meteorite is central, but shock synthesis and lightning-like formation pathways broaden the landscape.

### What changed recently

Stagno and collaborators reviewed quasicrystals at high pressures and temperatures, emphasizing natural quasicrystals such as icosahedrite and decagonite and the role of extreme conditions [@stagno2023extreme].
Takakura and collaborators’ study of natural icosahedrite showed phason-wave modulation that may help reconstruct formation conditions [@takakura2025icosahedrite].

### Key frontier questions

**Can quasicrystals serve as shock-history tracers?** Phason strain, modulation wavelength, composition, and associated minerals may encode pressure-temperature-time histories.

**Are natural quasicrystals more common than currently known?** Meteorites, impact glasses, fulgurites, nuclear-test debris, and industrial accident products may contain unrecognized quasicrystalline grains.

**Can shock synthesis become a deliberate materials route?** If high-pressure/high-temperature events stabilize phases unreachable by equilibrium metallurgy, shock synthesis could be used intentionally.

**Can natural quasicrystals teach us stability principles?** Extreme formation environments may reveal compositions and pathways missed by ordinary lab synthesis.

## 17. Frontier: oxide, surface, and two-dimensional quasicrystals

### Core issue

Two-dimensional quasicrystalline surfaces are attractive because they can be studied with STM, LEED, SXRD, photoemission, and controlled adsorption.
They also provide templates for epitaxy, catalysis, magnetic arrays, and electronic states.

### What changed recently

Schenk and collaborators solved aspects of the transformation from a two-dimensional honeycomb oxide into a dodecagonal quasicrystal driven by electrostatic forces [@schenk2022oxide].
Haller and collaborators reported a host-atom-driven transformation of a honeycomb oxide into a dodecagonal quasicrystal: Ba, Sr, or Eu adsorption reorganizes the honeycomb oxide layer into a dodecagonal tiling [@haller2026oxide].
The Eu case is especially suggestive because it extends oxide quasicrystals toward lanthanide magnetic-moment grids.

### Key frontier questions

**Can 2D quasicrystals be fabricated with atomic precision?** Surface systems are among the best candidates for controlled atomic-scale quasicrystal engineering.

**Can quasicrystalline surfaces template other materials?** A quasicrystalline substrate could impose quasiperiodic order on molecular layers, magnetic atoms, superconductors, or 2D materials.

**Can oxide quasicrystals become functional materials?** Possible areas include catalysis, work-function engineering, magnetic moment arrays, and aperiodic electronic lattices.

**Can host-atom transformation generalize beyond transition-metal oxides?** The proposed extensions to honeycomb-like materials such as graphene, silica, or ice-like networks are speculative but compelling [@haller2026oxide].

## 18. Frontier: time quasicrystals and spacetime quasicrystals

### Core issue

The word “quasicrystal” is being generalized beyond static spatial order.
Some systems exhibit quasiperiodic temporal order; others propose geometric analogues in spacetime.

### What changed recently

He and collaborators experimentally realized discrete time quasicrystals using a quasiperiodically driven, strongly interacting spin ensemble in diamond, reporting long-lived responses at multiple incommensurate frequencies [@he2025time].
Luo and collaborators proposed discrete time quasi-crystal behavior in Rydberg atomic chains by coupling two discrete time crystals with incommensurate driving frequencies [@luo2026rydberg].
Boyle and Mygdalas proposed Lorentzian analogues of Penrose/Ammann-Beenker-like self-similar quasicrystals in Minkowski spacetime [@boyle2026spacetime].

### Key frontier questions

**Is quasiperiodic order a spatial phenomenon or a general ordering principle?** Time quasicrystals suggest that quasiperiodicity can organize non-equilibrium dynamics, not just atoms in space.

**Which temporal quasicrystals are genuine phases rather than driven responses?** The distinction matters.
Robustness, symmetry breaking, lifetime, and thermodynamic-limit behavior are central.

**Can spacetime quasicrystals be more than formal geometry?** The spacetime constructions are speculative.
Their value may be mathematical even if they do not describe physical spacetime.

**Can time-quasiperiodic drives simulate higher-dimensional quasicrystal physics?** Multiple incommensurate frequencies often act as synthetic dimensions.

## 19. Cross-cutting open problems

### 19.1 A nonperiodic thermodynamics toolkit

Build first-principles methods that compute energy, entropy, surface energy, and nucleation barriers directly for nonperiodic quasicrystals rather than approximants.
The Baek et al. nanoparticle-extrapolation approach is one major path [@baek2025stability].

### 19.2 Phason engineering

Turn phason modes from descriptive variables into controllable knobs for growth, wave propagation, defect healing, topology, and memory.

### 19.3 Minimal interaction design

For a target quasicrystal, determine the simplest particle, molecular, photonic, or mechanical interaction that assembles it reliably.

### 19.4 Unified theory of quasiperiodic electronic states

Develop a classification of extended, localized, critical, multifractal, superconducting, and topological states without relying on Bloch theory.

### 19.5 Moiré quasicrystal phase diagrams

Map the dependence of correlated states on twist angles, density, displacement field, relaxation, and interlayer coupling in genuinely incommensurate multilayer systems.

### 19.6 Topological taxonomy without periodicity

Unify real-space invariants, noncommutative invariants, higher-dimensional ancestors, phason pumps, and rotational/topological charge descriptions.

### 19.7 Diffraction inverse theory

Characterize what can and cannot be recovered from diffraction, especially for systems with phason disorder, finite-size effects, or homometric alternatives.

### 19.8 Functional comparison with hyperuniform disorder

For each application—photonic bandgaps, waveguiding, thermal transport, lasing, mechanical response—determine whether periodic crystals, quasicrystals, hyperuniform disorder, or gyromorphs are best.

### 19.9 Physical realization of monotile/quasicrystal hybrids

Translate mathematical monotiles, matching rules, and substitutions into physical particles or metamaterials.

### 19.10 Natural quasicrystal archaeology

Search extreme-condition materials for quasicrystals and use their phason/defect structure to infer formation histories.

## 21. Short glossary

**Approximant:** A periodic crystal locally similar to a quasicrystal. **Cut-and-project set:** A structure obtained by projecting points from a higher-dimensional lattice through an acceptance window. **Diffraction spectrum:** Fourier-space scattering measure; pure point spectra correspond to Bragg peaks. **FLC:** Finite local complexity; only finitely many patches up to translation within any bounded size. **Meyer set:** A Delone set with strong arithmetic regularity; central in mathematical quasicrystal theory. **Phason:** Internal-space rearrangement degree of freedom in a quasicrystal. **Pure point diffraction:** Diffraction consisting of sharp Bragg peaks. **Quasiperiodic:** Ordered by incommensurate frequencies or projections rather than periodic translations. **Random tiling:** A tiling ensemble with nonperiodic order but often entropic/disordered features. **Tiling hull:** Closure of all translates of a tiling under the local topology.

---

## 22. Bottom line

The frontier around aperiodic quasicrystals is becoming less about whether nonperiodic order is possible and more about whether it is **programmable**.
The live problems ask how to compute nonperiodic stability, how to exploit phasons, how to machine-learn new alloys, how to assemble soft and colloidal quasicrystals, how superconductivity and magnetism work without translational symmetry, how moiré systems make tunable quasiperiodic quantum matter, how topology survives without Brillouin zones, and how mathematical diffraction can distinguish genuinely quasicrystalline order from the broader zoo of aperiodic structures.

In one sentence:

> Quasicrystals are becoming a general technology of finite control over infinite nonperiodic order.
