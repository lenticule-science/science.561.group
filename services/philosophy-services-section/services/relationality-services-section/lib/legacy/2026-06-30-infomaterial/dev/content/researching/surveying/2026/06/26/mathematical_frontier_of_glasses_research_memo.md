# Executive orientation

The word **glass** names a family of mathematical pathologies more than a single phase.  A glass is a many-degree-of-freedom system whose observed state is solid-like on accessible time scales while lacking the periodic translational order that defines a crystal.  The mathematical core is therefore not merely disorder.  It is the coexistence of local constraints or short/medium-range order with a configuration space whose connected components, bottlenecks, metastable basins, and relaxation time scales are nontrivial functions of system size, dimension, temperature, density, preparation protocol, and observation time.

This memo treats glass as an object at the intersection of equilibrium statistical mechanics, nonequilibrium dynamics, probability, geometry, topology, optimization, and computation.  The most developed rigorous mathematics is mean-field spin glass theory: the Sherrington--Kirkpatrick model, mixed $p$-spin models, the Parisi variational formula, Guerra interpolation, Talagrand's proof, and Panchenko's ultrametricity theorem [@sherrington1975; @parisi1979; @guerra2003; @talagrand2006; @panchenko2013ultra; @panchenko2013book].  The most physically central but least rigorous object is the finite-dimensional structural glass: an interacting-particle system whose relaxation time grows by many orders of magnitude without an obvious crystalline order parameter [@debenedetti2001; @berthier2011].  The most mathematically productive bridge between these regimes is mean-field structural glass theory, especially $p$-spin/RFOT analogies, high-dimensional hard spheres, Gardner marginality, and jamming [@crisanti1992; @crisanti1993; @parisi2010hard; @charbonneau2017; @kurchan2013].

The 2020--2026 frontier is not a single consensus program.  It includes: rigorous extension of spin-glass formulas and algorithmic consequences [@mourrat2025; @chatterjee2026; @huang2024sampling; @gamarnik2025; @montanari2026]; numerical access to deeper supercooling through swap and irreversible Monte Carlo [@ninarello2017; @scalliet2022; @ghimenti2024; @boehmer2025; @simon2026]; renewed tests of random first-order transition theory and density-functional formulations [@biroli2023rfot; @kirkpatrick2024]; Gardner and marginal stability as a link between glass and jamming [@jin2022; @wang2024gardner; @deng2024; @shang2024yielding]; topological data analysis of amorphous order [@hiraoka2016; @sorensen2020; @minamitani2025; @wu2025topdefects]; and machine-learning approaches that expose the latent information in static structure while raising new questions about transferability, interpretability, and universality [@bapst2020; @jung2023; @jung2024transfer; @jung2025roadmap].

The organizing principle of this memo is: **a glass is a many-body system whose relevant order parameter is not a low-dimensional symmetry-breaking field, but a relation among configurations, histories, and constraints**.  This relation appears as an overlap distribution in spin glasses, a point-to-set length or configurational entropy in structural glasses, a rigidity/contact network in jamming, a persistence diagram in topological analyses, a dynamical activity field in kinetically constrained models, and a metastable-state graph in landscape theory.

# 1. Core mathematical object

## 1.1 A working definition

Let $N$ degrees of freedom live in a configuration space $\Omega_N$.  For structural glass formers, $\Omega_N$ is typically
\[
  \Omega_N = (\Lambda^N / S_N) \setminus \{\text{forbidden overlaps}\},
\]
where $\Lambda \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ is a periodic box and $S_N$ quotients identical particles.  For spin glasses, $\Omega_N=\{-1,+1\}^N$ or the sphere $S^{N-1}(\sqrt N)$.  For jammed hard particles, $\Omega_N$ is a semialgebraic set defined by non-overlap inequalities.  The system carries either an energy $H_N:\Omega_N\to\mathbb{R}$, a hard-constraint feasible set $\mathcal F_N\subset \Omega_N$, a stochastic dynamics with generator $\mathcal L_N$, or all three.

A **glassy regime** is present when the following properties coexist.

1. **No global crystalline order.**  The one-particle density or spin expectation does not encode a periodic lattice.  For particle systems, the structure factor $S(k)$ may have broad peaks and local motifs but not the Bragg spectrum of a crystal.  For spin systems, disorder-averaged magnetization may vanish even though overlaps between replicas are nontrivial.
2. **Local or medium-range order.**  The system is not an independent random configuration.  Pair correlations, bond-orientational correlations, locally preferred structures, topological cycles, contact networks, or amorphous elastic domains can be statistically reproducible [@hiraoka2016; @sorensen2020; @minamitani2025].
3. **Frustration and constraints.**  Local preferences cannot be simultaneously satisfied globally.  This can arise from quenched random couplings in spin glasses, competing local packing motifs in liquids, excluded volume in hard spheres, or kinetic rules in constrained dynamics.
4. **Metastability and ruggedness.**  The energy/free-energy landscape has many basins, barriers, saddles, and marginal directions.  A basin is not necessarily a topological connected component; it can be a metastable region whose escape time is exponentially or super-Arrheniusly large on the observation scale.
5. **Slow nonequilibrium dynamics.**  Relaxation times $\tau_\alpha$ become very large compared with microscopic times.  Below laboratory glass transition temperature $T_g$, the system falls out of equilibrium; $T_g$ is protocol-dependent, not a sharp thermodynamic point in ordinary finite-time experiments [@ediger1996; @debenedetti2001].
6. **Aging and memory.**  Observables depend on waiting time $t_w$, thermal/mechanical history, and preparation.  Mathematically this means that time-translation invariance and the equilibrium fluctuation-dissipation theorem need not apply.

The word **glass transition** is ambiguous.  In experiments, it often denotes a kinetic crossover at which viscosity or relaxation time exceeds the observation window.  In mean-field theories, it may denote a dynamical transition $T_d$ where metastable states first trap dynamics, a Kauzmann or ideal glass transition $T_K$ where configurational entropy extrapolates to zero, a Gardner transition inside the glass phase where a basin fractures hierarchically, or a jamming point at zero temperature and finite pressure/density [@gotze2009; @kirkpatrick1987; @parisi2010hard; @charbonneau2017; @jin2022].

## 1.2 Order without crystallinity

A crystal is described by broken continuous translation and rotation symmetries.  Its order parameter is approximately a density field
\[
  \rho(x)=\rho_0+\sum_{G\in L^*\setminus\{0\}} \rho_G e^{iG\cdot x}
\]
with reciprocal lattice vectors $G$.  A structural glass lacks such long-range periodic density order, but it may have:

- **Two-point translational order:** pair correlation $g(r)$ and structure factor $S(k)$ have reproducible shells.
- **Orientational order:** bond-order invariants such as Steinhardt--Nelson--Ronchetti $Q_l,W_l$ detect local symmetries not equivalent to crystallinity.
- **Amorphous order:** correlations between equilibrium configurations under boundary pinning or overlap fields; the point-to-set length is a central example in RFOT-inspired analysis [@berthier2011; @biroli2023rfot].
- **Topological order of point clouds:** persistent homology records robust loops/cavities across length scales and has been used to identify medium-range order in silica, metallic glasses, and amorphous silicon [@hiraoka2016; @sorensen2020; @hirata2020; @minamitani2025].
- **Mechanical order:** contact networks, isostaticity, force-chain statistics, elastic moduli, nonaffine response, and vibrational spectra in jammed or amorphous solids [@ohern2003; @wyart2005; @lerner2021].

Thus **amorphous** does not mean structureless.  A Poisson point process is random but not a realistic glass.  A glassy configuration is better viewed as a constrained random object: it has suppressed overlaps, local motifs, medium-range correlations, and long-lived dynamical correlations.

## 1.3 Structural glass, spin glass, amorphous solid, jamming, random medium, liquid, quasicrystal

The following distinctions are mathematical rather than taxonomic.

| Regime | Typical space | Disorder | Relevant order parameter | Dynamics | What makes it glassy? |
|---|---:|---|---|---|---|
| Crystal | particle configurations modulo Euclidean symmetry | none or weak | Fourier density modes, lattice defects | phonons, diffusion of defects | not glassy; long-range periodic order |
| Quasicrystal | aperiodic but ordered tiling/model set | deterministic aperiodicity | pure-point diffraction with noncrystallographic symmetry | phasons, defects | not glassy by default; global quasiperiodic order |
| Equilibrium liquid | particle configurations | annealed thermal disorder | $g(r)$, $S(k)$ only short-ranged | mixing/ergodic on accessible times | not arrested |
| Supercooled liquid | particle configurations | annealed disorder, suppressed crystallization | dynamic heterogeneity, growing amorphous correlations | very slow but still equilibrated if simulated long enough | approaches glassy arrest |
| Structural glass | particle configurations/history-dependent ensemble | annealed disorder frozen by kinetics | overlap, amorphous order, configurational entropy | aging, activated hops | arrested amorphous solid |
| Spin glass | spin configurations, usually quenched random couplings | quenched disorder | overlap distribution $P(q)$, RSB structure | slow relaxation/aging | frustration plus many pure/metastable states |
| Amorphous solid | inherent structures or mechanically stable particle networks | structural disorder | elastic moduli, vibrational spectrum, nonaffine fields | solid response; plastic events under drive | mechanically rigid without lattice |
| Jammed matter | feasible set of hard constraints/contact network | disorder may be annealed or protocol-generated | contact number, force network, rigidity matrix | athermal, constrained | onset of rigidity at marginality |
| Random medium | fields/particles in quenched random environment | quenched spatial disorder | sample-dependent observables | depends on model | disorder alone need not imply glassiness |
| Plasma | charged particles with long-range forces | thermal/disorder possible | Debye screening, collective modes | kinetic/Vlasov/Boltzmann | typically fluid; glassiness only in special strongly coupled regimes |

The structural glass/spin glass analogy is profound but limited.  Spin glasses have explicit quenched disorder in the Hamiltonian; structural glasses usually do not.  Mean-field structural glass theory often replaces self-generated disorder by replicas and metastable amorphous states, making the analogy operational but not literally identical [@kirkpatrick1987; @biroli2023rfot].

## 1.4 Slow dynamics, kinetic arrest, aging, memory

The standard structural relaxation time $\tau_\alpha$ is defined from an intermediate scattering function or overlap correlation.  For example,
\[
F_s(k,t)=\frac1N \sum_{j=1}^N \left\langle e^{i k\cdot (r_j(t)-r_j(0))}\right\rangle,
\qquad F_s(k,\tau_\alpha)=e^{-1}F_s(k,0).
\]
Glassy slowdown is not simply diffusion with a large constant.  It is spatially heterogeneous: some regions are mobile while others are immobile.  A common four-point susceptibility is
\[
\chi_4(t)=N\left( \langle Q(t)^2\rangle-\langle Q(t)\rangle^2\right),
\qquad
Q(t)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N w(|r_i(t)-r_i(0)|),
\]
where $w$ is a window function.  Peaks of $\chi_4(t)$ define dynamic heterogeneity length and time scales [@berthier2011; @dauchot2023].

Aging is captured by two-time functions.  For an observable $A$, define
\[
C(t,t_w)=\langle A(t_w+t)A(t_w)\rangle-\langle A(t_w+t)\rangle\langle A(t_w)\rangle.
\]
Equilibrium gives $C(t,t_w)=C(t)$ and, for conjugate response $R(t)$, fluctuation-dissipation relations.  Aging systems show $C(t,t_w)$ depending on both arguments.  The fluctuation-dissipation ratio
\[
X(t,t_w)=\frac{T R(t,t_w)}{\partial C(t,t_w)/\partial t_w}
\]
need not equal $1$; in mean-field aging dynamics this ratio is tied to effective temperatures and RSB structure, but finite-dimensional experimental interpretation remains delicate.

Memory means that the state cannot be summarized by instantaneous thermodynamic variables.  Protocol variables -- waiting time, cooling rate, shear history, annealing depth, pressure path -- are part of the mathematical state description.  In structural glasses, the measure sampled at time $t$ is therefore often not a Gibbs measure $\mu_{\beta,N}\propto e^{-\beta H_N}$ but a nonstationary law $\nu_{t,N}^{\rm protocol}$.

# 2. Foundational models

## 2.1 Sherrington--Kirkpatrick model

The SK model is the canonical mean-field spin glass.  Spins $\sigma_i\in\{-1,+1\}$ have Hamiltonian
\[
H_N(\sigma)=-\sum_{1\le i<j\le N}J_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j-h\sum_{i=1}^N\sigma_i,
\qquad J_{ij}\sim \mathcal N(0,1/N),
\]
with quenched Gaussian couplings.  The free energy density is
\[
 f_N(\beta,h)=-\frac{1}{\beta N}\mathbb E_J\log Z_N(\beta,h),
 \qquad Z_N=\sum_{\sigma} e^{-\beta H_N(\sigma)}.
\]
The natural order parameter is the replica overlap
\[
q_{12}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \sigma_i^{(1)}\sigma_i^{(2)}.
\]
At high temperature the replica-symmetric ansatz is stable.  At low temperature it fails; the Parisi solution uses a probability measure $\zeta$ on $[0,1]$ or a nondecreasing function $x(q)$ as an infinite-dimensional order parameter [@sherrington1975; @parisi1979; @mezard1987].

**Captures:** quenched frustration, many pure states, overlap distributions, ultrametric state organization, exact mean-field free energy.  **Fails:** short-range geometry, particle packing, self-generated disorder, realistic structural relaxation, activation in finite dimension.

## 2.2 Edwards--Anderson model

The Edwards--Anderson model is the short-range spin glass:
\[
H_{\Lambda}(\sigma)=-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\subset\Lambda}J_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j-h\sum_{i\in\Lambda}\sigma_i,
\qquad J_{ij}\text{ i.i.d. with mean }0.
\]
Here $\Lambda\subset\mathbb Z^d$ and only nearest neighbors interact [@edwards1975].  It is physically closer to magnetic spin glasses than SK, but mathematically much harder.  Competing pictures include droplet/scaling and replica-symmetry-breaking-like structures.  Rigorous results are fragmentary compared with SK, especially in low finite dimensions.

**Captures:** spatial locality, quenched disorder, domain-wall excitations.  **Fails:** exact solvability; no accepted complete description of low-temperature Gibbs states in physical dimensions.

## 2.3 Mixed and pure $p$-spin models

The mixed $p$-spin Hamiltonian on Ising or spherical configurations is
\[
H_N(\sigma)=\sum_{p\ge 2}\beta_p H_{N,p}(\sigma),\qquad
H_{N,p}(\sigma)=N^{-(p-1)/2}\sum_{i_1,
\ldots,i_p}g_{i_1\cdots i_p}\sigma_{i_1}\cdots\sigma_{i_p}.
\]
For the spherical model, $\sigma\in S^{N-1}(\sqrt N)$.  The covariance is
\[
\mathbb E H_N(\sigma^1)H_N(\sigma^2)=N\xi(R_{12}),
\qquad R_{12}=N^{-1}\sigma^1\cdot\sigma^2,
\quad \xi(x)=\sum_p\beta_p^2 x^p.
\]
Pure $p\ge 3$ spherical models display a one-step RSB structure and have become central models for both structural glass phenomenology and random landscape geometry [@crisanti1992; @crisanti1993; @auffinger2013complexity; @subag2017].

**Captures:** a separation between dynamical and static transitions, exponentially many stationary points, RFOT-like phenomenology, tractable random landscapes.  **Fails:** finite-dimensional activation, explicit local structure, conservation laws, hydrodynamics, particle geometry.

## 2.4 Random energy model

Derrida's REM assigns independent Gaussian energies $E_\sigma$ to the $2^N$ configurations:
\[
 Z_N(\beta)=\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}^N} e^{-\beta E_\sigma},
 \qquad E_\sigma\sim \mathcal N(0,N/2).
\]
The model has a freezing transition when the entropy of states at the dominant energy vanishes [@derrida1981].  It is the analytically simplest example of entropy crisis and extreme-value-dominated thermodynamics.

**Captures:** energy-level statistics, entropy crisis, frozen phase.  **Fails:** correlations between nearby configurations, geometry of paths/barriers, realistic dynamics.

## 2.5 Structural glass particle models

A standard structural glass model has particle coordinates $r_i\in\Lambda\subset\mathbb R^d$ and Hamiltonian
\[
H_N(r_1,
\ldots,r_N)=\sum_{i<j} v_{\alpha_i\alpha_j}(|r_i-r_j|),
\]
where particle types $\alpha_i$ are chosen to frustrate crystallization.  Examples include the Kob--Andersen binary Lennard-Jones mixture [@kob1995], polydisperse soft spheres, hard-sphere mixtures, and molecular models.  The overlap between two configurations $X,Y$ can be defined by
\[
 Q(X,Y)=\frac1N\sum_{i,j} w(|r_i^X-r_j^Y|),
\]
where $w$ is short-ranged and species-aware.

**Captures:** self-generated disorder, supercooling, dynamical heterogeneity, aging, realistic local structure.  **Fails:** exact solvability; finite simulation time; crystallization unless carefully suppressed; difficulty equilibrating near experimental $T_g$ without advanced algorithms.

## 2.6 Hard-sphere glasses and high-dimensional mean-field theory

Hard spheres of diameter $D$ have potential
\[
 v(r)=\begin{cases}0,&r\ge D,\\ +\infty,& r<D.
 \end{cases}
\]
Their thermodynamics is entropic.  The configuration space is the complement of collision cylinders.  The packing fraction $\varphi$ replaces inverse temperature as the control parameter.  Replica computations in $d\to\infty$ give a mean-field theory of the glass and jamming transitions, including dynamical arrest, ideal glass behavior, Gardner marginality, and jamming criticality [@parisi2010hard; @kurchan2013; @charbonneau2017].

**Captures:** packing-dominated glassiness, jamming, high-dimensional exact mean-field structure.  **Fails:** direct exactness in $d=2,3$; hydrodynamics and crystallization can matter; finite-dimensional activated processes may restore ergodicity below mean-field dynamical transitions.

## 2.7 Mode-coupling theory

Mode-coupling theory (MCT) starts from density correlators.  For a normalized intermediate scattering function $\phi_k(t)$, a schematic form is
\[
 \ddot\phi_k(t)+\nu_k\dot\phi_k(t)+\Omega_k^2\phi_k(t)
 +\Omega_k^2\int_0^t m_k(t-s)\dot\phi_k(s)\,ds=0,
\]
where the memory kernel $m_k$ is approximated as a quadratic functional of density correlators and static structure factors [@leutheusser1984; @bengtzelius1984; @gotze2009; @janssen2018].  MCT predicts a dynamical singularity at $T_c$ or $\varphi_c$ with two-step relaxation and scaling laws.

**Captures:** early supercooling, plateau formation, two-step relaxation, semi-quantitative dynamic scaling.  **Fails:** the ideal MCT singularity is rounded in real finite-dimensional systems by activated processes; it does not by itself solve aging, configurational entropy, or deep glass thermodynamics.

## 2.8 Random first-order transition theory

RFOT combines mean-field $p$-spin/RFOT phenomenology with finite-dimensional droplet or mosaic arguments [@kirkpatrick1987; @lubchenko2007; @biroli2023rfot].  The basic free-energy cost for rearranging a region of size $\ell$ is modeled as
\[
\Delta F(\ell)\sim \Upsilon \ell^{\theta}-T s_c \ell^d,
\]
where $\Upsilon$ is an amorphous surface tension, $s_c$ the configurational entropy density, and $\theta$ an exponent for mismatch cost.  Optimizing gives a mosaic length and activated barrier.  In mean-field, the dynamical transition $T_d$ and static Kauzmann transition $T_K$ are sharp; in finite dimensions, $T_d$ is expected to become a crossover because activation restores ergodicity.

**Captures:** entropy crisis, metastable states, activated mosaic relaxation, a possible thermodynamic ideal glass transition, links to point-to-set correlations.  **Fails:** rigorous control in finite dimension; quantitative ambiguity in $s_c,\Upsilon,\theta$; disputed experimental uniqueness.

## 2.9 Kinetically constrained models

Kinetically constrained models (KCMs) put trivial or nearly trivial equilibrium measures together with constrained Markov dynamics.  A simple Fredrickson--Andersen model has spins $n_i\in\{0,1\}$ with product equilibrium measure and transitions allowed only if nearby facilitation constraints are satisfied [@fredrickson1984; @ritort2003; @hartarsky2025].  A generator has the form
\[
(\mathcal L f)(\eta)=\sum_i c_i(\eta)\left[p(1-\eta_i)+(1-p)\eta_i\right]
\left(f(\eta^i)-f(\eta)\right),
\]
where $c_i(\eta)$ is a kinetic constraint and $\eta^i$ flips site $i$.

**Captures:** dynamic facilitation, heterogeneous mobility, large-deviation transitions in trajectory space, slow dynamics without thermodynamic singularity [@jack2007; @chandler2010].  **Fails:** static amorphous order by construction; microscopic structural realism; thermodynamic glass transition mechanisms.

## 2.10 Jamming models

For frictionless spheres with centers $r_i$ and radii $R_i$, non-overlap constraints are
\[
 |r_i-r_j|\ge R_i+R_j.
\]
Soft-sphere jamming often uses repulsions
\[
V=\sum_{i<j}\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha}\left(1-\frac{|r_i-r_j|}{R_i+R_j}\right)_+^{\alpha}.
\]
A jammed packing is mechanically stable under allowed perturbations.  In rigidity language, contacts define a graph $G$ and a rigidity matrix $R_G$.  Isostaticity for frictionless spheres in $d$ dimensions gives approximately
\[
 z=\frac{2N_c}{N}\to 2d
\]
after removing rattlers and boundary modes.  Near jamming, excess contact number, pressure, shear modulus, and vibrational spectra show critical scaling [@ohern2003; @donev2004; @wyart2005; @parisi2010hard].

**Captures:** athermal rigidity, constraint satisfaction, marginal stability, criticality, force/contact networks.  **Fails:** thermal glass transition unless coupled to finite-temperature dynamics; friction, shape, and preparation protocols complicate universality.

# 3. Geometry and symmetry

## 3.1 Configuration-space geometry

For particle systems, configuration space is a high-dimensional manifold with excluded regions.  Hard spheres give a particularly geometric object:
\[
\mathcal F_N(\varphi)=\{(r_1,\ldots,r_N)\in\Lambda^N/S_N: |r_i-r_j|\ge D\text{ for all }i<j\}.
\]
Increasing $\varphi$ changes the topology and metric bottlenecks of $\mathcal F_N(\varphi)$.  A glassy state is not necessarily a connected component; it can be a region that is connected only through paths whose entropic necks are exponentially narrow.  For soft potentials, replace feasible-set geometry by level-set and sublevel-set geometry:
\[
\{X:H_N(X)\le E\}, \qquad \{X:\nabla H_N(X)=0\}, \qquad \text{basins under gradient descent.}
\]
The Stillinger--Weber inherent-structure map sends a configuration to the local minimum reached by steepest descent, thereby partitioning configuration space into basins [@stillinger1995].  This partition is useful but not canonical: it depends on metric, potential smoothing, boundary conditions, and minimization rule.

## 3.2 Basins, barriers, saddles, and metastability

A metastable basin $B$ is dynamically meaningful if the exit time
\[
\tau_B=\inf\{t:X_t\notin B\}
\]
has a distribution concentrated at times much larger than local mixing inside $B$.  In reversible Markov dynamics, barrier heights can be expressed through capacities and spectral gaps; in glass theory they are often estimated through free-energy saddles.  For random landscapes such as spherical $p$-spin models, the complexity of critical points can be computed asymptotically using Kac--Rice/random matrix methods [@auffinger2013complexity; @subag2017].  Such results give precise versions of the intuition that low-temperature landscapes contain exponentially many minima and saddles.

## 3.3 Frustration

Frustration is the impossibility of simultaneously satisfying local preferences.  In the EA model, the product of signs of $J_{ij}$ around a plaquette can be negative, making all pair interactions unsatisfiable.  In structural glasses, frustration is not usually quenched in the Hamiltonian; it is geometric or kinetic.  Icosahedral local order in three-dimensional liquids is locally favorable for some potentials but incompatible with space-filling periodic order without defects.  In hard spheres, the non-overlap constraints define a high-dimensional packing problem with many mutually incompatible dense arrangements.

A useful mathematical distinction is:

- **quenched frustration:** randomness in $H_N$ fixed before thermodynamics, as in spin glasses;
- **self-generated frustration:** many metastable amorphous packings arise without quenched randomness, as in structural glass formers;
- **kinetic frustration:** transition graph bottlenecks arise from constraints even when equilibrium is simple, as in KCMs.

## 3.4 Replica symmetry breaking and ultrametricity

In a Gibbs measure $G_N$, draw independent replicas $\sigma^1,\sigma^2,\ldots$ and consider overlaps $R_{\ell\ell'}$.  Replica symmetry means the low-temperature state is effectively described by a single overlap value, while RSB means the overlap distribution is nontrivial.  In the Parisi picture, pure states are arranged ultrametrically:
\[
 R_{23}\ge \min(R_{12},R_{13})
\]
in the thermodynamic limit, after appropriate interpretation.  Panchenko proved that random measures satisfying the Ghirlanda--Guerra identities are ultrametric, establishing a major part of the Parisi picture for mean-field spin glasses [@panchenko2013ultra; @panchenko2013book].

Ultrametricity is not a generic property of all rugged landscapes.  It is a theorem in a specific mean-field probabilistic setting and an organizing hypothesis in structural-glass mean-field theory.  Evidence for hierarchical subbasins in high-dimensional hard spheres and the Gardner phase is strong in mean-field and numerical studies, but finite-dimensional structural glasses require caution [@kurchan2013; @charbonneau2017; @jin2022].

## 3.5 Marginal stability

A stable crystal has phonons and a positive elastic matrix modulo Goldstone modes.  A marginal glass has many nearly soft directions.  In a Hessian $M=\nabla^2 H(X_*)$ at an inherent structure, the vibrational density of states $D(\omega)$ may show excess low-frequency modes beyond Debye scaling $D_{\rm Debye}(\omega)\sim \omega^{d-1}$.  Quasilocalized excitations and $\omega^4$-type nonphononic tails are active topics in amorphous-solid theory [@lerner2021; @moriel2024omega; @moriel2024boson; @lerner2025het].

In jammed matter, marginality appears through isostaticity and scaling.  In Gardner theory, marginality appears as full RSB inside a glass basin; the basin fractures into a hierarchy of subbasins, producing anomalous susceptibilities and aging [@kurchan2013; @charbonneau2017; @jin2022; @wang2024gardner].

## 3.6 Why glass is not merely random

A Poisson point process has no long-lived rigidity.  A random graph need not be mechanically stable.  A random energy assignment has no locality.  Glassiness requires correlations and constraints.  Several mathematical signatures distinguish glass from unstructured randomness:

- $g(r)$ has packing shells and excluded-volume structure;
- $S(k)$ may show hyperuniform or nearly hyperuniform suppression in special jammed systems;
- persistent homology detects nontrivial loops and cavities with stable lifetimes;
- rigidity matrices have near-isostatic spectra;
- overlap distributions and point-to-set correlations detect amorphous state structure;
- dynamic correlations $G_4(r,t)$ are spatially structured, not independent in space-time.

# 4. Statistical mechanics and dynamics

## 4.1 Gibbs measures, quenched averages, annealed disorder

For quenched disorder $J$, the physically relevant free energy is quenched:
\[
 F_N^{\rm q}= -\beta^{-1}\mathbb E_J\log Z_N(J),
\]
not annealed:
\[
 F_N^{\rm a}= -\beta^{-1}\log\mathbb E_J Z_N(J).
\]
This distinction is central in spin glasses.  Structural glasses usually have no quenched $J$; disorder is annealed but can become effectively frozen by kinetic arrest.  The mathematical challenge is that metastable amorphous states are not given by an external random environment but emerge from the interacting-particle measure and dynamics.

## 4.2 Replica method

The replica method uses
\[
\mathbb E\log Z=\lim_{n\to 0}\frac{\mathbb E Z^n-1}{n},
\]
computes $\mathbb E Z^n$ for integer $n$, then analytically continues.  For SK, the replicated order parameter is an $n\times n$ overlap matrix $Q_{ab}$.  Replica symmetry assumes $Q_{ab}=q$ for $a\ne b$; Parisi RSB imposes a hierarchical block structure before $n\to 0$.  The method is not rigorous as originally formulated, but it generated the correct variational formula later proved by Guerra and Talagrand [@parisi1979; @guerra2003; @talagrand2006].

In structural glass theory, replicas are used differently.  One couples copies of a liquid to force them into the same metastable amorphous state, then studies the free energy as a function of overlap.  The Franz--Parisi potential is a central object:
\[
 V(q)= -\frac{T}{N}\mathbb E_{X^0}\log \int dX\, e^{-\beta H(X)}\delta(Q(X,X^0)-q) - \text{constant}.
\]
A secondary minimum at high overlap is interpreted as a metastable glass state.  In finite dimensions, defining and proving the status of this minimum remains difficult.

## 4.3 Parisi formula and Guerra--Talagrand program

For mixed $p$-spin models, the limiting free energy is given by a variational problem over probability measures on $[0,1]$.  In the SK case, a convenient formulation solves a nonlinear antiparabolic PDE.  Given a measure $\zeta$ on $[0,1]$, let $\Phi_\zeta(t,x)$ solve
\[
\partial_t \Phi_\zeta(t,x)= -\frac{\xi''(t)}{2}\left(\partial_{xx}\Phi_\zeta(t,x)+\zeta([0,t])(\partial_x\Phi_\zeta(t,x))^2\right),
\]
with terminal condition $\Phi_\zeta(1,x)=\log 2\cosh x$.  The Parisi functional has the schematic form
\[
\mathcal P(\zeta)=\log 2 + \Phi_\zeta(0,h) - \frac12\int_0^1 t\xi''(t)\zeta([0,t])\,dt,
\]
with conventions varying by model.  The theorem states
\[
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\mathbb E\log Z_N=\inf_{\zeta}\mathcal P(\zeta).
\]
Guerra proved the upper/lower bound direction usually called the interpolation bound; Talagrand completed the proof for SK; subsequent work extended it to broad mixed $p$-spin classes [@guerra2003; @talagrand2006; @panchenko2013book; @mourrat2025; @chatterjee2026].

The significance is not just a formula.  It gives a rigorous bridge from probabilistic interpolation, variational PDEs, stochastic control, and hierarchical random measures to the physics of spin glass order.

## 4.4 Ghirlanda--Guerra identities and ultrametricity

The Ghirlanda--Guerra identities express stochastic stability of overlap arrays.  In one form, for bounded $f$ of overlaps among $n$ replicas and bounded $\psi$,
\[
\mathbb E\langle f\psi(R_{1,n+1})\rangle
=\frac1n\mathbb E\langle f\rangle\mathbb E\langle\psi(R_{12})\rangle
+\frac1n\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\mathbb E\langle f\psi(R_{1\ell})\rangle.
\]
Panchenko's theorem states that overlap arrays satisfying these identities are ultrametric, under suitable hypotheses [@panchenko2013ultra].  This is one of the clearest examples where a physically motivated glass concept became a precise theorem.

## 4.5 Complexity/configurational entropy

The **complexity** $\Sigma(f)$ is the exponential rate of metastable states of free-energy density $f$:
\[
\mathcal N_N(f)\asymp \exp(N\Sigma(f)).
\]
The **configurational entropy** $s_c$ is the log-number of amorphous states per particle, after subtracting vibrational entropy.  In inherent-structure language,
\[
S_{\rm total}(T)=S_{\rm vib}(T)+S_c(T),
\]
though the decomposition is not unique.  In RFOT, $s_c\to 0$ at $T_K$.  In rigorous random landscape theory, complexity can sometimes be computed for critical points or minima via Kac--Rice formulas [@auffinger2013complexity; @subag2017].  Translating this into finite-dimensional structural glasses remains open.

## 4.6 RFOT and finite-dimensional activation

Mean-field RFOT has a dynamical transition where ergodicity breaks even though no thermodynamic singularity occurs.  Finite-dimensional activated events destroy the strict dynamical singularity.  RFOT's finite-dimensional claim is instead that relaxation is controlled by rearranging mosaics whose barrier grows with a static amorphous length.  The theory is influential and quantitatively suggestive, but its finite-dimensional mathematical status is not comparable to the Parisi formula [@lubchenko2007; @berthier2011; @biroli2023rfot].

## 4.7 Mode-coupling theory and dynamic heterogeneity

MCT gives a closed approximate equation for two-point correlators.  It predicts a plateau, von Schweidler decay, and power-law divergence of $\tau_\alpha$ near an ideal $T_c$.  In real glass formers, $T_c$ is usually a crossover; activated relaxation continues below it [@gotze2009; @janssen2018].  MCT is therefore best treated as an asymptotic theory of early supercooling and a generator of controlled approximations in some high-dimensional or mean-field-like limits, not as a final theory of the laboratory glass transition.

Dynamic heterogeneity is measured by four-point functions.  Define local mobility $c_i(t)=w(|r_i(t)-r_i(0)|)$ and field $c(r,t)=\sum_i c_i(t)\delta(r-r_i(0))$.  Then
\[
G_4(r,t)=\langle c(0,t)c(r,t)\rangle-\langle c(0,t)\rangle^2
\]
and
\[
\chi_4(t)=\int G_4(r,t)\,dr.
\]
A central unresolved issue is whether the length scale inferred from $G_4$ is governed by thermodynamic amorphous order, kinetic facilitation, elastic effects, or a mixture depending on regime.

## 4.8 Aging and nonequilibrium statistical mechanics

For quenches below glass transition regimes, the system explores deeper basins with time.  Mean-field spin glasses have solvable aging equations in which time-reparametrization, weak long-term memory, and FDT violations appear.  Finite-dimensional structural glasses show rejuvenation, memory, and aging experimentally, but rigorous nonequilibrium descriptions are limited.

Trajectory-space large deviations provide one mathematical framework.  For a time-integrated activity $K[0,t]$, define a biased dynamical partition function
\[
Z_t(s)=\left\langle e^{-sK[0,t]}\right\rangle.
\]
KCMs and related atomistic models can exhibit active/inactive dynamical phase transitions in this ensemble [@jack2007; @chandler2010].  The open mathematical challenge is to connect such trajectory transitions to ordinary unbiased physical dynamics in finite-dimensional liquids.

# 5. Topology and modern mathematical structure

## 5.1 Persistent homology of amorphous structure

Given a point cloud $X=\{r_i\}_{i=1}^N$, build a filtration of simplicial complexes $K_\epsilon(X)$ such as Vietoris--Rips, Čech, or alpha complexes.  Persistent homology records the birth and death of homology classes:
\[
H_k(K_{\epsilon_b})\to H_k(K_{\epsilon_d}).
\]
The multiset of pairs $(\epsilon_b,\epsilon_d)$ is a persistence diagram $D_k(X)$.  Stable distances such as bottleneck or Wasserstein metrics make this a genuine computational-topological statistic [@edelsbrunner2002; @carlsson2009].

For glasses, persistence diagrams have been used to identify medium-range order that is hard to capture with pair correlations or local bond-order parameters.  Hiraoka and collaborators demonstrated hierarchical amorphous structures via persistent homology; later work applied topological learning to oxide glasses and metallic glasses; recent work connects amorphous structural order to nonaffine elasticity and low-energy vibrations [@hiraoka2016; @nakamura2015; @sorensen2020; @hirata2020; @minamitani2025].

## 5.2 What topology adds, and what it does not

Deep use of topology means at least one of the following:

1. the construction is stable under perturbations and has a metric/statistical interpretation;
2. the topological invariant distinguishes structures not separated by classical order parameters;
3. the invariant enters a predictive model for dynamics, elasticity, or relaxation;
4. the topology is tied to configuration-space connectivity, feasible-set components, or rigidity.

Superficial metaphor occurs when “rugged landscape topology” simply means “many minima” without computing invariants or proving statements about connectivity.  Persistent homology of particle configurations is genuine computational topology.  Ultrametricity of pure states is a metric-tree theorem in mean-field probability.  Jamming as constraint satisfiability or rigidity is geometric/topological when it concerns feasible-set boundaries, contact networks, and constraint manifolds.  Calling memory effects “topological” without an invariant is not yet mathematics.

## 5.3 Topology of jamming and satisfiability

Jamming can be formulated as a constraint-satisfaction transition.  The feasible set $\mathcal F_N(\varphi)$ shrinks with packing fraction.  At jamming, a configuration lies on the boundary of feasibility and has a contact network satisfying mechanical stability.  Rigidity theory studies whether infinitesimal motions preserve constraints:
\[
R_G u =0,
\]
where $u$ is a displacement vector and $R_G$ the rigidity matrix.  Nontrivial kernels correspond to floppy modes.  Isostaticity is the balance between constraints and degrees of freedom.

Recent work explicitly frames jamming as a topological satisfiability transition with contact-number hyperuniformity and criticality [@shang2026topsat].  This is promising because it seeks invariants and scaling statements about contact networks, not just a metaphor of being “stuck.”  It remains to be seen which aspects are universal across particle shapes, friction, boundary conditions, and finite dimension.

## 5.4 Networks of metastable states

A coarse-grained landscape can be represented by a graph $\mathcal G$ whose vertices are inherent structures or metastable states and whose edges are transition states.  Edge weights encode barriers or transition rates.  Then glassy relaxation becomes a problem in metastable Markov chains, spectral gaps, capacities, and random graph geometry.  This representation is concrete in small systems and random-energy-like models, but constructing it for realistic $N$ is computationally explosive: the number of minima can scale exponentially, and many physically relevant transitions pass through collective pathways not captured by local searches.

## 5.5 Computational topology and glass research opportunities

Topological tools are likely to be most useful when combined with physical observables.  Promising directions include:

- persistence images or landscapes as features for predicting mobility or nonaffine displacement;
- zigzag persistence for time-dependent rearrangements;
- sheaf/cosheaf methods for local-to-global compatibility of contact networks;
- Morse-theoretic summaries of energy sublevel sets in small or mean-field systems;
- Mapper/Reeb graphs for low-dimensional projections of metastable-state networks;
- rigorous stability/error bounds for topological summaries under thermal noise.

The mathematical frontier is not to “apply TDA to glass data” but to identify which topological features correspond to physical mechanisms and which are merely high-dimensional descriptors.

# 6. Rigorous mathematical results

## 6.1 Mean-field spin glasses: the rigorous success story

The most complete rigorous theory of glasses is for mean-field spin glasses.  The central results are:

**Parisi formula.**  The thermodynamic limit of the quenched free energy for SK and broad mixed $p$-spin models is the infimum of the Parisi functional over probability measures on $[0,1]$ [@guerra2003; @talagrand2006; @panchenko2013book].

**Guerra interpolation.**  Guerra introduced an interpolation proving one side of the Parisi bound and providing a robust method now used far beyond the original SK model [@guerra2003].

**Talagrand's proof.**  Talagrand completed the proof of the Parisi formula for SK, turning a nonrigorous replica calculation into a theorem [@talagrand2006].

**Panchenko ultrametricity.**  Panchenko proved that the Ghirlanda--Guerra identities imply ultrametricity of the asymptotic Gibbs measure, rigorously validating a central geometric claim of Parisi theory in its proper mean-field setting [@panchenko2013ultra].

**Complexity of spherical spin glasses.**  Kac--Rice/random matrix methods compute the exponential complexity of critical points, making the landscape picture precise in spherical models [@auffinger2013complexity; @subag2017].

Recent mathematical work pushes toward constructive proofs, quantum extensions, sampling algorithms, and more general models [@manai2024; @huang2024constructive; @huang2024sampling; @mourrat2025; @chatterjee2026].

## 6.2 What assumptions make these results possible?

The SK and mixed $p$-spin proofs rely on mean-field covariance structures.  The Hamiltonian is Gaussian with covariance depending only on overlap:
\[
\mathbb E H_N(\sigma^1)H_N(\sigma^2)=N\xi(R_{12}).
\]
This high symmetry collapses the problem to overlap arrays.  It enables concentration, interpolation, cavity methods, and variational formulas.  Finite-dimensional structural glasses do not have this covariance structure.  Particle positions have Euclidean geometry, conservation laws, excluded volume, and possible crystallization.  Therefore Parisi theory is a guide, not a theorem, for ordinary window glass or colloids.

## 6.3 Finite-dimensional spin glasses

The EA model remains much less understood.  Rigorous work exists on existence of thermodynamic limits, ground states, metastates, domain walls, and special regimes, but there is no finite-dimensional analogue of the Parisi solution with comparable completeness.  Even the correct physical picture in $d=3$ and nonzero field is debated.  This is an important warning: adding locality to a solved mean-field glass can destroy solvability.

## 6.4 High-dimensional hard spheres and structural-glass mean field

The hard-sphere glass in $d\to\infty$ is the most developed mean-field structural-glass theory.  Replica calculations provide equations for dynamical, Kauzmann, Gardner, and jamming transitions [@parisi2010hard; @kurchan2013; @charbonneau2017].  The claim “exact in infinite dimension” is a physics statement supported by controlled mean-field methods, not identical in status to Talagrand's proof for SK.  Still, it has produced sharp predictions: isostatic jamming, force and gap distributions, full RSB marginality, and scaling exponents.

A rigorous mathematical program here would need to control the $d,N\to\infty$ limits, hard-core geometry, replica variational principles, and finite-dimensional corrections.  Partial rigorous results in sphere packing, rigidity, and high-dimensional probability exist, but a full theorem comparable to the Parisi formula for hard-sphere glasses is not currently available.

## 6.5 Jamming and rigidity

Jamming has rigorous subproblems.  For a fixed packing, one can classify local, collective, or strict jamming and test infinitesimal rigidity via linear programming or rigidity matrices [@donev2004].  Maxwell counting and isostaticity can be formalized, though boundary conditions, rattlers, prestress, friction, and particle shape complicate the exact statements.  Critical scaling at jamming is strongly supported numerically and by theory, and recent work frames jamming as first-order with quenched disorder and criticality or as topological satisfiability [@deng2024; @shang2026topsat].  Fully rigorous universality of jamming exponents in finite dimensions remains open.

## 6.6 Kinetically constrained models

KCMs have a substantial rigorous probability literature because the equilibrium measure can be simple while dynamics is constrained.  Questions include ergodicity, spectral gaps, mixing times, infection fronts, bootstrap percolation analogies, and hydrodynamic limits [@ritort2003; @hartarsky2025].  The rigor here is strongest precisely because the models idealize away static structural complexity.  They provide mathematical laboratories for slow dynamics and dynamic facilitation, not complete structural-glass models.

## 6.7 Computational complexity and algorithmic barriers

Spin glass theory has become central in random optimization and theoretical computer science.  The **overlap gap property** (OGP) describes a forbidden interval of overlaps among near-optimal solutions; it can imply failure of broad classes of stable algorithms.  Recent work proves shattering and OGP phenomena in Ising $p$-spin and random constraint-satisfaction models [@gamarnik2025; @kizildag2025].  These results give mathematically precise versions of the idea that a rugged landscape can be algorithmically hard.

However, computational hardness is not synonymous with physical glassiness.  A model can be hard for algorithms but not a material glass; a glassy material can be physically slow for reasons not captured by worst-case or average-case algorithmic complexity.  The productive interface is to ask when overlap geometry, metastable clusters, and algorithmic thresholds coincide.

# 7. Computational frontier

## 7.1 Molecular dynamics

Molecular dynamics integrates Newtonian or Langevin equations:
\[
 m_i\ddot r_i=-\nabla_i H-\gamma \dot r_i+\sqrt{2\gamma k_BT}\,\eta_i(t).
\]
MD gives time-resolved trajectories, dynamic heterogeneity, aging, rheology, and vibrational spectra.  Its main limitation is time scale: ordinary MD cannot equilibrate molecular glass formers near laboratory $T_g$.  Widely used software includes LAMMPS, HOOMD-blue, and GROMACS [@plimpton1995; @anderson2020hoomd; @abraham2015gromacs].  Analysis packages and enhanced sampling frameworks such as freud and PLUMED support order parameters, neighbor graphs, collective variables, and biased dynamics [@tribello2014plumed; @vananders2020freud].

## 7.2 Monte Carlo, swap Monte Carlo, and irreversible algorithms

Standard Metropolis Monte Carlo proposes local moves.  Swap Monte Carlo adds particle-type or diameter swaps and can equilibrate polydisperse glass formers far deeper than ordinary dynamics [@ninarello2017; @scalliet2022].  This is mathematically important because it separates equilibrium structure from physical dynamics: swap moves are unphysical for many materials but reveal equilibrium states inaccessible to natural dynamics.

Recent algorithms extend swap ideas to molecular models and irreversible Monte Carlo for hard spheres/disks [@ghimenti2024; @boehmer2025; @simon2026].  These methods are changing the mathematical question.  Instead of asking only “what happens before simulations fall out of equilibrium?”, one can compare deeply equilibrated configurations with physical relaxation and test whether static amorphous order grows enough to explain slowdown.

## 7.3 Energy landscape algorithms

Common methods include steepest-descent inherent structure mapping, conjugate-gradient minimization, eigenvector following, nudged elastic band, activation-relaxation techniques, basin-hopping, metadynamics, and transition path sampling.  They produce databases of minima and saddles, allowing graph and spectral analysis of landscape connectivity.  The mathematical difficulty is sampling bias: the set of found minima is not the set of thermodynamically relevant minima, and transition-state networks become exponentially large.

## 7.4 Mean-field numerical computation

Mean-field equations for $p$-spin models, hard-sphere replicas, MCT, and RFOT often reduce to variational/PDE/integral equations.  Numerical solution of Parisi PDEs, state-following equations, and MCT kernels is a specialized computational area.  ModeCouplingTheory.jl is one example of a modern open-source implementation of MCT calculations [@pihlajamaa2023].

## 7.5 Spin glass solvers and optimization algorithms

Algorithms include simulated annealing, parallel tempering, population annealing, belief propagation/survey propagation, approximate message passing, branch-and-bound for exact ground states in special graphs, semidefinite relaxations, quantum annealing, and stochastic localization-inspired sampling.  The frontier is not just faster solvers; it is the relation between Gibbs geometry and algorithmic phase transitions.  Recent rigorous work on sampling spherical spin glasses and OGP/shattering gives a direct mathematical link between landscape organization and algorithmic success/failure [@huang2024sampling; @gamarnik2025; @kizildag2025; @montanari2026].

## 7.6 Graph methods

Particle systems produce graphs: Delaunay/Voronoi graphs, contact networks, bond networks, cage graphs, facilitation graphs, and transition networks.  Graph neural networks have shown that static structure contains predictive information about future mobility, contradicting older pessimistic readings of weak two-point correlations [@bapst2020; @jung2023; @jung2024transfer; @jung2025roadmap].  The mathematical challenge is interpretability: a high-performing graph neural net may encode local packing motifs, elastic fields, high-order correlations, or finite-size artifacts.

## 7.7 Topological data analysis pipelines

A glass TDA pipeline typically:

1. constructs a point cloud from atoms, particles, voids, or local environment descriptors;
2. builds an alpha/Rips/weighted complex;
3. computes persistence diagrams in $H_0,H_1,H_2$;
4. vectorizes diagrams via persistence landscapes, images, kernels, or optimal transport distances;
5. correlates features with dynamics, elasticity, composition, cooling rate, or vibrational modes.

The frontier is to add uncertainty quantification and physical null models.  A persistence feature is meaningful only if it survives thermal noise, finite-size effects, density changes, and comparison with appropriate random packings.

## 7.8 Machine learning of glassy dynamics

Machine learning now addresses three mathematical questions:

- **Representation:** Which structural descriptors contain enough information to predict mobility or rearrangements?
- **Transfer:** Does a learned map work across temperatures, dimensions, interaction potentials, and preparation protocols?
- **Mechanism:** Can learned features be reduced to interpretable correlation functions, defects, soft modes, or amorphous order?

Graph networks and physics-inspired MLPs have demonstrated substantial predictive power from static configurations [@bapst2020; @jung2023; @jung2024transfer].  The 2025 roadmap consolidates the agenda: datasets, benchmarks, interpretability, active learning, and integration with theory [@jung2025roadmap].  The risk is that ML becomes an interpolation engine.  The opportunity is to discover new order parameters by constraining architectures and testing transferability.

## 7.9 Computation as mathematics, not just approximation

Computation produces mathematical insight when it identifies stable exponents, falsifies proposed order parameters, reveals algorithmic phase transitions, constructs counterexamples, or suggests variational principles.  Examples include:

- swap MC allowing tests of equilibrium RFOT-like predictions below ordinary simulation arrest [@scalliet2022];
- high-dimensional hard-sphere simulations comparing finite-$d$ systems to replica predictions [@charbonneau2017];
- numerical Gardner diagnostics using susceptibilities, aging, and jamming scalings [@jin2022; @wang2024gardner];
- ML descriptors showing that structure-dynamics links are stronger than low-order correlation functions suggest [@bapst2020; @jung2025roadmap];
- TDA features revealing medium-range order invisible to pair statistics [@hiraoka2016; @minamitani2025].

# 8. Experimental and physical interface

## 8.1 Scattering

X-ray, neutron, and light scattering measure static and dynamic structure factors:
\[
S(k)=\frac1N\left\langle \sum_{ij}e^{ik\cdot(r_i-r_j)}\right\rangle,
\qquad
F(k,t)=\frac1N\left\langle \rho_k(t)\rho_{-k}(0)\right\rangle.
\]
MCT takes $S(k)$ as input, making scattering data mathematically central [@gotze2009; @janssen2018].  The weakness is also clear: two-point structure changes modestly near the glass transition, while dynamics changes enormously.  This mismatch motivates higher-order correlations, overlap fields, TDA, and ML descriptors.

## 8.2 Rheology

Rheology probes response to shear.  In a liquid, shear stress relaxes; in a glass or jammed solid, there is an elastic response and possible yield stress.  Mathematical observables include stress autocorrelations, viscosity
\[
\eta=\frac{V}{k_BT}\int_0^\infty \langle \sigma_{xy}(t)\sigma_{xy}(0)\rangle\,dt,
\]
nonlinear flow curves, avalanche statistics, and elastoplastic models.  Yielding of amorphous and granular matter links glass physics to depinning, absorbing-state transitions, and marginal stability [@shang2024yielding; @deng2024].

## 8.3 Calorimetry and thermodynamics

Calorimetry measures heat capacity and enthalpy recovery.  It is central to the Kauzmann/entropy-crisis problem: extrapolated supercooled-liquid entropy may approach crystal entropy at $T_K$.  But extrapolation is not proof of a thermodynamic transition.  The mathematical issue is separating vibrational entropy from configurational entropy and controlling nonequilibrium protocol dependence [@ediger1996; @debenedetti2001; @lubchenko2007; @biroli2023rfot].

## 8.4 Microscopy and colloids

Colloidal suspensions allow direct particle tracking and measurement of local structure, mobility, cage rearrangements, and dynamic heterogeneity [@hunter2012; @dauchot2023].  The 2026 colloidal work by Laermann and collaborators is notable because it proposes equilibrium two-particle correlation signatures related through fluctuation-dissipation ideas to response, suggesting emergent large-scale solid-like behavior in dense suspensions [@laermann2026].  Whether this yields a universal thermodynamic transition in three dimensions is still unsettled.

## 8.5 Polymers, metallic glasses, oxide glasses

Polymer glasses add chain connectivity and entanglement.  Metallic glasses add electronic bonding, icosahedral motifs, shear transformation zones, and crystallization competition.  Oxide glasses add network topology and composition-dependent constraints.  These systems motivate mathematical models beyond identical spheres: networks, polydispersity, angular forces, bond switching, and multiscale constraints.  Persistent homology and topological learning have been especially visible in oxide/metallic/amorphous-silicon studies [@sorensen2020; @hirata2020; @minamitani2025].

## 8.6 Granular and jammed systems

Granular media are athermal and dissipative.  Temperature is not a thermodynamic control variable; packing, stress, friction, driving protocol, and boundary conditions matter.  This makes granular jamming both mathematically attractive and treacherous.  Constraint geometry and rigidity are clean in ideal frictionless spheres, but experiments include frictional contacts, history-dependent forces, and non-equilibrium driving [@liu1998; @ohern2003; @donev2004; @deng2024].

# 9. Current frontier, 2020--2026

## 9.1 Gardner transition and marginal glass phases

**Established in mean-field:** high-dimensional hard-sphere and spin-glass calculations predict a Gardner transition where a stable glass basin becomes marginal and fractures into a hierarchy of subbasins with full RSB [@kurchan2013; @charbonneau2017].

**Active 2020--2026 frontier:** simulations test whether Gardner-like signatures survive in $d=2,3$ hard spheres, soft spheres, and structural glass models.  Recent work links the emergence of jamming scaling to Gardner diagnostics in hard spheres/disks [@jin2022; @wang2024gardner].

**Uncertainty:** finite-dimensional Gardner behavior may be rounded, avoided, protocol-dependent, or visible only near jamming.  Distinguishing a true phase transition from a crossover is difficult because relaxation times and finite-size effects are severe.

## 9.2 High-dimensional glasses

High-dimensional limits are a rare setting where structural glass theory approaches exactness.  The frontier is to turn replica predictions for hard spheres into controlled mathematics and to understand which predictions remain in $d=3$.  Quantum and active variants have begun to enter high-dimensional glass theory [@winer2024].

## 9.3 Jamming criticality and mixed-order character

Jamming shows discontinuous onset of rigidity/contact number together with critical scaling and diverging lengths.  Recent work emphasizes quenched disorder, mixed first/second-order character, marginal stability, and topological satisfiability [@deng2024; @shang2024yielding; @shang2026topsat].  The strongest established facts concern idealized frictionless packings; universality across friction, shape, driving, and dimension is open.

## 9.4 Machine learning and glassy landscapes

ML has shifted the debate about whether structure predicts dynamics.  Graph networks and physics-inspired networks extract predictive information from static configurations [@bapst2020; @jung2023; @jung2024transfer].  The 2025 roadmap frames the main problems as benchmark datasets, transferability, interpretable learned order parameters, and links to theory [@jung2025roadmap].

A research-level mathematical question is: does there exist a low-complexity statistic $\Phi(X)$ such that conditional laws of mobility or relaxation are approximately Markovian in $\Phi$?  ML can search for $\Phi$, but proof requires stability, transfer, and physical interpretation.

## 9.5 Dynamic facilitation revisited

KCMs have long argued that glassiness can arise from sparse mobility defects and facilitation rather than growing static order [@ritort2003; @chandler2010].  Recent atomistic simulations directly analyze facilitation and equilibration pathways [@herrero2024; @chacko2023].  The frontier is not “static versus dynamic” in a binary sense; it is to determine which observables and regimes are controlled by static amorphous order, kinetic facilitation, elasticity, or their coupling.

## 9.6 Experimental-depth simulations near laboratory glass transition

Swap MC and related algorithms now equilibrate models at depths previously inaccessible.  Scalliet, Berthier, and Zamponi simulated a supercooled liquid for a physical time window comparable to tens of milliseconds in model units, and newer molecular models target motion near experimental glass-transition conditions [@scalliet2022; @simon2026].  This is a frontier because many theories differ only in the deeply supercooled regime.

## 9.7 Topological data analysis and medium-range order

Persistent homology is becoming a serious structural language for amorphous materials.  The 2025 amorphous-silicon work is particularly interesting because it links topological structural order to nonaffine elastic response and low-energy vibrations [@minamitani2025].  Newer work on topological defects in glasses further suggests that topology may become mechanistic rather than descriptive [@wu2025topdefects].

## 9.8 Quantum glasses

Quantum spin glasses, quantum hard spheres, Rydberg arrays, and SYK-related models are active.  Rigorous quantum Parisi formulas are beginning to appear for mean-field transverse-field models [@manai2024], while high-dimensional quantum hard spheres adapt structural-glass concepts to path-integral or quantum settings [@winer2024].  The field is emerging: quantum glassiness can mean many-body localization-like nonergodicity, spin-glass order, structural quantum effects, or slow dynamics in open systems, and these are not equivalent.

## 9.9 Active glasses

Active matter introduces non-equilibrium driving at the particle scale.  Effective temperature is generally insufficient because detailed balance is broken.  Active glasses appear in biological and synthetic systems and pose new mathematical problems for large deviations, non-Hermitian generators, entropy production, and motility-induced constraints [@sadhukhan2024].  Many proposals are plausible, but a universal active glass theory is not established.

## 9.10 Glassy learning and optimization landscapes

Spin glass concepts now inform neural-network loss landscapes, Bayesian inference, random constraint satisfaction, and sampling.  The rigorous frontier includes OGP, shattering, algorithmic thresholds, and stochastic localization [@gamarnik2025; @huang2024sampling; @kizildag2025; @montanari2026].  The analogy with physical structural glasses should be used carefully: neural networks are trained by non-equilibrium algorithms on data-dependent landscapes, not by Gibbs sampling of particle configurations.

# 10. Open mathematical problems

| Problem | Why it is hard | What would count as progress |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of the finite-dimensional structural glass transition | Experiments show kinetic arrest; equilibrium transition is inaccessible or avoided; crystallization and finite-time protocols intervene. | A theorem or controlled limit proving/disproving a thermodynamic singularity in a realistic finite-dimensional model, or rigorous bounds relating relaxation to static amorphous length. |
| Finite-dimensional RFOT | Mosaic arguments involve poorly controlled interfaces between amorphous states; metastable states are not sharply defined. | A variational definition of amorphous states and surface tension with finite-dimensional estimates, perhaps in pinned or Kac-type models. |
| Activated dynamics below MCT | Mean-field dynamical transitions are rounded by rare events; activation is nonperturbative. | Sharp asymptotics for relaxation times in models where MCT-like plateaus coexist with provable activated escape. |
| Universality of jamming exponents | Exponents may depend on friction, dimension, particle shape, preparation, and interaction law. | Rigorous universality classes for constraint-satisfaction/rigidity transitions, with finite-size scaling theory. |
| Gardner transition in $d=2,3$ | Marginal signatures are noisy and aging-prone; true transition may be rounded. | Finite-size scaling observables that distinguish transition from crossover, or a theorem showing avoided criticality. |
| Aging and memory | Nonequilibrium measures depend on full protocol; no general Gibbs state description. | A compact state variable or renormalized dynamical theory predicting two-time functions and memory protocols. |
| Dynamical heterogeneity | Many mechanisms produce heterogeneous motion; four-point functions are hard to measure and interpret. | A theorem or validated scaling theory relating $G_4$, facilitation, static length, and elastic propagators. |
| Replica symmetry breaking beyond mean field | Locality and droplets may destroy or modify RSB; overlap structures are harder to define. | Rigorous metastate/overlap results for EA-type models or Kac structural glasses in finite dimensions. |
| Computational hardness of glass equilibration | Physical dynamics, Markov-chain mixing, and algorithmic complexity are different notions. | Complexity-theoretic or probabilistic lower bounds for natural local dynamics in glass-forming particle models. |
| Relation to learning landscapes | Neural losses are non-Gibbsian, overparameterized, and data-dependent; many minima can be connected. | Precise mapping between overlap geometry, generalization, sampling, and trainability in random/nonrandom models. |
| Topological order parameters | Persistence features may be descriptive rather than causal. | Stability, null-model, and transfer theorems showing topological features predict dynamics/mechanics across models. |
| Active and quantum glass theories | Detailed balance fails or quantum coherence enters; classical thermodynamic tools may not apply. | Unified large-deviation/replica/Keldysh or operator-algebraic frameworks with testable predictions. |

# 11. Cross-regime comparison

| Transition/comparison | Mathematical property that changes | Main order/disorder variable | Main dynamical change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gas $\to$ liquid | density correlations grow; condensation | density, compressibility | collision-dominated to collective hydrodynamics |
| Liquid $\to$ crystal | continuous translations/rotations break to lattice subgroup | Bragg peaks, lattice order | diffusion gives way to phonons/defects |
| Liquid $\to$ quasicrystal | long-range aperiodic order appears | pure-point diffraction with noncrystallographic symmetry | phason/phonon modes rather than ordinary diffusion |
| Liquid $\to$ supercooled liquid | no new obvious static symmetry; relaxation time grows | dynamic heterogeneity, weak static length growth | two-step relaxation, caging |
| Supercooled liquid $\to$ glass | ergodicity lost on observation scale; measure becomes protocol-dependent | overlap, amorphous order, configurational entropy | aging, memory, kinetic arrest |
| Glass $\to$ amorphous solid under minimization | inherent structure selected; thermal motion removed | Hessian/contact network/nonaffine fields | vibrations and plastic rearrangements |
| Glass $\to$ jammed packing | hard constraints become binding; rigidity matrix loses floppy modes | contact number, force/gap distributions | athermal rigidity, critical scaling |
| Spin glass high $T\to$ low $T$ | overlap distribution becomes nontrivial | $P(q)$, Parisi measure | slow relaxation, aging |
| Random medium vs glass | quenched disorder may exist without metastable hierarchy | sample-dependent fields | not necessarily slow unless barriers/constraints exist |
| Plasma vs glass | long-range collective fields and screening dominate | Debye length, distribution function | kinetic relaxation/collisions, usually ergodic fluid-like |

The key mathematical shift from crystal to glass is the loss of a finite-dimensional symmetry-breaking order parameter.  The key shift from liquid to glass is not an obvious change in equal-time two-point structure but a change in the connectivity and transition rates of configuration space.  The key shift from glass to jammed matter is from thermally activated metastability to constraint-saturated mechanical rigidity.

# 12. Annotated bibliography

The YAML frontmatter contains CSL-compatible references.  The annotations below are selective; they emphasize sources useful for designing new mathematical research.

## 12.1 Foundational spin glass theory

**Edwards and Anderson, 1975** [@edwards1975].  Introduced the short-range spin-glass model and the Edwards--Anderson order parameter.  It remains the canonical finite-dimensional model whose low-temperature structure is still not fully settled.

**Sherrington and Kirkpatrick, 1975** [@sherrington1975].  Introduced the infinite-range solvable model that made mean-field spin glass theory possible.  The model's false replica-symmetric solution forced the development of RSB.

**Parisi, 1979/1980** [@parisi1979; @parisi1980].  Introduced the hierarchical order parameter and RSB ansatz.  These papers are the origin of ultrametric state organization and the infinite-dimensional order parameter used in the Parisi formula.

**Mézard, Parisi, and Virasoro, 1987** [@mezard1987].  The classic book-length account of replica theory, pure states, TAP equations, ultrametricity, and spin-glass methods.  Still essential for translating between physics notation and modern rigorous results.

**Derrida, 1981** [@derrida1981].  The REM is the cleanest solvable model of freezing and entropy crisis.  It is a useful baseline for understanding what is lost when correlations and geometry are removed.

**Crisanti and Sommers; Crisanti, Horner, and Sommers, 1992--1993** [@crisanti1992; @crisanti1993].  The spherical $p$-spin model gives a tractable landscape with dynamical/static transition separation.  It is one of the main bridges between spin glasses and structural-glass RFOT phenomenology.

## 12.2 Foundational structural glass and dynamics

**Stillinger, 1995** [@stillinger1995].  Introduced a landscape/inherent-structure view that remains central in simulation and conceptual work.  It is valuable because it turns glassy dynamics into geometry of basins and saddles.

**Debenedetti and Stillinger, 2001** [@debenedetti2001].  A foundational review of supercooled liquids and glass transition phenomenology.  Useful for thermodynamic constraints and the experimental meaning of $T_g$.

**Berthier and Biroli, 2011** [@berthier2011].  A major theoretical review covering RFOT, MCT, dynamic heterogeneity, facilitation, simulations, and experiments.  It remains one of the best entry points for mathematical researchers entering structural glass theory.

**Götze, 2009; Janssen, 2018** [@gotze2009; @janssen2018].  Götze is the standard monograph on MCT; Janssen is a modern concise primer.  Together they clarify what MCT predicts and why its ideal transition is not the final finite-dimensional story.

**Ritort and Sollich, 2003; Chandler and Garrahan, 2010** [@ritort2003; @chandler2010].  These sources develop dynamic facilitation and KCMs.  They are essential for understanding theories where slowdown is primarily kinetic rather than thermodynamic.

## 12.3 RFOT, high-dimensional hard spheres, Gardner, and jamming

**Kirkpatrick, Thirumalai, and Wolynes, 1987/1989; Lubchenko and Wolynes, 2007** [@kirkpatrick1987; @kirkpatrick1989; @lubchenko2007].  Foundational RFOT works.  They connect $p$-spin-like mean-field theory to finite-dimensional mosaic relaxation.

**Parisi and Zamponi, 2010** [@parisi2010hard].  Canonical review of mean-field hard-sphere glasses and jamming.  It is the central source for replica treatment of hard-sphere glass thermodynamics.

**Kurchan, Parisi, Urbani, and Zamponi, 2013** [@kurchan2013].  A key high-dimensional hard-sphere paper deriving the high-density/Gardner regime.  It is important for marginal stability and full RSB in structural glasses.

**Charbonneau et al., 2017** [@charbonneau2017].  A modern review connecting exact infinite-dimensional theory to finite-dimensional glass and jamming.  Especially useful for knowing which predictions survive numerically.

**Biroli and Bouchaud, 2023** [@biroli2023rfot].  A recent critical assessment of RFOT.  Valuable because it separates strong theory-driven claims from what is directly validated.

**Kirkpatrick and Thirumalai, 2024** [@kirkpatrick2024].  A recent density-functional restatement of universal RFOT aspects.  Useful for researchers looking for continuum variational formulations.

**Jin, 2022; Wang, Pan, and Jin, 2024** [@jin2022; @wang2024gardner].  Current computational studies of Gardner physics and its relation to jamming scalings.  Important for the finite-dimensional Gardner debate.

**Deng, Miskin, and Jaeger, 2024; Shang, Guan, and Jin, 2024/2025** [@deng2024; @shang2024yielding; @shang2026topsat].  Recent jamming frontier work emphasizing quenched disorder, criticality, marginal yielding, and topological satisfiability.  These are useful for formulating modern mathematical jamming problems.

## 12.4 Rigorous mathematics

**Guerra, 2003; Talagrand, 2006** [@guerra2003; @talagrand2006].  The rigorous foundation of the Parisi formula.  Any mathematical work on glass theory should understand why interpolation and concentration succeed here.

**Panchenko, 2013** [@panchenko2013ultra; @panchenko2013book].  Proved ultrametricity under Ghirlanda--Guerra identities and systematized the rigorous SK theory.  This is the cleanest theorem-level realization of hierarchical glass geometry.

**Auffinger, Ben Arous, and Černý, 2013; Subag, 2017** [@auffinger2013complexity; @subag2017].  Rigorous complexity of random spherical landscapes.  These papers are central for connecting glass language to random topology and random matrix theory.

**Mourrat, 2025; Chatterjee, 2026** [@mourrat2025; @chatterjee2026].  Recent high-level accounts of the rigorous Parisi program and its extensions.  Useful for identifying what remains out of reach.

**Manai and Warzel, 2024** [@manai2024].  A quantum Parisi formula for mean-field transverse-field spin glasses.  Important because quantum glass theory is becoming more mathematical.

**Huang, Sellke, Montanari, and Pham, 2024** [@huang2024constructive; @huang2024sampling].  Constructive and algorithmic approaches to spherical spin glasses.  These works point toward a merger of Parisi theory, algorithms, and sampling.

**Gamarnik, Jagannath, and Kızıldağ, 2025; Kızıldağ, 2025; Montanari, 2026** [@gamarnik2025; @kizildag2025; @montanari2026].  Current rigorous work connecting spin-glass geometry to computational complexity, OGP, shattering, and learning/optimization.  Essential for the algorithmic frontier.

## 12.5 Computation and machine learning

**Ninarello, Berthier, and Coslovich, 2017** [@ninarello2017].  Established swap Monte Carlo models that equilibrate deeply supercooled liquids.  It changed what numerical tests of glass theories can access.

**Scalliet, Berthier, and Zamponi, 2022** [@scalliet2022].  A landmark deep-equilibration simulation.  Important for testing theories close to laboratory-relevant time scales.

**Bapst et al., 2020; Jung, Biroli, and Berthier, 2023/2024; ML Roadmap, 2025** [@bapst2020; @jung2023; @jung2024transfer; @jung2025roadmap].  The core modern ML sequence for predicting glassy dynamics from structure.  These works force a re-evaluation of what “hidden order” might mean.

**Pihlajamaa and Janssen, 2023** [@pihlajamaa2023].  An example of modern open-source computational infrastructure for MCT.  Important because reproducibility of theoretical numerics is becoming central.

**Herrero and Berthier, 2024; Chacko, Jack, and Berthier, 2023** [@herrero2024; @chacko2023].  Recent direct tests of dynamic facilitation in atomistic systems.  Useful for comparing thermodynamic and kinetic mechanisms.

**Simon, Scalliet, and Berthier, 2026; Böhmer et al., 2025** [@simon2026; @boehmer2025].  Current efforts to reach molecular motion near experimental glass transition regimes with advanced Monte Carlo.  These works help close the gap between simulation and experiment.

## 12.6 Topology and amorphous materials

**Edelsbrunner, Letscher, and Zomorodian, 2002; Carlsson, 2009** [@edelsbrunner2002; @carlsson2009].  General mathematical foundations of persistent homology and TDA.  Necessary background for distinguishing real topology from metaphor.

**Hiraoka et al., 2016; Nakamura et al., 2015** [@hiraoka2016; @nakamura2015].  Early influential applications of persistence diagrams to amorphous solids and medium-range order.  These papers show why topology can see beyond pair correlations.

**Sørensen et al., 2020; Hirata et al., 2020** [@sorensen2020; @hirata2020].  Applications to oxide and metallic glasses.  Useful for connecting topological summaries to materials-specific structure-property relations.

**Minamitani et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025** [@minamitani2025; @wu2025topdefects].  Recent frontier papers connecting topological/amorphous structural features to vibrations, nonaffine elasticity, and defects.  These are among the most relevant sources for a mathematically serious topology-of-glasses program.

## 12.7 Experimental/physical interface and emerging regimes

**Hunter and Weeks, 2012; Dauchot, Ladieu, and Royall, 2023** [@hunter2012; @dauchot2023].  Reviews of colloidal/granular/molecular glass experiments and what can be measured.  Important for knowing how mathematical observables appear in data.

**Laermann et al., 2026** [@laermann2026].  Recent colloidal evidence for emergent glass-transition signatures using two-particle correlations and response ideas.  Useful as an example of mathematically structured experimental inference.

**Lerner and Bouchbinder, 2021; Moriel, Lerner, and Bouchbinder, 2024/2025** [@lerner2021; @moriel2024omega; @moriel2024boson; @lerner2025het].  Modern sources on low-energy excitations, boson peaks, and heterogeneous elasticity in structural glasses.  Essential for the amorphous-solid frontier.

**Sadhukhan, Nandi, and Gov, 2024; Winer et al., 2024; Sachdev, 2024; Brodoloni et al., 2025** [@sadhukhan2024; @winer2024; @sachdev2024; @brodoloni2025].  Sources for active and quantum glass directions.  These are emerging regimes where the correct mathematical framework is still forming.

# 13. Research design heuristics

A mathematician entering glass theory should choose the regime before choosing the tool.  The following heuristics reduce category errors.

1. **If you want theorems now, start with mean-field spin glasses.**  The Parisi formula, ultrametricity, and random landscape complexity offer precise objects and active open problems.
2. **If you want finite-dimensional physical relevance, accept partial rigor.**  Structural glass theory is rich but not theorem-complete.  Work with carefully idealized models, Kac limits, pinned systems, or computable observables.
3. **If you study topology, specify the space.**  Are you taking homology of particle point clouds, configuration-space feasible sets, contact networks, or metastable-state graphs?  These are different mathematical objects.
4. **If you use ML, demand transfer and interpretation.**  A predictor at one temperature/model is not an order parameter.  Cross-model transfer, uncertainty estimates, and relation to known fields matter.
5. **If you study jamming, state the ensemble.**  Compression protocol, friction, particle shape, boundary conditions, and rattler removal can change conclusions.
6. **If you invoke RSB in structural glass, mark the level of evidence.**  It may be theorem-level in mean-field spin glasses, controlled/accepted in infinite-dimensional hard-sphere physics, numerical in finite-dimensional simulations, and speculative in some experiments.
7. **If you discuss a transition, separate thermodynamic, dynamic, algorithmic, and experimental meanings.**  The same phrase “glass transition” can refer to distinct mathematical objects.

The field's central unresolved mathematical problem is to construct a finite-dimensional theory that simultaneously explains amorphous order, activated dynamics, aging, marginal stability, and empirical universality without importing mean-field conclusions beyond their domain of validity.
