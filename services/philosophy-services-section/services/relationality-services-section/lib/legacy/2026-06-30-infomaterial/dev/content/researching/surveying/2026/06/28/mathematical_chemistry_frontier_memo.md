**Date:** June 28, 2026  
**Scope:** This memo interprets “mathematical chemistry” in the sense you specified: not just mathematics applied to real molecules, but formal systems in which mathematical objects interact as if they were chemical species. In this view, terms, graphs, proofs, programs, equations, or abstract “molecules” enter a soup; local rules make them collide, combine, reduce, catalyze, replicate, inhibit, or decay; and the resulting population dynamics become the computation.

## Executive takeaways

The current frontier is a convergence of **chemical reaction network theory, artificial chemistry, graph rewriting, molecular programming, differentiable simulation, category theory, and physical chemical computing**. The deepest shift is from “Can chemistry compute?” to “Can chemical interaction be used as a general semantics for mathematics, computation, and self-organization?”

The most mature branch is **chemical reaction networks**: abstract species and reactions are modeled as deterministic ODEs, stochastic processes, Petri nets, vector-addition systems, or population protocols. This branch now has substantial theory about computability, robustness, rate independence, error bounds, and model extensions such as zero detection. Recent work is sharpening exactly which computations continuous or stochastic CRNs can perform, and under what assumptions. ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11047-018-9723-9))

The most literal version of your phrase—**mathematical expressions reacting chemically**—comes from the Chemical Abstract Machine and artificial chemistries such as AlChemy. In the Chemical Abstract Machine, program states are “chemical solutions” of freely moving molecules that react by rules; in AlChemy, lambda-calculus expressions are molecules, function application is molecular collision, and lambda reduction is reaction. Recent work has revived this line by studying endogenous selection in program soups and categorical frameworks that connect algebraic objects to dynamical chemical populations. ([lix.polytechnique.fr](https://www.lix.polytechnique.fr/~fvalenci/papers/cham.pdf))

The highest-momentum frontier today is probably the union of **trainable CRNs** and **chemical reservoir computing**. On one side, differentiable programming is making reaction networks trainable rather than purely hand-designed; on the other, real self-organizing chemical systems such as the formose reaction have been used as chemical reservoirs for nonlinear classification, prediction, and time-series tasks. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03456?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

A second major frontier is **structural chemistry as graph and hypergraph rewriting**. Classical chemical graph theory treats molecules as graphs, but newer work treats reactions as higher-order transformations, directed hypergraphs, or double-pushout graph rewrites. This is important because chemical interaction is rarely just pairwise: reactions consume and produce multisets, mechanisms rewrite substructures, and self-organization depends on networks of transformations rather than isolated molecules. ([osti.gov](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/2447040))

The hardest unsolved questions are no longer only about universality. They are about **control without overdesign, physical realizability, compositional semantics, thermodynamic cost, noise, scale, and meaning**: what should count as the output of a chemical mathematical system when the interesting object is not a number but an autocatalytic organization, attractor, evolving graph ecology, or learned readout?

## 1. What “mathematical chemistry” means in this memo

The term **mathematical chemistry** traditionally means the use of mathematical methods in chemistry: chemical graph theory, molecular descriptors, reaction networks, quantum chemistry, topology, kinetics, and related modeling. That traditional field is itself being modernized by new encodings such as hypergraphs, higher-order descriptors, and data-rich graph representations. ([osti.gov](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/2447040))

Your formulation points to a more radical version:

| Chemical notion | Mathematical/computational analogue |
|---|---|
| Molecule | A formal object: term, graph, string, proof, type, program, species, or symbolic expression |
| Reaction | A local rewrite, reduction, transition, inference, binding event, or transformation |
| Soup / solution | A multiset, population, concentration vector, graph mixture, or spatial field |
| Kinetics | ODEs, stochastic simulation, nondeterministic rewriting, Petri-net reachability, or real chemistry |
| Catalyst | An object that enables transformation while being preserved or regenerated |
| Equilibrium / attractor | Stable computation, fixed point, learned reservoir state, organization, or self-maintaining structure |
| Product / output | A count, concentration, graph, reduced term, proof, attractor, classification readout, or emergent ecology |

The core intellectual move is to treat **interaction itself** as the primitive. Instead of starting with a single program and evaluating it step by step, the chemical view starts with many objects in a shared medium. Computation happens because local interactions change the population. This makes the chemical metaphor especially natural for concurrency, distributed computation, biological regulation, origin-of-life models, artificial life, and analog computation.

## 2. Historical anchor: from chemical metaphor to formal machine

The central historical predecessor is Berry and Boudol’s **Chemical Abstract Machine**. It models a computational state as a chemical solution containing molecules; computation proceeds when molecules interact by reaction rules, and membranes can localize reactions. The model was designed to give operational semantics for concurrent calculi, where independent components move freely and communicate when they “meet.” ([lix.polytechnique.fr](https://www.lix.polytechnique.fr/~fvalenci/papers/cham.pdf))

That idea matters because it shifted the semantics of computation away from a single control thread. In a chemical machine, there is no privileged instruction pointer. The global state is a population; any enabled local reaction may fire. This makes the model well suited to asynchronous concurrency, process calculi, distributed systems, and eventually molecular programming. ([lix.polytechnique.fr](https://www.lix.polytechnique.fr/~fvalenci/papers/cham.pdf))

The other key ancestor is **AlChemy**, introduced by Walter Fontana and Leo Buss. AlChemy treats lambda expressions as molecules; collisions apply one expression to another; lambda reduction produces the reaction product; and the population evolves as expressions are selected, reduced, inserted, and removed from the soup. Recent work has returned to this model to study self-organization, autocatalytic-like structures, and the possibility of steering useful functional programs through endogenous selection rather than externally imposed fitness. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.03534v1))

Together, CHAM and AlChemy establish the two poles of the field: **chemical semantics for concurrency** and **chemical dynamics for symbolic objects**.

## 3. The main research line: chemical reaction networks as a programming language

A **chemical reaction network** is a finite set of formal reactions such as:

\[
A + B \rightarrow C,\quad C \rightarrow A + D.
\]

In classical chemistry, the symbols are molecular species. In mathematical chemistry-as-computation, they may instead be abstract signals, counters, states, program fragments, or logical predicates. The same reaction network can be interpreted as deterministic mass-action ODEs, stochastic jump processes, Petri nets, population protocols, or executable molecular designs. ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11047-018-9723-9))

The mature theoretical question is: **what can such networks compute?** Work in this area has connected CRNs to Boolean circuits, vector addition systems, Petri nets, register machines, Fractran, Turing machines, primitive recursive functions, and population protocols. The boundary between decidable and undecidable behavior is thin because small changes in model assumptions can move the system from analyzable finite-state dynamics to universal computation. ([authors.library.caltech.edu](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/gf8h4-ta232))

The frontier has become more refined. Researchers are no longer satisfied with showing that CRNs are universal in principle. They ask whether a network computes **robustly**, **quickly**, **rate-independently**, and with bounded error under continuous or stochastic dynamics. Recent work on robust predicate and function computation in continuous CRNs, analog computation by mass-action systems, and polynomial equivalence among extended CRN models is trying to classify what computation remains possible under realistic dynamical constraints. ([drops.dagstuhl.de](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.DISC.2025.19))

A useful way to describe this branch is: CRNs are becoming an **intermediate representation** for chemical computation, analogous to how a compiler intermediate representation sits between high-level code and hardware. The “hardware” may be a wet-lab DNA system, a biochemical controller, an analog simulator, or a mathematical model.

## 4. Molecular programming: when the formal chemistry becomes laboratory chemistry

The bridge from abstract CRNs to physical chemistry is **molecular programming**, especially DNA strand displacement. In this paradigm, a desired formal reaction network is compiled into DNA molecules whose binding and displacement reactions emulate the target network. Tutorial and survey work explicitly treats CRNs as designed computational objects and discusses implementing arbitrary abstract CRNs in test tubes using DNA. ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11047-018-9723-9))

This is one of the clearest examples of “mathematical interactions as chemical interactions” becoming literal. A formal reaction such as \(X + Y \rightarrow Z\) can be transformed into a set of DNA strand-displacement gates; the mathematical species are represented by oligonucleotide signals, and reaction rules become molecular mechanisms. Tools such as Nuskell translate formal CRNs into DNA strand-displacement designs, while experimental papers have demonstrated programmable chemical controllers based on this formalism. ([dna.caltech.edu](https://www.dna.caltech.edu/~badelt/nuskell/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The frontier is not just expressivity; it is **scalability and reliability**. DNA implementations face leak reactions, unintended crosstalk, slow kinetics, degradation, fuel/waste accumulation, and readout challenges. Recent work on leakless polymerase-dependent strand-displacement systems aims to improve composability and emulate arbitrary CRNs more cleanly, showing that the field is still actively trying to close the gap between formal CRN universality and robust laboratory-scale programmability. ([drops.dagstuhl.de](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.DNA.31.11?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This branch has a practical research program: make chemical programs compile, run, scale, and verify.

## 5. Artificial chemistry: symbolic objects in a reaction soup

Artificial chemistry is the branch closest to your wording. Its objects do not have to be real molecules; they can be lambda terms, strings, graphs, combinators, or other mathematical structures. A reaction is a rule that transforms one or more such objects. The goal is often not just computation, but **self-organization**, **autocatalysis**, **evolvability**, and **open-ended novelty**.

AlChemy is the canonical example. It uses lambda calculus as the underlying molecular language, so interaction between expressions is computation. Because lambda calculus is computationally universal but can also exhibit nontermination, artificial chemistries impose operational choices: which molecules collide, how many reductions are allowed, which products enter the soup, and which molecules are removed. These choices create a population dynamics over programs rather than a single program evaluation trace. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.03534v1))

The current revival is interesting because it treats program soups as evolving ecologies. Recent AlChemy work explores endogenous selection: instead of an external fitness function, the system’s own interactions can favor expressions that participate in useful or persistent organizations. This connects artificial chemistry to origin-of-life research, autocatalytic sets, artificial life, and program synthesis. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.03534v1))

There are also graph-based artificial chemistries such as chemlambda and chemSKI, where computation proceeds through local graph rewrites inspired by lambda calculus, interaction combinators, or SKI combinatory logic. These systems are more experimental and less institutionally mature than CRN theory, but they are important because they push the chemical metaphor to its limit: computation is a population of local graph reactions rather than symbolic evaluation by a global interpreter. ([imar.ro](https://imar.ro/~mbuliga/arxiv-local-pdf/1309.6914.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The major open question is whether artificial chemistry can move from evocative demonstrations to a disciplined theory of **semantic emergence**: when a soup stabilizes, grows, cycles, or differentiates, what exactly has it “computed”?

## 6. Graph rewriting and hypergraph chemistry: reactions as structural transformations

Traditional CRNs usually abstract away internal molecular structure. A species is just a label. That is powerful, but it misses the fact that real chemistry transforms structured objects. A molecule is not merely “A”; it is a graph of atoms and bonds, and reactions rewrite subgraphs.

This motivates **chemical graph rewriting**. Molecules are graphs, reactions are local graph transformations, and a reaction network is generated by applying rules to all matching substructures. A 2025 line of work on computation in chemical graph rewriting networks frames biological systems as computations by chemical transformations and studies whether graph-rewriting chemistries can emulate Turing machines or compute directly over chemical graphs. ([ucrisportal.univie.ac.at](https://ucrisportal.univie.ac.at/en/publications/computation-in-chemical-graph-rewriting-networks/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

A major formal framework here is **double-pushout graph rewriting**, which gives mathematically precise rules for replacing one graph pattern with another while controlling what context is preserved. Recent work on rules, hypergraphs, and probabilities describes chemical systems at three levels: local reaction rules, generated reaction hypergraphs, and stochastic population dynamics over the resulting state space. ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/complexsystems/article/file?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcsy.0000022&type=printable))

This is a powerful conceptual advance because it connects three things that are often separated:

1. **Mechanism:** local rewrite rules that look like chemistry.  
2. **Network:** the enormous reaction hypergraph generated by those rules.  
3. **Dynamics:** stochastic or deterministic evolution over populations of structures.

Hypergraphs are especially natural because chemical reactions are many-to-many: multiple reactants can produce multiple products. Recent work argues that chemistry contains higher-order relationships that ordinary pairwise graphs cannot express cleanly, and that directed hypergraphs and hypergraph neural networks can encode reactions and multi-atom interactions more faithfully. ([pubs.rsc.org](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2026/dd/d5dd00533g))

This area also modernizes classical mathematical chemistry. Chemical graph theory is no longer only about molecular descriptors or topological indices; it is increasingly about richer encodings of molecules, reactions, mechanisms, data, and simulation outputs. ([osti.gov](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/2447040))

## 7. Differentiable chemical systems: learning the reactions

A major new frontier is the use of machine learning and differentiable programming to design chemical dynamics. Instead of hand-writing reaction networks, researchers train them.

One example is recurrent neural chemical reaction networks, which frame CRNs as molecular analogues of recurrent neural networks and study how auxiliary species and fast reactions can produce desired dynamics. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03456?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Another example is differentiable programming for indexed CRNs and reaction-diffusion systems. This line uses automatic differentiation to optimize reaction parameters and, in some cases, spatially extended chemical dynamics. Differentiable Gillespie approaches pursue related goals for stochastic reaction simulation by making stochastic chemical trajectories compatible with gradient-based optimization frameworks such as PyTorch, JAX, or Julia. ([drops.dagstuhl.de](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.DNA.31.4))

This changes the research question. Instead of asking, “Can I prove this hand-built network computes a function?” the question becomes, “Can I define a loss function, train a chemical system, and then verify that the learned dynamics are robust, interpretable, physically realizable, and thermodynamically plausible?”

The opportunity is large because chemical systems naturally support massive parallelism, analog state, nonlinear dynamics, and distributed interaction. The danger is that learned CRNs may become black boxes with fragile parameter dependence. A frontier research program will need both machine learning and formal methods: train the chemistry, then prove or certify what the trained chemistry does.

## 8. Chemical reservoir computing: using real reaction networks as computers

The most concrete recent breakthrough is **chemical reservoir computing**. Reservoir computing uses a complex dynamical system as a high-dimensional nonlinear medium. Inputs perturb the system; a usually simple readout layer is trained to extract the desired output. The internal system does not need to be fully designed.

A 2024 Nature paper reported chemical reservoir computation in a self-organizing reaction network based on the formose reaction. The system performed nonlinear classification tasks, predicted dynamical systems, and handled time-series forecasting, showing that complex reaction networks can provide useful emergent computational capability. ([nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07567-x))

This is important because it departs from the usual molecular-programming strategy. Rather than building every reaction gate bottom-up, reservoir computing exploits a naturally complex chemical medium. It treats chemistry less like a circuit board and more like a trainable physical substrate. ([nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07567-x))

A related 2026 line of work proposes **ChemComp**, a compilation framework for mapping mathematical ODE abstractions to chemical reservoirs or physically realizable CRNs. The ambition is to translate scientific computing tasks into chemical reaction systems, making CRNs part of an analog computing stack. ([nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44335-026-00053-9))

This branch may be the most practically exciting because it does not require exact molecular-level programmability. Its bottlenecks are different: encoding inputs, measuring outputs, maintaining reproducibility, avoiding drift, and understanding what kinds of tasks chemical reservoirs are actually good at.

## 9. Category theory and compositional chemical semantics

Category theory is entering this frontier because chemical systems are intrinsically compositional. Reactions compose into networks; networks compose through shared species; open systems interact through boundaries; algebraic syntax maps to dynamics.

A 2026 paper on categorical algebraic artificial chemistry constructs a functor that gives dynamics to an algebraic model of interacting components and explicitly generalizes the Fontana-Buss AlChemy tradition. Its goal is to separate and connect the algebraic side—what the molecules are and how they combine—from the dynamical side—how populations evolve. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09431))

The broader importance is that category theory may provide the missing “compiler correctness” layer. If a lambda soup, CRN, Petri net, graph rewrite system, and DNA implementation are all different presentations of chemical interaction, then one wants translations among them that preserve behavior. Category-theoretic tools are well suited for specifying such compositional translations.

This is still early, but strategically important. The field has many models; it needs maps between models.

## 10. Autocatalysis, thermodynamics, and origin-of-life mathematics

Chemical interaction is not only computation in the computer-science sense. It is also a theory of self-maintaining organization. Autocatalytic sets, hypercycles, metabolic closures, and reaction-network instability are central to origin-of-life research and to artificial life.

Recent CRN theory studies when network topology implies instability, feedback, or autocatalytic cores. Work on autocatalytic cores and unstable subnetworks connects stoichiometric structure, generalized kinetics, and positive feedback. Other recent work develops algorithms for enumerating autocatalytic subsystems in large metabolic networks. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11486?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

A key issue is that topology alone rarely determines dynamics. Reaction rates, thermodynamic driving, boundary conditions, conservation laws, compartmentalization, and stochastic effects matter. Recent work on network topology and relaxation dynamics emphasizes that the link between structure and out-of-steady-state behavior remains underdeveloped. ([nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-73104-5?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This matters for mathematical chemistry because “chemical interaction” is not just rewriting. Real chemical systems consume energy, dissipate heat, build gradients, and maintain nonequilibrium states. A mature theory must connect formal computation to thermodynamic cost and physical viability.

## 11. Where the frontier is sharpest

### A. From universality to robust, resource-bounded chemical computation

Earlier work often aimed to show that a chemical model is Turing-complete. The frontier now asks: complete under what noise, rates, volume, time, energy, and readout assumptions? Robust CRN computation, analog mass-action arithmetic, and equivalence of extended CRN models are all attempts to make chemical computation mathematically precise enough to compare across models. ([drops.dagstuhl.de](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.DISC.2025.19))

### B. From hand-designed reactions to learned reaction systems

Differentiable CRNs and neural CRNs suggest that one can train chemical dynamics the way one trains neural networks. This could create chemical controllers, oscillators, classifiers, memory systems, and pattern generators that are too difficult to design manually. The challenge is verification and interpretability. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03456?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### C. From symbolic molecules to evolving program ecologies

AlChemy-like systems treat programs as molecules and allow populations of programs to self-organize. The interesting question is not whether lambda calculus is universal; it is whether chemical population dynamics can discover, preserve, combine, and evolve useful computational organizations. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.03534v1))

### D. From species labels to structured graph molecules

Graph rewriting and hypergraph chemistry move beyond species-as-labels. They let the system generate new molecular structures and new reaction networks as it evolves. This is essential for modeling synthesis, metabolism, polymerization, artificial life, and open-ended chemical novelty. ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/complexsystems/article/file?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcsy.0000022&type=printable))

### E. From designed circuits to physical chemical substrates

Chemical reservoir computing shows that useful computation may emerge from complex physical chemistry without explicitly programming every reaction. This points toward a future in which chemistry is used as an analog coprocessor for certain nonlinear dynamical tasks. ([nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07567-x))

## 12. Main bottlenecks and open problems

**1. Semantics of emergence.**  
For a CRN computing a predicate, output semantics are relatively clear: a species count stabilizes or a concentration approaches a value. For artificial chemistries, graph soups, and autocatalytic organizations, output semantics are much harder. Does the system compute an attractor, a distribution, a maintained organization, a transformation pathway, or a reusable structure?

**2. Verification under realistic dynamics.**  
Many chemical systems are nonlinear, stochastic, and parameter-sensitive. The field needs verification tools that can handle approximate computation, noisy reactions, finite volume, leak reactions, uncertain rates, and stochastic extinction.

**3. Compilation across levels.**  
A promising theory might start with a high-level mathematical interaction language, compile it to CRNs, compile those to DNA strand displacement or another wet chemistry, and then certify that the physical implementation preserves the intended behavior. Pieces of this stack exist, but a general, reliable compiler chain remains a frontier problem. ([dna.caltech.edu](https://www.dna.caltech.edu/~badelt/nuskell/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**4. Physical scale.**  
Laboratory molecular programs remain much smaller and more fragile than abstract CRN theory permits. Reservoir computing avoids some design burdens, but it introduces reproducibility and control issues.

**5. Energy and thermodynamics.**  
Formal reactions often ignore fuel, waste, reversibility, entropy production, and nonequilibrium constraints. A serious physical theory of mathematical chemistry needs to account for what computations cost.

**6. Spatiality and compartmentalization.**  
Many formal models assume well-mixed solutions. Real chemistry occurs in compartments, membranes, droplets, surfaces, gels, cells, and reaction-diffusion fields. Spatial chemical computation is still less developed than well-mixed CRN computation, though differentiable reaction-diffusion work is pushing in this direction. ([drops.dagstuhl.de](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.DNA.31.4))

**7. Strong universality for real chemical structures.**  
It is easier to show that a graph-rewriting chemistry can emulate a Turing machine using specially encoded graphs. It is harder to show that it can compute naturally over chemically meaningful graphs without artificial encodings. This distinction is now explicit in the graph-rewriting frontier. ([ucrisportal.univie.ac.at](https://ucrisportal.univie.ac.at/en/publications/computation-in-chemical-graph-rewriting-networks/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**8. Interoperability of formalisms.**  
The field contains CRNs, Petri nets, stochastic chemical kinetics, lambda calculi, graph rewriting, hypergraphs, reaction-diffusion systems, reservoir computers, and categorical models. The frontier needs translations that preserve behavior, complexity, approximation, and physical implementability.

## 14. Bottom-line assessment

The frontier of “mathematical chemistry” is best understood as the attempt to make **reaction** a foundational mathematical operation. In ordinary mathematics, one often studies objects and functions between objects. In this frontier, one studies **populations of objects whose local interactions transform the population**. That small shift changes the ontology: computation becomes ecological, concurrent, stochastic, thermodynamic, and potentially self-organizing.

The most credible near-term advances will likely come from **CRN theory, differentiable CRN design, DNA/molecular programming, and chemical reservoir computing**. These areas have formal models, active implementation pathways, and measurable tasks. The most conceptually ambitious advances will come from **artificial chemistry, graph rewriting, hypergraph chemistry, and categorical semantics**, where the goal is not merely to compute known functions but to understand how mathematical structures can collide, reproduce, compose, and evolve.

The field’s central unresolved problem is to connect four levels without losing meaning:

\[
\text{formal interaction} \rightarrow \text{reaction network} \rightarrow \text{dynamical behavior} \rightarrow \text{physical or semantic output}.
\]

Whoever builds reliable bridges across those levels will have something close to a true mathematical chemistry of interaction.
