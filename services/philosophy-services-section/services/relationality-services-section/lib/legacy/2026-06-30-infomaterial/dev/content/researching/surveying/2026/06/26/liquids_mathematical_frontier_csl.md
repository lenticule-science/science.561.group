# Mathematical frontier of liquids: a research memo, 2026

## Executive thesis

There is no single mathematical definition of “a liquid” comparable to “a group” or “a manifold.” In current research, a liquid is a **regime of matter and motion** represented by a compatible stack of mathematical objects:

\[ \text{particle configurations} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{correlation functions / Gibbs or nonequilibrium measures} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{density, momentum, energy fields} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{hydrodynamic PDEs and stochastic field theories}. \]

A liquid is distinguished by **dense short-range order**, **absence of persistent global positional order**, **mobile local structure**, **finite transport coefficients**, and **ability to support flow**.
The frontier is not just “better simulations.” It is the unresolved mathematical passage between microscopic many-body dynamics, mesoscopic statistical structure, and macroscopic continuum laws—especially outside equilibrium, near glassiness, in active matter, in turbulent regimes, and in quantum or electronic fluids.

Liquid-state theory remains organized around correlation functions, especially the radial distribution function \(g(r)\), static structure factor \(S(k)\), Ornstein–Zernike theory, density functional theory, and molecular dynamics.
Hansen and McDonald’s *Theory of Simple Liquids* is still a canonical advanced reference, and modern work continues to refine integral-equation and density-functional formulations rather than replacing them wholesale.
([books.google.com](https://books.google.com/books/about/Theory_of_Simple_Liquids.html?id=pbJfOUqZVSgC&utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## 1. Core mathematical object: what is a liquid?

### 1.1 Microscopic object

At the particle level, a classical one-component liquid is modeled by \(N\) particles in a domain \(\Omega\subset \mathbb{R}^d\), with phase space

\[ \Gamma_N=(\Omega^N\times(\mathbb{R}^d)^N)/S_N, \]

Hamiltonian

\[ H_N(q,p)=\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{|p_i|^2}{2m} +\sum_{1\le i<j\le N}\phi(|q_i-q_j|) +\cdots , \]

and a Gibbs measure, canonical or grand-canonical,

\[ d\mu_{N,\beta}(q,p)=Z_N^{-1}e^{-\beta H_N(q,p)}\,dq\,dp . \]

The dots indicate many-body forces, constraints, charges, orientational variables, or internal molecular degrees of freedom.
A “simple liquid” usually means stable, tempered, mostly short-range interactions at density high enough that correlations are nontrivial but below crystalline or glassy arrest.
Rigorous equilibrium expansions are strongest at low density or high temperature; dense realistic liquids remain much less tractable.
Cluster expansion results give uniform convergence of Mayer/virial expansions for stable and tempered pair potentials in a high-temperature, low-density region, but not in the dense liquid regime where packing and metastability dominate.
([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00220-012-1576-y?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

A useful microscopic observable is the empirical density

\[ \rho_N(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^N \delta(x-q_i(t)), \]

with empirical momentum

\[ \pi_N(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^N p_i(t)\,\delta(x-q_i(t)). \]

Hydrodynamic theory asks whether, after scaling space and time, \(\rho_N\), \(\pi_N\), and the empirical energy converge to deterministic or stochastic fields solving continuum equations.

### 1.2 Statistical object: short-range correlations without global order

For a homogeneous isotropic liquid, the two-point structure is encoded by

\[ g(r) = \frac{1}{\rho N} \left\langle \sum_{i\neq j}\delta(r-|q_i-q_j|) \right\rangle \quad \text{up to conventional shell-volume normalization}. \]

Equivalently, the total correlation function is

\[ h(r)=g(r)-1. \]

The static structure factor is

\[ S(k) = \frac1N \left\langle \left| \sum_{j=1}^N e^{-ik\cdot q_j} \right|^2 \right\rangle = 1+\rho \widehat{h}(k), \]

again up to Fourier convention.
In a normal liquid away from a critical point, \(g(r)\) has pronounced first-neighbor and second-neighbor peaks but these damp with distance. \(S(k)\) has broad diffuse peaks rather than Bragg delta peaks.
This is the precise sense in which liquids have **local order but no fixed global positional structure**.
Modern scattering and simulation studies still use \(S(k)\) and \(g(r)\) as the primary bridge between microscopic structure and experiment.
([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12621243/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 1.3 Dynamical object: mobile local order and flow

“Mobile local order” means that local neighborhoods are structured, but particle identities exchange and the structure relaxes.
Formally, the relevant distinction is not simply spatial disorder; it is **relaxation of stress and density correlations**.
A liquid may have a transient cage around each particle, but the cage lifetime is finite in an ergodic liquid.
In contrast, a glass has amorphous local structure but loses ergodicity on experimental or simulation time scales.

Transport is represented by coefficients such as diffusion \(D\), shear viscosity \(\eta\), bulk viscosity \(\zeta\), and thermal conductivity \(\kappa\).
For equilibrium fluids, these are expressed through Green–Kubo relations.
For example,

\[ D=\frac1d\int_0^\infty \langle v(0)\cdot v(t)\rangle\,dt = \lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{2dt} \left\langle |q(t)-q(0)|^2\right\rangle , \]

and, for shear viscosity,

\[ \eta = \frac{V}{k_B T} \int_0^\infty \langle P_{xy}(0)P_{xy}(t)\rangle\,dt , \]

where \(P_{xy}\) is an off-diagonal microscopic stress component.
The fluctuation-dissipation and Green–Kubo framework is classical, but practical and rigorous issues remain: long-time tails, finite-size effects, poor statistical convergence, and lack of proven decay of correlations in many dense liquids.
Recent computational-method papers still emphasize that viscosity estimation from Green–Kubo formulas requires long simulations and careful uncertainty quantification.
([www-f1.ijs.si](https://www-f1.ijs.si/~ramsak/km1/kubo.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 1.4 Continuum object

At macroscopic scales, a simple Newtonian liquid is represented by density \(\rho(x,t)\), velocity \(u(x,t)\), pressure \(p(x,t)\), and possibly temperature \(T(x,t)\).
Conservation of mass and momentum gives

\[ \partial_t \rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho u)=0, \]

\[ \partial_t(\rho u)+\nabla\cdot(\rho u\otimes u) = -\nabla p+\nabla\cdot \tau + f, \]

with Newtonian stress

\[ \tau = \eta(\nabla u+\nabla u^{T}) + \left(\zeta-\frac{2\eta}{d}\right)(\nabla\cdot u)I . \]

For an incompressible liquid,

\[ \nabla\cdot u=0, \]

and the Navier–Stokes equation becomes

\[ \rho(\partial_t u+u\cdot\nabla u) = -\nabla p+\eta \Delta u+f . \]

The incompressible Euler equation is the inviscid limit \(\eta=0\).
Stokes flow is the low-Reynolds-number limit in which inertia is neglected:

\[ -\nabla p+\eta\Delta u+f=0, \qquad \nabla\cdot u=0. \]

These equations are not “the definition of a liquid”; they are continuum closures of a microscopic and statistical object.
The Clay Mathematics Institute’s Navier–Stokes problem concerns existence and smoothness for the three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, underscoring that even the canonical continuum model is mathematically incomplete in 3D.
([claymath.org](https://www.claymath.org/millennium/navier-stokes-equation/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## 2. Foundational models of liquids

### 2.1 Interacting particle systems

The general model is a many-body system with positions \(q_i\), momenta \(p_i\), and interactions \(\phi\).
In equilibrium,

\[ Z_N(\beta,V) = \frac{1}{N!h^{dN}} \int e^{-\beta H_N(q,p)}\,dq\,dp . \]

Pressure, free energy, entropy, compressibility, and correlations follow from \(Z_N\) or from the grand partition function

\[ \Xi(z,\beta,V) = \sum_{N=0}^\infty z^N Z_N(\beta,V). \]

This framework captures dense packing, thermal fluctuations, phase coexistence, and molecular structure.
It fails when the true physics requires electronic quantum mechanics, chemical reactions, polarization, hydrodynamic long-range couplings, or strong nonequilibrium forcing unless these are explicitly added.

### 2.2 Lennard–Jones fluids

The Lennard–Jones pair potential is

\[ \phi_{\mathrm{LJ}}(r) = 4\varepsilon \left[ \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12} - \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^6 \right]. \]

It combines steep short-range repulsion with longer-range attraction and remains the canonical model of a simple atomic liquid.
It is mathematically useful because it is simple enough for simulation and integral-equation theory but rich enough to have vapor, liquid, solid, critical, and metastable regimes.
NIST maintains benchmark Lennard–Jones data from molecular dynamics and Monte Carlo for validation of codes and equations of state.
([nist.gov](https://www.nist.gov/mml/csd/chemical-informatics-group/lennard-jones-fluid-properties?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Its limitations are equally important: it is classical, pairwise, isotropic, chemically featureless, and not quantitatively reliable for many molecular liquids without modifications.

### 2.3 Hard-sphere fluids

The hard-sphere potential is

\[ \phi_{\mathrm{HS}}(r) = \begin{cases} +\infty, & r<\sigma,\\ 0, & r\ge \sigma . \end{cases} \]

It isolates excluded-volume entropy.
Hard spheres are central to packing, jamming, freezing, colloids, Enskog kinetic theory, and glassy caging.
They capture the geometry of dense repulsive liquids but miss attractions, cohesion, temperature-dependent bonding, and soft deformation.

### 2.4 Lattice fluids and lattice gases

A lattice gas has occupation variables \(n_i\in\{0,1\}\), with Hamiltonian

\[ H(n) = -J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} n_i n_j -\mu \sum_i n_i . \]

Under the transformation \(s_i=2n_i-1\), it is equivalent to an Ising model in a magnetic field.
The Lee–Yang analysis of the Ising/lattice-gas relation remains a foundational rigorous bridge between fluids and spin systems.
([link.aps.org](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRev.
87.410?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Lattice gases with velocity populations were later used as discrete kinetic models whose hydrodynamic limits approximate Navier–Stokes.
The Frisch–Hasslacher–Pomeau model showed that a properly symmetric lattice gas can simulate Navier–Stokes behavior, and later reviews clarified the hydrodynamics and limitations of lattice-gas methods.
([link.aps.org](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevLett.
56.1505?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Lattice models are rigorous and computationally tractable, but they suppress continuous molecular geometry.
They are excellent for universality and hydrodynamic-limit questions, less so for atomistic liquid structure.

### 2.5 Liquid-state integral equations and the Ornstein–Zernike equation

The Ornstein–Zernike equation decomposes the total correlation \(h(r)=g(r)-1\) into a direct correlation function \(c(r)\):

\[ h(r) = c(r) + \rho\int c(|r-r'|)h(r')\,dr' . \]

In Fourier space,

\[ \widehat h(k) = \frac{\widehat c(k)}{1-\rho\widehat c(k)}, \qquad S(k) = \frac{1}{1-\rho\widehat c(k)} . \]

Closures such as Percus–Yevick, hypernetted-chain, mean spherical approximation, and reference-hypernetted-chain approximate the unknown relation between \(h\), \(c\), and \(\phi\).
Recent 2024 work still treats Ornstein–Zernike theory as a powerful but closure-dependent framework for predicting structure factors, pressure, and compressibility of classical fluids.
([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18680?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

What it captures: equilibrium pair structure, compressibility, equations of state, approximate solvation structure.

What it fails to capture: exact many-body correlations, glassy relaxation, hydrodynamic fluctuations, irreversible flow, and nonequilibrium driving without additional theory.

### 2.6 Classical density functional theory

Classical density functional theory uses a density field \(\rho(x)\) and a Helmholtz free-energy functional

\[ F[\rho] = F_{\mathrm{id}}[\rho] + F_{\mathrm{ex}}[\rho], \]

where

\[ F_{\mathrm{id}}[\rho] = k_BT\int \rho(x)(\log(\Lambda^d\rho(x))-1)\,dx . \]

The equilibrium density minimizes the grand potential

\[ \Omega[\rho] = F[\rho] + \int (V_{\mathrm{ext}}(x)-\mu)\rho(x)\,dx . \]

Classical DFT is central for inhomogeneous liquids: interfaces, adsorption, solvation, freezing, and confinement.
Modern reviews emphasize that freezing can be treated as a transition from a homogeneous fluid density to a highly inhomogeneous periodic density; dynamic DFT extends the framework to nonequilibrium Brownian colloids under an adiabatic approximation.
([vegargje.folk.ntnu.no](https://vegargje.folk.ntnu.no/bibliografi/litteratur/Evans%20et%20al.%20-%202016%20-%20New%20developments%20in%20classical%20density%20functional%20t.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Recent work continues to extend classical DFT for solvation and to formulate “metadensity” or machine-assisted variants, but the exact excess functional \(F_{\mathrm{ex}}\) is unknown except in special cases.
([link.aps.org](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevLett.
134.107301?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 2.7 Molecular dynamics

Molecular dynamics integrates Newtonian or stochastic equations,

\[ m_i \ddot q_i = -\nabla_{q_i} U(q_1,\dots,q_N), \]

possibly with thermostats or barostats.
It produces trajectories from which \(g(r)\), \(S(k)\), diffusion, viscosity, relaxation times, and phase behavior are estimated.

MD captures microscopic time-dependent correlations and nonlinear nonequilibrium response.
Its limitations are finite size, finite time, force-field error, rare-event sampling, thermostat artifacts, and difficulty proving convergence to hydrodynamic behavior.
LAMMPS, GROMACS, OpenMM, and PLUMED are now standard infrastructure for atomistic and enhanced-sampling liquid simulations.
([lammps.org](https://www.lammps.org/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 2.8 Continuum fluid models

The Euler, Navier–Stokes, Stokes, and fluctuating hydrodynamic equations capture conservation laws and long-wavelength modes.
They intentionally discard molecular structure, replacing it by constitutive laws and transport coefficients.
Their strengths are universality and multiscale relevance; their weakness is that constitutive closure is not automatic.

For example, the Newtonian stress assumption is invalid for polymeric liquids, yield-stress fluids, dense suspensions, liquid crystals, active fluids, and many biological or soft materials.
Rheology studies therefore replace \(\tau=2\eta D(u)\) by memory kernels, internal variables, conformation tensors, Q-tensors, or yield criteria.
Recent soft-matter rheology reviews emphasize that stress relaxation is often non-Maxwellian and microstructure-dependent.
([pubs.rsc.org](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2023/sm/d3sm00736g?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## 3. Geometry and symmetry of liquids

### 3.1 Local geometry

The geometry of a liquid is local and statistical.
Important objects include:

\[ g(r),\quad S(k),\quad \rho^{(n)}(x_1,\dots,x_n), \]

Voronoi and Delaunay complexes, coordination numbers, bond-orientational order parameters \(Q_\ell\), local icosahedral order, and persistent-homology descriptors of cages and voids.

For a crystal, density has a Fourier expansion with Bragg peaks:

\[ \rho(x)\approx \rho_0+\sum_{G\in L^*} \rho_G e^{iG\cdot x}. \]

For a liquid, the equilibrium one-body density is constant in the absence of external fields,

\[ \langle \rho(x)\rangle=\rho_0, \]

while the two-body density is nontrivial.
Thus a liquid has broken local arrangements in instantaneous configurations, but the ensemble remains statistically homogeneous and isotropic.

### 3.2 Flow maps and diffeomorphism groups

Let \(M\) be the fluid domain.
A continuum fluid motion is a time-dependent map

\[ \eta_t:M\to M, \]

where \(\eta_t(a)\) is the position at time \(t\) of the material particle labeled by \(a\).
The Eulerian velocity is

\[ u(x,t)=\dot\eta_t\circ\eta_t^{-1}(x). \]

For incompressible flow,

\[ \eta_t\in \mathrm{Diff}_{\mathrm{vol}}(M), \]

the group of volume-preserving diffeomorphisms.

Arnold’s geometric interpretation identifies the incompressible Euler equation as geodesic motion on \(\mathrm{Diff}_{\mathrm{vol}}(M)\) with respect to the \(L^2\) kinetic-energy metric.
Ebin and Marsden put this on rigorous Sobolev-manifold footing for the Euler equation.
([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-31031-7_7?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This geometric viewpoint is foundational because it says the Euler equation is not merely a PDE; it is a geodesic equation on an infinite-dimensional Lie group.

### 3.3 Vorticity, helicity, and topology

For a three-dimensional velocity field,

\[ \omega=\nabla\times u \]

is vorticity.
The helicity is

\[ \mathcal H(u) = \int_M u\cdot \omega\,dx . \]

In ideal incompressible Euler flow, vortex lines are transported by the flow map and helicity is conserved under suitable boundary conditions.
Moffatt’s work made helicity a measure of average linking and knottedness of vortex lines; modern topological fluid mechanics studies when such quantities are true invariants and when viscosity or reconnection destroys them.
([cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-fluid-mechanics/article/some-topological-aspects-of-fluid-dynamics/E139B6426152FE1971F49427533A9301?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Experiments by Kleckner and Irvine generated knotted vortices in ordinary fluids, and later work tracked helicity transfer through reconnection events.
These studies are mathematically significant because they connect a topological invariant of ideal flow with measurable finite-viscosity vortex dynamics.
([faculty.ucmerced.edu](https://faculty.ucmerced.edu/dkleckner/papers/KnottedVortices.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 3.4 Symplectic and Poisson structures

Ideal fluid equations can be written in Hamiltonian form using noncanonical Poisson brackets.
In compressible or complex fluids, the phase space includes densities, momenta, entropy, and order parameters.
Morrison’s noncanonical Hamiltonian formulation and Holm’s Euler–Poincaré framework show how fluid equations arise by symmetry reduction from variational principles.
([web2.ph.utexas.edu](https://web2.ph.utexas.edu/~morrison/94IFSR640_morrison.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This matters for liquids because many modern liquid-like systems—liquid crystals, active fluids, magnetohydrodynamic fluids, superfluids, and complex fluids—are best understood as fluids with additional advected geometric structures.

## 4. Statistical mechanics and dynamics

### 4.1 Correlation hierarchy

The \(n\)-point density is

\[ \rho^{(n)}(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = \left\langle \sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_n}^{\neq} \prod_{\alpha=1}^n \delta(x_\alpha-q_{i_\alpha}) \right\rangle . \]

For a homogeneous fluid,

\[ \rho^{(2)}(x,y)=\rho^2 g(|x-y|). \]

The full hierarchy contains more information than \(g(r)\), but most tractable liquid theories close at the pair level or approximate higher correlations.

Dynamic correlations include the intermediate scattering function

\[ F(k,t) = \frac1N \left\langle \rho_k(t)\rho_{-k}(0) \right\rangle , \qquad \rho_k(t)=\sum_j e^{-ik\cdot q_j(t)}, \]

and the dynamic structure factor

\[ S(k,\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{i\omega t}F(k,t)\,dt . \]

These are the mathematical objects measured by neutron and X-ray scattering and used to identify relaxation, diffusion, sound modes, and glassy slowing.

### 4.2 Free energy, entropy, virial expansion

The Helmholtz free energy is

\[ F(N,V,T)=-k_BT\log Z_N . \]

Pressure can be expanded at low density as

\[ \frac{\beta p}{\rho} = 1+B_2(T)\rho+B_3(T)\rho^2+\cdots , \]

with

\[ B_2(T) = -\frac12\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left(e^{-\beta\phi(r)}-1\right)\,dr . \]

The virial expansion is rigorous in a domain of low density/high temperature for stable interactions, but its analytic continuation to the dense liquid, liquid-vapor transition, and freezing regimes is a much harder problem.
([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00220-012-1576-y?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 4.3 Phase transitions

Liquid-vapor coexistence is a first-order transition in density, terminating at a critical point.
Freezing is a transition from uniform density to periodic density.
Glass formation is not simply freezing: it is a dynamical arrest or metastability phenomenon with amorphous structure.
The modern glass literature treats dynamic heterogeneity, configurational entropy, mode-coupling theory, random first-order transition ideas, and computational access to deeply supercooled states as still-active research fronts.
([link.aps.org](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/RevModPhys.
83.587?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

A major mathematical warning: a liquid-glass “transition” is not as rigorously established as a crystal transition or Ising-type phase transition in finite-dimensional equilibrium statistical mechanics.
The evidence is partly dynamical, computational, and phenomenological.

### 4.4 Hydrodynamic limits

A hydrodynamic limit is a law of large numbers for empirical fields.
For stochastic interacting particle systems, one proves

\[ \rho_N(x,t)\to \rho(x,t) \]

where \(\rho\) solves a PDE such as diffusion, Burgers, Euler, or Navier–Stokes-type equations.
Kipnis and Landim’s framework is foundational for stochastic lattice systems; Yau’s relative entropy method and Varadhan’s non-gradient method are core techniques.
([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-662-03752-2?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

For deterministic hard-sphere gases, Lanford’s theorem derived the Boltzmann equation for short times in the Boltzmann–Grad limit.
A major recent advance by Deng, Hani, and Ma extends hard-sphere-to-Boltzmann derivation to arbitrarily long times as long as the Boltzmann solution remains regular, but this is a rarefied-gas kinetic limit, not a derivation of dense liquid hydrodynamics.
([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.07818?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Boltzmann-to-fluid limits, including incompressible Navier–Stokes limits, have rigorous theories under assumptions on entropy bounds, scaling, and solution regularity; Saint-Raymond, Golse, Bardos, Levermore, and collaborators are central in this program.
([cmls.polytechnique.fr](https://www.cmls.polytechnique.fr/perso/golse/Surveys/FGEcm04.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 4.5 Nonequilibrium statistical mechanics

For driven liquids, shear flows, active matter, and heat-conducting states, equilibrium Gibbs measures are insufficient.
Macroscopic fluctuation theory describes large deviations of density and current fields in driven diffusive systems.
Its action functional typically has the form

\[ \mathcal I[\rho,j] = \frac12 \int (j-J(\rho))^\top \chi(\rho)^{-1} (j-J(\rho))\,dx\,dt, \]

subject to

\[ \partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot j=0 . \]

This is powerful for stochastic diffusive systems, but dense momentum-conserving liquids, active suspensions, and molecular fluids remain less rigorously controlled.
([link.aps.org](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/RevModPhys.
87.593?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The Dean–Kawasaki equation attempts to describe the empirical density of interacting Brownian particles:

\[ \partial_t\rho = \nabla\cdot \left( D\rho\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho} \right) + \nabla\cdot(\sqrt{2D\rho}\,\xi). \]

It is exact at a formal level for Brownian particles but is a singular stochastic PDE.
Recent mathematical work emphasizes that its multiplicative \(\sqrt{\rho}\) noise and distribution-valued density make it a difficult object, not merely a convenient simulation equation.
([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.13467?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## 5. Topology and modern mathematical structure

### 5.1 Vortex topology

Topology is deep in fluid mechanics when it constrains the evolution through invariants or homotopy classes.
In ideal fluids, vortex lines are advected and helicity measures average linkage.
In viscous fluids, reconnection breaks ideal topology, but helicity may be redistributed across scales.

This is not metaphorical topology: helicity is an integral invariant, vortex knots are embedded curves or tubes, and reconnection is a change in isotopy class.
Modern results even characterize helicity as essentially the only regular integral invariant of volume-preserving transformations under appropriate assumptions.
([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4776527/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 5.2 Liquid crystals

A nematic liquid crystal has fluid mobility but orientational order.
Its order parameter may be a director

\[ n(x)\in S^2/\{\pm 1\}=\mathbb{RP}^2, \]

or a Landau–de Gennes Q-tensor,

\[ Q(x)\in \{Q\in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}: Q=Q^T,\ \mathrm{tr}\,Q=0\}. \]

The topology of defects follows from the order-parameter manifold.
For nematics,

\[ \pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2)\cong \mathbb{Z}_2, \]

so line defects have a topological classification.
Landau–de Gennes theory regularizes defect cores by allowing \(Q\) to vary through biaxial or isotropic states.
Mathematical work has connected Q-tensor models, defect asymptotics, and hydrodynamic Ericksen–Leslie/Beris–Edwards systems, with rigorous results mostly under dimensional, regularity, or parameter restrictions.
([aimsciences.org](https://www.aimsciences.org/article/doi/10.3934/cpaa.
2012.11.1303?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 5.3 Active fluids and active nematics

Active matter consists of constituents that convert energy into motion or stress at the microscale.
Continuum active-nematic models typically add an active stress

\[ \sigma^{\mathrm{active}}=-\zeta Q, \]

where \(\zeta>0\) or \(\zeta<0\) distinguishes extensile and contractile activity.
Active nematics exhibit spontaneous flow, defect proliferation, and active turbulence.
Topological active matter uses real defect topology, boundary modes, and non-Hermitian or nonequilibrium response structures; it is not merely calling swirls “topological.” ([ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2010.00364?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Recent mathematical and physical work studies thermodynamic constraints on Toner–Tu hydrodynamics, dense active fluids, active defect control, active fluids with higher conservation laws, and hydrodynamic breakdown/restoration.
These are active 2024–2026 frontiers rather than closed theories.
([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.02283v1?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 5.4 Quantum fluids and superfluids

In a superfluid, vorticity is concentrated on quantized vortex lines with circulation

\[ \oint u\cdot dl=\kappa=\frac{h}{m} \]

or the appropriate circulation quantum.
Quantum turbulence is a tangle of quantized vortices in superfluid helium or Bose–Einstein condensates.
It shares cascade phenomenology with classical turbulence but has discrete vortex cores, reconnections, Kelvin waves, and a different dissipation mechanism.
([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3970860/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Recent work on two-dimensional and three-dimensional quantum turbulence studies vortex statistics, stochastic geometry, and topological pathways to turbulence.
([link.aps.org](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevResearch.
7.
L022063?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 5.5 Topological data analysis of liquid structure

Persistent homology, Euler characteristic curves, and related descriptors convert a molecular configuration into a filtered simplicial complex and track connected components, loops, and voids across scales.
This is useful for cages, pores, local packing, ionic conduction pathways, and glassy structure.
Recent work uses Euler characteristic and persistent homology for molecular dynamics and materials simulations.
([pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.jctc.
2c00766?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The distinction is important: TDA is mathematically meaningful as a stable summary of point-cloud geometry, but it is not automatically a theory of dynamics.
It becomes liquid theory only when connected to correlation functions, transport, free energy, or metastable states.

## 6. Rigorous mathematical results and proof programs

### 6.1 Navier–Stokes and Euler

For incompressible Navier–Stokes in three dimensions,

\[ \partial_t u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p=\nu\Delta u, \qquad \nabla\cdot u=0, \]

global weak Leray–Hopf solutions exist, but global smoothness and uniqueness remain open.
This is the Clay Millennium problem.
([claymath.org](https://www.claymath.org/millennium/navier-stokes-equation/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

For Euler,

\[ \partial_t u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p=0, \qquad \nabla\cdot u=0, \]

there is a sharp modern connection between weak regularity and energy conservation.
Onsager predicted that weak Euler solutions with Hölder exponent greater than \(1/3\) conserve energy, while below \(1/3\) anomalous dissipation is possible.
The conservation side is now rigorously understood in Besov-type classes, and convex-integration constructions have produced dissipative weak solutions below the Onsager threshold.
([epubs.siam.org](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/18M1178864?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 6.2 Hydrodynamic limits

Rigorous hydrodynamic limits are strongest for stochastic systems.
The standard structure is:

1. prove local equilibrium;
2. identify the macroscopic flux;
3. use entropy, martingale, replacement, or non-gradient methods;
4. show convergence to a PDE.

Kipnis–Landim, Yau, Varadhan, Olla, and related schools form the core mathematical lineage.
Recent developments include fractional hydrodynamic limits, active lattice gases, and path-integral formulations for nonequilibrium fluctuations.
([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-662-03752-2?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

For deterministic Newtonian dense liquids, the comparable result is far beyond current techniques.
The Boltzmann–Grad limit gives kinetic theory for rarefied gases, not dense liquids.
Dense liquids require recollision control, strong correlations, absence of molecular chaos, and phase coexistence.

### 6.3 Rigorous equilibrium statistical mechanics of fluids

Some pieces are rigorous:

- cluster and virial expansions for stable potentials at low density/high temperature;
- thermodynamic limits under stability and temperedness assumptions;
- certain lattice-gas phase transitions;
- special continuum models with long but finite interaction range.

Lebowitz, Mazel, and Presutti proved a liquid-vapor transition for continuum particles with finite but long interaction range.
However, a rigorous liquid-vapor transition for realistic Lennard–Jones-type or hard-core plus short-range attractive potentials remains an outstanding problem, as Presutti and later discussions emphasize.
([link.aps.org](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevLett.
80.4701?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Hard-sphere freezing in three dimensions is also not proved in a form matching the physical transition.
This is one of the clearest gaps between simulation/experiment and mathematical theorem.

### 6.4 Liquid crystal PDEs

For liquid crystals, rigorous analysis exists for Oseen–Frank, Ericksen–Leslie, Beris–Edwards, and Landau–de Gennes systems.
Results include existence, regularity, singular limits, and derivations between Q-tensor and director theories under suitable assumptions.
Global strong solutions are known in some two-dimensional settings, while three-dimensional active or fully coupled systems remain much harder.
([epubs.siam.org](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/13093529X?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 6.5 Turbulence

Kolmogorov 1941 scaling is not a theorem of Navier–Stokes.
Rigorous turbulence theory has partial results: energy inequalities, bounds on attractors in some models, Onsager-type Euler results, stochastic forcing results, shell models, and mathematically controlled examples of anomalous dissipation.
Direct numerical simulations now explore possible singular or near-singular structures and anomalous-dissipation precursors, but the mathematical bridge from 3D Navier–Stokes to physical turbulence remains incomplete.

### 7.1 Molecular dynamics and Monte Carlo

### 7.2 Machine-learned interatomic potentials

Machine-learned interatomic potentials replace fixed analytic potentials by learned energy functions

\[ E_\theta(q_1,\ldots,q_N) \]

subject to permutation, translation, rotation, and sometimes equivariance constraints.
Forces are

\[ F_i=-\nabla_{q_i}E_\theta . \]

DeepMD introduced a deep neural-network many-body potential respecting physical symmetries and trainable from ab initio data; DeePMD-kit has become a major open-source implementation.
([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.09571?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Equivariant graph neural network potentials such as NequIP and MACE use \(E(3)\)-equivariant representations to improve data efficiency and accuracy.
([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9068614/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The mathematical frontier is not just accuracy; it is:

\[ \text{symmetry}+\text{stability}+\text{uncertainty}+\text{long-time dynamics}+\text{thermodynamic consistency}. \]

Open MLIP questions include transferability, extrapolation, active learning, long-range electrostatics, rare-event sampling, and whether “foundation” interatomic potentials can be trusted outside their training distribution.
Recent 2025–2026 reviews explicitly frame these as open problems rather than solved engineering details.
([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07327v1?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 7.3 Lattice Boltzmann and mesoscopic methods

Lattice Boltzmann methods discretize kinetic distribution functions \(f_i(x,t)\) over lattice velocities \(c_i\):

\[ f_i(x+c_i\Delta t,t+\Delta t) - f_i(x,t) = -\frac{1}{\tau} \left(f_i-f_i^{\mathrm{eq}}\right) +\text{forcing}. \]

They recover Navier–Stokes in a hydrodynamic limit and are effective for complex boundaries, multiphase flows, porous media, and soft matter.
Modern reviews present LBM as an alternative way to solve Navier–Stokes-like dynamics while retaining kinetic structure.
([journals.sagepub.com](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/09544062221077583?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The mathematical issue is asymptotic consistency: which discrete collision rules converge to which continuum PDE, with which viscosity, boundary condition, fluctuation structure, and stability regime?

### 7.4 CFD: finite volume, finite element, spectral, DNS, LES

Continuum simulations discretize Navier–Stokes, Euler, Stokes, or complex-fluid PDEs.
Finite volume methods emphasize conservation; finite element methods emphasize weak formulations and geometry; spectral methods emphasize high-order accuracy for smooth flows.

Direct numerical simulation resolves all dynamically relevant scales but is limited by Reynolds number.
Large-eddy simulation resolves large scales and models subgrid stress.
The mathematical problem is closure: subgrid models should be stable, consistent, and predictive outside calibration data.
Reviews of LES emphasize that subgrid modeling is unavoidable when turbulence is not fully resolved.
([oden.utexas.edu](https://www.oden.utexas.edu/media/reports/2002/0222.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 7.5 Differentiable simulation, neural operators, and reduced-order models

Differentiable molecular and fluid simulation computes gradients through trajectories or PDE solvers.
JAX-MD made molecular simulation differentiable and hardware-accelerated; JAX-Fluids extends differentiability to high-order compressible and multiphase CFD.
([ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.04232?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Neural operators learn maps between function spaces,

\[ \mathcal G: \text{initial/boundary data} \mapsto \text{solution fields}, \]

rather than maps between finite-dimensional vectors.
They can accelerate parametric PDE simulation, inverse design, and surrogate modeling, but their reliability for turbulent, multiscale, out-of-distribution fluid regimes remains an open research issue.
([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.15325?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Physics-informed neural networks, transformer neural operators, and learned LES closures are active, but current reviews emphasize missing benchmarks, generalization tests, and closure guarantees.
([pubs.aip.org](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/pof/article/36/10/101301/3315125/A-comprehensive-review-of-advances-in-physics?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 7.6 Where computation is producing mathematical insight

Computation is generating new mathematical questions in at least five ways:

1. **Testing closure theories:** comparing Ornstein–Zernike closures, DFT functionals, mode-coupling approximations, and hydrodynamic constitutive laws against controlled simulations.
2. **Discovering hidden order:** using bond-order fields, TDA, graph embeddings, and machine learning to identify local structures correlated with dynamics.
3. **Probing rare regimes:** deeply supercooled liquids, cavitation, nucleation, active turbulence, quantum vortex tangles.
4. **Generating conjectures:** about scaling exponents, universality classes, anomalous transport, long-time tails, and glassy length scales.
5. **Learning operators:** approximating solution maps while exposing which invariances and conservation laws are necessary for stable prediction.

## 8. Experimental and physical interface

### 8.1 Scattering

X-ray and neutron scattering measure the static structure factor \(S(k)\).
The radial distribution function is inferred by Fourier inversion subject to finite-window and model constraints.
Modern work still treats this inverse problem as statistically nontrivial, especially for liquids and glasses where isotropic disorder removes crystallographic simplifications.
([wiki.mlz-garching.de](https://wiki.mlz-garching.de/_media/n-lecture05%3Asalmon.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The mathematical link is:

\[ S(k) = 1+\rho\int e^{-ik\cdot r}(g(r)-1)\,dr . \]

Thus scattering constrains pair correlations, which constrain integral-equation closures, DFT functionals, and force fields.

### 8.2 Rheology

Rheology measures stress response to imposed strain or strain rate.
For a Newtonian liquid,

\[ \sigma_{xy}=\eta \dot\gamma . \]

For viscoelastic liquids, one studies the complex modulus

\[ G^*(\omega)=G'(\omega)+iG''(\omega), \]

memory kernels, relaxation spectra, and nonlinear yielding.
Soft-matter rheology now treats gels, yielding liquids, suspensions, and biological fluids as microstructure-dependent continuum systems rather than simple Newtonian liquids.
([pubs.rsc.org](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2023/sm/d3sm00736g?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 8.3 Microrheology and particle tracking

Microrheology infers local mechanical properties from probe-particle motion.
Passive microrheology uses thermal fluctuations and fluctuation-dissipation assumptions; active microrheology drives probes and measures response.
Squires and Mason’s review remains canonical for how Brownian motion of probes connects to viscoelastic moduli.
([eng.uc.edu](https://www.eng.uc.edu/~beaucag/Classes/Properties/Polymer%20Physics%20Quizzes%202022/HW%2010%20Polymer%20Physics%202022/FluidMechanicsofMicrorheology2010annurev-fluid-121108-145608.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Particle tracking, PIV, and flow visualization estimate velocity fields, vorticity, strain, coherent structures, and transport statistics, providing empirical constraints for stochastic and continuum models.

### 8.4 Colloids, soft matter, active fluids

Colloids are mathematically valuable because particles are large enough to image and track, but still undergo Brownian dynamics.
They realize hard-sphere-like fluids, glass transitions, crystallization, gels, and active suspensions in experimentally tunable regimes.

Active systems—bacterial suspensions, cytoskeletal extracts, epithelial layers, active nematics—force mathematics beyond detailed balance.
Light-controlled cyanobacterial active nematics and machine-learning-based inference of active-nematic hydrodynamics are examples where experiment, PDE modeling, topology of defects, and data-driven parameter inference meet.
([nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43246-024-00474-8?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 8.5 Quantum liquids and electron fluids

Superfluid helium and ultracold Bose gases realize quantum hydrodynamics with quantized vortices.
Electron hydrodynamics occurs in ultraclean conductors when momentum-conserving electron-electron collisions dominate momentum relaxation.
Reviews and experiments report viscous electronic transport in graphene and other clean materials, with viscosity interpreted as momentum diffusion.
([research-portal.uu.nl](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/hydrodynamic-electronic-transport/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Strange metals are more speculative: hydrodynamic or Planckian descriptions are active, but not a universally settled microscopic theory.
Reviews emphasize their violation of ordinary Fermi-liquid expectations and their characteristic \(T\)-linear resistivity.
([par.nsf.gov](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10410450?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## 10. Open mathematical problems

### Problem 1: 3D Navier–Stokes regularity

**Statement.** Prove or disprove global smoothness and uniqueness for 3D incompressible Navier–Stokes with smooth finite-energy initial data.

**Why hard.** The energy estimate controls

\[ \|u(t)\|_{L^2}^2+2\nu\int_0^t\|\nabla u(s)\|_{L^2}^2ds, \]

but the nonlinear vortex-stretching term is supercritical relative to known bounds.

**Likely advance.** A new scale-critical a priori estimate, a geometric depletion mechanism for vortex stretching, or a rigorous counterexample construction.

### Problem 2: Turbulence and anomalous dissipation

**Statement.** Explain, from Navier–Stokes, how

\[ \lim_{\nu\to0}\nu \int_0^T\|\nabla u^\nu\|_{L^2}^2dt \]

can remain positive.

**Why hard.** Dissipation persists only if gradients blow up as viscosity vanishes.
This connects weak limits, singularities, intermittency, and loss of compactness.

**Likely advance.** A bridge between Onsager-critical weak Euler theory and vanishing-viscosity Navier–Stokes limits.

### Problem 3: Derive dense liquid hydrodynamics from microscopic dynamics

**Statement.** Starting from Hamiltonian particles at liquid density, derive Navier–Stokes or fluctuating hydrodynamics with explicit transport coefficients.

**Why hard.** Dense liquids violate molecular chaos; recollisions, caging, phase transitions, and long-time tails matter.
Existing Boltzmann derivations address rarefied gases, not dense liquids.

**Likely advance.** A theory combining local equilibrium, quantitative mixing, Green–Kubo convergence, and controlled coarse-graining for deterministic many-body systems.

### Problem 4: Rigorous liquid-vapor transition for realistic continuum potentials

**Statement.** Prove liquid-vapor coexistence for Lennard–Jones-like or hard-core plus short-range attractive potentials in physical dimensions.

**Why hard.** Continuum translation symmetry, dense packing, interfaces, droplets, and competing solid phases obstruct lattice-style Peierls arguments.

**Likely advance.** A continuum Pirogov–Sinai or renormalization method that handles droplets and excludes crystallization in the relevant parameter regime.

### Problem 5: Hard-sphere freezing

**Statement.** Prove the existence of a first-order fluid-solid transition in the three-dimensional hard-sphere model.

**Why hard.** Entropy alone drives ordering; no energy difference separates phases.
One must compare enormous configuration-space volumes of disordered and crystalline packings.

**Likely advance.** New geometric probability estimates for dense sphere packings and crystalline defect ensembles.

### Problem 6: Finiteness and positivity of transport coefficients

**Statement.** Prove that \(D,\eta,\kappa\) exist and are positive finite for realistic liquid models.

**Why hard.** Green–Kubo formulas require integrable time correlations.
Hydrodynamic long-time tails can produce slow decay, especially in low dimensions.

**Likely advance.** Quantitative mixing estimates for interacting particle dynamics or a rigorous mode-coupling/fluctuating-hydrodynamic theory.

### Problem 7: Liquid-glass transition

**Statement.** Identify whether glassiness is a true thermodynamic transition, a dynamical transition, a crossover, or model-dependent mixture.

**Why hard.** Relaxation times grow beyond accessible scales while static structural changes are subtle.
Candidate order parameters—configurational entropy, overlap, point-to-set length, dynamic heterogeneity—do not yet form a universally rigorous theory.

**Likely advance.** A theorem connecting metastable state counts, dynamical large deviations, and finite-dimensional relaxation barriers.

### Problem 8: Nonequilibrium steady states of dense liquids

**Statement.** Construct a general mathematical theory of driven steady states with currents, shear, entropy production, and fluctuations.

**Why hard.** There is no Gibbs potential for generic nonequilibrium states.
Momentum conservation, boundaries, thermostats, and multiplicative noise complicate large deviations.

**Likely advance.** Extension of macroscopic fluctuation theory from diffusive lattice gases to dense off-lattice momentum-conserving systems.

### Problem 9: Active fluid theory

**Statement.** Derive active hydrodynamic PDEs from microscopic active particles and prove well-posedness, stability, and defect dynamics.

**Why hard.** Active systems break detailed balance, often have nonreciprocal interactions, multiplicative noise, and self-generated flows.
Defects are singular and dynamically created.

**Likely advance.** A kinetic-to-hydrodynamic derivation with controlled closure and a PDE theory for active stresses coupled to Q-tensor defects.

### Problem 10: Mathematical reliability of multiscale ML simulation

**Statement.** Give error bounds for ML potentials and neural fluid solvers over thermodynamic and long-time dynamical observables.

**Why hard.** Small force errors can bias invariant measures, rare events, transport coefficients, and phase boundaries.
Training loss is not the same as statistical-mechanical accuracy.

**Likely advance.** Structure-preserving learning with invariant-measure error estimates, uncertainty quantification, and active-learning guarantees.

## 11. Cross-regime comparison

| Regime | Order | Correlations / \(S(k)\) | Dynamics | Continuum or kinetic limit | Mathematical change |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Crystal | Long-range periodic positional order | Bragg peaks at reciprocal lattice vectors | Particles vibrate around sites; defects move | Elasticity, phonons, dislocations | Broken translation symmetry; density has periodic order parameter |
| Quasicrystal | Long-range aperiodic order | Pure-point diffraction with noncrystallographic symmetry | Solid-like | Elasticity plus phasons | Long-range order without periodic lattice |
| Liquid | Short-range mobile order | Diffuse peaks; \(g(r)\to1\) away from criticality | Ergodic flow; stress relaxes | Euler/Navier–Stokes/fluctuating hydrodynamics | No fixed global positional order; finite viscosity and diffusion |
| Glass | Amorphous local order | Similar static \(g(r)\) to liquid | Dynamically arrested; aging; broken ergodicity on observation time | Elastoplasticity, aging, activated dynamics | Dynamics changes more sharply than pair structure |
| Gas | Weak correlations at low density | \(g(r)\approx1\), except collision core | Ballistic between collisions | Boltzmann equation; Euler/NS after kinetic limit | Molecular chaos approximately valid |
| Plasma | Charged collective medium | Screening; dielectric response; plasma modes | Long-range collective oscillations | Vlasov–Poisson, Vlasov–Maxwell, MHD | Long-range Coulomb fields and quasineutrality replace short-range neutral correlations |

Plasmas differ from neutral liquids because long-range Coulomb interaction creates collective fields, Debye screening, and kinetic equations such as Vlasov–Poisson or Vlasov–Maxwell.
The Debye length and collective behavior are defining scales absent from ordinary neutral liquids.

## Research-design takeaway

For a mathematically sophisticated researcher, the most fertile frontier is the interface between four structures:

\[ \boxed{ \text{short-range statistical order} } \quad + \quad \boxed{ \text{long-wavelength conservation laws} } \quad + \quad \boxed{ \text{topological/geometric constraints} } \quad + \quad \boxed{ \text{computable but uncontrolled closures} }. \]

The central unresolved task is to turn the physically successful ladder

\[ \text{particles} \to \text{correlations} \to \text{free energy / transport} \to \text{hydrodynamics} \]

into theorems with quantified error, stability, and universality.
Liquids sit precisely where this ladder is hardest: too dense for molecular chaos, too mobile for static order, too fluctuating for naive continuum closure, and too important for purely qualitative theory.
