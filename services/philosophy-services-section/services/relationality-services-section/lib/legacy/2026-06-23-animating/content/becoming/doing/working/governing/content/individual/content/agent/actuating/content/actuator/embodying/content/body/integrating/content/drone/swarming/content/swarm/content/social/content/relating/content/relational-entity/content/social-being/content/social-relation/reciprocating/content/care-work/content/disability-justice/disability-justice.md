# disability-justice

Disability justice is a political framework, developed by disabled activists of color, that grounds disability in intersecting systems of oppression rather than treating it as an individual medical condition.
It was articulated by the performance collective Sins Invalid and by organizers including Patty Berne, Mia Mingus, and Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinha.
Generated by: care-work — disability justice is care work reorganized as a collective right rather than an extracted service, the demand that the tending bodies need be built into shared life instead of pumped out of the few or denied to the many.

The framework is best understood against the disability rights movement that preceded it.
Disability rights focused on legal inclusion and accommodation within existing institutions — the right to a ramp, an interpreter, a reasonable adjustment, an end to formal discrimination.
Disability justice argues that this does not go far enough, because the institutions themselves are organized around ableism, and entangled with racism, capitalism, and settler colonialism.
Access cannot be secured simply by adding ramps to structures built on exclusion; the structures, and the social order that produced them, are the problem.
Where disability rights sought a place within the existing world, disability justice questions whether that world can be made just without being remade.

At the framework's center is a reconception of access.
Access is not a special provision for exceptional bodies but a condition that must be built into infrastructure, policies, and norms from the start.
The accommodation model places the burden on the disabled individual to request an exception, to disclose, to justify the need, to be the problem that the institution then graciously solves.
Disability justice reverses the direction of the question.
It shifts from "what do you need?" — asked of the individual, who must account for themselves — to "what have we failed to build?" — asked of the collective, which bears the responsibility.
Access becomes a baseline that communities owe their members rather than a favor extended to those who can prove they deserve it.

Underlying all of this is the principle of interdependence: the recognition that no one is self-sufficient, that every life depends on the care and labor of others, and that the fantasy of the independent individual who needs nothing is exactly that — a fantasy, and a harmful one, since it casts those whose dependence is visible as deficient while concealing the dependence of everyone else.
Piepzna-Samarasinha's *Care Work: Dreaming Disability Justice* (2018) documents how disability justice communities build care infrastructure through mutual aid, through what Mingus calls "access intimacy" — the felt ease of being with someone who understands your access needs without elaborate explanation — and through collective labor, rather than through institutional channels.
Alongside Alison Kafer's work on disability futures, this is the insistence that the future be reimagined through access and interdependence rather than organized, as it usually is, around the exclusion and abandonment of those deemed unproductive.

Within the reciprocal relations, disability justice is where care work becomes an explicit politics.
It takes the reciprocal possibility latent in care — care as mutual obligation rather than extracted service — and makes it a demand on how common life is built.
It draws directly on communitas, the condition of shared obligation, refusing the immunitarian move by which each person protects only themselves and the vulnerable are left to absorb the risk; and it relies on mutual aid as the practice through which access is actually produced when institutions will not provide it.
Its deepest claim is that interdependence is the condition of all life, not the special burden of some, and that a community is measured by whether it builds for that truth or abandons those who make it visible.
