# the-commons

The commons are resources governed collectively by the communities that use them.
They are neither private property, owned by an individual or corporation, nor state property, owned by a government, but commonly held — managed through shared agreements, reciprocal obligation, seasonal rotation, and the direct participation of the people who depend on them.
Pastureland, fisheries, forests, waterways, seed stocks, knowledge, and care networks have all been organized as commons across human history.
Generated by: reciprocating — the commons is the collective, rule-bound stewardship of a shared resource, mutual aid formalized into a standing arrangement for governing what a community holds in common.

The destruction of the commons is, historically, the origin of capitalism.
In England between the fifteenth and nineteenth centuries, common lands — where villagers had grazed animals, gathered fuel, foraged food, and organized collective life for centuries — were systematically enclosed: fenced off, privatized, and converted into the property of individual landowners.
The people who had depended on the commons were expelled, and with no land to sustain themselves they were driven into the cities and into wage labor, selling their hours to factory owners because the alternative, after enclosure, was starvation.
This conversion of commonly held resources into private property was not incidental to capitalism but constitutive of it: capitalism requires a class of people who own nothing but their capacity to work and must sell it to survive, and enclosure manufactured that class.
People who can feed themselves from common land do not need wages.
The same process ran across the globe through colonialism, where Indigenous lands held through collective stewardship were carved into parcels, assigned to settlers, and defended by colonial states — dispossessing Indigenous peoples not only of land but of the governance systems that had managed it.

The commons is not only a historical institution but an ongoing practice, which some theorists call "commoning."
Commoning is the active, collective governance of a shared resource through direct participation: deciding together how much to harvest, when to rest the land, how to distribute what is produced, and how to respond when someone takes more than their share.
This governance does not require a state.
For millennia, communities have managed commons through customary law, social pressure, shared labor, and rotating responsibilities.
Elinor Ostrom documented this empirically in *Governing the Commons* (1990), studying irrigation systems, fisheries, forests, and pastures around the world and identifying the design principles — clear boundaries, rules matched to local conditions, participation by users in making the rules, monitoring, graduated sanctions, accessible conflict resolution — through which communities sustainably manage shared resources without either privatization or state regulation.
Contemporary commoning includes community gardens, tool libraries, seed exchanges, open-source software, community land trusts, and cooperative housing: not nostalgic throwbacks but active constructions of non-capitalist relations in the present.

Against this stands the most influential argument for privatization, Garrett Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" (1968), which holds that shared resources are inevitably overexploited because each individual takes as much as possible while the costs are spread across all.
Ostrom's work showed the argument to be empirically wrong: what Hardin described was not a commons but an open-access resource with no governance at all — no agreements, no community, no mutual obligation.
Real commons are precisely the opposite, dense with rules, monitoring, and collective enforcement that prevent the overuse Hardin treated as inevitable.
The "tragedy" narrative works ideologically rather than descriptively.
By presenting the destruction of the commons as natural and unavoidable, it justifies the enclosure that destroyed them, in a circular argument: we must privatize because commons fail, but the evidence of failure is drawn from situations where the commons had already been dismantled or never existed.

The commons is the reciprocal counterpart to private property, and the two name opposed ways of relating to a resource.
Where property converts a shared thing into an exclusive holding that one party controls and others are barred from, the commons keeps the thing held together and binds the holders into mutual obligation for its care.
The commons is thus the resource-governance form of the whole reciprocal family: mutual aid is the everyday cooperation, the commons is that cooperation crystallized around a thing the community must steward together, and enclosure — the conversion of the commons into private property — is the operation by which domination turns one back into the other.
