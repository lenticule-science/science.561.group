## 1. A Recognizable Thing That Keeps Changing

Imagine a simple joke format: “Nobody: … Absolutely nobody: … Me: [doing something absurd].” The words after “Me” can change endlessly. The joke can become political, personal, cruel, affectionate, or surreal. It can appear as text, an image, a TikTok caption, or a classroom joke. Yet people still recognize it as “the same kind of thing.”

That is the puzzle behind the older concept of the meme. Long before “meme” meant a funny internet image, scholars were asking how recognizable cultural patterns persist while changing. A melody survives through different voices. A proverb moves from grandparents to group chats. A religious ritual is repeated for centuries but never performed exactly the same way twice. A political slogan spreads because it is short, repeatable, and useful in conflict.

The question is not simply “How do things go viral?” It is: what makes a cultural pattern remain itself while being repeated, altered, misunderstood, translated, mocked, and reused?

**Key idea:** The meme names an old problem: how cultural forms recur with variation.

## 2. Before Dawkins: Imitation, Tradition, Ritual, and Social Reproduction

Before Richard Dawkins coined “meme,” social theorists already had several languages for cultural recurrence. Gabriel Tarde treated imitation as one of the basic processes of social life. In *The Laws of Imitation*, he argued that social order is made from countless repetitions: people copy accents, fashions, techniques, beliefs, and desires from one another (Tarde 1903). A new style or idea begins somewhere, then spreads through imitation.

Émile Durkheim shifted attention from copying between individuals to the force of collective life. “Social facts” such as law, religion, money, and moral rules exist outside any one person and pressure people to act in certain ways (Durkheim 1895). His “collective representations” are shared symbols and categories through which a group understands itself, as in religious rituals that make a community feel real to itself (Durkheim 1912).

Anthropologists and folklorists studied tradition, diffusion, myth, custom, and ritual. They noticed that stories and practices travel, but also that institutions stabilize them: schools teach patriotic songs; churches preserve liturgies; families repeat recipes; states print slogans.

**Key idea:** Pre-Dawkins theories treated recurrence as imitation, shared social force, tradition, and institutional reproduction.

## 3. Dawkins’s 1976 Move: The Meme as Cultural Replicator

In *The Selfish Gene*, Dawkins proposed “meme” as a name for a cultural replicator: a unit of cultural transmission analogous to the gene (Dawkins 1976). His examples were deliberately ordinary: tunes, ideas, catchphrases, clothing fashions, and ways of making pots or arches. The point was not that culture literally has DNA. The point was that Darwinian thinking might apply wherever there is copying, variation, and differential survival.

This clarified something important. A melody can spread because it is catchy, not because it benefits the singer. A slogan can survive because it is memorable, not because it is true. A superstition can persist because it is easy to teach and emotionally sticky. Dawkins gave readers a compact way to ask: what properties help a cultural item get copied?

But the analogy also oversimplified. Genes have a physical copying mechanism and relatively clear inheritance lines. Cultural items do not. A joke may be half-remembered, translated, combined with another joke, or used sarcastically. What exactly is the meme: the wording, the idea, the performance, the social use, or the family of variants?

**Key idea:** Dawkins made cultural recurrence look Darwinian, but left the identity of the cultural unit unresolved.

## 4. Strong Memetics: Dennett, Blackmore, and Quasi-Autonomous Memes

Daniel Dennett extended the meme idea into a broad Darwinian account of culture. In *Darwin’s Dangerous Idea*, he argued that Darwinian processes can explain design-like complexity beyond biology, including cultural patterns (Dennett 1995). Memes, in this view, are not just things people choose; they are informational patterns that compete for space in human minds and institutions.

Susan Blackmore pushed this further in *The Meme Machine*. For her, imitation created a second evolutionary process alongside genetic evolution. Humans became especially powerful “meme machines”: creatures whose brains, languages, and technologies allow memes to copy themselves through us (Blackmore 1999).

This was compelling because it explained why bad, false, or harmful ideas can flourish. A chain letter, conspiracy theory, or addictive slogan may survive because it is good at getting repeated, not because it is good for people. The criticism was equally strong. Many scholars argued that memetics made culture too automatic, treated people as passive hosts, and ignored meaning, power, interpretation, and material media. It often assumed clean replication where culture usually involves reconstruction.

**Key idea:** Strong memetics made memes into active cultural replicators, but often weakened human interpretation and context.

## 5. Sperber: Culture Is Reconstructed, Not Merely Copied

Dan Sperber offered a major correction in *Explaining Culture*. He called his approach an “epidemiology of representations” (Sperber 1996). The metaphor is medical but not crude: just as diseases spread through populations by many bodily and environmental pathways, representations spread through minds, talk, objects, institutions, and situations.

Sperber’s key point is that cultural transmission is often not exact copying. People reconstruct what they receive. A child learning a folktale does not photocopy it mentally; they remember striking scenes, infer missing motives, simplify hard parts, and retell it in familiar forms. A proverb survives because many people can regenerate something close enough to it.

This leads to “attractors”: stable forms toward which many reconstructions tend. A rumor may drift toward a more emotionally satisfying version. A myth may settle into memorable oppositions: hero and monster, purity and danger, betrayal and revenge. Stability comes not from perfect replication but from repeated transformation in predictable directions.

**Key idea:** Sperber shifted the focus from copying fidelity to reconstructive stability and cultural attractors.

## 6. Population-Level Cultural Evolution: Bias, Drift, and Selection

Boyd and Richerson developed a population-level theory of cultural evolution in *Culture and the Evolutionary Process* (Boyd and Richerson 1985). Instead of asking whether one meme is selfish, they modeled how cultural variants change in frequency across groups. This allowed culture to be studied with tools similar to population biology, while still recognizing social learning.

Several biases matter. Prestige bias means people copy admired or high-status models; a cooking technique spreads faster when used by a famous chef. Conformity bias means people copy what many others are doing; a slang term can become normal because everyone seems to use it. Content bias means some forms are easier to remember or prefer; rhyming proverbs beat clumsy ones. Drift means some variants spread by chance. Selection means some variants persist because they fit environments, institutions, or practical needs.

Joseph Henrich used these ideas to explain cultural learning, prestige, and cumulative culture: the way human groups build complex skills no single person could invent alone (Henrich 2015). Alex Mesoudi later presented cultural evolution as an interdisciplinary synthesis across psychology, anthropology, archaeology, economics, and history (Mesoudi 2011).

**Key idea:** Population cultural evolution explains recurrence by tracking how variants change across groups under learning biases, drift, and selection.

## 7. The Semiotic, Pragmatic, and Media-Studies Correction

A sign does not simply spread like a seed. It is interpreted. A political slogan shouted at a rally, printed on a T-shirt, quoted in a news headline, and mocked in a meme may have the same words but different meanings. The phrase is not only copied; it is framed.

This is where semiotic and pragmatic approaches matter. Semiotics studies signs: how words, images, gestures, and sounds stand for something. Pragmatics asks how meaning depends on use. A wink, for example, is not just an eye movement. It can flirt, mock, conspire, soften a joke, or signal irony depending on the situation.

Media studies adds that channels shape circulation. A proverb in oral tradition depends on memory and occasion. A newspaper slogan depends on print institutions. An image macro depends on templates, captions, platforms, and searchable archives. A TikTok sound invites bodily repetition, lip-sync, editing, and algorithmic circulation. In these cases, transmission includes citation, parody, remix, and stance-taking.

**Key idea:** Cultural forms do not merely replicate; people use them to mean, frame, mock, align, and perform.

## 8. Internet Meme Studies: Shifman and Families of Variants

Limor Shifman gave internet meme studies one of its most useful definitions. In *Memes in Digital Culture*, she argued that internet memes are not single viral objects. They are groups of digital items that share content, form, and stance, were created with awareness of one another, and circulated through imitation and transformation (Shifman 2014).

This definition fits internet culture better than the older “one meme, one object” model. “Distracted Boyfriend” is not just one stock photograph. It is a family of captioned versions using the same visual relation: one figure desires something new while neglecting something already possessed. An image macro is not only an image plus text; it is a repeatable grammar. A TikTok sound is not only audio; it becomes a format for gestures, cuts, facial expressions, and jokes.

Shifman’s “stance” is especially important. Memes often share an attitude: mockery, cringe, admiration, complaint, deadpan exaggeration. The same template can carry different content, but it remains recognizable because users know how to inhabit its pose.

**Key idea:** Internet memes are best understood as families of related items, not isolated viral units.

## 9. Computational Meme Tracking: Phrases, Bursts, and Limits

Computational researchers tried to measure cultural recurrence at web scale. Leskovec, Backstrom, and Kleinberg’s “Meme-tracking and the Dynamics of the News Cycle” studied short phrases that moved through blogs and news sites (Leskovec, Backstrom, and Kleinberg 2009). Their system clustered variants of quoted phrases so researchers could track how public language rose, mutated, and faded.

This was powerful because it made cultural diffusion visible. A political quote might appear first in a speech, then in news headlines, then in blogs, then in altered versions. The researchers showed that online attention is bursty: phrases spike rapidly, compete with one another, and disappear as the news cycle moves on.

But lexical tracking has limits. It works best when the meme is a relatively stable phrase. It struggles with image templates, sounds, gestures, irony, screenshots, translation, and cases where meaning survives after the wording changes. “Same meme” is easy to count when the words are similar; it is harder when a joke becomes a pose, a rhythm, or a visual relation.

**Key idea:** Computational tracking reveals large-scale diffusion, but lexical similarity is only one kind of meme identity.

## 10. The Genealogy and the Unresolved Identity Question

The concept of the meme did not appear from nowhere. Tarde saw social life as imitation and repetition. Durkheim showed that collective symbols and institutions exceed individual minds. Dawkins named a cultural replicator and sharpened the analogy with genes. Dennett and Blackmore made memetics into a stronger Darwinian picture of culture. Sperber objected that culture is usually reconstructed, stabilized by attractors rather than copied perfectly. Boyd, Richerson, Henrich, and Mesoudi modeled population-level change through learning biases, drift, and selection. Semiotic, pragmatic, and media-studies approaches restored interpretation, framing, media form, and use. Shifman then redefined internet memes as families of related items. Computational work showed how some variants can be tracked at scale.

The unresolved question remains simple and difficult: when do variants count as the same meme? Is sameness in wording, image structure, melody, use, emotional stance, social function, descent history, or audience recognition? A proverb can change words and stay itself. A slogan can keep words and change sides. A TikTok sound can lose its original context and gain a new one.

**Key idea:** The genealogy of the meme leads to an identity problem: cultural recurrence is recognizable, but not reducible to exact copying.

# Annotated Bibliography

Tarde, Gabriel. 1903. *The Laws of Imitation*.
Tarde matters because he made imitation central to social theory before “memes” existed. His work frames society as patterns of repetition, invention, and spread.

Durkheim, Émile. 1895. *The Rules of Sociological Method*; 1912. *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*.
Durkheim matters because he showed that cultural patterns are not merely individual habits. Social facts and collective representations explain how symbols, rituals, and institutions become durable forces.

Dawkins, Richard. 1976. *The Selfish Gene*.
Dawkins coined “meme” as a unit of cultural transmission analogous to the gene. His move clarified cultural selection but also introduced the unresolved problem of what exactly counts as a cultural unit.

Dennett, Daniel C. 1995. *Darwin’s Dangerous Idea*.
Dennett matters because he generalized Darwinian explanation beyond biology. His account made memes part of a wider theory of design, mind, and culture.

Blackmore, Susan. 1999. *The Meme Machine*.
Blackmore gave one of the strongest versions of memetics. She argued that imitation creates a second evolutionary system and that humans are especially effective vehicles for memes.

Sperber, Dan. 1996. *Explaining Culture: A Naturalistic Approach*.
Sperber matters because he challenged simple copying models. His epidemiology of representations explains cultural stability through reconstruction and attractors.

Boyd, Robert, and Peter J. Richerson. 1985. *Culture and the Evolutionary Process*.
Boyd and Richerson made cultural evolution mathematically and population-theoretically serious. They modeled how social learning, bias, selection, and drift change cultural variants over time.

Henrich, Joseph. 2015. *The Secret of Our Success*.
Henrich matters for explaining prestige-biased learning, cumulative culture, and the collective nature of human intelligence. His work shows why culture is not just information spreading, but skill and knowledge accumulating across groups.

Mesoudi, Alex. 2011. *Cultural Evolution: How Darwinian Theory Can Explain Human Culture and Synthesize the Social Sciences*.
Mesoudi presents cultural evolution as a broad interdisciplinary framework. His work is useful for connecting models of transmission with evidence from psychology, anthropology, archaeology, and economics.

Shifman, Limor. 2014. *Memes in Digital Culture*.
Shifman matters because she adapted the meme concept to internet culture. Her definition of internet memes as groups of items sharing content, form, and stance remains central to digital meme studies.

Leskovec, Jure, Lars Backstrom, and Jon Kleinberg. 2009. “Meme-tracking and the Dynamics of the News Cycle.”
This paper matters because it showed how phrase variants could be clustered and tracked across large online news and blog systems. It also reveals the limits of tracking memes only by words.
