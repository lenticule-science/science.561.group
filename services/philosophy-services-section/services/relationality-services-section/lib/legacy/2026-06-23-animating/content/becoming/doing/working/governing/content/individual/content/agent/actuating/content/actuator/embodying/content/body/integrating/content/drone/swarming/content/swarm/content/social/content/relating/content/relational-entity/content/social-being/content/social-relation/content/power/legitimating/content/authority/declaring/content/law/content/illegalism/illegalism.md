# illegalism

Illegalism is the anarchist practice of refusing to recognize the authority of law and acting accordingly — stealing, squatting, counterfeiting, smuggling, forging documents, and otherwise violating the legal order not as aberrant behavior but as a principled response to a system of domination.
Generated by: illegalism is law read from the standpoint of the one it criminalizes — the refusal of the legal order by those who deny its right to grant or withhold permission in the first place.
Where direct action acts without asking permission, illegalism acts *against* the very framework that claims the authority to grant it.

The argument rests on the anarchist analysis of law and legitimacy.
If law is not a neutral protector of rights but an instrument that enforces property relations, maintains class divisions, and criminalizes resistance to hierarchy, then obeying the law is not a moral obligation but a form of obedience to a coercive system.
Breaking it is not inherently wrong; it may be the only coherent response to a legal order that is itself, in the protection it gives to exploitation and dispossession, the deeper offender.
Individual *expropriation* — theft from the wealthy — is the most characteristic illegalist practice and draws on Proudhon's maxim that "property is theft": if existing property is itself a kind of robbery backed by the state, then taking from property owners is not theft but recovery, redistribution carried out now and individually rather than postponed to a collective revolution that may never come.

Illegalism crystallized as a distinct tendency in late-nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century France and Italy.
Marius Jacob, whose exploits helped inspire the fictional gentleman-burglar Arsène Lupin, practiced systematic burglary targeting the wealthy and channelled the proceeds to anarchist causes and people in need.
The Bonnot Gang (1911–1912) fused illegalism with propaganda of the deed, using the new technology of the automobile to carry out bank robberies staged as demonstrations of anarchist practice.
These activities provoked fierce debate within the movement.
Critics charged that individual illegalism merely reproduces the acquisitive logic of the system it claims to oppose, substitutes private adventurism for collective solidarity, and draws down state repression onto the broader movement; defenders answered that to counsel patience while people suffer deprivation enforced by property law is itself a form of complicity.

That debate persists in contemporary illegalist practice, which is generally less spectacular and more quotidian: shoplifting, fare evasion, squatting, dumpster diving, software piracy, and other everyday violations of property law.
These are rarely organized as a program; they are practiced as ways of meeting needs outside the wage system and of refusing, in daily life, to grant property relations any legitimacy.
The relationship between illegalism and mutual aid is especially significant: when people share stolen food, occupy unused buildings, or copy and freely distribute proprietary software, they are at once violating property law and practicing horizontal cooperation, so that the same act can be read as theft or as the building of an alternative.

Within this cluster illegalism is law approached from below, from the position of those it defines as criminal — the refusal that completes the picture of the legal order by showing what it looks like to those it binds against their will.
It is the legal counterpart of the refusal of work, and its central unresolved question is whether it constitutes genuine resistance to a system of domination or merely individual survival within a system that, undisturbed, goes on.
