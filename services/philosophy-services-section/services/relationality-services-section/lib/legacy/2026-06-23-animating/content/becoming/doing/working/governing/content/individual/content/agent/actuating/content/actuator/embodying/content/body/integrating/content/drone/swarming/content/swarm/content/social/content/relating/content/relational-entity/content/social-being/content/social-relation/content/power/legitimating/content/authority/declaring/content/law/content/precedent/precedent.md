# precedent

Precedent is the principle that a prior decision binds later ones — that a court resolving a case today should follow the rule announced by courts that decided like cases before.
Generated by: precedent is law's self-accumulation across time, the mechanism by which a single ruling, once made, propagates forward and shapes the cases that come after it.
The Latin maxim is *stare decisis et non quieta movere* — "to stand by what has been decided and not disturb settled matters" — and it is the backbone of common-law reasoning, the reason a lawyer can predict how a court will rule by studying what courts have ruled, and the reason law develops case by case rather than by legislative fiat alone.

The doctrine operates along two dimensions. *Vertical* stare decisis binds lower courts to the decisions of the higher courts above them, so that a trial court must follow the appellate court, and all courts must follow the supreme court, within a jurisdiction. *Horizontal* stare decisis binds a court, presumptively, to its own prior decisions, lending stability and consistency to its own line of cases over time.
Together these give the law its characteristic shape: a hierarchy of binding authority in which a decision's force depends on where in the structure it was made.
The common law's nuanced handling of precedent was systematized by Blackstone in his *Commentaries* (1765) and given its most ambitious modern jurisprudential reading by Ronald Dworkin in *Law's Empire* (1986).

Dworkin's account makes precedent the heart of what he called law as *integrity*.
Law, he argued, is not merely a heap of rules but a structure of principle, and every new decision must "fit" with the decisions that came before, so that the law can be read as the coherent expression of a consistent set of principles.
A judge deciding a hard case is, on this view, like a novelist writing the next chapter of a collective work begun by others: bound to honor what has been written, free to develop it, but never free to ignore it.
Hans Kelsen, from a very different angle, located an analogous structure at the foundation of every legal system in the *Grundnorm* — the presupposed basic norm from which all legal validity ultimately derives, the first premise that makes every later decision a *legal* decision at all.

Precedent is not immovable, and the ways it can be set aside are as important as the ways it binds.
A court may *distinguish* a precedent, holding that the present case differs in some material respect so that the prior rule does not reach it — a move that leaves the precedent standing but narrows its domain.
Or a court with the requisite authority may *overrule* it outright, declaring that the prior decision was wrong and shall no longer be followed.
Overruling does not erase history — the old case still happened, and decisions made in reliance on it are not retroactively undone — but it withdraws the rule's forward force, so that it no longer governs cases to come.
The tension between the stability that stare decisis protects and the correction that overruling permits is one of the permanent dramas of a legal system, visible in every contested reversal of a long-settled rule.

Within this cluster precedent is the temporal dimension of adjudication — the way a court's decision reaches beyond the parties before it to bind the future.
It is the principal vehicle of legal formatting's propagation, since a ruling that a given practice counts as evidence of good faith spreads through later cases and professional guidance, and it is the quiet engine of ambient governance, shaping conduct everywhere through the long shadow that settled decisions cast over every negotiation conducted in their light.
