# genocide

Genocide is the deliberate destruction of a people as such — not merely mass killing but the systematic effort to eliminate a group's capacity to exist as a group.
Raphael Lemkin coined the term in 1944, in *Axis Rule in Occupied Europe*, joining the Greek *genos* ("race" or "people") to the Latin *-cide* ("killing"), to name what the existing legal vocabulary could not: the targeting not of individuals but of a collectivity, its institutions, its culture, and its future.
The 1948 UN Convention defines genocide as acts committed "with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group" — including killing, causing serious bodily or mental harm, inflicting conditions calculated to bring about physical destruction, preventing births, and forcibly transferring children.
Generated by: settler-colonialism, in its eliminating mode — genocide is the carrying-through of the settler logic of elimination, though it occurs in non-settler contexts as well.

The analytical power of the concept lies in what it names beyond the body count.
Genocide is defined not by the number killed but by the intent to destroy the group as a group, which is why its methods extend well past direct killing.
The Indian boarding-school system in the United States and the residential-school system in Canada — captured in the slogan "kill the Indian, save the man" — were genocidal not principally because many children died in them (though many did) but because they were designed to destroy Indigenous nations as cultural and political entities by severing children from language, kin, land, and ceremony.
Forced transfer of children, suppression of language, and destruction of governance can all be instruments of genocide, the cultural and the physical operating as a single structure rather than as separate offenses.

Patrick Wolfe's analysis brings the concept into this library's branch directly: under settler-colonialism, genocide is not an event but a structure.
The elimination of Indigenous peoples continues for as long as the settler state exists on Indigenous land, pursued through legal erasure (blood-quantum rules that define a people out of existence on paper), epistemic erasure (the suppression of knowledge systems), linguistic erasure (the destruction of languages), and spatial erasure (the ongoing seizure of land).
Physical killing is one mechanism among many; the structure as a whole is the elimination of the native, and it operates at every scale, in slow administrative forms as readily as in open massacre.
This is why the settler-colonial reading refuses to treat genocide as a finished historical episode.

Within this library genocide sits downstream of settler-colonialism as the completion of its eliminatory logic, while remaining a concept that also describes destruction outside the settler frame — the Holocaust that prompted Lemkin's coinage, the campaigns against the Armenians, the Tutsi, the Yazidis.
What unifies these is the intent to end a people's existence as a people, by whatever combination of means.
Reading genocide alongside settler-colonialism, coloniality, and violence keeps the concept from being narrowed to spectacular mass killing alone, and keeps in view the slower, legal, and cultural machineries through which the destruction of a group can be pursued without a single dramatic event to mark it.
