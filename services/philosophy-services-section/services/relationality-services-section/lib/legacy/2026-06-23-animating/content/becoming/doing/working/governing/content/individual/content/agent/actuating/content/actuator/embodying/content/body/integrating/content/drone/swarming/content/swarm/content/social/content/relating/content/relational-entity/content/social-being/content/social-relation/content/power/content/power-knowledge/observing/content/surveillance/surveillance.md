# surveillance

Generated by: power-knowledge, observing — power's need to know its subjects, the apparatus that gathers the knowledge power runs on; panopticism generalized to the population.

Surveillance is the systematic monitoring of populations, communications, and movements by state agencies, corporations, or other institutions.
It is the practical face of power's need to know its subjects: the apparatus through which the knowledge that power runs on is gathered, stored, and acted upon.
A basic and politically consequential distinction runs through the field.
Targeted surveillance monitors specific individuals suspected of specific activities and, at least in principle, requires justification before it begins.
Mass surveillance collects data on entire populations as a matter of routine, and in doing so treats everyone as a potential subject of suspicion.
The shift from the first to the second inverts the usual burden: instead of monitoring following from cause, monitoring becomes the standing condition against which cause is later sought.

The history of political surveillance in the United States illustrates the trajectory.
It runs from the Palmer Raids of 1919–1920, through the FBI's decades of domestic monitoring and counterintelligence, through the National Security Agency's interception of international telegrams under Project SHAMROCK (exposed by the Church Committee in 1975), to the post-2001 expansion of mass surveillance under the PATRIOT Act and Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act.
Across this lineage the legal frameworks and the technologies evolve together, each new capability normalizing the next and widening the population treated as a legitimate object of observation.

Digital surveillance has transformed the field by making mass data collection cheap, automated, and pervasive.
Social media monitoring, cell-site simulators, facial recognition, and metadata analysis allow agencies to map social networks, track movements, and predict behavior without the labor-intensive infiltration that earlier operations such as COINTELPRO required.
What once demanded informants and physical tails can now be accomplished at scale by software acting on data that people generate simply by living digitally connected lives.
The reach of observation expands while its cost and visibility shrink.

Surveillance is best understood as panopticism generalized from the institution to the population.
Where the Panopticon induced self-policing through the architectural possibility of being seen, mass surveillance induces a similar effect across an entire society: the knowledge that one may be watched at any time.
Its effect on political movements is anticipatory.
The knowledge that surveillance is occurring shapes what people say, where they meet, and whether they organize at all — a chilling effect that does not require prosecution to be effective, because the watched begin to police themselves.
In this sense surveillance is the observing arm of power/knowledge: it gathers the knowledge through which subjects are classified, populations are governed, and conduct is conducted from a distance.
Under capital it specializes further into surveillance capitalism, where the same monitoring apparatus is turned to the extraction and sale of behavioral data, and the population is watched not only to be governed but to be sold.
