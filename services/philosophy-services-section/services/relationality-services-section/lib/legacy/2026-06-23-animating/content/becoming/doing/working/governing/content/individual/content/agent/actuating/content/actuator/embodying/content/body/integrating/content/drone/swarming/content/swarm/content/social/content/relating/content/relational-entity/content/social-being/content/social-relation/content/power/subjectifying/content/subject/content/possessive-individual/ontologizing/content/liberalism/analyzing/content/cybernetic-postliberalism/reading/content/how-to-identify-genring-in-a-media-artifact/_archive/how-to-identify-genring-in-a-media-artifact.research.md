This is a working procedure for identifying [genring](../terms/genring.md) in a specific media artifact — a film, a novel, a news segment, a social-media post, a museum exhibit, an advertisement.
Genring is the reduction of a living people to a fixed literary type.
The procedure walks through how to recognize the operation in concrete cases.

This how-to assumes familiarity with the term [genring](../terms/genring.md), the related operation [genre coherence](../terms/genre-coherence.md), and the broader analytical framework specified in [Genring and Ontogenring](./genring-and-ontogenring.md).

## What you'll need

- The artifact itself — accessible for repeated viewing, reading, or examination.
- A way to take notes (paper, document, recording).
- About 30-90 minutes for a single artifact, depending on its complexity.
- Background knowledge of the people the artifact depicts: what is their actual ongoing life like, what variety exists within the community, what their tradition is in its living form.
  If you do not have this background, the procedure cannot be completed honestly — you can only identify what is *missing* from the artifact's depiction by knowing what the actual depicted people are like.
  Read or consult sources from the people themselves before continuing.

## Step 1: Identify the depicted people

Watch, read, or examine the artifact and identify the people it depicts.
Be specific.
Not "Indigenous people" but "the Lakota," not "Asian people" but "the Hmong," not "rural Americans" but "Pennsylvania Dutch farmers" or "Appalachian coal-mining communities."

Write down the specific people the artifact depicts.
If the artifact depicts more than one people, treat each separately.
If the artifact deliberately blurs distinctions between peoples (e.g., "Native Americans" as a single category covering hundreds of distinct nations), this is itself a finding — note it.

## Step 2: Inventory what is selected

Make a list of the features the artifact uses to depict the people.
Be concrete.
What costumes appear?
What food is shown or referenced?
What landscapes?
What rituals or ceremonies?
What occupational roles?
What emotional registers?
What time period is depicted?

Pay attention to what is visually or emotionally striking.
The features that the artifact uses to *make the people legible* are the selection step's output.
Note them as features: "buckskin and beadwork," "the alpine cabin with painted shutters," "the elder with weathered hands," "the festival dance," "the silent stoic father."

## Step 3: Inventory what is excluded

Now compare your list against the actual ongoing life of the depicted people.
What aspects of their actual life are absent?

This is where Step 1's background knowledge matters.
The Lakota mathematician, the Bavarian Marxist, the Kurdish anarchist filmmaker, the Pennsylvania Dutch software engineer — these subjects exist, in living variety.
The artifact's selection has to *not* depict them in order to maintain the genre figure.
What is the artifact not showing?

Common exclusions to look for:

- Contemporary urban-cosmopolitan members of the community.
- Members whose work is in fields the artifact codes as outside the genre (math, finance, technology, contemporary art, abstract intellectual labor).
- Members whose political-cultural positions deviate from the genre's prescribed register.
- The community's actual internal disagreements, debates, factions.
- The community's living institutions: their schools, their universities, their newspapers, their digital infrastructure.
- The community's transformations: how their tradition has changed across recent generations.
- The community's intermarriages, migrations, multilingual diaspora.

Write down what is excluded.
The exclusion is as load-bearing as the selection.

## Step 4: Identify the codification

The selected features have been assembled into a stable character template.
Identify the template.
What is the genre figure the artifact is producing or reproducing?

Common Western-discursive genre figures: the Noble Indian, the Mountain Kurd, the Hearty Bavarian, the salt-of-the-earth American farmer, the dignified African elder, the wise Asian sage, the irrepressible Italian, the stoic Russian.
Each has its costume, its setting, its emotional register, its narrative arc.
Each is recognizable as a *type* the discourse has stabilized.

Sometimes the artifact reproduces an existing genre figure; sometimes it contributes to producing a newer one.
Either way, name the figure.
If the figure is composite (combining elements of multiple existing genres), name the composite.

## Step 5: Identify the substitution work

The genre figure now functions in the discourse as a stand-in for the people.
Note how the artifact does this.
Does it claim to represent the people accurately?
Does it disclaim representation while still producing the figure?
Does it use the figure to make claims about the people the artifact never directly attributes to them?

Watch for these substitution moves:

- The figure as the people: "this is what the [people] are like."
- The figure as the people's authentic core: "the real [people], beneath the surface."
- The figure as the people's nostalgic past: "the [people] as they used to be, before."
- The figure as the people's destiny: "the [people] as they should/will be."
- The figure as a moral lesson the people teach: "what the [people] can teach us."

Each move performs the substitution.
The artifact's reader, viewer, or visitor encounters the genre figure and registers it as the people, often without noticing that they have not encountered the people themselves.

## Step 6: Identify the structural function

The artifact serves a function in the broader apparatus.
Identify it.

Common functions:

- *Authenticity-anchoring for a national-cultural narrative.* The artifact secures the depicting culture's claim to access authentic folk life through its depiction of the people.
  The reader, viewer, or visitor (often from the depicting culture, not the depicted people) is reassured that authenticity exists somewhere, available to be referenced.
- *Justification for a political-economic relation.* The artifact frames the depicted people in a way that legitimates the depicting culture's relation to them: the empire's mission, the settler-state's territorial claim, the multicultural-liberal project's inclusion-without-transformation.
- *Sentimental-affective consumption.* The artifact provides the depicting culture's audience with affective material (admiration, grief, awe, fascination, romantic projection) drawn from the depicted people without the audience having to engage with the people on their own terms.
- *Erasure of the present.* The artifact stabilizes the people in a past or pre-modern frame, making it harder for the audience to encounter the people's present.

Some artifacts perform multiple functions simultaneously.
Note all that apply.

## Step 7: Identify the genre-coherence pressure

[Genre coherence](../terms/genre-coherence.md) is the structural pressure that maintains the genre figure against the centrifugal forces of actual living variety.
Even if the artifact itself is not coercive, it participates in a broader pressure that demands the depicted people remain in the genre figure's position.

Note how the artifact contributes to this pressure.
What happens, in the artifact's framing, when a member of the depicted people deviates from the genre figure?
Are deviating members invisible?
Are they framed as not really members?
Are they framed as a problem?
As a comic exception?
As a tragic loss?
As a confirmation of the genre figure (the deviating member rediscovers their roots)?

The pressure is what connects genring to fascist violence (developed in [genre coherence](../terms/genre-coherence.md) and [fascist grammar](../terms/fascist-grammar.md)).
An artifact need not be fascist to participate in the pressure.
The participation is what to identify.

## Step 8: Write the analysis

Compile your findings into a short analytical statement.
The structure:

1. **The artifact:** what it is, where and when it appears, who its audience is.
2. **The depicted people:** specifically named.
3. **The selection:** what features the artifact uses.
4. **The exclusion:** what features the artifact omits, and what those exclusions accomplish.
5. **The codification:** the genre figure produced or reproduced.
6. **The substitution:** how the artifact substitutes the figure for the people.
7. **The structural function:** what role the artifact plays in the broader apparatus.
8. **The genre-coherence pressure:** what the artifact does to deviating members.

The analytical statement should be specific, evidenced from the artifact, and grounded in actual knowledge of the depicted people.
Avoid generalities about representation; the genring analysis is most useful when it is concrete enough to identify what the artifact actually does.

## Common pitfalls

**Mistaking accurate-feature-depiction for non-genring.** An artifact can use accurate features (the costumes are correct, the food is real, the rituals exist) and still perform genring through what it excludes and how it codifies.
Accurate features do not exempt an artifact from analysis.

**Mistaking admiration for non-genring.** Many artifacts that perform genring are admiring rather than derogatory.
The Noble Indian, the Wise African Elder, the Spiritual Asian Sage are admiring genre figures; they are not less genring for being admiring.

**Mistaking the artist's identity for the artifact's function.** A member of the depicted people can produce an artifact that performs genring, often because the apparatus's market rewards the genring product.
The artist's identity is one input to the analysis, not its conclusion.

**Mistaking critique within the artifact for non-genring.** An artifact can include critique of the genring it performs (the meta-knowing wink, the layered self-aware framing) and still distribute the genre figure.
The critique often functions as the artifact's claim to be doing something else; the genring proceeds underneath.

**Generalizing from one artifact to all media.** This procedure analyzes a specific artifact.
The analysis tells you about that artifact and what it does within the broader apparatus.
It does not tell you that all media depicting the people performs genring; conclusions of that scope require analyzing many artifacts.

## Where to take the analysis next

The genring analysis often opens into adjacent analyses:

- *The temporal-middle elimination.* Once the genring is identified, ask what [temporal middle](../terms/temporal-middle.md) the genre figure forecloses — what living position of the depicted people the figure cannot accommodate.
  See [Settlerism and the Cybernetic Subject](./settlerism-and-the-cybernetic-subject.md) for the structural register.
- *The fascist-grammar deployment.* If the artifact participates in a political-cultural movement that uses the genre figure as part of a [fascist grammar](../terms/fascist-grammar.md) sequence (declaring crisis, naming a pure community, denouncing enemies, etc.), trace the deployment.
- *The settler-colonial register.* If the depicted people are an indigenous people on settler-colonized territory, the analysis sits within the settler-colonial register specifically — see [Settlerism and the Cybernetic Subject](./settlerism-and-the-cybernetic-subject.md).
- *The platform-amplification.* If the artifact circulates on platforms, the [platformal feedback architecture](./platformal-feedback-architecture.md) shapes which features get amplified and which dampened.

The genring analysis is one analytical move; it sits within the broader [methods](./methods-of-cybernetic-postliberal-analysis.md) that organize cybernetic-postliberal analysis as a practice.
