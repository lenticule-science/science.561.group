# domination

Domination is the structural condition in which one party's capacity for self-determination is systematically constrained by another's exercise of power.
It is not a single act of force but a sustained arrangement — a relationship maintained through hierarchy, authority, coercion, dependence, or the organization of material and social life so that refusal is punished and compliance is compelled.
Generated by: **hierarchy** — domination is power passed through a fixed, vertical arrangement of positions, the form power takes once it stops circulating and hardens into rank.

Sociology's classic treatment is Max Weber's.
Weber distinguished power (*Macht*), the ability to impose one's will against resistance, from domination (*Herrschaft*), the probability that a command will be obeyed by a given group.
Domination, for Weber, is power that has become *expected* — institutionalized into a standing relation of command and obedience.
He famously identified three pure types by the kind of claim they make to legitimacy: traditional (sanctified by the way things have always been), charismatic (resting on devotion to an exceptional leader), and rational-legal (grounded in impersonal rules and offices).
Each is a different answer to the same question — why do the dominated obey? — and Weber's insight is that durable domination almost always seeks legitimacy, dressing the bare fact of command in a story about why it is right.

The critical tradition presses past the question of legitimacy to the question of structure.
Here domination names not only the obeyed command but the whole organization of life that makes refusal unaffordable.
The anarchist formulation states the broadest version: domination is the problem as such — not this or that particular kind, but the structural relationship itself, wherever it appears.
The point of that breadth is an analytical habit of reading across domains.
The domination of the state over its subjects, the employer over the worker, the colonizer over the colonized, the man over the woman, the adult over the child — these are read not as separate problems requiring separate theories but as instances of one structural relationship.
Where liberalism opposes some forms of domination (tyranny, censorship) while accepting others as legitimate (the employer's authority, the property owner's right to exclude), this tradition refuses the exceptions.

Concretely, domination operates through interlocking mechanisms rather than through naked force, which is precisely what makes it durable.
The worker who "freely" sells their labor is dominated not by a gun but by the prior fact that the means of subsistence are owned by others, so that the alternative to obedience is destitution.
The colonized subject is governed through law, schooling, and the destruction of prior forms of self-rule, not only through the soldier's rifle.
This is why domination is best diagnosed as a relation rather than an event: any particular employer or official may be kind or cruel, but the structural position that lets them command and others comply is built into the arrangement and survives the replacement of any individual occupant.

Domination is therefore the trunk from which the labor-and-capital cluster grows.
Exploitation is domination operating economically — the extraction of value from the worker's labor, sustained by the owner-worker hierarchy.
Capital, the wage relation, surplus-value, and the disciplinary apparatus of the state are all specifications of how a dominant position is held and what is taken through it.
Its reciprocal counterpart is solidarity: where domination divides in order to rule and isolates the subordinated so they face the structure alone, solidarity is the practice of refusing to be divided, the binding of the dominated into a force that can contest the relation that holds them.
