A ***think-aloud protocol*** is a research method in which participants verbalize their thoughts while performing a task — reading a document, writing a text, or solving a problem — so researchers can observe cognitive processes that are normally invisible.

[Linda Flower](../../../../../../references/people/linda-flower.md) and [John Hayes](../../../../../../references/people/john-hayes.md) used think-aloud protocols to develop the cognitive process model of writing, revealing that composing is recursive rather than linear — writers plan, draft, and revise in interleaving cycles, not in the prewrite-write-revise sequence that writing pedagogy had assumed [@flowerhayes1981]. Their protocol data showed what actually happens when people write, not what they report afterward.

[Karen Schriver](../../../../../../references/people/karen-schriver.md) extended the method to document testing, developing [protocol-aided audience analysis](./protocol-aided-audience-analysis.md). By watching readers think aloud while using a document, writers can identify exactly where comprehension breaks down — something that self-report surveys miss, because readers often don't know what they misunderstood [@schriver1997].

Think-aloud protocols are central to PTGAE for two reasons:

1. **As evidence for writing principles.** PTGAE's rules about [revision](../../essay-writing/terms/revision.md), [writer-based prose](../../rhetoric-and-composition/terms/writer-based-prose.md), and [cognitive load](../terms/cognitive-load.md) rest on protocol research, not intuition.
2. **As a testing method.** PTGAE's paraphrase test and task test are simplified versions of protocol-aided testing.
