# Speech Acts

## What this node is

Speech acts are the **posit** morphism read at the [language](../../language.md) base: the point where an utterance stops merely meaning and starts *doing*. To assert, to command, to promise, to declare, to express — Austin's "how to do things with words" — is to give *force* to propositional content, and force is value entering predication, which is exactly what positing is.

This placement is derived from the research library's own theory, not imported from elsewhere. The corpus's account of a performed act factors it as a Hoare triple `{P} B {Q}` (precondition, body, postcondition) whose body, at a single predication cell, is a *positing* morphism — and the positing morphism is one of four illocutionary force-classes, the four corners of Belnap's four-valued bilattice over that cell, plus a non-acting identity centre. So the five-way structure of saying-as-doing is the bilattice the corpus already uses for evidence, now read as *force*: a speech act is a posit that moves a common ground, and even abstention is an act because it emits a record of the agent's performance.

## What it generates / contains

- [language-and-power](content/language-and-power/) — how illocutionary force is distributed, withheld, and contested: speech as the exercise of authority, the perlocutionary movement of a shared common ground, and the politics of who may perform which acts felicitously.

The locution → illocution → perlocution structure (form and sense; force; effected uptake) and the felicity conditions that license an act are the reference surface this node will carry as it matures; the primary sources the corpus already holds are Austin 1962 and Searle 1969.

## Orientation

Up is [grammar](../grammar/grammar.md) (the predicate morphism: the well-formed content force is applied to) and [language](../../language.md) (the base). The positing morphism in mathematics generates the [value logics](../../../predication/content/positing/content/mathematical-logic/mathematical-logic.md); here the same morphism, at the language base, generates illocutionary force — the two are one morphism read at two bases, which is why a speech act and a four-valued judgement share the bilattice.
