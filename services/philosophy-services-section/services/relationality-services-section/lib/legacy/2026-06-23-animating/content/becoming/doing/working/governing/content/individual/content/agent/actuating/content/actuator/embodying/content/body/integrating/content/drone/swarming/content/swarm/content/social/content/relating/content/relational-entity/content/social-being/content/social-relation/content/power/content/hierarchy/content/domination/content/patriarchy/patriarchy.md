# patriarchy

Patriarchy is the structure of gendered domination: the arrangement of social life so that masculine authority is concentrated in institutions — the family, religion, law, the economy, the state — and feminized people and roles are subordinated to it, with the arrangement held in place by coercion, custom, economic dependence, and the threat of violence.
The word is older than its critical use; it once simply named "rule by the father."
Feminist theory took it up to name something larger than any individual household: a pervasive ordering of power along the axis of gender that outlasts any particular man and reproduces itself across generations.
Generated by: domination, organized along the axis of gender — the same fixed ranking that anarchism and feminism trace through capital, the state, and colony, here cut by the line between masculine and feminine.

The crucial clarification is that patriarchy is not the claim that every man dominates every woman.
It is the claim that institutions are built to vest authority in masculine roles and to subordinate feminized ones, and that this is enforced by force when custom fails.
The family under patriarchy operates as a small unit of governance — historically the father commands, others obey — and that template is echoed at every scale: boss over worker, state over subject, clergy over congregant. bell hooks insisted on naming it together with the systems it runs alongside, in the compound phrase "imperialist white-supremacist capitalist patriarchy," precisely to block the move that would treat gender domination as separable from race, class, and empire.
What makes patriarchy distinctively hard to see and to fight is its intimacy: it operates inside the household and the relationship, the spaces marked private and therefore treated as beyond political contest.

One of patriarchy's load-bearing operations is the assignment to women of the labor that the wider economy refuses to pay for: childcare, domestic work, emotional maintenance, care of the sick and the old.
This work is made to disappear by being renamed love, duty, or nature rather than work.
Feminist political economy argues that this unwaged reproductive labor is not incidental to capitalism but one of its conditions of possibility — the system extracts not only surplus from the waged worker but the daily and generational replenishment of workers themselves, performed for free under the sign of devotion.
To call it patriarchy rather than mere custom is to insist that the arrangement is political and therefore contestable.

The anarchist tradition, articulated early by Emma Goldman, holds that freedom is indivisible: a revolution that frees workers but keeps women subordinate has not understood what domination is.
Goldman criticized both the patriarchal left, which told women to wait until after the revolution, and the liberal feminism that sought women's inclusion in the state rather than the abolition of the hierarchies the state secures.
Anarcha-feminism — the practice Goldman lived before the name existed — treats patriarchy, capital, state, and colony as mutually reinforcing structures: the nuclear family disciplines labor and trains obedience; property law dispossesses women; colonial regimes imposed European gender hierarchies on societies that had ordered gender otherwise.
You cannot dismantle one of these while preserving the others.

Read through this library, patriarchy is hierarchy cut along gender — it runs on the same machinery as every other form of domination: authority (the claimed right to command), coercion (violence against women and gender-nonconforming people), and obedience (compliance trained from childhood).
Its reciprocal counterpart is the same as domination's in general — solidarity and the building of relations one can leave without punishment.
Within the wider analysis it does not stand alone but interlocks with race, class, age, and colony; the matrix of domination and intersectionality are the tools for reading how gender domination is lived differently depending on every other position a person occupies.
