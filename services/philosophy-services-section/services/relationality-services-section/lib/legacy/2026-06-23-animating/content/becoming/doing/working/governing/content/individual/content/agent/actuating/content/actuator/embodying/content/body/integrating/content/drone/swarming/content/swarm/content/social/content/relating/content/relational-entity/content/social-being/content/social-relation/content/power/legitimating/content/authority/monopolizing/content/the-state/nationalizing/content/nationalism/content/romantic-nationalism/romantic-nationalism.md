# romantic-nationalism

Romantic nationalism is the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century movement that fused Romantic aesthetics — the valorization of feeling, organic community, rootedness, and the particular over the universal — with the political claim of nationhood.
Its founding premise, articulated by Johann Gottfried Herder in the 1780s, is that each people (*Volk*) possesses a unique spirit, a **volksgeist**, expressed through its language, songs, myths, and customs.
That spirit is what makes a people a people; the political task is to make it visible, collect it, preserve it, and defend it.
Generated by: **nationalism** — romantic-nationalism is its aesthetic and historical form, the branch that supplies nationalism with culture, feeling, and a usable past.

The movement arose against Enlightenment universalism, the claim that there is one human nature, one reason, and one set of rights applicable to all.
Herder countered that universalism was a mask for French cultural dominance, and that authentic human life is irreducibly particular: rooted in a specific language, a specific landscape, a specific inheritance.
Herder's own intent was pluralist — every people is equally dignified in its particularity, none superior — but the framework he built was immediately available for harder uses.
If each people has a spirit, then someone must curate it: decide which songs, which tales, which costumes count as authentic, and which do not.
That curation is where the reduction of a living community to a fixed type begins.

The movement is best read through its products.
The Brothers Grimm assembled "German" folk tales; Fichte's *Addresses to the German Nation* (1808) summoned a German people into being; the Finnish *Kalevala* was compiled by Lönnrot from oral fragments; the Czech National Revival and the Irish Literary Revival did comparable work; the Pahlavi regime in Iran deployed Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh* as proof of an ancient national spirit.
In each case the cultural materials that "prove" the nation exists were produced by the movement that needed them.
Benedict Anderson's phrase for the result is the "imagined community" — not a fiction but a community that becomes real because enough people imagine it the same way, and romantic-nationalism is precisely the machinery that synchronizes the imagination through shared songs, stories, festivals, and costumes.

The legacy is genuinely ambivalent, and a grad student should hold both sides.
For colonized and peripheral peoples, the Herderian framework offered a way to assert cultural dignity against imperial homogenization — to say that one's language and inheritance are not backward but worthy.
For dominant nations, the same framework offered a way to reduce both their own population and their neighbours to consumable folk types, fixing who counts as authentically of the nation and who does not.
Romantic-nationalism produces **the-folk**, the curated image of rural, linguistically pure, pre-modern communal life, by way of **volksgeist** and the **invention-of-tradition**.
Read against its neighbours, it is the cultural workshop of nationalism: it makes the feelings, symbols, and pseudo-ancient customs that the broader doctrine needs, and it furnishes the cast of figures that **palingenetic-ultranationalism** later recruits for its myth of national rebirth.
