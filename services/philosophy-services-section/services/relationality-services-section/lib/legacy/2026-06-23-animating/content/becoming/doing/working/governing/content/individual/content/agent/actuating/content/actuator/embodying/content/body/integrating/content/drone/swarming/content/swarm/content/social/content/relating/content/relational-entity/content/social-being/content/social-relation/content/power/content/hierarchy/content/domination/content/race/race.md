# race

Race is a system of classification that assigns human populations to hierarchically ranked categories on the basis of phenotype, ancestry, or geographic origin.
The central insight of modern scholarship is that race is not a biological reality but a political technology — a way of organizing domination that produces the very categories it claims merely to discover.
Human genetic variation is real, but it does not cluster into the groupings that racial classification imposes; the boundaries of "Black," "white," "Asian," and the rest are drawn by history and power, not found in the body.
Generated by: domination, organized along the axis of race — the line dominant groups and states draw through a population to sort who may rule and who must be ruled.

Karen and Barbara Fields (*Racecraft*, 2012) name the operation by which race comes to seem natural: just as witchcraft creates witches by accusing people and organizing social life around the accusations, racecraft creates races by classifying people and organizing social life around the classifications.
The races are entirely real in their consequences — they shape who lives where, who works at what, who is killed by police, who inherits wealth — but they are not real in the way biological science can detect.
Michael Omi and Howard Winant (*Racial Formation in the United States*, 2014) supply the complementary account of how the categories change: race is the product of an ongoing, historically situated process in which social, economic, and political forces determine the content and meaning of racial categories.
The Irish were not "white" in nineteenth-century America and then became white; the categories shift when the politics shift.

The most important claim this library makes about race is that it is not a parallel axis simply added alongside the others.
Following the interlocking-systems framing of the Combahee River Collective and Patricia Hill Collins, race operates through the other structures of domination rather than beside them.
Capital accumulates through racial differentiation: Cedric Robinson's account of racial capitalism holds that race is a condition of capitalism's possibility, the means by which the working population is divided, hierarchized, and made differentially exploitable — not a prejudice that capitalism happens to carry.
Patriarchy and the nation-state likewise distribute their effects along the color line, determining which families count as legitimate and which lives count as grievable.
To analyze race as an isolated variable, on this view, is to miss what it is and how it works.

Race is conventionally distinguished from ethnicity in that racial categories are primarily imposed from outside — by states, by dominant groups — while ethnic categories can be claimed and inhabited from within; but the boundary between the two is unstable and politically contested, and the same population can be racialized at one moment and treated as merely ethnic at another.
The library flags one position as an open problem at the intersection of race and settlerism: the place of Black peoples within the settler frame — neither native nor settler in the classical sense — which Afropessimist thinkers such as Frank Wilderson and Saidiya Hartman have theorized and which the analysis here treats as unresolved.
Downstream of race in this library sit the more specific concepts: racialization (the making of the category), racial-formation (the process by which it shifts), racecraft (the sleight that hides the act behind the belief), the color-line (Du Bois's name for the global division), and double-consciousness (the standpoint racial domination imposes on those it subordinates).
