This procedure maps a specific platform's feedback architecture — identifying its nested feedback loops, the timescales at which each operates, and what each loop is regulating.
The procedure draws on Stafford Beer's Viable System Model as the analytical framework and applies it to a platform that can be observed by a user or analyst.
The output is a structural account of the platform as a cybernetic system.

## What you need before you start

A specific platform.
The procedure works for any platform with an algorithmic content or interaction layer: social media platforms (Twitter/X, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Facebook), content platforms (Substack, Medium), gaming platforms with social layers (Discord, Steam), professional platforms (LinkedIn), dating platforms, and others.
The procedure does not apply to platforms with no algorithmic layer (a simple email list, a static website).

A working hypothesis about what the platform is regulating: what state is the platform's feedback architecture maintaining?
For most platforms the primary answer is *user engagement*, but the specific form of engagement regulation varies and the more specific your hypothesis the more useful the mapping.

## Step 1: Identify the lowest-layer regulation (Layer 1)

The lowest layer is real-time regulation — the feedback loop that operates at the timescale of individual interactions (seconds to minutes).
For a content platform, this is the recommendation engine: the loop that detects a user's interaction with content (view, like, share, comment, skip, scroll-past) and adjusts the next content served.

For each identifiable Layer 1 loop, specify:

- **The sensing signal:** what the platform detects (engagement signal, skip signal, time-on-content, reaction type, comment sentiment)
- **The response:** what the platform does with the signal (amplifies similar content, modifies recommendation, adjusts content queue)
- **The timescale:** how quickly the loop operates (seconds for recommendation updates; minutes for trending-topic adjustment)
- **What state it is maintaining:** the hypothesis (engagement rate, session length, return frequency)

For a social media platform, a typical Layer 1 loop: user sees recommended post → user engages (like, comment, share) → algorithm registers engagement signal → algorithm increases probability of serving similar content to this user → user sees more content matching engagement pattern.
The loop operates at second-to-minute timescales and maintains the state of user-content match quality (measured by engagement rate).

## Step 2: Identify the campaign-level regulation (Layer 2)

The Layer 2 loop operates at the timescale of hours to days — the level at which the platform regulates its response to campaigns, trends, viral phenomena, and content-policy events.
This layer is harder to observe directly from the user position, but its effects are observable.

Identify Layer 2 loops by looking for the platform's response to events that play out over hours to days:

- **Trend amplification:** when a topic is trending, how does the platform respond?
  What does it do with the trend information?
- **Viral content handling:** when a piece of content goes viral, what does the platform do?
- **Content-policy response:** when a content-policy issue arises (misinformation spread, harassment campaign, coordinated inauthentic behavior), how does the platform respond, and over what timescale?

The Layer 2 regulation is typically less algorithmic and more institutional: human teams making decisions about trends, policy applications, and content amplification within the algorithmic layer.
Document the decision-making structure as best you can from public reporting and the platform's own documentation.

## Step 3: Identify the product-level regulation (Layer 3)

The Layer 3 loop operates at the timescale of months to years — the level at which the platform regulates its product through new features, interface redesigns, algorithm overhauls, and business-model adjustments.
This layer is visible in the platform's public announcements, product update notes, and earnings calls.

For Layer 3, document:

- **Feature addition and removal:** what features has the platform added or removed, and when?
- **Algorithm change announcements:** what changes to the recommendation algorithm has the platform announced?
- **Business model evolution:** how has the platform's revenue model changed?
- **What each change was responding to:** what state was the platform trying to correct for?
  (Declining engagement?
  Advertiser pressure?
  Regulatory attention?
  Competitive pressure?)

The Layer 3 regulation is the platform's response to conditions at a scale that Layer 1 and Layer 2 cannot address.
It is the platform's capacity to redesign itself in response to its environment.

## Step 4: Map the subject-formation implications of each layer

Each layer's regulation has implications for what subjects the platform produces.
For each layer identified, ask: what subject-formation does this layer's regulation require of the users whose engagement it is regulating?

Layer 1 regulation typically requires: continuous availability (the user must be present), emotional responsiveness (the user's emotional state must be legible through engagement signals), and content-genre inhabitation (the user's engagement patterns must correspond to recognizable genre preferences).

Layer 2 regulation typically requires: participation in trending topics (the user's engagement with trending topics provides the signal the layer uses), tolerance of policy enforcement (the user must accept the platform's content-policy judgments as applied to content they may value), and identity-legibility (the user's participation must be legible enough for the platform's trend-detection to classify it).

Layer 3 regulation requires: adaptation (the user must adapt to redesigns and algorithm changes), continued participation through friction (the user must remain engaged despite the changes that Layer 3 introduces), and behavioral flexibility (the user must be able to inhabit new genres and features the platform introduces).

The [neurotic platformal intellectual](../teaching/explanations/neurotic-platformal-intellectual.md) is the subject produced by the interaction of all three layers — a subject who must be continuously available, responsive, legible, trend-participatory, policy-tolerant, adaptive, and flexible across all three timescales simultaneously.
The neurosis is the management of these simultaneously demands.

## Step 5: Identify what the platform cannot regulate

No Viable System is viable against all disturbances.
Ashby's law of requisite variety implies that the platform's regulation can only match the variety of disturbances it can sense.
Identify, from the analysis of the three layers, what kinds of user behavior the platform cannot sense and therefore cannot regulate:

- **Behavior that produces no digital exhaust:** face-to-face conversation, reading without engagement, attention without any interaction signal
- **Behavior that produces false signals:** deliberate ironic engagement, coordinated behavior that manipulates the algorithm's genre-detection
- **Behavior that exits the platform:** leaving the platform, reducing session frequency, switching to non-algorithmic platforms

These are the regulatory blindspots.
They are analytically important because they identify where the platform's cybernetic governance cannot reach.
