Jurisdiction is one of governance's most consequential and least examined concepts.
In the ordinary view, an authority simply *has* jurisdiction over some matter — a court has jurisdiction over a case, an agency over a regulation, a parent over a household decision.
The analytical view this subdomain commits to refuses the having-picture.
Jurisdiction is a *relation* — between the authority, the matter, and the parties — that has to be established each time, that can be asserted and contested, and that has its own structure of operations distinct from the substantive decisions it makes possible.

The subdomain's terms name those operations. *Jurisdiction* itself is the abstract relation. *Jurisdictional invocation* is the move by which an authority asserts jurisdiction over a matter — the filing of a complaint that names this court, the regulator's notice that brings a matter under its scope, the captain's order that asserts command-jurisdiction over a situation. *Jurisdictional submission* is the corresponding move by which a party accepts (or is found to have accepted) the authority's jurisdiction — appearance, performance, the failure to object in time. *Jurisdictional declination* is the authority's own refusal to take jurisdiction — the court that finds it lacks subject-matter jurisdiction, the agency that defers to another, the captain who refers the matter to higher authority. *Jurisdictional excess* names what happens when an authority acts beyond its jurisdiction — the act that is *ultra vires* (beyond power) and is on that ground void or voidable. *Jurisdictional vires* names the determination, made within or above the jurisdictional structure, of whether an act lay within the authority's power.

The framework draws on legal scholarship on jurisdiction (the long literature on subject-matter and personal jurisdiction in American procedure; Hart and Wechsler's *Federal Courts*; the international-law tradition on extraterritorial jurisdiction) and on the Hohfeldian analysis of [governance](../hohfeldian/) more generally.
It also draws on the project's own work on the *predicated-governance* substrate (in [`predicated-work/theories/predicated-governance/`](../../../predicated-work/theories/predicated-governance/)), which formalizes jurisdiction as a typed relation in a specific computational substrate.

The substantive sociological stakes are large.
Jurisdictional analysis is what is required to read settler-colonial legal systems honestly: the colonial state asserts jurisdiction over Indigenous peoples and lands that it has not lawfully obtained, and the asserted jurisdiction is then enforced through the same operations the framework names.
Reading those operations as jurisdictional rather than as substantive — as moves over the *relation* of authority rather than as instances of an authority that simply exists — is part of what makes the analysis politically tractable.
