# communitas

Communitas is the condition of shared obligation — the exposure, vulnerability, and debt that bind people into community.
It names not what a community has in common but what its members owe one another.
The concept was developed by the Italian philosopher Roberto Esposito in *Communitas: The Origin and Destiny of Community* (2010), who traced the word back to the Latin *munus* — at once obligation, gift, and duty.
A community, on this reading, is a body of people bound together by a shared *munus*: a debt they carry toward each other, a duty that precedes and constitutes the relation.
Generated by: reciprocating — communitas is the binding of people through mutual exposure, the reciprocal relation understood as a tie of owing rather than of having.

Esposito's argument cuts against the dominant modern understanding of community.
Liberal and communitarian theory alike tend to treat community as a collection of individuals who share something — an identity, a territory, a set of properties or values — so that to belong is to possess a common attribute.
Esposito reverses this.
Community, in the sense of *communitas*, is not a possession but a dispossession: it is the condition of being exposed to claims one did not choose, of owing something to others that can never be fully discharged.
To be in community is not to hold something in common but to be opened, made vulnerable, placed under obligation by the very fact of relation.
Community is what one owes, and owing has no end.

The concept is sharpened by its opposite, *immunitas*.
If *communitas* is the condition of shared obligation, *immunitas* is exemption from it — the *im-munus*, the release from the duty that the community imposes.
Esposito argues that a great deal of modern governance operates by immunizing subjects against the demands of communitas: protecting the individual from the exposure, risk, and vulnerability that shared obligation entails, and in doing so dissolving the very ties that made a community a community.
The immunized subject is safe, bounded, and exempt — and also, by the same stroke, alone.
Modernity's promise of protection is thus also, on Esposito's account, a steady erosion of the mutual exposure that binds people together.

The concept does real work in contemporary accounts of collective care.
When the rhetoric of "you do you, I'll do me" replaces shared standards — common mask norms, collective harm-reduction practices, the maintenance of accessible space — what is being dismantled is communitas: the infrastructure of mutual obligation through which a community protects its most exposed members.
Under that dismantling, shared care comes to look excessive, intrusive, even authoritarian, while the burden of risk falls on those who cannot protect themselves individually and who depended on the collective standard that has been withdrawn.
The immunitarian move — each protecting only themselves — leaves the vulnerable to absorb the danger that others now refuse to mitigate together.

Within the reciprocal relations, communitas names the obligational ground beneath the others.
It is what disability justice draws on when it insists that access and care are a collective duty rather than an individual misfortune, what collective care and access intimacy presuppose, and what mutual aid practices as the daily honoring of a debt that is never settled.
Where solidarity is standing-with against domination and mutual aid is the cooperation that meets needs, communitas is the deeper claim that the bond itself is constituted by what we owe one another, exposed and unexempted, before any of us chose it.
