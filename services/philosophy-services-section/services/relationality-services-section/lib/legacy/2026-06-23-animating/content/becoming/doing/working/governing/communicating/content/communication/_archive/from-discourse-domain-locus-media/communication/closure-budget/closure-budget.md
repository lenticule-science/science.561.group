Closure budget names the simple fact that readers cannot perform unlimited closure work in a single artifact. There is some quantity of unaided completion the audience will do before they stop, give up, skim, or invent. The artifact that asks for more than that quantity has overdrawn the budget, and the rest of its content is read at a degraded quality if it is read at all.

Closure budgets are real and they are *audience-relative*. A proof aimed at experts can ask its reader for many large closures across complicated apparatus. The same proof aimed at a different audience is asking for closures that audience will not perform; the artifact has overdrawn the budget for that audience, even if its content is unchanged. The budget is determined by what the audience has been trained to close, not by the difficulty of the material.

## What overdrawing produces

When the budget is overdrawn, several things happen, none of them good for the artifact:

- **Skimming.** The reader stops doing closure work and just samples the surface, picking up some of the words but not the structure.
- **Invention.** The reader fills the gaps with what plausibly fits and continues, producing an internal version of the artifact's argument that may diverge substantially from what the writer intended.
- **Abandonment.** The reader stops reading. The artifact has communicated its first n pages and nothing else.
- **[Legibility-debt](legibility-debt.md).** The artifact has now produced a permanent maintenance cost: every reader who attempts the artifact in the future will pay the same overdraw, and every secondary writer who tries to summarize or extend the artifact will inherit the difficulty of recovering what its original readers were supposed to do.

## What budgeting looks like

Honoring the closure budget is a craft skill. It looks like: choosing audience claims that are narrow enough to be true; pausing to expand a closure when the surrounding closures have already been many; distributing the closure work across an artifact rather than piling it in one place; refactoring a passage that asks for many small closures into a passage that asks for one large but well-licensed one. None of this is automatic, and none of it is achieved by adopting a more "rigorous" register. It is achieved by attending to what the audience has actually been asked to do.
