# affinity-group

An affinity group is a small group of people — usually three to fifteen — organized by mutual trust and shared analysis rather than by formal membership, bylaws, or hierarchy.
It is the basic organizational unit of anarchist practice, particularly in insurrectionary and direct-action traditions.
The term comes from the Spanish *grupos de afinidad* of the late-nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century anarchist movement, where small circles bound by personal trust and shared conviction formed the cells of a much larger movement.
Generated by: voluntary-association — the affinity group is the smallest durable unit of free association, equals bound by trust who federate upward without ever submitting to a center that commands.

What holds an affinity group together is not a charter or an institution but trust.
The members know one another personally, share an understanding of what they are doing and why, and can decide quickly because they have already built the relationships that make genuine agreement possible.
The group exists to act, not to perpetuate itself; it has no standing apparatus to maintain, no membership to recruit for its own sake, no office to keep open.
This is informal organization at its most basic — a form of coordination that lives entirely in the relationships among its members and dissolves when those relationships end.

Affinity groups work because they solve a problem that is otherwise hard for non-hierarchical movements: how do you coordinate action without hierarchy?
A large group deliberating every decision from scratch is too slow for many situations, especially under pressure.
A leader who decides on everyone's behalf reproduces exactly the authority the group exists to oppose.
The affinity group threads this needle.
It is small enough for real consensus, cohesive enough for rapid decision-making, and autonomous enough that no external authority dictates its actions.
Within it, decisions can be made in minutes by people who already trust each other's judgment, without anyone being put in charge of the others.

Affinity groups scale through coordination rather than command.
Multiple groups converging on a common action coordinate through *spokescouncils*: each group sends a delegate — a spokesperson, not a representative — who conveys the group's position and carries information back.
The delegate does not decide for the group; they carry a specific, revocable mandate and can be recalled the moment they exceed it.
This is federation at the organizational level: autonomous groups acting in concert without subordinating themselves to a central body, the same logic by which confederacy coordinates whole communities, worked out here among handfuls of people.

The model is also unusually resistant to repression, and this is part of why it persists.
There is no central leadership to arrest, no membership list to subpoena, no treasury to seize, no headquarters to raid.
When the state moves against this kind of organizing, it cannot decapitate it, so it attacks the one thing that holds it together — trust itself — through infiltrators, informants, and the practice of falsely accusing members of being informants to turn a group against itself.
Trust is thus both the affinity group's greatest strength and its sole real vulnerability.
Within the reciprocal relations, the affinity group is the concrete, human-scale instance of voluntary association: free entry, free exit, and a bond that lasts exactly as long as the trust that sustains it.
