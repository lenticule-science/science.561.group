A person loses their job during a recession.
In the self-help genre, this is a "pivot" — an opportunity to reinvent yourself.
In the activist genre, it is evidence of systemic exploitation.
In the therapeutic genre, it is a wound requiring healing.
The recession is the same recession.
The job loss is the same job loss.
What changes is the genre — the set of expectations determining what the event means, what feelings are appropriate, and what counts as resolution.

Genre calibration names the active process by which these expectations are tuned.
The concept synthesizes three bodies of theory: [Lauren Berlant](../../../../../../references/people/lauren-berlant.md)'s account of genre as a structure of anticipation, [Sara Ahmed](../../../../../../references/people/sara-ahmed.md)'s account of how affects circulate between bodies and objects to produce orientation, and [Norbert Wiener](../../../../../../references/people/norbert-wiener.md)'s cybernetic model of systems that self-correct through feedback.
Each describes a piece of how structural crisis gets converted into personal experience.
Genre calibration names how they operate together.

## Berlant: genre as anticipation, not interpretation

Berlant's use of genre departs from literary criticism.
In *Cruel Optimism* (2011), genre names a structure of anticipation — a set of expectations about how situations unfold, what counts as crisis, what counts as resolution, and what feelings belong at each stage.
Genre is not applied to events after the fact.
It shapes what events *are*.
By the time someone is interpreting a job loss, the genre has already determined which aspects are visible (personal failure or personal growth), which are background (structural conditions), and what resolution looks like (emotional transformation rather than material change).

Berlant's concept of [crisis ordinariness](../../../../philosophy/theories/western/theories/political-philosophy/theories/liberalism/terms/crisis-ordinariness.md) is the condition where ongoing instability is absorbed into daily life rather than recognized as rupture.
Crisis does not arrive as an event.
It persists as a condition — and genre is what makes the persistence livable.
The self-help genre, the therapeutic genre, the political analysis genre all provide formats for metabolizing ongoing crisis without demanding structural resolution.
People maintain attachment to these formats even when the formats demonstrably fail to produce the outcomes they promise.
This is [cruel optimism](../../critical-theory/theories/affect-theory/theories/berlantian/terms/cruel-optimism.md): attachment to conditions that harm you, sustained because the attachment itself provides a livable structure of feeling.

## Ahmed: affects circulate, stick, and orient

Berlant shows that genre structures what we expect.
[Sara Ahmed](../../../../../../references/people/sara-ahmed.md) shows how that structuring works at the level of affect — how feelings move between bodies and objects, accumulate, and orient people toward certain futures.

In *The Cultural Politics of Emotion* (2004), Ahmed argues that affects do not reside in subjects or objects.
They are produced through circulation.
Fear is not in the child or in the bear — it emerges through their contact, shaped by histories of prior contact.
Through repeated circulation, certain objects become "sticky" with particular affects: the word "immigrant" accumulates fear and disgust through its movement between political speeches, news reports, and everyday conversation, independent of any specific immigrant.

In *The Promise of Happiness* (2010), Ahmed extends this to show how affects orient bodies toward social norms.
Happiness is not a neutral feeling but a technique of social direction.
"Happy objects" — marriage, career, family — accumulate positive affective value and function as "straightening devices," aligning people with normative life paths.
Those oriented toward the right objects are legible as good subjects.
Those oriented otherwise — the feminist killjoy, the unhappy queer, the melancholic migrant — register as failures or threats.

This matters for genre calibration because Ahmed shows the mechanism by which genres *take hold*.
A genre is not just a narrative structure (Berlant) — it is an affective economy in which certain responses accumulate value and others are deflated.
The "pivot" genre for job loss works because resilience and growth-mindedness have accumulated positive affective stickiness through their circulation across wellness media, LinkedIn culture, and therapeutic discourse.
The objects within that genre (self-care, networking, upskilling) are happy objects that orient the subject toward normative recovery.
Grief, rage, and structural analysis are sticky with negative affect — they are killjoy responses that disrupt the genre's promised arc.

## Wiener: feedback without central control

Genre calibration is not a top-down process.
No institution sits in a room tuning people's expectations.
The tuning happens through feedback.

[Norbert Wiener](../../../../../../references/people/norbert-wiener.md)'s cybernetics (1948) describes how systems maintain stability through distributed self-correction.
A system detects deviation from a desired state and adjusts.
The adjustment is local and automatic — no central controller is required.
A thermostat maintains temperature through feedback: sense deviation, correct, repeat.
Wiener showed that this mechanism operates identically in machines, organisms, and social systems.
In *The Human Use of Human Beings* (1950), he argued that society "can only be understood through a study of the messages and the communication facilities which belong to it" — that social systems, like organisms, are "bound together by a system of communication" with "circular processes of a feedback nature."

Genre calibration is a feedback process in this sense.
When someone narrates their job loss as a pivot and receives social reinforcement (likes, supportive comments, professional opportunities), the genre is confirmed — the narrative arc resolved satisfactorily, the feeling rules were followed, the affective economy rewarded the performance.
When someone narrates their job loss as evidence of structural failure and receives discomfort, silence, or the suggestion to "focus on what you can control," the genre is corrected — the deviation from the expected narrative is dampened.
No one coordinates this.
The correction is distributed across every interaction, every platform algorithm that boosts engagement over analysis, every HR culture that rewards resilience framing over systemic critique.

[Stafford Beer](../../../../../../references/people/stafford-beer.md)'s Viable System Model extends this: organizations maintain viability through nested feedback layers operating on different timescales.
Genre calibration operates similarly — the wellness industry calibrates on one timescale (years of accumulated content), platform algorithms on another (real-time engagement optimization), interpersonal feeling rules on another (the immediate social cost of being a killjoy).
These layers do not coordinate.
They converge.

## What genre calibration names

The synthesis is: genre structures anticipation (Berlant), affects circulate and orient bodies toward normative objects within those genres (Ahmed), and the whole process self-corrects through distributed feedback without central coordination (Wiener).
Genre calibration names this unified process — the continuous, distributed tuning of expectations so that structural crisis is experienced as personal narrative, and personal narrative is experienced as resolution.

The concept emphasizes that this tuning is not static.
It is constantly being adjusted — by media, by platform interactions, by the feeling rules embedded in institutions and relationships.
The tighter the calibration, the more efficiently structural contradiction is converted into affect.
And because the calibration happens through feedback rather than command, it is self-sustaining: the system does not need anyone to believe in it.
It only needs enough people to follow the genre's arc, producing the affective signals that confirm the genre for the next person.
