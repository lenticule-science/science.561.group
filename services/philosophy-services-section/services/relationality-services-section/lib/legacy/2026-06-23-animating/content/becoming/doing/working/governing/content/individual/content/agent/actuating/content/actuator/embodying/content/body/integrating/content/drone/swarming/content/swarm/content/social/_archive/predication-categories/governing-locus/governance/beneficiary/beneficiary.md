A beneficiary is the party whose interests an institutional structure is constituted to serve.
The [trustee](./trustee.md) manages property for the beneficiary's benefit.
The [steward](../../../terms/steward.md) manages a household for the owner's benefit.
The [office](../../../terms/office.md) exists to serve a function that benefits a determinate set of people.
The [ship](../../../terms/ship.md) sails for the benefit of whoever the [charter](./charter.md) names.

The beneficiary does not hold the position, does not command the operation, and may not even know about it.
A trust can exist for the benefit of a minor who has no knowledge of the trust.
A public office serves citizens who never consented to the specific officeholder.
What makes someone a beneficiary is not their role in the structure but the structure's constitutive orientation toward their interests.

## What distinguishes a beneficiary

**From a principal.** A [principal](./principal.md) delegates authority and can revoke it.
A beneficiary has no authority to delegate — they hold no power over the structure except the right to its benefits.
A principal creates the relationship; a beneficiary is named by it.
In a trust: the settlor (who creates the trust) is the principal; the beneficiaries are the parties the trust serves.
These may overlap but are structurally distinct.

**From a stakeholder.** A stakeholder is anyone affected by an action or decision.
Stakeholders are identified empirically — you discover who is affected.
Beneficiaries are identified constitutively — they are named (or determined by a rule) in the founding document.
A factory's neighbors are stakeholders in its emissions decisions.
The factory's shareholders are beneficiaries of its charter.
The difference is between being affected and being served.

**From a client.** A client contracts for services.
The relationship is bilateral — the client pays, the provider serves.
A beneficiary may receive without contracting.
The relationship need not be bilateral: the structure may serve beneficiaries who give nothing in return, because the structure's purpose is to serve them, not to transact with them.

**From a subject.** A subject is governed.
A beneficiary is served.
These can overlap (a citizen is both subject to law and beneficiary of public services) but the concepts are distinct.
You can be a subject without being a beneficiary (a subject of a tyranny that serves only the ruler).
You can be a beneficiary without being a subject (the beneficiary of a charitable trust is not governed by the trust).

## The beneficiary in governance structures

In a [trust](./trustee.md): the beneficiary holds equitable title.
Legal title belongs to the trustee, but the trust exists for the beneficiary.
The trustee's absolute no-self-benefit constraint is the structural expression of the beneficiary's priority.

In an [office](../../../terms/office.md): the beneficiary is the public or community the office serves.
The officeholder exercises authority within the office's action domain, but the office's deontic profile (what the holder must, may, and must not do) is shaped by the beneficiary's interests.
An office that serves only the holder is a sinecure — a degenerate case.

In a [ship](../../../terms/ship.md): the charter determines the beneficiary.
A merchant ship's beneficiary is the charterer (whose cargo is carried).
A warship's beneficiary is the nation (whose defense is served).
A ship chartered for an [endeavor](../../../terms/endeavor.md) serves whoever the endeavor is directed toward — the people whose liberation, sovereignty, safety, or flourishing the endeavor aims at.

In an [endeavor](../../../terms/endeavor.md): the beneficiary is determined by the aim.
An endeavor toward Black Liberation serves Black people.
An endeavor toward Indigenous Sovereignty serves Indigenous peoples.
The committing agent is not the beneficiary — the committing agent is the one who strives; the beneficiary is the one for whose sake the striving occurs.
These can overlap (a community endeavoring for its own liberation is both committer and beneficiary) but the roles are structurally distinct.

## Etymology

From Latin *beneficium* — a good deed, a kindness, a favor. *Bene* (well) + *facere* (to do).
The beneficiary is the one to whom good is done.
The word entered English law through the ecclesiastical benefice — a church office held for the benefit of a parish.
The parish is the beneficiary; the priest is the officeholder.
The structure exists to serve the parish, not the priest.
