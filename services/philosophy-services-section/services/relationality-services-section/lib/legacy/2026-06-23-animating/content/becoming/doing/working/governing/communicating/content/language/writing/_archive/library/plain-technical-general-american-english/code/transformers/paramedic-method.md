The ***Paramedic Method*** is a step-by-step procedure for revising wordy, nominalized prose, developed by [Richard Lanham](../../../../../../references/people/richard-lanham.md) in *Revising Prose* [@lanham2006]. It treats [revision](../../essay-writing/terms/revision.md) as a learnable, repeatable skill rather than an intuitive art.

The steps:

1. Circle the prepositions.
2. Circle the "is" forms (am, are, was, were, been, being).
3. Find the action — often buried in a [nominalization](../terms/nominalization.md) ("the implementation of" → "implement"; "the investigation of" → "investigate").
4. Put the action in a verb.
5. Find the agent — who or what performs the action.
6. Put the agent in the subject position.
7. Cut the excess.

The method targets what Lanham called the [official style](teaching/explanations/official-style.md) — the passive, nominalized, hedged prose that dominates institutional writing. The Official Style persists not because writers lack skill but because institutions reward the appearance of authority over clarity. A sentence like "The implementation of the program was facilitated by the committee through the utilization of available resources" becomes "The committee implemented the program with available resources."

Lanham used the [lard factor](teaching/explanations/lard-factor.md) — the percentage of words in a passage that can be cut without losing meaning — to make revision concrete. Typical institutional prose runs 50–70% lard.

The Paramedic Method operationalizes the same principles [Joseph Williams](../../../../../../references/people/joseph-williams.md) described from a linguistic perspective: put characters in subjects and actions in verbs [@williams2006]. Where Williams explains *why* this works (readers process sentences by looking for agents and actions), Lanham provides a mechanical procedure for *doing* it.
