A first officer is the [office](../../../../../terms/office.md) responsible for translating [command](./captain.md) intent into operational execution.
The first officer is second in the [ship](./ship.md)'s hierarchy: subordinate to the [captain](./captain.md), superior to all other officers and [crew](./crew.md).
The captain sets direction; the first officer makes it happen.

## What the first officer does

The first officer holds three functions that together bridge command and execution:

1. **Watch management** — maintaining continuous awareness of the ship's operational state.
  What work is in progress, what is blocked, what has changed, what needs attention.
  In naval practice, the executive officer (XO) is responsible for the ship's internal administration and the conduct of the crew (US Navy Regulations, Chapter 10).
  The watch function is not passive observation — it is active maintenance of the conditions under which work can proceed.

2. **Crew dispatch** — matching work to workers by capability, authority, and capacity.
  Fayol (1916) identified this as the core of the management function: ensuring the right person is doing the right work at the right time with the right authority.
  The first officer does not do the work — the first officer routes it to the officer or crew member who can.

3. **Feedback maintenance** — ensuring that outcomes flow back to inform future decisions.
  Barnard (1938) identified the executive function as maintaining the system of cooperative effort.
  The first officer closes the loop: observing outcomes, assessing variance from plan, and feeding adjustments back into the dispatch cycle.
  Without this function, the ship operates open-loop — executing plans without learning from results.

## The delegation structure

The first officer's authority is delegated from the captain.
This delegation is:

- **Bounded** — the first officer operates within the captain's grant, not beyond it
- **Revocable** — the captain can relieve the first officer at any time
- **Non-transferring of accountability** — the captain remains accountable for everything the first officer does.
  Delegation distributes authority; it never lifts accountability.

The first officer can further delegate to subordinate officers, creating a chain of command.
Each delegation is bounded by the delegating officer's own authority — you cannot delegate what you do not hold.

## First officer vs. manager

The first officer resembles a general manager but differs in three respects:

First, the first officer serves a **bounded venture**, not a perpetual institution.
The ship has a charter with a declared purpose; the first officer serves that purpose for the duration of the venture.
A manager in a company serves the ongoing institution.

Second, the first officer holds **total operational accountability** during their watch.
US Navy Regulations state that the executive officer "shall be primarily responsible for the ship's organization and administration."
Not responsible for their own work — responsible for the ship's operations as a whole.

Third, the first officer is explicitly **immune from routine execution**.
The office exists for coordination, dispatch, and feedback — not for doing the work itself.
An officer who both executes and coordinates has a conflict of interest: they can mark their own work as complete without review.
Mintzberg (1979) identifies this separation as essential: the managerial role coordinates the work of others, not performs it.

## Installation and vacancy

Like all offices, the first officer position persists independently of its holder.
When the position is vacant, the ship lacks its operational coordinator — work may continue but coordination suffers.
Installation is a constitutive act: the captain (or commissioning authority) invests a person into the office, and the full normative profile becomes binding immediately.

In naval tradition, the ceremony of assuming the position — reading orders, formally accepting responsibility — makes the transfer of authority visible.
The authority attaches to the office at the declared moment, not gradually.
