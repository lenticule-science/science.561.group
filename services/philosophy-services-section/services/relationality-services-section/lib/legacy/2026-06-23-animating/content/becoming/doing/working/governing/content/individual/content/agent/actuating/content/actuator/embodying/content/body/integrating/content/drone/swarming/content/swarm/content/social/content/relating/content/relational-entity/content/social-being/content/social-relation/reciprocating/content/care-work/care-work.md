# care-work

Care work is the labor of tending bodies, relationships, and communities.
It encompasses childcare, eldercare, disability support, emotional labor, feeding, cleaning, healing, teaching, and the daily maintenance of the social bonds that make collective life possible.
It is the work that produces and sustains people themselves rather than commodities.
Care work is performed disproportionately by women, by people of color, by migrants, and by the undocumented; it is very often unwaged, and where it is waged it sits among the lowest-paid work in any economy.
Generated by: reciprocating — care work is the labor through which relating sustains the people it constitutes, the tending by which a community keeps its members alive and able to go on.

Feminist economists and social-reproduction theorists have argued that care work is not peripheral to the economy but foundational to it.
It produces and maintains the very labor power that capital requires: without the daily and generational work of feeding, housing, raising, healing, and sustaining workers, there is simply no workforce to be hired.
Capital therefore depends on this labor while systematically refusing to recognize it as labor at all — treating it as a natural emanation of femininity, an expression of love or instinct, rather than as work that demands real skill, time, and energy.
Silvia Federici and the Wages for Housework movement of the 1970s made this contradiction explicit: by naming housework as work and demanding a wage for it, they exposed how capitalism rests on a vast unpaid foundation it has hidden inside the home and called "natural."
Federici's *Caliban and the Witch* traced this arrangement back to the violent historical process by which women's reproductive labor was enclosed and subordinated alongside the enclosure of common land.

The same labor, however, also points beyond capitalist social relations entirely, and this is what makes care work a hinge concept.
The very tending that capital extracts without paying is, in another setting, the substance of the reciprocal relations.
Mutual aid networks, kinship systems, and community care practices organize care through reciprocity rather than through the market or the state.
In many Indigenous and diasporic communities, care circulates through kin networks that resist the nuclear family's privatization of reproductive labor — the child is raised, the elder is tended, the sick are kept by a web of relations rather than by a single isolated household or a purchased service.
Here care is not a commodity extracted from the unpaid but the daily honoring of obligation, the practice through which communitas — what a community owes its members — is actually carried out.

This is why care work appears under reciprocating despite its capture by capital: in its reciprocal form it simply *is* the mutual care of kinship, mutual aid, and communitas, the tending that equals do for one another.
What capital does is take this reciprocal practice and extract it — unwaged, feminized, racialized — as "reproductive labor" that produces the labor power it then buys at market.
The same act of care can therefore be either a freely given strand of a community's mutual obligation or a resource pumped out of those least able to refuse it; which it is depends not on the work itself but on the relations that organize it.

The devaluation of care work is not incidental but structural, and it sets the agenda for what grows out of this concept.
Disability justice takes the reciprocal possibility and makes it a political demand, insisting that care is not charity bestowed on the unfortunate but a collective obligation and a form of political practice — access and interdependence built into the infrastructure of common life rather than extracted from the exhausted few.
Any political project that does not reorganize care, that leaves it unpaid and unseen at the bottom of the hierarchy, reproduces the domination it claims to oppose.
