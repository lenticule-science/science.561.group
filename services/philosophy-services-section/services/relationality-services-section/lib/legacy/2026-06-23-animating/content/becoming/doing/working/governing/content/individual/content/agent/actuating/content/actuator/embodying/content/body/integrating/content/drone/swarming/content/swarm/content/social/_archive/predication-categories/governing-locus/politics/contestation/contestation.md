# Contestation

The active, organized challenge to a polity's existing distribution of authority, membership, or legitimating claims.
Politics, on the sociology of political life, is always partly the study of contestation: polities are not equilibria, they are sustained patterns under continual challenge.

Charles Tilly's work on contentious politics is the canonical sociological reference: strikes, riots, social movements, revolutions are all forms of contention through which the boundaries of polity, citizenship, and authority are reshaped.
The *contentious politics* tradition (Tilly, McAdam, Tarrow) treats these forms as a family with recognizable structural features rather than as exotic disruptions.

## Contestation and polity-form

What counts as contestation depends on the polity-form:

- In state polities, contestation often takes the form of confronting the state — protest, electoral mobilization, civil disobedience, insurrection.
- In state-refusing polities, contestation can take the form of withdrawal — moving away from a decision rather than confronting it.
  The Lakota right of [consensus-by-withdrawal](theories/lakotaean-politics/concepts/consensus-by-withdrawal.md) is contestation built into the polity-form itself.
- In settler-colonial contexts, contestation by Indigenous polities often takes the form of *refusal* — refusing recognition, refusing citizenship, refusing the categories through which the settler state would absorb them (Simpson [simpson2014](references/simpson2014.md); Coulthard [coulthard2014](references/coulthard2014.md)).

## Contestation and reproduction

A polity is the cumulative outcome of contestation managed: which challenges are absorbed, which are deflected, which become founding moments, which produce schism.
The sociology of politics is in large part the study of these patterns of management — what Tilly called "the repertoires of contention" and what political historians call "regime trajectories."
