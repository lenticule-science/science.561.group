The anarchist analysis of communication treats it not as the neutral transport of pre-formed messages but as a site of organizing in its own right.
Who speaks, to whom, in what register, through which infrastructure, with what audibility — these are the structural questions communication carries, and the anarchist tradition has insisted that the answers organize political possibility before any specific argument is made.

The subdomain's terms — communication, media, narrative, propaganda — name the four axes the analysis develops. *Communication* is the basic relation: anarchism's commitment to non-coercive, voluntary, horizontal forms of life means that communicative practices that depend on hierarchy or coercion (from broadcast monologue to corporate platform extraction) are themselves political problems, not neutral channels. *Media* names the material infrastructure — print, radio, web, mesh, mutual-aid relays — through which communication is carried; anarchist media history is a history of seeing infrastructure as politically constitutive, from the nineteenth-century anarchist press through community radio through contemporary federated and peer-to-peer networks. *Narrative* names the work of giving shape to political experience so that it is intelligible to those who shared it and to those who did not; the anarchist analysis (drawing on Berlant, on the situationists, on the contemporary movement-narrative tradition) refuses both the heroic and the despairing genres in favor of forms that hold open the possibility of acting. *Propaganda*, in the anarchist sense, is not deception but the work of producing the cultural and affective conditions under which liberatory action becomes thinkable — what propaganda-by-the-deed names at one extreme and what zine culture, mutual-aid newsletters, and movement journalism name at others.

The subdomain reads against the cybernetic-postliberal analysis of platform-mediated communication: where corporate platforms format communication to extract engagement and produce subjects who self-regulate inside the format, the anarchist communication tradition has long developed counter-formats, refusal practices, and infrastructure under the participants' control.
The continuity between mid-twentieth-century anarchist print networks, late-twentieth-century radical media, and contemporary federated networks is part of the substance.
