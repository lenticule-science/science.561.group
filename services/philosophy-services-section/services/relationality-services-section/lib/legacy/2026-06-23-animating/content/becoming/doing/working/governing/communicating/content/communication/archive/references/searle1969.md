# Searle 1969 — *Speech Acts*

John R. Searle, *Speech Acts: An Essay in the Philosophy of Language* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1969). The systematic elaboration of [Austin's](austin1962.md) framework into a structured theory of illocutionary types and felicity conditions.

## What it establishes

Searle takes Austin's locutionary/illocutionary/perlocutionary triple and develops the *illocutionary* component into a precise structural theory. Three of his contributions are load-bearing for the [speech-act](../objects/speech-act.md) object as the corpus carries it:

1. **Felicity conditions as constitutive rules.** For each illocutionary type Searle specifies a *constitutive rule set*: preparatory conditions that must hold for the act to be performable, sincerity conditions that the speaker must satisfy if the act is sincere, and an essential condition that *defines* the act (the essential condition for promising is that the utterance count as an undertaking of the obligation to do the promised act). The rules are *constitutive* in the sense that they define the act rather than merely regulating its performance.

2. **Propositional content / illocutionary force decomposition.** Every illocutionary act decomposes into a *propositional content* (what is said) and an *illocutionary force* (how the said is taken — as assertion, as request, as promise). The decomposition is the model's core algebraic structure: the same proposition can carry different forces, and the same force can carry different propositions.

3. **The principle of expressibility.** Whatever can be meant can be said: there is no thinkable communicative content for which a sufficient natural-language sentence cannot in principle be constructed. The principle is the bridge that lets philosophy of language stand in for philosophy of mind in many speech-act analyses.

The book also develops detailed treatments of *promising* (the central case study), *referring* (how propositional content's noun-phrase component picks out entities), and *predicating* (how its predicate component characterises them).

## Searle's later developments cited but not housed here

The five-fold taxonomy of illocutionary types — *assertive*, *directive*, *commissive*, *expressive*, *declaration* — is from Searle's 1976 paper "A Classification of Illocutionary Acts," not from this book; it is the canonical organisation of the illocutionary-force space and is what the [speech-act](../objects/speech-act.md) object inherits as its taxonomy. The 1976 paper is itself a refinement of Searle 1969's earlier four-fold attempt; this entry cites the book that introduced the framework, with the taxonomy understood as its later canonical form.

## Why this corpus cites it

Searle 1969 is the book that turned Austin's lectures into the structural theory that downstream pragmatics, philosophy of language, and AI dialogue systems all build on. The [speech-act](../objects/speech-act.md) object's *felicity-conditions* clause and the proposition/force decomposition both come from this book directly. Without it, the [speech-act](../objects/speech-act.md) object is a vocabulary; with it, the object is a theory.

The book is also the historical reference for the *speech-act-theoretic* account of meaning that competes with truth-conditional and use-theoretic accounts in the philosophy of language — the distinction matters when the corpus articulates what *kind* of meaning [pragmatics](teaching/explanations/pragmatics.md) is the theory of.
