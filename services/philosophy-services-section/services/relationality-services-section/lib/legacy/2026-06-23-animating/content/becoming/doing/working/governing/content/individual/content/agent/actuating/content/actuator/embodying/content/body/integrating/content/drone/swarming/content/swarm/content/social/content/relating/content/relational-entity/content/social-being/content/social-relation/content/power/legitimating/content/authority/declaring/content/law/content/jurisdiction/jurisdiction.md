# jurisdiction

Jurisdiction is the authority of a legal body to hear a case, enforce a law, or exercise power over persons and territory.
Generated by: jurisdiction is the bounding of legal authority — the answer to two questions law must always settle, namely which body has authority over a given matter and what the scope of that authority is.
Because authority is never unlimited, every court, agency, and legal order operates within bounds, and jurisdiction is the name for those bounds.
It is, in effect, the spatial and substantive geometry of legal power: the lines that decide where one body's writ runs and another's begins.

Jurisdiction operates along several axes at once. *Territorial* jurisdiction limits a court's authority to events or persons within a geographic boundary — a state court in Kansas cannot adjudicate a dispute that occurred entirely in Maine. *Subject-matter* jurisdiction limits authority by the type of case — a bankruptcy court cannot hear a murder trial. *Personal* jurisdiction limits authority to parties with a sufficient connection to the forum, so that a court cannot simply compel a stranger with no ties to it to appear.
These limits are not pedantic technicalities; they are the conditions under which a court's judgment counts as a legitimate exercise of authority rather than an overreach.

In federal systems like that of the United States, jurisdiction is layered.
Federal courts hear cases involving federal law, constitutional questions, or disputes between citizens of different states; state courts handle most criminal law, family law, property disputes, and tort claims.
The boundary between federal and state jurisdiction is itself a perpetual site of political contest, because where a case is heard shapes how it comes out.
When a legislature passes a law it often determines which courts will hear disputes under it — a choice with real consequences, since federal and state courts apply different procedures, draw from different jury pools, and sit within different hierarchies of binding precedent.

The sociological interest in jurisdiction lies in what its boundaries *produce*.
Jurisdictional lines create forum shopping, the strategic selection of a favorable court by a party with the resources to choose.
They produce regulatory gaps, where conduct that crosses jurisdictional lines becomes difficult for any single authority to reach.
And they create uneven legal landscapes, in which the same conduct is lawful in one place and criminal in another, so that a person's very legal status changes as they move through space — a fact felt acutely at borders and in the patchwork of laws governing, say, abortion or cannabis.
Jurisdiction is thus never a neutral grid; its boundaries allocate advantage and exposure.

Within this cluster jurisdiction is the form authority takes once it is bounded by law: it derives from sovereignty, which it parcels into manageable competences, and it is what adjudication presupposes, since a court must have jurisdiction before it can decide.
Its overlaps and gaps give rise to legal pluralism, the condition in which multiple legal orders coexist over the same people and territory, where the question of *whose* law counts is settled less by logic than by power.
