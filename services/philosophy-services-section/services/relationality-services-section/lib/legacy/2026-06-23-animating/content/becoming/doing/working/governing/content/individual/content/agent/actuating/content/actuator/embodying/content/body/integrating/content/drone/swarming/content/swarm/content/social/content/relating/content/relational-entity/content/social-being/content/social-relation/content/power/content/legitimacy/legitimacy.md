# legitimacy

Legitimacy is the recognized rightfulness of an arrangement of power — the belief in its validity that lets it command without the constant application of force.
Generated by: legitimacy is power acknowledged as rightful; it is the quality whose addition converts bare power into authority.
It names the difference between a system people comply with because they are forced to and one they comply with because they accept it as binding.
A legal order has legitimacy when those subject to it treat its rules and decisions as authoritative rather than as mere threats — and an order that retains legitimacy can govern far more cheaply, and far more durably, than one that must compel every act of obedience.

Max Weber gave the classic typology of its sources, sorting the grounds on which authority is believed valid into three kinds. *Traditional* legitimacy rests on custom and inheritance — authority is rightful because it has always been so. *Charismatic* legitimacy rests on the extraordinary personal qualities attributed to a leader. *Legal-rational* legitimacy rests on procedural correctness: the rules were made through recognized processes by authorized officials, and the officials are legitimate because they hold properly constituted offices.
Modern states claim this third kind almost exclusively — their power is presented as legitimate because it was enacted and is exercised through proper procedure rather than through the character of a ruler or the weight of the past.

This procedural account is powerful, but it is also conspicuously circular.
The procedures are legitimate because they are legally established, and the legal establishment is legitimate because it was produced through proper procedures.
At some point the chain of justification has to rest on acceptance rather than on further justification — on the brute social fact that enough people treat the system as authoritative for it to function as one.
This is why legitimacy is a *sociological* concept and not merely a philosophical one: it names a fact about belief (people accept the system), not a normative verdict (the system deserves to be accepted).
The two can come apart, and the gap between them is where the interesting questions live.

Legitimacy crises occur when that gap grows too wide to sustain — when the distance between an order's formal claim to authority and its actual social acceptance becomes unbridgeable.
Mass incarceration, discriminatory policing, unequal access to legal representation, and visible corruption each erode legitimacy by making the system's claim of procedural fairness demonstrably false for particular populations.
Crucially, legitimacy can be lost *unevenly*: a system may retain the acceptance of those it serves while forfeiting it among those it subjects, and continue to function so long as the former hold enough power to sustain it.
This connects to broader accounts of hegemony — the cultural and ideological work through which a particular arrangement of domination is made to appear natural, inevitable, and rightful to those it governs.

Within this cluster legitimacy is the ingredient that turns power into authority.
Power is the bare capacity to prevail over resistance; legitimacy is the recognition that makes its exercise count as rightful; authority is the product.
The rule of law, the office, the constitution, the election, the court's reasoned judgment are all, among other things, machines for generating and conserving legitimacy — devices that let power present itself as something obeyed by acknowledgment rather than extracted by force.
