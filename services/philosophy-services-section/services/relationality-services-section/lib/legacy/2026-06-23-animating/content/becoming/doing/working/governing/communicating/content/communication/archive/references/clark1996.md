# Clark 1996 — *Using Language*

Herbert H. Clark, *Using Language* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1996). The canonical contemporary statement of the *language-as-joint-action* program and the most thoroughly developed pragmatic-substrate model in the discourse-pragmatics literature.

## What it establishes

Clark's central thesis is that **using language is a joint action**: speakers and hearers do not act independently and signal across a gap; they coordinate continuously, adjusting in real time to each other's contributions and difficulties. The book's machinery formalises this thesis at three levels:

1. **Levels of action in language use.** Every communicative act decomposes into four nested levels: *executing* (the speaker physically produces a signal), *presenting* (the speaker presents the signal as meaningful to the hearer), *signalling* (the hearer recognises the signal's intended meaning), *proposing* (the speaker proposes a joint project, which the hearer takes up or declines). The four-level structure makes precise where breakdowns can occur and what kind of repair each requires.

2. **Common ground as substrate.** Communication operates against an accumulating substrate of *common ground* — propositions the participants take to be shared and take each other to take to be shared. Common ground is the shared background that makes elliptical, indirect, and presupposition-laden language work. The book extends Stalnaker's 1978 semantic common ground with three structural additions: communal vs. personal common ground (what community membership shares vs. what joint history shares), the *grounding* process by which propositions enter common ground, and the *principle of least collaborative effort* (joint actions minimise the combined work of speaker and hearer, not the speaker's work alone).

3. **Joint projects.** Conversations are organised into hierarchically-nested joint projects: an entire conversation, an exchange within it, an adjacency pair within the exchange, a turn within the pair, a phrase within the turn. At each level, the participants negotiate what they are doing and confirm completion. The hierarchical structure is what licenses the discipline's account of *how conversations close coherently* even though their content is co-constructed in real time.

## Why this corpus cites it

Clark 1996 is the founding reference for the [common-ground](../objects/common-ground.md) object — specifically for the elaborations beyond Stalnaker's semantic update model that make the object empirically adequate to actual discourse. Without Clark, common ground is a logical update operation; with him, it is a structured substrate with bases, mutuality structure, communal/personal layering, and grounding processes.

The book is also load-bearing for [pragmatics](teaching/explanations/pragmatics.md) more broadly: the joint-action frame reorganises pragmatics from a study of *speaker meaning* (Grice's project) to a study of *coordinated meaning* (Clark's project), keeping the Gricean machinery as a special case but relocating it within a more general theory of communicative coordination. The corpus's [audience](teaching/explanations/audience.md) concept inherits this: an audience is not just *who is being addressed* but *who is being coordinated with* in a joint project.

## Position in the literature

Clark's program builds on three independent lineages: Lewis's 1969 *Convention* and 1979 "Scorekeeping in a Language Game" (the game-theoretic and dynamic-semantic strand), Grice's cooperative principle and conversational implicature (the speaker-meaning strand), and the Sacks-Schegloff-Jefferson conversation-analysis tradition (the empirical-discourse strand). Clark synthesises all three into a single theoretical apparatus and adds substantial empirical work of his own (referring expressions, audience design, common ground in collaborative tasks).

The book is the canonical entry point to the joint-action program; subsequent developments include Pickering & Garrod's *interactive alignment* model (Cognitive Science, 2004), the *common-ground in dialogue systems* literature in computational linguistics, and the *plan-based dialogue* tradition (Cohen, Perrault, Allen) that takes joint projects as planning problems.
