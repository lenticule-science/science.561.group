***Style*** is the characteristic manner of a piece of writing — the sum of its [diction](./diction.md), [syntax](./syntax.md), [tone](./tone.md), rhythm, and formal choices. Style is not decoration added to content; it is inseparable from content. How something is said shapes what it means.

The plain style — short sentences, common words, active [voice](./voice.md) — communicates directness and clarity. The ornate style — complex sentences, unusual vocabulary, layered clauses — communicates nuance and deliberation. Neither is inherently better; each serves different purposes and [audiences](./audience.md). The mistake is applying one where the other belongs: writing a safety warning in ornate prose, or writing philosophy in primer sentences.

[Joseph Williams](../../../../../../references/people/joseph-williams.md) distinguished between style as choice and style as habit [@williams2006]. Writers who have studied style can shift registers deliberately — formal for an academic paper, conversational for a letter, spare for a news report. Writers who haven't studied it tend to write in a single default register, usually the one they've absorbed from reading. The [paramedic method](../../plain-language/terms/paramedic-method.md) and [revision](../../essay-writing/terms/revision.md) techniques in this vault's [style guide](../../../terms/style-guide.md) are tools for making style conscious and deliberate.

[Richard Lanham](../../../../../../references/people/richard-lanham.md) identified the [official style](../../../terms/official-style.md) — nominalized, passive, abstract — as the dominant bad style of institutional writing, arguing that it persists not because it's natural but because it conceals agency and avoids accountability [@lanham2006].
