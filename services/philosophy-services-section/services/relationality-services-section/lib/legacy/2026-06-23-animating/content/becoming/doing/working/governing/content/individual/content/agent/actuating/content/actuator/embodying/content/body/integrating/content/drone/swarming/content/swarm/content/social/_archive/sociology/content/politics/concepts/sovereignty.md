# Sovereignty

The condition of a polity's authority being final within its own scope — its decisions not subject to override by an external authority of the same kind.
Classical European political theory locates sovereignty in the territorial state (Bodin, Hobbes); the politics domain treats this as one historically specific configuration of a more general structural relation.

## Sovereignty against recognition

A central contemporary critique of state-centric sovereignty comes from Indigenous political theorists.
Glen Coulthard ([coulthard2014](references/coulthard2014.md)) argues that the politics of recognition — where Indigenous polities are recognized *by* the settler state, and in being recognized are absorbed into its constitutional order — reproduces colonial domination under the form of accommodation.
Audra Simpson ([simpson2014](references/simpson2014.md)) develops this into a politics of *refusal*: Kahnawà:ke Mohawk sovereignty is exercised through refusing American or Canadian citizenship, refusing to require settler-state recognition for the polity to exist.
Sovereignty is enacted by the polity itself, not bestowed by another.

Aileen Moreton-Robinson's work on Indigenous sovereignty against the white-possessive state extends this analysis.
Sovereignty is not a status conferred by international recognition; it is the polity's own self-constitution as the final authority within its kinship and territorial relations.

## Nested and overlapping sovereignties

Simpson's analysis shows that a sovereign political order can exist *nested* within a sovereign state — not as a subordinate jurisdiction, but as a separate polity whose sovereignty is in tension with the encompassing state's claims.
This is not the federalism of constitutional subdivisions; it is the structural condition of contemporary Indigenous nations whose sovereignty predates and outlives the settler state.

## What sovereignty is not

- It is not synonymous with state.
  A people can be sovereign without being a state.
- It is not bestowed by recognition.
  Recognition by another polity is one mode through which sovereignty becomes legible externally; it is not what makes sovereignty real.
- It is not exclusive of relationality.
  The Lakota understanding of sovereignty — see [lakotaean-politics](theories/lakotaean-politics/_index.md) — is bound up with relations to land, ancestors, and other-than-human relatives, not with abstract jurisdictional supremacy.
