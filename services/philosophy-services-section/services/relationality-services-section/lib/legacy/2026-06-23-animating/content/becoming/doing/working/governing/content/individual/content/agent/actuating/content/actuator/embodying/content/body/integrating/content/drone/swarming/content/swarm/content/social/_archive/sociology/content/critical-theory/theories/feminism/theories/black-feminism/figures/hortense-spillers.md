Hortense J.
Spillers (b.
1942) is the figure through whom the [black-feminism](../_index.md) subdomain holds its analysis of how the symbolic-political grammar of American kinship is constituted by slavery's ungendering of the captive, and of why the inherited categories of "woman," "mother," "kinship," and "gender" cannot be applied to Black flesh without remaking what those categories mean.
Gertrude Conaway Vanderbilt Professor of English at Vanderbilt University.

The full biographical note lives at [`library/references/people/hortense-spillers`](../../../../../../../../../../references/people/hortense-spillers.md).
This page records what black-feminism in particular takes from her.

## What this subdomain takes from her

- **Flesh vs. body.** The analytical distinction at the center of "Mama's Baby, Papa's Maybe" (1987).
  The *body* is the gendered, kinship-recognized, juridical-protected entity that the inherited categories of feminist theory presuppose.
  The *flesh* is the body unprotected, ungendered, and held in the captive condition of slavery — what was made of African captives in the Middle Passage and on the plantation.
  The reduction of bodies to flesh is the constitutive violence of the New World Atlantic system, and it is the analytical fact that mainstream feminist theory has not been equipped to work from.
  The distinction is one of the most consequential analytical moves in late-twentieth-century Black feminist thought.
- **The ungendering of the slave.** Slavery's symbolic order ungendered captive Africans — denied them the kinship-grammatical positions (mother, father, daughter, son) that gender presupposes.
  The maternal line under slavery was genealogically forced (the child followed the condition of the mother) but symbolically unrecognized. *Mama's baby, papa's maybe* names this constitutive paradox of African-American kinship.
  The argument refuses the standard feminist framing in which gender is a universal category whose particular instantiations differ; gender is itself produced by structures (the kinship grammar) from which the captive was systematically excluded.
- **The American grammar book.** Spillers reads American symbolic-political grammar as constituted by slavery's grammatical violence.
  Contemporary U.S. cultural-political life still operates within the categories that violence installed — and those categories cannot be navigated without confronting the foundational violence.
  The framing is methodologically generative: it locates the Black-feminist analytical task at the level of grammar (the categorial and syntactic substrate of meaning), not only at the level of representation or experience.
- **The interior life and the cultural critic.** Spillers's later essays develop the analytical-aesthetic register of Black women's specific positioning in the American symbolic order — neither fully woman in the white-protective sense nor outside the gender-system, but in a position that both registers cannot accommodate.
  The Black-feminist critic's task is partly to recover the interiority of Black subjects — the inner lives, intellectual productions, aesthetic projects — that the dominant culture's reductive frames render invisible.
  The methodological orientation converges with [Saidiya Hartman](saidiya-hartman.md)'s critical fabulation.
