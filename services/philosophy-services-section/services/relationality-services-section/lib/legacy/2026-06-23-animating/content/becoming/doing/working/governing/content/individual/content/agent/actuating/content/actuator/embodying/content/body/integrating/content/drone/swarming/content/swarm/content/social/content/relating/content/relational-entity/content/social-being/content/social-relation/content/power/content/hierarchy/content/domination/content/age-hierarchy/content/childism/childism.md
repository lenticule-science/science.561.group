# childism

Childism is prejudice against children as a group.
The term was developed by Elisabeth Young-Bruehl in *Childism: Confronting Prejudice Against Children* (Yale University Press, 2012), where she argues that prejudice directed at children works through the same mechanisms as racism, sexism, and homophobia: it constructs its target group as fundamentally different from and inferior to the dominant group, and it uses that construction to legitimize a broad range of practices — from neglect and abuse to paternalistic overprotection — that are not, in fact, in the interests of the subordinated group.
Generated by: age-hierarchy — childism is the prejudice that age-based domination rests on.

Young-Bruehl describes the childist stereotype as a foundational fantasy: a belief system that constructs "the child" as an immature being, produced and owned by adults, who may use it to serve their own needs and desires.
This reaches well beyond physical abuse.
It includes the well-meaning parent who pushes a child to fulfill the parent's own ambitions, the educator who treats students as vessels to be filled rather than agents to be respected, and the legal system that treats children as the property of their parents rather than as persons with interests of their own.
The breadth is the point: childism can run through tenderness and possession as easily as through hostility.

Childism operates at a different level of analysis than adultism.
Where adultism names the discriminatory practices and institutional arrangements through which adults exercise power over children, childism names the underlying prejudice — the cognitive and affective orientation that makes those practices seem natural, reasonable, or justified.
In Young-Bruehl's framework childism is the prejudice and adultism is its institutional expression; the one supplies the belief, the other carries it into rules and routines.

Childism also differs from misopedy.
Misopedy names an antipathy toward childhood itself — a structural hostility that treats childhood as a condition to be overcome.
Childism is broader: it includes not only hostility but also possessiveness, sentimentalization, and instrumentalization.
A culture can be thoroughly childist without being overtly hostile to children — it is enough that it treats them as means to adult ends rather than as persons whose interests matter independently.
Together with adult-supremacy, the term that names the full political system, these four concepts let the analysis of age-hierarchy distinguish attitude, prejudice, practice, and structure rather than collapsing them into a single complaint.
