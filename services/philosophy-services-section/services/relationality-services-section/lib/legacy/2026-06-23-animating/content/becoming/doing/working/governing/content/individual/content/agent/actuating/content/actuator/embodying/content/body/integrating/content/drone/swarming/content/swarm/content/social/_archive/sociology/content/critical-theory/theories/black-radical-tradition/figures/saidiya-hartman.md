Saidiya V.
Hartman (b.
1961) is the figure through whom the [black-radical-tradition](../_index.md) holds its analysis of how slavery did not end — how its grammar, its categories, its distributions of life and death persist as the structuring conditions of Black life in the post-emancipation present.
University Professor at Columbia and a 2019 MacArthur Fellow.

## What this subdomain takes from her

- **The afterlife of slavery.** Hartman's framing in *Lose Your Mother* (2007): "If slavery persists as an issue in the political life of black America, it is not because of an antiquarian obsession with bygone days or the burden of a too-long memory, but because black lives are still imperiled and devalued by a racial calculus and a political arithmetic that were entrenched centuries ago.
  This is the afterlife of slavery — skewed life chances, limited access to health and education, premature death, incarceration, and impoverishment."
  The phrase has become one of the subdomain's central organizing claims.
- **The scenes of subjection.** *Scenes of Subjection* (1997) refuses to reproduce the spectacular scenes of slave violence that abolitionist texts and contemporary historiography both circulate — Frederick Douglass's account of his aunt's beating becomes the case in point.
  Hartman argues that recirculation of those scenes does not produce empathy; it produces consumption, and reinscribes the position of the captive as object.
  The book's methodological move is to read instead the everyday, mundane scenes — the coffle, the marketplace, the stage — where subjection is enacted in less legible registers.
- **Critical fabulation.** The methodological response to the structural absence of enslaved people's interiority from the archive.
  The records that survive were produced by the slave system; they record what mattered to slaveholders and what could be made legible to property law.
  Hartman's method (developed across *Lose Your Mother*, "Venus in Two Acts" [2008], *Wayward Lives, Beautiful Experiments* [2019]) is to write at the limit of the archive — to fabulate without falsifying, to imagine the inner lives the records cannot supply while marking the speculation as such.
  The method is the subdomain's central methodological inheritance.
- **The wayward.** *Wayward Lives, Beautiful Experiments* (2019) reads the lives of young Black women in early-twentieth-century Philadelphia and New York as practitioners of a freedom that historiography has not learned to recognize — refusal of work, refusal of family, refusal of the narratives prepared for them, the invention of forms of life from inside conditions that should have made them impossible.
  The waywardness is not failure; it is method.
- **Refusal of recovery.** Across Hartman's work, a refusal of the redemptive narrative — the move that takes a life destroyed by slavery and tries to rescue it through historiographic recovery.
  The refusal insists that the loss is the loss, that no narrative work undoes it, and that the analytical task is to live with the unredeemability.

## Where her work figures elsewhere

Hartman is one of the load-bearing figures for the subdomain's analysis of slavery as a continuing structure rather than a past event, and for its methodological grappling with the limits of the archive.
Her concept of fugitivity converges with [Fred Moten](fred-moten.md)'s.
