# rights

A right is a claim that one party can make against another, backed by some authority — legal, moral, customary, or divine.
Generated by: rights are the claims law recognizes and enforces; in a legal system a right is an enforceable claim, so that if someone violates your right you can seek a remedy through the courts.
But the concept reaches well beyond law into political philosophy, ethics, and everyday moral reasoning, where to assert a right is to assert that some treatment is owed to you as a matter of standing rather than charity.

The deepest fault line in the theory of rights runs between two traditions.
The *natural rights* tradition — Locke, Paine, and the American and French revolutionary declarations — holds that certain rights exist prior to and independent of any legal system, inherent in personhood itself, such that a legitimate government must recognize rather than create them.
The *legal positivist* tradition — Bentham, Austin, Hart — rejects this: rights exist only where law creates and enforces them.
Bentham's verdict was withering: "Natural rights is simple nonsense; natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense — nonsense upon stilts."
The dispute is not settled, and it structures live arguments about constitutional interpretation, international human rights, and the very basis of political obligation.

A second, cross-cutting distinction separates *negative* from *positive* rights.
Negative rights require others merely to refrain from acting — the right to free speech means the government may not silence you.
Positive rights require others to act — the right to education means someone must provide schools.
This maps roughly onto the division between civil-political rights (speech, assembly, fair trial) and social-economic rights (housing, healthcare, food).
Western liberal democracies have historically prioritized the negative rights; socialist and social-democratic traditions have prioritized the positive.
The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) proclaims both, but the machinery of enforcement overwhelmingly favors the negative, which is one reason the social-economic rights so often remain aspirations rather than entitlements.

The properly sociological question about rights is not whether they exist in some philosophical sense but what they *do*.
Rights claims are political tools: they mobilize support, frame grievances as violations rather than misfortunes, and either constrain or legitimize state action depending on who wields them.
The civil rights movement did not merely assert that Black people have rights — it forced legal and institutional recognition of those rights through sustained, costly, collective action.
Rights, in this sense, are not discovered but *won*; they are the sedimented victories of past struggle.
And because they are won rather than given, they can also be eroded, narrowed, and rolled back when the balance of power that secured them shifts.

Within this cluster rights are the claims law recognizes and enforces — the entitlements carved out against authority and against other persons.
They depend on legal personhood, which determines who can hold a right at all; they are vindicated through due process and adjudication; and they are asserted precisely against the sovereignty whose power they are meant to limit, making the history of rights inseparable from the history of struggles to constrain the state.
