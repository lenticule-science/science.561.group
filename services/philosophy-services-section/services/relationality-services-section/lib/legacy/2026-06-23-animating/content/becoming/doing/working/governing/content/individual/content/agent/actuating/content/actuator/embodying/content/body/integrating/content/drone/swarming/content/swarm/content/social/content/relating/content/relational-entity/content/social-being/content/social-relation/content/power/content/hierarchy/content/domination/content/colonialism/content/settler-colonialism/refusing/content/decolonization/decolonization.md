# decolonization

Decolonization is the repatriation of Indigenous land and life.
In the strong sense this library takes from Eve Tuck and K.
Wayne Yang's "Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor" (2012), it is not a metaphor for social justice, critical pedagogy, or the diversification of settler institutions.
Decolonization means the return of stolen land, the restoration of Indigenous governance over that land, and the end of settler sovereignty as the organizing principle of political life.
Generated by: refusing settler-colonialism — decolonization is the undoing of that structure, and a refusal to let the specificity of the land demand dissolve into a general progressive agenda.
Its positive ground — Indigenous resurgence, grounded normativity, Indigenous sovereignty — is prior to the settler and seated elsewhere; it is not produced by colonization, and decolonization restores it rather than inventing it.

Tuck and Yang's insistence on the literal meaning is not a gatekeeping move but a refusal to let the demand be diluted.
The metaphorical use of "decolonize" — decolonize the curriculum, decolonize your mind, decolonize technology — treats colonialism as essentially a mindset or a cultural pattern that can be addressed through attitudinal change within existing institutions.
This framing leaves the material basis of settler-colonialism, the occupation of Indigenous land, untouched and usually unmentioned.
As such it is itself a settler move to innocence: it allows settlers to feel decolonial without confronting the structure they inhabit and benefit from.
Naming the literal sense is what keeps the word from becoming a way of doing nothing in particular.

Decolonization in its non-metaphorical sense is incommensurable with the reform of settler institutions.
This does not mean reform is worthless; it means decolonization and institutional reform are different projects, with different requirements and different endpoints, and the difference cannot be wished away.
Settler property law cannot be reformed into the return of land, because settler property law is the legal technology by which the land was taken; the demand for land return is incommensurable with it by design.
Honest engagement with decolonization, Tuck and Yang argue, begins by acknowledging this incommensurability rather than resolving it prematurely into something more comfortable — a partnership, a co-management scheme, a reconciliation that leaves sovereignty where it was.

A point this library states carefully: decolonization is defined here as a refusal of settler-colonial domination, but the life it aims to repatriate is not downstream of colonization.
Indigenous sovereignty, governance, and relationship to land precede the settler and persist through the colonial structure; decolonization restores relations the structure tried to sever rather than producing them anew.
This is why its constructive face is resurgence — the rebuilding of Indigenous political life on its own ground — and why decolonization sits in this library alongside land-back, which names its concrete material content, and recognition-refusal, which names the rejection of the settler's offer to acknowledge Indigenous existence on the settler's own terms.
The three are the refusals through which the answer to settler-colonialism is drawn.
