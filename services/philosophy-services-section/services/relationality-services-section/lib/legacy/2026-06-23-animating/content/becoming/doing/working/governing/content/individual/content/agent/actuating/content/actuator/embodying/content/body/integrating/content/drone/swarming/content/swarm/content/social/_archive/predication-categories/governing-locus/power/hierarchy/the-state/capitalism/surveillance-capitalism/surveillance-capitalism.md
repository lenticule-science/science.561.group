**Surveillance capitalism** is Shoshana Zuboff's term, developed in her 2015 paper [Big Other](zuboff2015) and book-length [Age of Surveillance Capitalism](zuboff2019), for an accumulation logic that she argues is structurally distinct from industrial capitalism.
The logic, on her account, originated at Google in the early 2000s, was generalized across the major technology platforms through the 2010s, and (Zuboff argues) has come to operate across consumer products, education, healthcare, finance, and government to such an extent that it constitutes the defining accumulation logic of the contemporary developed economy.
Whether the regime is genuinely as dominant as Zuboff claims is contested in the reception literature (see *Critique* below).

## The four-step accumulation cycle

Zuboff's central analytic claim is that surveillance capitalism operates through a four-step cycle:

1. **Behavioral surplus extraction.** The platform observes and records user behavior in excess of what is required to deliver the ostensible service.
  Searches, clicks, dwell times, locations, social interactions, biometric signals, conversational fragments, and increasingly home-, car-, and body-resident sensor data are ingested as the *raw material* of the surveillance-capitalist firm.
2. **Computation of prediction products.** The behavioral data are processed through machine-learning systems that compute *predictions* — predictions of what users will buy, click, watch, vote for, find persuasive, be susceptible to.
  The prediction products are the firm's distinctive output, more valuable than (and analytically distinct from) the consumer service that generated the underlying behavioral data.
3. **Sale of predictions in behavioral futures markets.** The prediction products are sold to clients — advertisers, retailers, insurers, employers, political campaigns, governments — who use them to influence the predicted behavior.
  The transaction occurs *between the platform and the client*, with the user (whose behavior is the subject of the prediction) not a party to the transaction and typically unaware of it.
4. **Modification of behavior to improve the predictions.** Because more accurate predictions command higher prices, the platform has a continuing interest in improving prediction quality.
  The most reliable way to improve prediction quality is to *constrain* the behavior being predicted — to nudge, gamify, addict, and habituate users into more predictable patterns.
  The behavioral-modification feedback loop is what distinguishes mature surveillance capitalism from a passive observation regime.

The fourth step is, on Zuboff's argument, the analytic novelty.
Earlier observation regimes (statistical sampling, market research, consumer surveys) collected data without modifying the behavior being observed.
Surveillance capitalism's economic structure rewards behavior modification, with the result that the platform's design, content, ranking, and notification logic are systematically tuned toward producing the behavioral patterns the platform's predictions describe.

## What is novel: the commodification of human experience

Zuboff's strongest claim is that surveillance capitalism represents the commodification of a category of being — *human experience* itself — that prior capitalist regimes did not commodify.
Industrial capitalism commodified labor power; consumer capitalism commodified attention; surveillance capitalism commodifies the *behavioral residue* of lived life — the data trail that human existence in a digitally-instrumented environment unavoidably produces.
The user does not sell their experience; the experience is *taken* and converted into a prediction product the platform owns.

This produces what Zuboff calls *Big Other* — the distributed, ambient, environmental sensing-and-shaping apparatus that surveillance capitalism builds out across the digitally-instrumented world.
Big Other differs from Orwell's Big Brother in not requiring a single sovereign center; the apparatus is distributed across many platforms, devices, and firms whose joint operation constitutes the regime.
Resisting it is not a matter of resisting a particular institution but of contesting the extractive logic itself.

## The political problem

Zuboff frames surveillance capitalism's challenge primarily in *political*-philosophical terms (rather than primarily economic): the regime produces an asymmetry of knowledge and power that is incompatible with the autonomy and self-governance liberal democracy presupposes.
The platform knows the user in ways the user does not know themselves, can act on the user in ways the user cannot perceive, and bears no accountability to the user for the actions it takes.
The asymmetry is, on Zuboff's account, qualitatively different from the asymmetries earlier institutional regimes produced and corresponds to no settled apparatus of accountability.

Her policy program — which the book's reception has criticized as both too modest (incremental antitrust and privacy reform) and too sweeping (challenging the basic accumulation logic) — calls for treating behavioral data extraction as a domain requiring constitutional-scale political contestation rather than incremental sector-by-sector regulation.

## Reception and critique

The book had enormous reception (general-readership bestseller, extensive policy-network engagement) and substantial critique.
Major lines:

- **Empirical overreach** — critics including Evgeny Morozov [morozov2019] argued that Zuboff's account treats genuinely diverse platform business models as a single logic, obscuring the substantial differences between Google's advertising model, Apple's hardware-and-services model, Amazon's retail-and-cloud model, and TikTok's content-recommendation model.
  The single "surveillance capitalism" frame may name a real phenomenon while overgeneralizing its scope.
- **Marxist critiques** (Srnicek [srnicek2017], Hardt-Negri lineage, Italian autonomist Marxism) argued that Zuboff's account neglects the broader political economy of contemporary capitalism — financialization, the ongoing extraction of unpaid labor, the global division of labor, the persistence of industrial production — within which surveillance is one mechanism among several rather than the defining accumulation logic.
- **Methodological critiques** noted that Zuboff's empirical base draws heavily on insider accounts of a small number of platform firms (especially Google and Facebook), with the generalization to "an age" outpacing the empirical evidence base.
- **The optimism critique** (Morozov, others) noted that Zuboff's diagnosis is paired with a relatively conventional liberal-reformist politics that may underestimate the depth of the structural change that would be required to displace the regime.

## Distinguished from

- **Platform capitalism** ([Srnicek](srnicek2017)) — Srnicek's typology distinguishes five platform forms (advertising, cloud, industrial, product, lean) of which the *advertising* platform corresponds most closely to Zuboff's surveillance-capitalism logic.
  Platform capitalism is the broader category; surveillance capitalism is one accumulation logic within it.
- **Big Brother / Orwellian surveillance** — the classical totalitarian surveillance image presupposes a sovereign center with explicit political-control objectives.
  Surveillance capitalism is decentralized, primarily commercial, and exercises power through prediction and behavior modification rather than through direct repression.
- **Privacy violation** — the privacy frame treats surveillance capitalism's harms primarily as violations of individual privacy rights.
  Zuboff explicitly rejects this framing as inadequate, arguing that the regime's primary harms are at the *systemic-power* level rather than the *individual-privacy* level and that privacy reform alone does not address them.
- **[Algorithmic regulation](algorithmic-regulation.md)** — the algorithmic-regulation literature describes a *regulatory form*; surveillance capitalism describes an *accumulation logic*.
  The two overlap (surveillance-capitalist platforms exercise algorithmic-regulatory functions over their users) but answer different analytical questions.
