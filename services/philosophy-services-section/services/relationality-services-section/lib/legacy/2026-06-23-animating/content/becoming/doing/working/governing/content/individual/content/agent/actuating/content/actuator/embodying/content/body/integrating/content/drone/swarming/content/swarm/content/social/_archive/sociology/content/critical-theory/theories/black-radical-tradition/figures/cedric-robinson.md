Cedric J.
Robinson (1940–2016) is the figure who named the [black-radical-tradition](../_index.md) — both the subdomain's namesake and the theorist whose 1983 *Black Marxism: The Making of the Black Radical Tradition* gave the tradition its self-description.
Professor of Black Studies and Political Science at UC Santa Barbara, he founded the Center for Black Studies Research there.

## What this subdomain takes from him

- **Racial capitalism.** Capitalism did not emerge as a racially neutral economic system that later incorporated racial hierarchy.
  From its origins, capital accumulation was structured through racial differentiation — the production of categorical distinctions among populations to cheapen labor, justify dispossession, and organize people into hierarchies of value.
  Robinson traced the racial logics of capitalism back to feudal Europe (the racialization of the Irish, the Slavs, the Roma) and argued that the Atlantic slave trade did not introduce race into a neutral economic system but scaled a logic already present.
  The full development is in the subdomain's term [racial-capitalism](../terms/racial-capitalism.md).
- **The Black radical tradition as lineage.** Robinson's central historiographic move is to treat slave revolt, maroon societies, Haitian revolutionaries, Du Bois, James, Wright, and twentieth-century Black liberation movements as a single tradition with continuous concerns — not because its participants share a method but because they share a vantage on the structure of modern domination and a commitment to its overthrow.
- **The tradition precedes and exceeds Marxism.** *Black Marxism* argues that Marxism, however indispensable, is a Western theoretical formation that misidentifies its own object when it treats race as superstructural.
  The Black radical tradition, by contrast, was forged in the immediate experience of racial-capitalist domination; its analytical resources are not borrowed from Marxism but developed from inside the condition Marxism describes from outside.
  The tradition is therefore both a critic and an extender of Marxism, not a regional application of it.
- **The terms of order.** Robinson's earlier *The Terms of Order* (1980) argues that political order is a Western construct that misrepresents itself as universal — that the figure of authority and the institutional architecture of leadership are historically and culturally specific, not necessary features of human social life.
  This frame underlies his later willingness to read the Black radical tradition as carrying alternative grammars of political life.

## Key works

- *The Terms of Order: Political Science and the Myth of Leadership* (1980)
- *Black Marxism: The Making of the Black Radical Tradition* (1983; with new prefaces in 2000 and 2020)
- *Black Movements in America* (1997)
- *Forgeries of Memory and Meaning: Blacks and the Regimes of Race in American Theater and Film before World War II* (2007)
- *Cedric J.
  Robinson: On Racial Capitalism, Black Internationalism, and Cultures of Resistance* (collected essays, ed. H.L.T. Quan, 2019)

## Where his work figures elsewhere

Robinson is the proximate source for the subdomain's [racial-capitalism](../terms/racial-capitalism.md) term and for the very framing of the tradition as a tradition.
[Ruth Wilson Gilmore](ruth-wilson-gilmore.md) extends his racial-capitalism analysis through carceral geography; [Angela Davis](angela-davis.md) extends it through abolition; [Saidiya Hartman](saidiya-hartman.md) extends it through the analysis of slavery's afterlife.
