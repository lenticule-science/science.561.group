# task

A task is a goal-directed unit of work: an organization of doing around a goal, decomposable into subtasks, that terminates when its goal is achieved, becomes impossible, or is released.
Where work is simply an episode of doing with an output, a task adds a *goal* that gives the doing its boundary — a definite condition under which it counts as finished.
Generated by: **practice** — a task is practice organized toward a goal, the unit by which concrete doing is bounded, directed, and tracked.

The goal is what defines the task.
The task succeeds when the goal is achieved, fails when the goal becomes impossible, and is abandoned when whoever holds it lets the goal go.
Without a goal there is activity but not a task: the goal is the thing that tells you when to stop and whether you have succeeded.
This is more than a bookkeeping point.
It is the goal that makes a task assignable, accountable, and assessable — and therefore the goal that makes a task a unit of *coordination* and *control* as well as of doing.
To assign someone a task is to hand them a bounded objective and hold them answerable for reaching it, which is why tasks are the elementary currency of organized, directed work and of the management of others' labor.

Tasks decompose hierarchically, and this nesting is the basic structure of all organized work.
A task breaks into subtasks, which break into smaller subtasks, down to atomic operations that cannot usefully be decomposed further.
The same nested structure appears across domains that otherwise share nothing: military planning runs mission to phases to tasks to subtasks; software development runs epic to story to task to subtask; project management runs project to work packages to activities.
The grain at which decomposition stops is practical rather than formal — one decomposes a step further when getting it wrong would be costly and stops when the step is reliable enough to treat as a single move.
The stakes set the grain.

A task's internal *plan* specifies how its subtasks relate: they may be sequential (do A, then B), conditional (if X, do A, else B), concurrent (do A and B at once), or cyclic (repeat A until some condition holds).
The plan need not be fixed in advance; it is often elaborated as earlier subtasks complete and more becomes known.
This is where task shades into the broader categories of planning and workflow — tasks compose into workflows, directed arrangements of tasks linked by dependency and information flow, and a workflow is itself a task seen at a higher level, with its own goal, its own subtasks, and its own plan.

It is worth holding the everyday, organizational sense of "task" together with what the critical analysis of work says about it, because the connection is real.
The decomposition of work into discrete, specifiable, assignable tasks is exactly the operation at the heart of scientific management and the labor process under capital: to break a craft into elementary tasks is to make each task simpler, each worker more substitutable, and the whole process more controllable from above — the mechanism Braverman analyzed as deskilling.
The task is thus not only a neutral planning primitive but, in a particular social setting, an instrument of control.
As a node, the task is the goal-bearing organizer of the practice cluster: it gives work its boundaries and its direction, sits below the plan that contains evolving sets of tasks, and decomposes down to the atomic operations from which all organized doing is built.
