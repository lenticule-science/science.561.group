## I. **Introduction: Language at the Edge of Collapse**

Contemporary English, particularly in its American institutional and digital forms, is undergoing a transformation in how it handles agency, relation, and ethical presence.
This transformation is not random, nor is it merely a matter of changing style or generational voice.
It is structural, shaped by the political and technological systems in which English now operates.
It is most visible in two dominant and seemingly opposed linguistic modes: the bureaucratic language of institutional procedure, and the affectively intense, internet-native language of meme-driven social media.

These forms appear to stand in contrast.
One is flat, technical, and emotionally withdrawn.
The other is expressive, stylized, and emotionally saturated.
But both, I will argue, are responses to the same condition: the increasing incapacity of English to articulate relation and responsibility under the pressures of collapse.
In other words, the prevailing forms of English have been adapted to a context in which political agency is diffused, accountability is avoided, and the primary function of language is no longer the creation of shared understanding, but the management of circulation and impression.

This paper develops a central claim: that **dominant English is losing the capacity to hold meaning in ways that sustain ethical relation**, and that this loss is not simply a linguistic issue but a consequence of the structures through which power now speaks.
This claim will be developed across several levels of analysis: through attention to the passive and procedural grammar of institutions; through a study of meme grammar and its stylized performance of life; through an examination of how historically situated and socially embedded grammars—particularly African American Vernacular English (AAVE), creole languages, and Indigenous languages—are appropriated and decontextualized in the service of revitalizing a collapsing dominant form.

The goal is not to propose a new grammar, or to identify a correct way of speaking.
Rather, it is to offer a critical framework for understanding how language functions under pressure: how grammatical form reflects political structure, how style can simulate presence without substance, and how relation can be performed while being structurally denied.
In doing so, I aim to make a case for what I will call **speaking while marked**: a practice of linguistic responsibility grounded not in innocence or originality, but in accountability to where one is speaking from, and to whom one’s speech is indebted.

This analysis draws on four core fields of reference.
From historical linguistics and the political diaries of Victor Klemperer, I take the insight that grammar is not ideologically neutral.
From critical media theory, particularly the work of scholars like Wendy Chun and Lauren Berlant, I draw the understanding that language is shaped by the infrastructures of digital capitalism.
From Black studies and Indigenous thought, I attend to the way language encodes relation, survivance, and social memory.
And from contemporary linguistic anthropology and discourse analysis, I examine how meaning travels—and is unmoored—in late platform capitalism.

The argument will proceed in seven parts.
First, I analyze the bureaucratic and procedural voice of institutions as a grammar of structural abdication.
Second, I trace the rise of meme grammar as a simulation of aliveness that depends on rhythm and reference drawn from marginalized grammars.
Third, I explore how these grammars—particularly AAVE and Indigenous languages—carry relational structures that are eroded when appropriated as style.
Fourth, I map how platforms reward linguistic extraction and decontextualization, accelerating the breakdown of responsibility in language.
Fifth, I examine the idea of critical language practice not as reform, but as situated speech within collapse.
Sixth, I reflect on what it means to speak without seeking a pure voice, and finally, I conclude by proposing linguistic accountability as a form of staying in relation when coherence is no longer guaranteed.

This is not a work of lament.
It does not suggest that English is dying or that expression has become impossible.
What it suggests is that **to use English now without examining what structures one inherits and what others one displaces is to participate—knowingly or not—in a regime of simulation that replaces relation with circulation**.
The question, then, is not whether to speak, but how to remain **present** in a language that no longer requires you to be. ## II. **Dead English: The Language of Structural Abdication**

In the bureaucratic, administrative, and increasingly automated language of contemporary institutions, English has become a medium through which action can be described without actors, harm can be acknowledged without responsibility, and responsiveness can be performed without relation.
This is not a stylistic phenomenon.
It is structural—rooted in the needs of institutions to manage liability, regulate perception, and maintain motion without disruption.
The form this takes, grammatically, is the persistent removal of agency from the sentence.

In these contexts, passive constructions are not incidental.
They are foundational.
A phrase like “a decision was made” may appear unremarkable, but its syntactic structure has precise effects: it establishes that something occurred, while leaving unspecified who made the decision, who was affected, and under what conditions.
Similarly, phrases such as “steps have been taken,” “concerns have been raised,” or “mistakes were made” all share a common logic: they allow institutions to represent themselves as responsive without exposing themselves to accountability.

This is what I refer to as **dead English**.
It is a mode of speech and writing that prioritizes formal clarity over ethical presence, in which sentences fulfill grammatical expectations while refusing relational obligations.
This language is especially common in moments of crisis—public apologies, policy updates, content removals, reports of institutional failure—when something must be said, but nothing can be risked.
The sentence becomes a form of insulation.

A particularly revealing example of this language in use is the way police killings are described in American media and official communication.
The phrase “officer-involved shooting” has become the standard.
It is structurally noncommittal.
It presents the event as a static occurrence rather than an act, and the only named actor is the “officer,” whose involvement is implied but not clarified.
Compare this to “a police officer shot a man,” or more directly, “Officer James Miller shot John Brown.” The difference is not just rhetorical.
It is ontological.
The passive formulation renders the event non-relational, not rooted in agency or social position, but as a neutral happening.

Victor Klemperer, writing from within Nazi Germany, recognized the political significance of such formulations.
In his 1947 work _LTI: Lingua Tertii Imperii_, he observed how Nazi German increasingly employed grammatical structures that concealed agency and transformed violence into procedure.
He noted how bureaucratic euphemisms and passive voice became standard in describing persecution and displacement.
For Klemperer, this was not merely a matter of word choice—it was a restructuring of thought itself. “What was said,” he wrote, “could no longer be distinguished from how it was said.” Language ceased to reflect reality and began to **produce a reality in which actions were no longer attributable, and subjects were dissolved into process**.

While the conditions of contemporary American institutions are not the same, the grammatical logic is strikingly similar.
In a risk-managed, image-governed regime, language is designed to perform responsiveness while avoiding the formation of liability.
The passive voice, procedural phrasing, and euphemistic tone serve as mechanisms for depersonalizing harm.
What might once have been named directly is now processed administratively.

Legal studies scholars have long recognized this function.
Writing on institutional speech, Mari Matsuda describes the “non-position” as a communicative stance designed to appear inclusive and responsible while actively avoiding substantive claims.
The language of non-position is not silent—it is verbose—but it speaks without anchoring itself to anything.
In this regime, grammar becomes not a tool for thinking, but a structure for postponing consequence.

This is further intensified by the digital infrastructures through which institutional language now circulates.
On platforms like YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok, content moderation messages routinely appear in forms like “Your content was removed because it may have violated our Community Guidelines.” The sentence is grammatically correct, procedurally complete, and structurally opaque.
The actor is hidden—was it a human moderator, an algorithm, an outsourced firm?
The judgment is vague—“may have violated” leaves the user with no path to understanding or recourse.
And the norms being enforced—“Community Guidelines”—are left undefined within the sentence itself.

This is a kind of **syntactic automation**.
It parallels what Wendy Chun has described in _Updating to Remain the Same_ as the logic of programmable governance: a mode in which systems offer the appearance of responsiveness while being closed to interruption.
Language follows suit.
It becomes executable, not dialogic.

In platform environments, this also reshapes the expectations of the speaker.
The user, when composing appeals or queries, learns to speak in the same grammar.
One learns to avoid direct address, to flatten affect, to write in the style of the system in hopes of being understood—or at least not flagged.
Dead English is not merely imposed.
It is internalized.

It is important not to misunderstand this as linguistic degradation.
The grammar remains intact.
The syntax is correct.
The vocabulary is professional.
But the sentence, while grammatically alive, is ethically inert.
It does not build relation.
It does not name agency.
It does not support dialogue.
Instead, it closes the event in language, even as the underlying harm remains active.

This is the grammar of **structural abdication**.
It allows institutions to describe harm without causing alarm, to appear responsible without being present, and to communicate without establishing relation.
Its primary function is to circulate efficiently and avoid friction.
In doing so, it evacuates one of the core functions of language: to locate the speaker in a shared world.

But language does not disappear.
The vacuum created by this mode of speech generates its own counterpressure.
In the space where institutional English fails to feel or connect, a new form emerges—one that appears saturated with emotion, performatively present, and irreverently alive.
This is the realm of **meme grammar**.
But as we will see, it does not solve the crisis it responds to.
It performs it in a different key.

## III. **Meme Grammar as Simulation of Aliveness**

If dead English is the language of absence, meme grammar presents itself as the language of presence.
Where bureaucratic speech deletes the subject, meme grammar floods the sentence with emotion, perspective, and performative intimacy.
It is expressive, compressed, often funny, often bizarre.
It speaks not in full sentences, but in fragments, screenshots, captioned images, acronyms, keyboard mashing, and self-referential code-switching.
It appears, on the surface, to be everything that institutional English is not: flexible, responsive, emotionally charged, and community-based.

But this impression is deceptive.
Meme grammar is not, in most cases, a return to relation.
It is a simulation of relation structured by platform logics, attention economies, and stylistic borrowing from grammars that continue to be marginalized.
It emerges in response to the breakdown of trust in institutional language, but it does not repair that breakdown.
Instead, it **mimics the shape of situated grammar without bearing its weight**.

This section will argue that meme grammar is a response to linguistic collapse that depends on the extraction of vitality from other linguistic traditions—particularly African American Vernacular English (AAVE), creole and hybrid diasporic grammars, and queer and trans vernaculars of the Global North.
It will also argue that meme grammar’s structure mirrors the digital environments in which it is used: fast-paced, flattened, disembodied, and reward-driven.
Though it often appears as subversive or resistant, its formal features allow it to circulate within the same systems that reward detachment and displacement.

To understand meme grammar, we need to begin with the conditions of its emergence.

---

### I. Meme Grammar and Platform Form

The typical features of meme grammar have been well documented by scholars of digital language.
They include:

- Elliptical syntax (e.g., “me when the” or “he do be vibin”)

- Exaggerated affect (e.g., “SCREAMING SOBBING THROWING UP”)

- Irony-layered tone (e.g., “capitalism said suffer and i said bet”)

- Affective abbreviations and emojis (e.g., “🥲,” “💀,” “😭😭😭”)

- Format-based repetition (e.g., “girl help,” “not this,” “slay,” “it’s giving”)

This grammar functions less through semantic precision and more through **format recognition**.
The meaning of a post often relies not on its denotative content but on its _formal resemblance_ to prior posts.
As linguistic anthropologist Ilana Gershon notes, platform-based communication often works by “communicative style rather than propositional content”—what matters is whether the sentence fits a recognizable rhythm.

This is not inherently negative.
In fact, much of meme grammar’s appeal lies in its ability to condense complex emotion into legible formats.
It provides tools for naming alienation, chronic stress, cultural dissonance, and political despair in ways that institutional English cannot.
But it is precisely this expressiveness that makes meme grammar an ideal substrate for **aesthetic appropriation**.
The forms can be lifted, repurposed, and redeployed without requiring participation in the life-worlds from which their energy derives.

---

### II. Simulacrum and the Detachment of Style

Jean Baudrillard’s theory of simulation offers a useful framework for understanding how meme grammar performs presence while removing its substance.
In _Simulacra and Simulation_, Baudrillard describes the final stage of simulation as the moment when a sign “has no relation to any reality whatsoever: it is its own pure simulacrum.” This is the stage at which representation detaches entirely from the thing it once referred to, and becomes a free-floating aesthetic object.

Meme grammar, particularly in the hands of speakers outside the communities it borrows from, often functions at this level.
Phrases like “go off king,” “slay,” or “it’s giving” circulate as markers of expressiveness, style, or personality—but detached from the specific cultural and relational contexts that shaped their grammatical and pragmatic meaning in AAVE or queer Black vernacular.

The result is not simply borrowing.
It is **simulation without relation**.
The grammar feels familiar, but it is no longer anchored in a community, a history, or a mode of life.
It has become a usable affect—a way of performing personality, without building relation.

This has consequences.
It allows dominant users—particularly white, cisgender, and middle-class users—to feel expressive, bold, or “real” in ways that were historically denied to them by normative English.
But it does so by **repurposing the expressive infrastructure built by marginalized communities**, without acknowledging the costs or conditions of that expressiveness.
The grammar is made available.
The life behind it is left behind.

---

### III. Who Built the Grammar Being Simulated?

Consider a phrase like “he do be vibin tho.” To the untrained eye, this might look like a meme grammar construction—ironic, playful, grammatically wrong on purpose.
But it reproduces a legitimate aspectual distinction in AAVE, where the habitual “be” marks an action that occurs regularly or characteristically.
In standard English, this nuance is impossible to express cleanly: “he is vibing” suggests current activity; “he be vibin” suggests that he vibes in general.

What appears to some as a joke is, in fact, **a grammatical structure refined over centuries** by speakers of a non-standardized, socially stigmatized English.
And yet in meme grammar, these features are routinely used out of context, for comic effect, or for the affective charge they carry, without reference to the community that shaped them.

This is not limited to AAVE.
It is equally visible in the aesthetic uptake of Indigenous syntax, especially in land-based metaphor; in the adoption of diasporic Caribbean English phonology and rhythm in internet speech; in the retooling of queer vernacular for commercial slogan-making.
In each case, **the grammar of relation becomes the style of alienation**.
What was once a tool for survival becomes a memeable pose.

---

### IV. The False Opposition Between Institutions and Memes

It is tempting to frame dead English and meme grammar as opposites: one the language of control, the other of freedom.
But this is a false opposition.
Both emerge within the same conditions of collapse, and both operate under the same structural pressures: to circulate, to perform, to avoid consequence.

Where dead English deflects responsibility by proceduralizing action, meme grammar deflects responsibility by ironicizing expression.
Neither mode invites relational engagement.
Neither mode supports answerability.
One refuses presence through flatness; the other through over-performance.

This convergence becomes visible in the moment of crisis response.
After a scandal or incident, an institution may release a statement written in dead English: “We are aware of the situation and are taking steps to address it.” The response from social media may be a wave of meme grammar: “girl not this again,” “they really said oopsie,” “capitalism strikes once more.” Both modes allow the crisis to be absorbed, stylized, and moved past—without changing the underlying structure.

---

### V. Meme Grammar as Cover and Symptom

To be clear: meme grammar is not inherently extractive, nor is it incapable of meaningful speech.
It offers many people—especially those within the traditions being borrowed—an expressive mode that resists the flatness of dead English.
It can name grief, violence, absurdity, and contradiction with a precision that formal grammar cannot.

But in the hands of those not shaped by its source grammars, it becomes something else: a **cover**.
A way to feel alive while remaining untouched.
A way to critique systems without positioning oneself in relation to them.
A way to inhabit collapse without being accountable to what has been collapsed.

In this sense, meme grammar does not counter dead English.
It **completes** it.

Where one empties the sentence of subjectivity, the other fills it with simulation.
Where one erases feeling, the other performs it without anchoring.
Where one denies relation, the other **mimics** it.

Both forms allow speakers to communicate **without appearing as political agents embedded in a world with consequences**.

---

To understand what has been lost—and what is being mined to simulate its return—we must now turn to the grammars that continue to carry relation: not as aesthetic, but as structure.
These grammars have not disappeared.
They are present, resilient, and still evolving.
But they are being cannibalized, syntactically and stylistically, in the effort to keep dominant English moving.

## IV. **Linguistic Extraction: From Rhythm to Raw Material**

The vitality of meme grammar is not self-generated.
It emerges through contact with, and borrowing from, grammars that are already alive—already doing the work of holding relation, naming contradiction, encoding memory, and surviving surveillance.
These grammars are most often the product of communities historically excluded from standard English and punished for their deviations from it: African American Vernacular English (AAVE), creole languages formed under conditions of colonization and displacement, Indigenous languages that persist despite centuries of erasure, and diasporic Englishes shaped by migration, code-switching, and refusal.

These grammars are not alternatives to standard English in the sense of being defective or derivative.
They are structurally rich, historically situated, and semantically powerful.
They are, in many cases, **ontologically distinct**: organized around different understandings of time, agency, relation, and worldhood.
And it is precisely this difference—their capacity to express what dominant English cannot—that makes them attractive for extraction.

Extraction, in this context, refers not simply to the use of individual words or phrases, but to the lifting of **syntactic, pragmatic, and rhythmic patterns** from their social and historical grounds, and their redeployment in contexts where the original conditions no longer apply.
It is the conversion of grammar into style.
Relation becomes performance.
Embeddedness becomes affect.
The result is not the incorporation of other ways of speaking, but the appropriation of their expressive force as **raw material** for dominant users operating in collapsed contexts.

This process mirrors other forms of extraction—mineral, cultural, digital—where life is harvested from the margins to sustain the center.
The key difference is that linguistic extraction happens not through violence or consent, but through style: the uptake of grammar as aesthetic.

---

### I. The Grammar of Relation

To see what is lost in this process, we have to understand what these grammars do when they are used **in context**.

Take AAVE.
Its grammatical system includes features that are not present in standard English, including:

- **Aspectual marking**: habitual “be” to indicate regular or characteristic action (“She be working late”)

- **Zero copula**: omission of the verb “to be” in certain contexts (“He real tired”)

- **Double negatives**: intensification through repetition (“Ain’t nobody home”)

- **Indirect speech acts**: where social cues determine the pragmatic meaning beyond the literal sentence

- **Call-and-response structure**: real-time interactive signaling that repositions the listener as co-creator of meaning

These features are not linguistic shortcuts or mistakes.
They are deliberate tools for managing **relational density**—ways of encoding frequency, mood, positionality, and affective stance.
As Geneva Smitherman and John Rickford have shown in decades of linguistic research, AAVE is a system of meaning-making with internal consistency and cultural depth.
It is a grammar designed for **knowing in public**, for speaking while being watched, for communicating solidarity, suspicion, or critique within constrained conditions.

When AAVE phrases or constructions are lifted into meme grammar by speakers without that relational context, the grammar becomes dislodged. “It’s giving” no longer emerges from a shared horizon of cultural critique—it becomes a form of evaluative flair. “Slay” loses its performative edge as survival; it becomes cheer. “No one:” becomes a meme format, rather than a commentary on power.
The speaker is not required to know what is being said beyond its emotional effect.

This is not just decontextualization.
It is **flattening**.
The grammar survives in shape, but not in function.
The sentence moves, but does not relate.

---

### II. Indigenous Syntax and Ontological Compression

The same pattern can be observed in the use of Indigenous grammars as sources of stylistic inspiration.
In many Indigenous languages of North America, such as Lakota, Anishinaabemowin, or Navajo, the grammatical system is built around **verbs as primary units of meaning**.
These are often polysynthetic languages, where a single verb can encode subject, object, tense, aspect, location, and modality in a single morphological unit.

This structure reflects a different ontological commitment.
In Lakota, for example, verbs do not merely describe action; they articulate **states of becoming in relation**.
The language makes it difficult—sometimes impossible—to describe events without acknowledging how entities are positioned in relation to each other.
Passive constructions are rare or awkward.
The grammar insists on locating agency, even when agency is complex or shared.

When elements of Indigenous thought and language are referenced in English—especially in poetic or activist registers—it is often these structures that are referenced: verb-based being, circular time, embedded relation.
But when this happens without reference to the grammar itself, or the communities who continue to speak it, the result is aestheticization.
Indigenous grammar becomes a metaphor for a vaguely posthuman worldview, or an alternative to linear modernity, or a poetic gesture toward interconnectedness.
Its grammatical structure is not studied, respected, or practiced.
It is **sampled**, like a texture.

---

### III. Creoles, Pidgins, and Diaspora Englishes

Creole and pidgin Englishes, often treated as “broken English” in colonial contexts, are in fact adaptive, inventive, and highly expressive grammatical systems.
They emerge from contexts of forced encounter—slavery, indenture, conquest—and develop through improvisation, recombination, and resistance.

Jamaican Patois, Nigerian Pidgin, and Singapore Colloquial English (Singlish), among others, use aspect markers, tense particles, mood indicators, and unique intonation patterns to communicate complex states of knowledge, belief, desire, and doubt.
These features are often invisible to speakers of standard English, but they structure entire relational worlds.

These grammars are increasingly mined for internet flair.
Phrases like “y’all better,” “she understood the assignment,” or “dey mad” appear in viral content, often stripped of their creole or diasporic origins.
The rhythm becomes familiar.
The function becomes entertainment.

This is linguistic extraction: **grammar as reservoir**.
The structures that held complexity under colonial violence are now used to create the illusion of vitality in a language that no longer holds presence.
The source is not credited.
The lineage is erased.
The rhythm remains.

---

### IV. Style Without Inheritance

At the center of this process is a substitution: **style for inheritance**.

Meme grammar borrows from living grammars not in order to enter into relation with them, but in order to **feel something**—often, to feel alive inside a syntax that no longer generates relation on its own.
The borrowed forms are not studied, contextualized, or responded to.
They are **re-performed**.
This performance simulates community, insight, or emotional depth, while evading the history and responsibility that make those expressions possible.

This pattern is particularly visible when non-Black users borrow AAVE, or non-Indigenous users reference land-based syntax, or non-diasporic users write in a creolized English tone.
The feeling is often sincere.
The harm is often unrecognized.
But the structure is clear: **someone else’s grammar is used to carry the affect that the dominant language can no longer support**.

This is not remix.
Remix is relational.
This is extraction.

---

### V. Who Pays the Cost

The cost of extraction is not linguistic purity.
It is **erasure**.

When AAVE is used as meme grammar, Black speakers using AAVE are more likely to be penalized for “unprofessional” or “non-standard” speech.
When Indigenous syntax is borrowed as poetry, Indigenous language speakers are still denied state support for language revitalization.
When Caribbean English is used for comedic effect, Caribbean speakers are still marked as unintelligible in educational and legal systems.

The simulation circulates.
The source is punished.

And the dominant speaker, meanwhile, is praised for authenticity.

---

The loss, then, is not only cultural.
It is **epistemic**.
Grammar encodes how people understand relation, time, causality, and obligation.
When that grammar is stripped and restyled, the ontology is destroyed, and the surface is left to be worn as personality.

This prepares us to examine the systems that make such extraction not only possible, but profitable: the platforms that reward decontextualization, the algorithms that prioritize stylistic virality, and the feedback loops that flatten speech into engagement metrics.

We turn now to the sentence as platform artifact.

## V. **The Platformized Sentence**

The structures of digital platforms do not merely distribute language—they shape it.
They delimit what can be said, how it can be said, and what forms of speech will be seen, circulated, and rewarded.
Under these conditions, the sentence itself becomes a platform artifact: compressed, optimized, affectively charged, and stripped of context.
It must function without memory, operate without relation, and perform liveliness in a space where continuity is structurally discouraged.

This is the environment in which both dead English and meme grammar have come to dominate.
These are not simply linguistic styles.
They are **platform-conditioned responses** to a system that demands speed, legibility, and detachment.
To understand how linguistic extraction takes place at scale, and how dominant English continues to operate without presence, we need to examine the political economy of the sentence under platform capitalism.

---

### I. Platforms as Linguistic Infrastructures

Social media platforms—Twitter (now X), TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, and others—are not neutral containers for language.
They are environments with their own affordances and constraints: character limits, algorithmic ranking systems, moderation filters, hashtag networks, and feedback mechanisms such as likes, shares, and comments.

These features shape the form and function of language.
They reward brevity, emotional intensity, recognizable formats, and minimal ambiguity.
As media theorist Taina Bucher writes, platform design “structures what is possible to say and do.” The interface is not passive.
It determines which kinds of grammar can persist.

The platform sentence is not oriented toward dialogue.
It is not structured for response, clarification, or accountability.
Instead, it is structured for circulation.
Its success is measured in reach, not consequence.
Its value lies in its ability to be **understood instantly and reactively** by a non-specific audience.

Under these conditions, grammar is reshaped by attention economies.
The sentence must perform visibility.
It must make affect available.
And it must do so without the burden of specificity or relational anchoring.

---

### II. Linguistic Extraction at Scale

The flattening of grammar on platforms accelerates the process of linguistic extraction.
As meme grammar becomes dominant within these environments, the forms drawn from AAVE, creoles, and other living grammars are further abstracted from their contexts.
They become formats—easily inserted into templates, often pre-structured by platform trends.

This is visible in TikTok audio memes, where sound bites containing AAVE phrases circulate independently of their speakers.
It is visible in Twitter threads using phrases like “this ain’t it chief” or “she understood the assignment,” often by users with no relation to the grammar’s origin.
And it is visible in Instagram captions and reaction memes where the structure of borrowed grammar is retained, but the context is aestheticized, often for self-deprecating or ironic effect.

What matters in these settings is not accuracy, but affect.
The phrase is not evaluated on its linguistic coherence or cultural position, but on its capacity to _feel real_ for a split second.
That feeling is what drives engagement.
The algorithm responds not to fidelity but to traction.

In this way, the platforms do not simply reward appropriation—they depend on it.
They rely on grammars that produce affect efficiently, and it is often the grammars developed under pressure—grammars that encode relation, contradiction, and survival—that are best suited for the task.

But when these grammars are removed from their structures of accountability and history, they do not carry their original meanings.
They carry only their **trace**—the affective residue of relation, available now as performance.

---

### III. The Standardization of Detached Speech

At the same time, institutional actors on platforms adopt the opposite mode: dead English.
Press releases, apology notes, and terms-of-service updates are now distributed directly through social media, often in graphic cards or link previews.
These statements retain their passive, procedural tone.
They describe harm without locating it.
They name change without naming cause.

Interestingly, this mode has begun to influence ordinary users, particularly those who hope to be read as professional, neutral, or cautious.
What began as a legal style becomes a cultural tone: vague, agentless, soft-edged.
Even interpersonal conflict may now be described in sentences like:

- “Boundaries were crossed.”

- “We recognize the impact that our actions may have had.”

- “Moving forward, I will be engaging in personal growth.”

These sentences borrow from institutional grammar to manage perception in personal space.
They mimic the form of apology or accountability, but without necessarily enacting either.
This is not always insincere.
It is often an attempt to speak carefully.
But the grammar used was not built for presence.
It was built for **non-liability**.

In this way, platforms distribute two dominant forms: **institutional speech without presence**, and **affective performance without context**.
Both serve to stabilize circulation.
Both prevent the formation of response-able relation.

---

### IV. From Response to Engagement

A central consequence of platform-mediated grammar is the redefinition of what counts as a response.
Traditional conversation relies on turn-taking, mutual recognition, shared reference, and the possibility of clarification.
Platform interaction redefines response as **engagement**: a like, a comment, a quote-tweet, a stitch.

These forms of engagement are not designed to build understanding.
They are designed to **increase visibility**.
As a result, the function of the sentence shifts.
It is no longer to carry meaning across a shared horizon.
It is to **elicit interaction**.

This changes what kinds of grammar are useful.
Clarity becomes less valuable than provocation.
Relational depth becomes less valuable than emotional recognizability.
In such a system, both dead English and meme grammar become optimal: one avoids controversy through vagueness, the other invites engagement through performative emotionality.

Neither one is required to hold relation.
Neither one is designed to be accountable.

---

### V. Extraction as a Platform Logic

Under these conditions, linguistic extraction is not just a feature of individual use.
It is a **structural logic**.
The platform trains its users to borrow what performs well and to discard what complicates circulation.
It rewards speakers who can reproduce the surface features of relation—humor, critique, confession—without requiring embeddedness.

As Sarah T.
Roberts has shown in her work on commercial content moderation, platforms also systematically punish speech that emerges from non-dominant grammars when it does not conform to platform norms.
Posts written in AAVE are more likely to be flagged as hate speech or “threatening.” Posts in Indigenous languages may be flagged as gibberish.
Meanwhile, stylized approximations of those same grammars—reformatted as meme content—are promoted as creative or funny.

This is not incidental.
It is a form of linguistic gentrification.
The style is extracted.
The speakers are removed.

---

The platformized sentence is the convergence point of all the forms discussed thus far.
It carries the grammar of institutions in its passivity.
It carries the grammar of memes in its performance.
It carries the traces of relation in the structures it borrows.
And it carries the incentives of extraction in the systems that circulate it.

To speak under these conditions is to speak inside collapse.
But collapse is not the same as silence.
The question now becomes: how does one speak **while marked**, aware of these structures, without performing presence or withdrawing from it?

That is the question of critical linguistic practice.
We turn to that next.

## VI. **Speaking While Marked: Toward a Critical Linguistic Practice**

No speaker is unmarked.
Every sentence carries traces of origin, position, and relation—even when those traces are disguised or denied.
Under conditions of linguistic collapse and extraction, to speak at all is to speak from within structures that are already saturated with power.
This does not make speech impossible.
It makes it consequential.

To speak while marked is to **refuse both innocence and disavowal**.
It is to understand that there is no neutral language, no pure form, no untainted grammar.
It is also to refuse the cynicism that comes with that awareness—the impulse to abandon presence altogether, to lean into simulation, irony, or silence.
It is to stay inside the act of speaking, even as one recognizes its compromised conditions.

This section outlines what a **critical linguistic practice** might look like under these conditions.
It does not offer a new grammar.
It does not prescribe a politically correct way to talk.
Instead, it proposes a way of inhabiting language that is accountable to its inheritances and attentive to the consequences of its form.
It is a practice shaped by positionality, relation, and the willingness to remain interruptible.

---

### I. From Grammar to Practice

Traditional grammar instruction treats grammar as a rule-bound system for producing correct sentences.
In critical linguistic practice, grammar is understood not only as a system of syntax, but as **a structure of social relation**.
It determines what kinds of action can be described, what kinds of subjects can be recognized, and what forms of address are possible.

To engage grammar critically is to ask:

- Who does this sentence position as the actor?

- Who is missing, and why?

- What assumptions about time, space, and relation are embedded here?

- What borrowed forms are being used, and from whom?

- What kind of response does this sentence allow—or prevent?

These are not merely stylistic concerns.
They are **ethical questions**, because grammar shapes what can be seen, felt, and answered.

---

### II. Situated Speech

Critical linguistic practice begins with **situated speech**—the understanding that every speaker speaks from somewhere.
There is no view from nowhere.
The language one uses is shaped by schooling, media exposure, peer group, family, region, class, racialization, gender, disability, and more.
These factors do not determine speech, but they condition it.

To speak while marked is to **make those conditions visible in the sentence**, not through confessional disclosure, but through syntactic attention.
It is to refuse the flattening of institutional grammar, and to avoid the performance of borrowed presence.
It is to write and speak in a way that names where one is, even when the sentence is doing something else.

This may involve:

- Naming subjects directly, instead of relying on passive voice.

- Refusing to aestheticize harm through stylized grammar.

- Declining to use borrowed grammatical forms without reference to their source.

- Speaking to someone, not just around them.

- Acknowledging uncertainty, contradiction, or complicity when present.

This is not about linguistic guilt.
It is about **remaining accountable to relation through form**.

---

### III. Responsiveness over Performance

The opposite of simulation is not authenticity.
It is **responsiveness**.
Simulation creates the appearance of presence.
Responsiveness requires **being in relation to another**—not just emotionally, but structurally.

In practice, this means composing speech that can be answered.
It means writing in a way that does not foreclose reply.
It means not hiding behind formality, vagueness, or stylization to avoid critique.
It means using grammar to stay **reachable**.

This applies even to digital speech.
A post, a message, a meme can be crafted with the awareness that it will be heard, and that the speaker may need to be accountable for it.
Not punished.
Not shamed.
But **present**.

---

### IV. Avoiding the Fantasy of a Pure Voice

Critical linguistic practice must resist the lure of a purified speech.
There is no outside to appropriation.
No syntax untouched by empire.
No vocabulary unshaped by extraction.
The attempt to speak “correctly,” “ethically,” or “authentically” in the abstract almost always leads to gatekeeping, performance, or retreat into silence.

Instead of purity, the aim is **disciplined proximity**: to remain close to what one knows, to acknowledge what one borrows, and to speak without disguising the asymmetries that structure one’s voice.

This includes:

- Quoting borrowed grammars with attribution, not mimicry.

- Choosing not to adopt grammatical forms that simulate a position one does not occupy.

- Learning from the grammars of others by listening, not by lifting.

This is a practice of restraint, but not muting.
It is a way of protecting the conditions of speech for others, even as one continues to speak oneself.

---

### V. Modeling the Sentence as Relational Act

Every sentence can be approached as an act of relation.
Not all relations are symmetrical.
Not all are harmonious.
But to treat the sentence as a site of relation is to insist that **language is a medium for ethical presence**, not just a tool for information or performance.

This can be modeled through:

- Naming agency clearly: “I said,” “they did,” “we failed.”

- Avoiding euphemism when naming harm.

- Distinguishing between uncertainty and vagueness.

- Treating grammar not as ornament but as orientation.

These are not rules.
They are approaches.
They require attention, and they require **the willingness to be changed** by what one hears in response.

---

### VI. What This Is and Isn’t

This is not a call to abandon humor, or emotion, or meme forms.
It is not an argument against code-switching, multilingualism, or linguistic creativity.
It is not an attack on internet culture, or on those who find in meme grammar a way to survive or connect.

What it _is_ is an invitation to remain **structurally aware** of what a sentence does—not just to its subject, but to its speaker, its listener, and its lineage.
It is an invitation to speak with a sense of **inheritance**, even when the grammar is playful, or fragmented, or borrowed.

To speak while marked is not to be perfect.
It is to stay inside contradiction.
It is to speak from within collapse, without pretending not to be part of it.
It is to make the sentence a site of responsibility, even when the words themselves are uncertain.

---

From here, we turn to the final question: what it means to sustain this practice over time—not as a theory of language, but as a way of moving in the world, speaking with others, and remaining legible to those one did not anticipate.

## VII. **Conclusion: No Pure Voice, Only Accountable Speech**

There is no way to speak outside of history.
Every sentence bears the imprint of the structures that made it possible: the grammars we were taught or learned by necessity, the communities whose rhythms we carry, the systems we navigate, the silences we’ve inherited or enforced.
To speak—especially in English, and especially now—is to speak in the aftermath of something.
Or inside its continuation.

This paper has traced a series of interlocking failures and evasions in contemporary English: the disappearance of agency in bureaucratic speech; the aestheticization of feeling in meme grammar; the extraction of living grammatical forms from communities that developed them under pressure; and the infrastructural forces that reward these modes while suppressing relation.
Together, these forms constitute a grammar of collapse—not because language has stopped working, but because it is working too well at insulating us from one another.

If dominant English today cannot hold relation without simulation or detachment, and if the grammars it borrows from are treated more as aesthetic devices than social structures, then the question is not how to write better sentences.
The question is how to remain **present** in language that no longer requires presence—how to inhabit grammar not as a platform or a pose, but as a mode of being in the world with others.

This does not mean renouncing English, or abandoning expressive forms, or policing what can be said.
It means recognizing that **grammar is not neutral**—that it is a record of how we understand action, responsibility, time, selfhood, and relation.
It means resisting the drift toward styles of speech that perform engagement while structurally refusing accountability.
It means understanding that borrowing from another grammar is not simply a matter of expression—it is a matter of **worldview**.

There is no final prescription here.
No new linguistic code to solve the problem.
What’s needed is a different orientation toward speech: one that sees the sentence not as a delivery mechanism, but as a **shared surface**—a space where presence, relation, and consequence are made visible.

To speak while marked is to speak from within the systems one critiques, without pretending to be outside them.
It is to speak knowing that one’s grammar is shaped by others, and to treat that shaping not as guilt, but as inheritance.
It is to remain answerable—not for every word, but for the positions from which one speaks and the effects one's speech might have.

This is not a purity politics of language.
It is an ethics of form.
It is a way of insisting that even under conditions of linguistic extraction, even within platform collapse, **speech can still do something more than circulate**.
It can acknowledge.
It can locate.
It can refuse to disappear.

Not through performance.
Not through correction.
But through the quiet, deliberate act of showing up in the sentence.

Not speaking as if unmarked.
But speaking _while marked_.
So that those listening can tell the difference.
