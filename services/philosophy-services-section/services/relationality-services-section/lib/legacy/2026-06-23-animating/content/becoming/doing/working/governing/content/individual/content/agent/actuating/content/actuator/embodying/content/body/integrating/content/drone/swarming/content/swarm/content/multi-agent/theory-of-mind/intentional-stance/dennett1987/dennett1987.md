# Dennett 1987 — The Intentional Stance

Dennett, D.C. (1987). *The Intentional Stance*.
Cambridge, MA: MIT Press / A Bradford Book.

The collected presentation of Dennett's *intentional- stance* framework — treating systems as rational agents with beliefs and desires for predictive purposes, regardless of underlying mechanism.
The book consolidates work originally published in earlier papers (1971 onwards) and extends it.

Key claims:

- **Three predictive stances** (physical, design, intentional) are available for any system; which is most useful depends on the system and prediction context.
- **Intentional-system status** is a matter of predictive utility, not metaphysics; a system "has" beliefs and desires iff treating it as such usefully predicts its behavior.
- **The "really has" question is partly defused.** Whether thermostats, animals, or AI "really have" mental states is reframed as a question about predictive practice.
- **Higher-order intentional systems** (those whose intentional-stance prediction usefully includes their representations of others' mental states) form a hierarchy; humans are several orders deep.

The framework has had wide influence in philosophy of mind, cognitive science, and (more recently) AI discourse around mental-state attribution.
The pragmatic framing has aged well as AI systems exhibit behavior amenable to intentional-stance prediction without clear metaphysical answers about their inner nature.

Dennett's approach contrasts with both *eliminative materialism* (denying mental-state talk corresponds to anything real) and *strong realism* (insisting mental states are real entities).
The intentional-stance framework offers a third option: mental-state attribution is a *predictive practice* whose validity is a matter of utility rather than metaphysics.
