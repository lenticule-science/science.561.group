This tutorial walks through a single rubber-hose realism reading from start to finish — not as a procedure (procedures are in the how-to texts) but as a demonstration: a worked example with explicit commentary on the analytical moves being made.
The aim is to make the analytical practice concrete enough that a reader who has read the theory can begin to apply it.

## Choose your artifact

The tutorial works best if you perform it rather than just reading it.
Before reading further, find a specific artifact to read.
Choose one of the following types (they work best for a first reading):

1. A military recruitment advertisement (television or online video, 30–90 seconds)
2. A technology company's product announcement video (the launch video for a phone, a platform feature, or a device)
3. A police or security-services recruitment image (still image from an institutional social media account)
4. A pharmaceutical advertisement for a medication that treats stress, anxiety, or depression

Each of these types reliably contains [rubber-hose realism](../teaching/explanations/rubber-hose-realism.md) because each represents conditions that produce suffering — military service, surveillance capitalism's behavioral extraction, policing, pharmaceutical dependency — through aesthetic choices that foreclose the suffering's recognition.

## The reading: a worked example

For this demonstration, I am reading a hypothetical but entirely typical military recruitment video — the kind that runs across multiple branches of service and has been produced in consistent genre across several decades.
I will mark analytical moves as I make them.

---

**[Initial observation]** The video opens on a young person in civilian clothes, looking out of a window at a grey urban street.
Their expression is purposeful but constrained.
The voiceover begins: *"You know there's more."*

**[Move 1: Identify the structural condition being represented]** The video is representing the civilian young person's life as insufficient — implying precarity, purposelessness, or limitation.
This is a structural condition (youth precarity, limited economic opportunity, social disconnection) being aestheticized as personal experience.
The structural conditions are real — youth unemployment, the shrinking of social mobility, the erosion of institutional paths to purpose — but they are represented as personal rather than structural: "you know there's more" locates the insufficiency in the subject's inner knowledge rather than in the structural conditions producing the feeling.

**[Move 2: Note what is visible]** The civilian life is shown in grey, constrained, static.
The recruitment footage that follows shows: aircraft in dramatic motion, close-ups of precision equipment, uniformed bodies in purposeful collective formation, landscapes of scale and grandeur.
The technology is shown with aesthetic reverence — slow-motion close-up of mechanical precision.
The bodies of recruits are shown in peak physical performance, in teamwork, in the posture of professional competence.

**[Move 3: Note what is foreclosed]** What is not in the video: any body that is injured, killed, or subject to the force the military exercises.
Any representation of the communities where the military operates.
Any representation of what military service requires of recruits' psychological and somatic life over time.
Any structural account of why the civilian life shown at the video's opening is grey and constrained (the structural conditions of youth precarity are the video's opening premise, but their structural cause is not represented — they are simply the condition from which the military offers escape).

**[Move 4: Identify the specific aesthetic foreclosure operations]** The aircraft in dramatic motion is shown with the rubber-hose aesthetic: the force is visible (the aircraft is powerful, fast, vast) but the consequence is not (there are no persons in the aircraft's operational environment, no representation of what the aircraft does).
The foreclosure is performed through framing — the shot does not include what the aircraft's power affects.
The precision equipment is aestheticized through slow-motion close-up — the lens reveals the mechanical beauty rather than the mechanical function.
The landscapes are grand and uninhabited — no communities appear in the operational environment.

**[Move 5: Identify the grammar]** The video's grammar: Crisis (the grey civilian life, the "you know there's more"), Pure community (the uniformed corps, the collective formation, the shared purpose), Destiny (implicit — the voiceover implies that joining is the expression of who the viewer already is and is meant to be), Inevitability (the video closes with the recruiter's information as if the decision has already been made).
The corrupt-enemy move is not explicit in this type of video but is present in the training sequences: the adversarial formations, the simulated conflict, the readiness for an opposition that is gestured at but not characterized.

**[Move 6: Identify the targeted subject]** The video's opening civilian — grey, constrained, purposeful but blocked — is constructed to resonate with a specific target: the young person experiencing the structural conditions of youth precarity whose genre-self-image already includes the warrior-self or the protector-self.
The video is recruiting into a specific [ontogenric](../teaching/explanations/ontogenring.md) genre: the professional-warrior-self whose participation in collective force is the expression of individual identity and potential.

---

## What the reading establishes

The reading establishes: the video represents the exercise of state military force through aesthetic choices that foreclose the recognition of what that force does to persons, while embedding the six-move fascist grammar structure and recruiting into a specific subject-formation genre.

What the reading does not establish: whether the person who made the video intended these effects, whether military service is bad, what political conclusions should be drawn.
The reading is analytical.
The further moves are for the reader.

## Your turn

Perform the same six moves on the artifact you chose before reading this tutorial.
Use the moves as a framework, not as a script — the specific artifact will require specific analytical observation.
What is your artifact representing?
What is visible?
What is foreclosed?
How is the foreclosure aesthetically performed?
What grammar is running?
What subject is being recruited?

Write out the analysis in full before consulting the how-to procedures.
Then compare what you produced with the procedure's structure and identify what you captured and what you missed.
The first reading is for getting your analytical instincts oriented; the procedure is for systematic completeness.
