A postliberal grammar analysis reads a political speech, text, campaign, or cultural artifact for the grammatical structure of its political claims — identifying which of the three grammars ([fascist](../teaching/explanations/fascist-grammar.md), liberal, [anarchist](../teaching/explanations/anarchist-grammar.md)) are operating, how they interact, and what the interaction produces.
This is a close-reading procedure applied to political language rather than literary language, with specific analytical outputs.

## What you can analyze

The procedure works on any bounded political speech act or text: a speech, a political advertisement, a campaign's social media strategy, a policy document's framing sections, a movement's public communications, a journalistic commentary, or any other artifact in which political claims are structured.
The procedure also works on cultural artifacts that embed political grammar — films, novels, songs, games — but the application requires establishing the political-claim structure within the aesthetic object first.

It does not work well on pure policy documents (which are technical rather than grammatical in the relevant sense), on images without text (which require the separate continuum-analysis procedure), or on documents so long that they contain multiple distinct grammatical structures (which should be divided into sections for separate analysis).

## Step 1: Read for crisis

The first move of [fascist grammar](../teaching/explanations/fascist-grammar.md), and the opening move of liberal grammar's response, is always crisis.
Read the artifact for its crisis-claim:

- Is a crisis named?
  What is it?
- What is the crisis's character: is it acute (immediate and requiring emergency response) or chronic (ongoing and requiring sustained attention)?
- What is the crisis's locus: external (a threat from outside the community) or internal (a corruption within the community)?
- What response does the crisis authorize?
  What does the crisis-claim make possible that would not otherwise be possible?

Liberal grammar will respond to the crisis-claim with gradualism, proceduralism, and expert management.
Fascist grammar will use the crisis to suspend normal legitimacy requirements.
Anarchist grammar will locate the crisis in the structures that liberal and fascist grammar both presuppose.

Note the crisis's specific content — what is named as the problem — and also its grammatical function, which is separable from its content.

## Step 2: Read for community

The second move is community definition — who are "we," and what is the community's character?

- Is a community named or implied?
- What are the community's defining characteristics?
  (National, class, racial, religious, professional, political)
- Is the community presented as unified and coherent, or as internally diverse and in tension?
- Is the community presented as pure, contaminated, besieged, or triumphant?
- Who is excluded from the community?
  How is the exclusion performed?

Liberal grammar will appeal to pluralism and to overlapping communities without a singular "we."
Fascist grammar will define a pure community whose unity is both its essence and its vulnerability.
Anarchist grammar will resist the community-as-identity move and emphasize the community as a space of relation rather than a fixed group.

The community-definition move is where [genring](../teaching/explanations/genring.md) often operates: the community is defined partly by the genre figures it contains (the authentic member) and partly by the genre figures of its enemies.

## Step 3: Read for the enemy

Fascist grammar requires an enemy; liberal grammar requires an opposition; anarchist grammar tends to substitute structures for enemies.
Read the artifact for its opponent-characterization:

- Is an opponent or enemy identified?
- Is the opponent's opposition characterized as a disagreement (different interests, different values) or as a corruption (the opponent is bad in their essence, not merely in their positions)?
- Is the opponent represented as having achieved parasitic power over the community?
- Does the representation of the opponent use genre figures?

The distinction between disagreement and corruption is the key diagnostic for fascist grammar's enemy-move.
Liberal grammar can have sharp opponents (the oligarchy, the corrupt administration) but tends to characterize the opposition as a structural or interest-based disagreement rather than an essential corruption.
Fascist grammar's corrupt-enemy cannot be reasoned with or reformed; they must be defeated and removed.

## Step 4: Read for suffering, destiny, and inevitability

These three moves are often compressed or embedded in the first three moves, but they should be identified separately:

**Suffering as sacrifice:** does the artifact represent the community's suffering as meaningful — as sacrifice that validates the struggle, purifies the community, or proves their devotion?
Or is suffering represented as waste — as unnecessary harm that the right policy would prevent?

**Destiny:** does the artifact invoke a historical destiny for the community — a special role, a historical mission, a trajectory that the crisis imperils and the struggle is meant to fulfill?

**Inevitability:** does the artifact represent the struggle's outcome as inevitable, as the natural expression of who the community already is?
Does it foreclose deliberation by positioning the political act as the expression of identity rather than a choice?

The presence of all three moves, combined with the first three, confirms full fascist grammar.
Liberal grammar uses none of these three; anarchist grammar sometimes uses destiny and inevitability in an inverted form (the logic of history tends toward liberation; the community's authentic impulse is toward freedom).

## Step 5: Map the grammatical interactions

Having identified which moves are present from each grammar, map how they interact:

- If fascist grammar moves and liberal grammar counter-moves are both present in the artifact or in the political discourse the artifact is part of, identify how the counter-moves are structured and whether they are reinforcing or attenuating the fascist grammar's energy
- If anarchist grammar appears, identify whether it is operating as a coherent subject-position (which the apparatus can metabolize) or as a practice-pointing beyond the grammar (which may not be fully metabolizable)
- If [soft surrealism](../teaching/explanations/soft-surrealism.md) is present — if the artifact deploys grammatical moves in full awareness of their own grammar-ness — identify where the self-awareness appears and what it does to the grammar's political effectivity

## Step 6: Write the analysis

A complete grammar analysis states:

1. The artifact's grammar: which moves are present from which grammars
2. The specific evidence for each move: quoted language, specific claims, representational choices
3. The grammatical interactions: how the grammars are operating in relation to each other
4. The political-affective function: what the grammar structure produces in its audience — what subject-formation, what affective activation, what political identification

The analysis should be specific to the artifact, should quote or reference specific evidence for each move, and should distinguish between the grammar (the structural claim) and the content (the specific ideological vocabulary).
A grammar analysis that can only be written about one artifact — that does not identify a portable structural pattern — has not reached the grammatical level; it is still a content analysis.

Keep the analysis to what the grammar analysis can establish.
The grammar analysis does not determine whether the artifact's underlying claims are true; it does not determine the practitioner's intentions; it does not determine what political conclusions the reader should draw.
It identifies the structural operation of the political claims.
The further conclusions require further analysis.
