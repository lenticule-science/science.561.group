Audience: players and GMs who want to understand how [dice mechanics](../terms/dice-mechanic.md) translate fictional uncertainty into concrete outcomes.

Learning goal: explain how different resolution systems work, what [modifiers](../terms/modifier.md) represent, and how the choice of dice mechanic shapes the feel of play.

Prerequisites: you should understand [session structure](./session-structure.md) and the [scene-choice-consequence loop](../../curricula/scenes-and-choices.md), because dice resolution is the mechanical layer that connects choices to consequences.

## Starting from a concrete example

An adventurer in [Brave Old World](../../../../texts/brave-old-world.md) wants to climb a crumbling wall to reach a window on the second floor.
The narrator says this is risky — the wall is unstable, and falling would hurt.
The adventurer rolls two six-sided dice and adds the results: 5 + 3 = 8.
In Brave Old World, 7–9 means success with a cost.
The narrator says: "You reach the window, but a section of wall collapses behind you.
You can't go back the way you came."

This moment captures the full resolution sequence: a fictional action with uncertain outcome, a mechanical procedure (roll 2d6, check the result band), and a narrated consequence that changes the fiction.
The dice did not decide *what happened* — the narrator did.
The dice decided *how well it went*.

## How resolution mechanics work

All TTRPG resolution systems share a common function: they translate a fictional intention with uncertain outcome into one of a few possible results, typically along a spectrum from full success to full failure.
The specific implementation varies, but three patterns cover most systems.

**Roll against a target number.** The player rolls dice and adds a [modifier](../terms/modifier.md) representing their [character](../../terms/character.md)'s relevant ability.
If the total meets or exceeds a target number (set by the GM based on difficulty), the action succeeds.
This is the most common pattern — Dungeons & Dragons, Pathfinder, and many other systems use it.
The target number encodes the GM's judgment about how hard the task is; the modifier encodes how capable the character is.

**Roll and interpret result bands.** The player rolls dice and the total falls into one of several predefined bands, each with a different narrative meaning.
[Brave Old World](../../../../texts/brave-old-world.md) uses this: 10+ is clean success, 7–9 is success with a cost, 6 or less is failure with consequences.
Powered by the Apocalypse games use the same bands.
This pattern puts less emphasis on difficulty calibration and more emphasis on the *quality* of the outcome — not just pass/fail, but how the fiction changes.

**Opposed rolls.** Both the acting player and the opposing force (another player, or the GM for a non-player character) roll dice.
The higher result wins, sometimes with a margin that determines how decisively.
This pattern is common in competitive situations — combat, contests, social conflicts — where both sides have agency.

## What modifiers represent

A modifier is a number added to (or subtracted from) a die roll to represent factors that make success more or less likely.
In [Brave Old World](../../../../texts/brave-old-world.md), a character with *investigation +1* adds 1 to their roll when investigating — the modifier represents the character's skill.
A character with *swimming −1* subtracts 1 when swimming — the modifier represents a weakness.

Modifiers come from a few sources across different systems:

- **Character ability** (attributes, skills, training)
- **Situational factors** (darkness, time pressure, surprise, advantageous position)
- **Equipment** (tools, weapons, magical aids)
- **Narrative circumstances** (an NPC who owes the character a favor, a plan set beforehand)

Modifiers are the bridge between the fiction and the mechanics.
They translate "my character is good at this" or "the situation is favorable" into a concrete mechanical advantage.
When a [game master](../../terms/game-master.md) assigns modifiers, they are making a judgment about the fictional circumstances — and that judgment is subject to the group's social negotiation, especially in games like Brave Old World where skills are set by [consensus](../../../../texts/brave-old-world.md).

## Worked example: tracing a full resolution in Brave Old World

The adventurer Mira (from [Character Creation](../../curricula/character-creation.md)) wants to decipher inscriptions on a ruin wall.
She has *investigation +1*.

1. **Fictional action**: "I examine the inscriptions carefully, comparing them to the maps I've drawn of similar markings."
2. **GM judgment**: The narrator decides this is risky — the inscriptions might be misleading, and time spent here means the bandits get closer.
3. **Roll**: 2d6 + 1 (investigation).
  Mira's player rolls a 4 and a 5: total 9 + 1 = 10.
4. **Result band**: 10+ is clean success.
5. **Narrated consequence**: "The inscriptions are a map.
  They show a passage through the mountains that matches nothing on your charts.
  You sketch it quickly — it's clear enough to follow."

If Mira had rolled a 7 (total 8 with modifier): success with a cost.
Maybe she deciphers the map but the bandits arrive before she finishes, forcing the party to flee with incomplete information.
If she had rolled a 3 (total 4 with modifier): failure.
Maybe the inscriptions are damaged beyond reading, or she misinterprets them and the party follows a path into danger.

Each result changes the fiction differently, and the narrator's job is to make each consequence *worth playing out* — not just "you fail" but "you fail *and* something happens that creates a new situation."

## The feel of different dice systems

The choice of dice shapes the *texture* of play in ways that go beyond probability.

Rolling a single d20 (twenty-sided die) produces a flat probability distribution: every number from 1 to 20 is equally likely.
This means extreme results happen often — heroes fail easy tasks 5% of the time and succeed at hard ones 5% of the time.
The experience is *swingy*.

Rolling 2d6 (as in Brave Old World) produces a bell curve centered on 7.
Results near 7 are common; extremes (2 or 12) are rare.
This means skilled characters fail less often, and the "success with a cost" band (7–9) is the most common result.
The experience emphasizes *complications over clean outcomes*.

Rolling dice pools (multiple dice, counting successes) produces results where competence shows as consistency — a character with more dice rolls more reliably, not just higher.
The experience emphasizes *reliability as a form of skill*.

None of these is better.
They produce different emotional textures, and choosing a [dice mechanic](../terms/dice-mechanic.md) is a design decision about what kind of stories the game wants to tell.

## Exercises

1. An adventurer in Brave Old World has *covert +2* and attempts to sneak past sleeping guards.
  Roll 2d6 (or imagine the result).
  For each of the three result bands (10+, 7–9, 6−), write one sentence describing what happens.
  Make each consequence change the fiction in a way that creates a new scene.

2. Compare a flat d20 roll to a 2d6 bell curve.
  If a task requires a total of 8 or higher, what is the approximate probability of success on each?
  What does this difference mean for how *reliable* skilled characters feel in play?
