Legal pluralism is the field that studies the *coexistence of multiple legal orders within a single social field*.
The field developed across roughly seventy years from colonial-administration anthropology through the 1970s–1980s "strong pluralism" debates to contemporary global legal pluralism.
Throughout, its central analytical commitment has remained: state law is one legal order among several, not the exclusive or definitive form of law in any actual social setting.

## Colonial-anthropology origins

The early-twentieth-century colonial administration of plural societies — British India, Dutch Indonesia, French and British Africa — generated a substantial practical and scholarly literature on how to administer territories where customary, religious, and colonial-imposed legal systems operated simultaneously.
Anthropologists working in these settings (Malinowski, Schapera, Gluckman, and others through the mid-century) documented indigenous legal orders that operated independently of, partially in conflict with, and partially in accommodation to colonial law.

Leopold Pospisil's [pospisil-1971] *Anthropology of Law* synthesized this earlier work and proposed that *every functioning subgroup of a society has its own legal system* — families, clans, lineages, and communities each generate normative orders with the four attributes Pospisil claimed essential to law: *authority*, *intention of universal application*, *obligatio* (binding character), and *sanction*.
This was an early statement of what would later be called *strong* legal pluralism: pluralism not as a description of post-colonial juridical layering but as a general property of social organization.

## Moore: the semi-autonomous social field

Sally Falk Moore [moore-1973] supplied a foundational concept that legal pluralism scholars have used ever since: the *semi-autonomous social field*.
A social field is semi-autonomous when it can *generate rules and induce or coerce compliance* but is also *vulnerable to rules and decisions emanating from the larger world by which it is surrounded*.
The dressmakers' guild, the dock workers' association, the ethnic neighborhood, the corporation — each is a semi-autonomous social field, generating internal normative order while also being penetrated by state law and other external orders.

The concept reorients the analyst from the question "what legal systems exist in this jurisdiction?" to "what semi-autonomous social fields are present, what internal rules do they generate, and how do they interact with state law and with each other?"
State law becomes one factor among many shaping behavior in a given social field, not the master frame within which all other normativity operates.

## Galanter: justice in many rooms

Marc Galanter [galanter-1981] supplied an influential parallel formulation from the law-and-society tradition. *Justice in Many Rooms* argued that the official courts of the state are only one site — one "room" — among many in which disputes are processed and norms are generated.
Workplaces, neighborhoods, families, trade associations, religious bodies, and professional disciplines all process disputes and generate enforceable norms; the state's courts handle a small fraction of the total.
Galanter's framing — *legal centralism* as the (mistaken) view that state courts are the central site of legal life, against the empirical reality of *indigenous law* and *private ordering* throughout social life — became one of the field's standard reference points alongside Moore's semi-autonomous social field and Griffiths's weak/strong distinction.

## Griffiths: weak vs. strong legal pluralism

John Griffiths [griffiths-1986] supplied the field's defining conceptual distinction. *Weak legal pluralism* is the recognition by state law that some matters are governed by other rule-systems (customary, religious, ethnic) within carve-outs that the state itself sets and enforces — colonial indirect rule, contemporary recognition of indigenous customary law within the constitutional framework. *Strong legal pluralism* is the empirical claim that *legal orders other than state law exist and operate independently of state recognition*, regardless of whether the state acknowledges them.

Griffiths argued that the weak conception is jurisprudentially derivative — it presupposes the state's ultimate authority to grant or deny recognition — while the strong conception is the genuinely sociological one.
His positive program: legal pluralism is the *empirical fact* that in any social arena, multiple normative orders operate simultaneously, with the state's law being one such order with no special analytical priority.

## Merry: the social-scientific synthesis

Sally Engle Merry's [merry-1988] field-defining review article surveyed the legal-pluralism literature and proposed a synthesis around three key claims: (1) legal pluralism is empirically pervasive, not exceptional; (2) it has both colonial and "new" (non-colonial) varieties — the new varieties including the legal orders of corporations, professional associations, religious communities, and civil-society organizations within Western states; (3) the analytical task is to study the *interactions* among coexisting legal orders, not just to enumerate them.
Her later work extended this into the analysis of how human-rights norms interact with local legal orders, particularly in colonized and post-colonial settings.

## Tamanaha: the conceptual critique

Brian Tamanaha [tamanaha-1993] mounted the most sustained critique of strong legal pluralism, arguing that the social-scientific concept is *folly* because it cannot specify, in a non-arbitrary way, which normative orders count as *legal* and which as merely *normative*.
If every rule-generating social field is "law," then the concept of law dissolves into the concept of normativity, and the analytical category does no work.
His proposed alternative — a *non-essentialist* concept of law that treats "law" as whatever a given social field calls law in a particular context — preserved much of the legal-pluralism program while abandoning the universalist claim that any rule-generating order is *ipso facto* law.

Tamanaha's later survey [tamanaha-2008] traces the field from its origins to its early-twenty-first-century state, including the global-legal-pluralism turn.

## Benda-Beckmann: the durable defense

Franz von Benda-Beckmann's [benda-beckmann-2002] influential reply argued that Tamanaha's critique conflates two questions: (1) what *should* the analytical concept of law be, and (2) what *is* the empirical situation in which multiple normative orders interact.
He defended the strong-pluralism project as ongoing empirical research rather than conceptual taxonomy, noting that the field's most productive work has been ethnographic studies of how multiple orders interact in specific settings — irrigation disputes in Nepal, land tenure in Indonesia, family law in West Africa — regardless of how the analyst answers Tamanaha's definitional question.

## Santos and Berman: global legal pluralism

Boaventura de Sousa Santos [santos-1987, santos-2002] generalized the framework to global scale, arguing that contemporary law is constituted through the interaction of multiple *interlegalities* — local, national, transnational, supra-state — that operate at different scales and through different mechanisms.
His metaphor of *interlegality* (analogous to interdisciplinarity) captures the way actual legal life crosses scale boundaries continuously.

Paul Schiff Berman [berman-2007] developed the *global legal pluralism* program as a normative-jurisprudential project: arguing that the proliferation of overlapping international, transnational, and sub-state legal orders is not a pathology to be resolved by harmonization but a feature of contemporary law that should be embraced through a *cosmopolitan pluralist* jurisprudence — one that uses procedural mechanisms (margin of appreciation, choice-of-law, recognition of foreign judgments) to manage interaction without requiring substantive unification.

## Key conceptual distinctions

Three distinctions structure the field:

1. **Weak vs. strong pluralism.** Recognition-by-state vs. empirical-coexistence-regardless-of-recognition.
2. **Classical vs. new pluralism.** Colonial layering of customary/religious/colonial law vs. coexisting normative orders within ostensibly unified Western states.
3. **State-centric vs. global pluralism.** Pluralism within a single state's territory vs. pluralism across overlapping international, transnational, and sub-state orders.

## What the tradition supplies

Three distinctive contributions:

1. **Empirical vocabulary for normative-order coexistence.** The tradition's enormous ethnographic record documents in detail how coexisting normative orders actually interact — through accommodation, conflict, forum-shopping, hybridization, mutual influence, and resistance.

2. **Critique of legal centralism.** The view that state law is *the* law — a presupposition built into much jurisprudence and political theory — is empirically false and analytically misleading.
  Other normative orders are not deviations from state law's monopoly; they are part of the basic structure of any complex social field.

3. **The semi-autonomous social field unit.** Moore's concept gives a workable unit of analysis for any setting in which one wants to ask: who generates rules here, who induces compliance, and how does this rule-generating capacity interact with other such capacities in adjacent fields?
