## Formal definition

A **Promise** is a triple $\Pi = (S, H, \Phi)$:

$$\Pi = (S : \mathrm{Person},\; H : \mathrm{Person},\; \Phi : \mathrm{FutureActSpecification})$$

where:
- $S$ is the **speaker** (promisor) — the party who makes the commitment; $S$ must be a [Person](person.md) in the normative sense: recognized as a node capable of incurring obligations; a machine, a crowd, or an unrecognized entity cannot promise in the normative sense (though analogues exist)
- $H$ is the **hearer** (promisee) — the party to whom the commitment is made; $H$'s role is not to accept or reject (that is contract formation) but to be the correlative party whose claim is generated by $S$'s commitment; the promisee does not need to do anything for the promise to take effect
- $\Phi$ is the **committed proposition** — a specification of what $S$ commits to do or bring about; $\Phi$ must be: *future* (the act has not yet occurred), *possible* (it is within $S$'s power to perform), and *specific enough to identify discharge* (both parties can tell when $\Phi$ has been performed)

**Four invariants.** $\Pi$ is a promise iff it satisfies:

1. **Performativity**: the utterance of the promise does not describe a pre-existing commitment — it *creates* one. "I will pick you up at seven" (prediction; not a promise) differs from "I promise to pick you up at seven" (promise; creates obligation). The performative marker may be explicit (*I promise*) or contextually established. The commitment exists because the speech act occurred; there is no prior fact being reported.

2. **Sincerity**: $S$ intends to perform $\Phi$ at the time of promising. A promise made without this intention is **insincere** — a lie about one's future actions — not a failed promise but a deceptive act. Insincerity violates the preparatory conditions of the speech act without voiding the normative consequence: an insincere promise still generates H's claim against S (Austin: "the act is done, even if done insincerely").

3. **Unilaterality**: only $S$ binds. $H$ acquires a claim $\mathbf{C}(H, \Phi, S)$ without incurring a corresponding duty. If $H$ also binds, the structure is no longer a promise but a Contract. A unilateral promise is the building block; a contract is two promises made simultaneously under formation conditions $V$.

4. **Privity of commitment**: the promise generates a duty in $S$ toward $H$ specifically. $S$ does not thereby acquire duties toward other persons. Third parties may benefit from $S$'s performance of $\Phi$ without being the correlative party of the duty — they receive the *effects* but not the *claim*. (This maps to the contract privity rule: the promisee has the claim; third-party beneficiaries have at most a derivative interest.)

## The speech act structure

J.L. Austin (*How To Do Things With Words*, 1955, published 1962) distinguished **constatives** (statements that describe facts, assessable as true or false) from **performatives** (utterances that do something in the world by being uttered). "I promise" is the canonical performative: saying it *is* making the promise, not describing an already-existing promise.

Austin's conditions for a valid performative (felicity conditions):
1. **Conventional procedure**: there must be a recognized social or legal procedure for this type of act; the participants must know and follow it
2. **Appropriate persons**: the speaker must be the appropriate person to perform this act in these circumstances
3. **Correct execution**: the procedure must be executed correctly and completely
4. **Required thoughts and feelings**: the participants must have the appropriate mental states (sincerity)
5. **Subsequent conduct**: the participants must behave consistently with the speech act after its performance

When felicity conditions fail, the speech act is **infelicitous** (misfires or abuses) but may still generate normative consequences. A promise under duress is infelicitous (the conditions are wrong) but may still be legally or morally binding.

Searle (*Speech Acts*, 1969) systematized Austin into a taxonomy of illocutionary acts. Promises belong to the **commissive** class: speech acts that commit the speaker to some future course of action. The defining feature of commissives: they adjust the *normative world* to the *speaker's words* (direction of fit: world-to-word, with speaker committed to ensuring fit). Contrast:
- Assertives (telling, describing): words adjust to fit the world; the speaker claims the world is a certain way
- Directives (ordering, requesting): speaker tries to make the hearer do something
- Declarations (pronouncing, appointing): utterance constitutes the change it describes (in the institutional context)
- Expressives (thanking, apologizing): speaker expresses psychological state

Commissives and declarations are closest to the contract/institution nexus: declarations constitute institutional facts; commissives create normative bonds. A promise is a commissive; a contract is a pair of commissives with a formation act.

## Credible commitment: Schelling and game theory

Thomas Schelling (*The Strategy of Conflict*, 1960) identified **credibility** as the central problem of strategic promises. A promise is credible when the promisee can rely on the promisor actually performing — not merely because the promisor said they would, but because the promisor's incentive structure at the time of required performance still aligns with performance.

The credibility problem: if $S$ promises to perform $\Phi$ at time $t' > t$, what ensures $S$ will actually perform at $t'$? At $t'$, $S$'s interests may have changed. The promise is *cheap talk* if $S$ can break it without cost.

**Commitment devices**: mechanisms that make the promise credible by eliminating $S$'s ability to defect:
- **Burning bridges**: $S$ takes an action that makes non-performance impossible or prohibitively costly (*Hernán Cortés* ordering his ships burned — his men had no retreat)
- **Hostages**: $S$ places something valuable in $H$'s hands, forfeited if $\Phi$ is not performed
- **Reputation**: $S$'s promise is credible because breaking it would damage $S$'s reputation for future interactions — the promise is backed by iterated game equilibria
- **Institutional enforcement**: the promise is embedded in a contract, enforceable by a third party (the court); $S$'s cost of breaking is the legal remedy

The game-theoretic formulation: a promise is a **conditional commitment** — $S$ commits to take action $a$ at $t'$ regardless of $S$'s private information or payoff at $t'$. A promise credibly changes the game tree: $H$ can take actions at $t$ (counting on $\Phi$) that $H$ would not take without the promise. The promise re-coordinates the game.

**Precommitment equilibria**: in extensive-form games, a player who can credibly commit to a future action changes the subgame perfect equilibrium. Schelling's insight: the ability to commit (to promise credibly) is a form of power — it is better to be unable to back down than to be able to. The threat that is incredible is worthless; the threat that is credible (backed by commitment) changes the game.

## Futures and promises in concurrent programming

In concurrent and distributed computing, a **future** (or **promise**) is a proxy for a value not yet computed — a first-class object representing the eventual result of an asynchronous computation.

The terminology comes from Baker and Hewitt (1977) and Liskov and Shrira (1988), but the concepts were unified and formalized in various ways:

A **future** $f$ is a placeholder for the eventual value $v$ of a computation $C$. The computation $C$ runs asynchronously; $f$ is available immediately. When $C$ completes, $f$ **resolves** to $v$. Before resolution, $f$ is in the **pending** state; after resolution, $f$ is either **fulfilled** (with value $v$) or **rejected** (with error $e$).

The normative reading of a future:
- Creating a future $f$ for computation $C$ is the **promise**: $S$ (the computation scheduler) commits to $H$ (the awaiting computation) that $f$ will eventually resolve
- $H$ can proceed with other computation; when $H$ needs $v$, $H$ awaits $f$ — suspending until $f$ resolves
- If $C$ fails, $f$ is rejected — breach with error propagation
- If $C$ succeeds, $f$ fulfills — discharge with value delivery

**JavaScript Promises** and async/await (ECMAScript 2015+): a `Promise` is an object that may have three states:
```
pending → fulfilled (resolve(value))
pending → rejected (reject(reason))
```

The `then(onFulfill, onReject)` method allows chaining: "when this promise settles, do the next thing." The `async`/`await` syntax desugars into promise chains — each `await` is a point where the caller defers until the promise resolves, without blocking the thread.

**Promise pipelining** (Liskov-Shrira, 1988; Mark Miller, E language): instead of waiting for $f$ to resolve before passing it to the next computation, pipeline the future — pass $f$ directly. The computation consumes $f$ speculatively; when $f$ resolves, the pipeline delivers the result. This eliminates the round-trip latency of "get result, then call next step" — the calls happen in parallel, the results propagate forward.

**The monad structure**: `Promise` forms a monad. The unit $\eta : A \to \mathrm{Promise}(A)$ wraps a value in an already-resolved promise. The bind $(\gg\!\!=) : \mathrm{Promise}(A) \to (A \to \mathrm{Promise}(B)) \to \mathrm{Promise}(B)$ chains asynchronous computations. The monad laws ensure that promise chaining is associative and that wrapping/unwrapping pure values does not add computational overhead. In this sense, the `Promise` monad is the **asynchronous computation type** — every async computation is a promise monad.

**Promises and the IO monad** (Haskell): the Haskell `IO` monad is a promise of a different kind — not a promise about when a value will be available, but a promise that the *computation structure* is there, waiting to be run. `IO a` is not an `a`; it is a description of how to obtain an `a` through effects. The `IO` monad defers execution; the promise is that when the program runs, the effects will be executed in sequence. The `IO` monad is the foundational promise underlying all Haskell programs: "this computation will, when run, produce this value."

## Nuclear reading

We work in the fiber Heyting algebra $H_t$ at a fixed history $t \in T$, with saturation nucleus $\sigma_t$ and transfer nucleus $\Delta_t$. Both are extensive, idempotent (Idempotence), meet-preserving (Meet Preservation), and commuting (Commutation).

**Definition (Active promise).** A promise $\Pi = (S, H, \Phi)$ at history $t$ is *potential* iff the committed proposition $\Phi \in H_t$ satisfies:
$$\sigma_t(\Phi) = \Phi \quad \text{and} \quad \Delta_t(\Phi) \neq \Phi$$
that is, $\Phi \in \mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_t) \setminus \mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t)$. This is the exact nuclear signature: **meaning-settled, transfer-open**. The promisor's declaration has been recognized and recorded in the past-accumulated structure of $H_t$ (hence $\sigma_t$-fixed), but the performance has not yet been committed to every forward extension (hence not $\Delta_t$-fixed).

**Remark (Why $\sigma_t$-fixedness models recognition).** The saturation nucleus $\sigma_t$ is the largest element of $H_t$ with the same restriction profile as its argument to all proper sub-histories $t_0 < t$. Saying $\sigma_t(\Phi) = \Phi$ means: $\Phi$'s status is fully determined by the accumulated record; no more past information would change it. The promise-forming speech act advances history to a new $t$ at which this condition holds.

**Remark (Acts do not change $\sigma_t$).** $\sigma_t$ is fixed by the site topology and the restriction maps already in place at $t$. The speech act of promising does not modify $\sigma_t$ at $t$; it advances the history to $t' = s_{\Pi} \star t$ where the new $\sigma_{t'}$ recognizes $\Phi$ as fixed. The distinction between $\sigma_t$ and $\sigma_{t'}$ is fundamental: the promisor's act changes the history, not the nucleus at the old history.

**Definition (Promise gap).** For an active promise at $t$, the *promise gap* is the interval $[\Phi, \Delta_t(\Phi)]$ in $H_t$. By extensiveness of $\Delta_t$, $\Delta_t(\Phi) \geq \Phi$; since $\Phi \notin \mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t)$, this gap is nonempty (i.e., $\Delta_t(\Phi) > \Phi$ in $H_t$). The gap measures how much additional settling is required to discharge the promise.

**Definition (Promise discharge).** The promise $\Pi$ is *discharged* at history $t'$ iff $\Phi \in H^*_{t'} = \mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_{t'}) \cap \mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_{t'})$ — the committed proposition is doubly stable at $t'$: meaning-settled and present in every forward extension from $t'$.

**Proposition (Conjunction of promises is a promise).** If $\Phi, \Psi \in \mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_t) \setminus \mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t)$ are two potential promises at $t$, then $\Phi \wedge \Psi \in \mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_t)$.

*Proof.* By Meet Preservation, $\sigma_t(\Phi \wedge \Psi) = \sigma_t(\Phi) \wedge \sigma_t(\Psi) = \Phi \wedge \Psi$, since $\sigma_t(\Phi) = \Phi$ and $\sigma_t(\Psi) = \Psi$. Therefore $\Phi \wedge \Psi \in \mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_t)$. $\square$

**Remark.** The conjunction $\Phi \wedge \Psi$ need not be in $\mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t)$: whether $\Delta_t(\Phi \wedge \Psi) = \Phi \wedge \Psi$ depends on both $\Phi$ and $\Psi$ individually. By Meet Preservation applied to $\Delta_t$: $\Delta_t(\Phi \wedge \Psi) = \Delta_t(\Phi) \wedge \Delta_t(\Psi)$. Since $\Phi \notin \mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t)$, we have $\Delta_t(\Phi) > \Phi$, so $\Delta_t(\Phi \wedge \Psi) \geq \Phi \wedge \Psi$ strictly in general — the conjunction of two unkept promises is itself unkept. This is consistent: the conjunction promise is still active.

**Promise vs. Contract.** A contract $(P_1, P_2, \Phi_{12}, \Phi_{21})$ at $t$ requires two meaning-settled propositions: $\Phi_{12}, \Phi_{21} \in \mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_t)$, each transfer-open. A bare promise has only one. The difference is not in the nuclear structure of either individual commitment — each is a $\sigma_t$-fixed, $\Delta_t$-open element — but in the bilateral structure: a contract is a pair of active promises where both $\sigma_t(\Phi_{12}) = \Phi_{12}$ and $\sigma_t(\Phi_{21}) = \Phi_{21}$ hold simultaneously under mutual formation conditions.

**Remark (Broken promise).** A promise at $t$ was formed because the speech act advanced history to a state where $\Phi \in \mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_t)$. The subsequent history records whether $\Phi$ reaches $\mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_{t'})$ at a later $t'$. If the history advances to $t'$ without $\Phi$ becoming $\Delta_{t'}$-fixed — if $\Delta_{t'}(\Phi) > \Phi$ still at the deadline — the promise is broken. This is a structural fact about the forward history, not a modification of any nucleus.

## Open questions

- Whether Searle's **sincerity condition** (the promisor intends to perform at the time of promising) has a fiber analog — whether $S$'s sincerity corresponds to a condition on the path from $t$ to $t'$ (the history must not diverge from a path where $\Phi$ will settle), and whether insincerity is therefore a structural fact about the history fiber, not merely a psychological fact about $S$.
- Whether the **promise monad** (the async computation monad) is formally related to the directed comonad $G_s$ of the relational machine — whether the `bind` operation on promises corresponds to the coKleisli composition in the comonad.
- Whether **Schelling's credibility** condition has a fiber reading: a promise is credible at $t$ iff there exists a path to $t'$ through which $\Phi$ reaches $H^*_{t'}$; a non-credible promise is one where no such path exists (the promisor lacks the structural means to perform, regardless of intention).
- The formal relationship between the promise and the Haskell `IO` monad — whether `IO a` is a promise in the sense of $\Pi = (\mathrm{RTS}, \mathrm{Program}, \Phi_a)$ where $\mathrm{RTS}$ (the runtime system) promises the program that executing the `IO` action will eventually produce a value of type $a$.
- Whether an **irrevocable promise** (a unilateral promise that cannot be withdrawn once made — the promissory estoppel case) corresponds to the `!` modality of linear logic applied to the promise proposition: $!\Phi$ means $\Phi$ cannot be consumed or revoked, only acted upon.
