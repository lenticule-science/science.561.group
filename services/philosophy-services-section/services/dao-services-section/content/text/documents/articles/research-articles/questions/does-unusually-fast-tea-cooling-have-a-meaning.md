## Question

Is there attested knowledge in Chinese philosophy, tea culture, folk belief, or related practice about the meaning of a cup of tea going cold unusually quickly?

## Initial answer

As of this initial survey, no consulted source attests a stable Chinese philosophical, divinatory, or folk-omen meaning for a cup of tea cooling *unusually quickly*.
The sources do attest several nearby ideas about tea and coldness, but they must not be collapsed into that claim.
The most responsible present answer is therefore: **no established meaning has been found, while regional oral traditions and recent vernacular belief remain open research gaps**.

## What the sources do support

### Cold tea as a change in the drink

**[Primary text]** Lu Yu's Tang-dynasty *Classic of Tea* says tea should be drunk continuously while hot and that, if it cools, its refined qualities dissipate with the vapor.
The passage concerns preparation, aroma, and drinking quality, not an omen and not an unusually rapid rate of cooling.
The same work calls tea's flavor or medicinal nature “most cold” (*wei zhi han*, 味至寒).
That is a premodern materia-medica quality, not a report that the liquid physically loses heat faster than expected.

Source: [Lu Yu, *The Classic of Tea*, chapters 1 and 5, text hosted by the Institute for the History of Natural Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences](https://agri-history.ihns.ac.cn/books/chajin.htm).

### Cold tea as a social metaphor

The expression *ren zou cha liang* (人走茶凉), literally “when the person leaves, the tea cools,” describes relationships or influence fading after someone departs or loses office.
The cooling follows social departure in the image; an unexpectedly cooling cup is not itself treated as a predictive sign.
Modern accounts disagree about the expression's precise textual origin, so this note does not assign it a settled ancient provenance.

Sources: [China Heritage, “When the Guest Departs, the Tea Goes Cold”](https://chinaheritage.net/journal/when-the-guest-departs-the-tea-goes-cold/) and [China's Central Commission for Discipline Inspection, “人走‘茶’应凉”](https://m.ccdi.gov.cn/content/b1/4c/4144.html).

### Tea handling as etiquette

The late-Qing compilation *Qingbai leichao* describes *duan cha song ke* (端茶送客): a host lifting a tea bowl could cue the end of an overlong visit.
The meaningful sign is the host's conventional gesture, not the beverage's temperature.

Source: [*Qingbai leichao*, “Serving Tea for Guests,” Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=511466&if=gb&remap=gb).

### Cold tea as an ordinary regional custom

A local-government account of Taojiang tea customs describes cooled tea kept ready and served to guests, especially in hot weather.
This is useful counterevidence to any blanket claim that cold tea is inherently an inauspicious Chinese sign.
It does not rule out a narrower local belief elsewhere.

Source: [Yiyang Municipal Government, “桃江茶俗”](https://www.yiyang.gov.cn/yiyang/1/15/14117/content_517832.html).

## Physical explanations to check first

A hot drink loses energy through conduction into the vessel and support, convection to surrounding air, thermal radiation, and evaporation from the exposed surface.
Recent cup-scale research emphasizes that omitting evaporation can substantially underpredict cooling, while controlled experiments show combined convection and evaporation shape the airflow above a drink.
A breeze, low humidity, a wide exposed surface, a small pour, a cool or highly conductive cup, a cold table, stirring, or a lower starting temperature can therefore make one cup seem anomalously fast.

Sources: [Shayunusov et al., “The influence of evaporation on the cooling small cup of hot water,” *International Communications in Heat and Mass Transfer* 169 (2025), article 109607](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.icheatmasstransfer.2025.109607); [Laguerre et al., “Experimental study of airflow and heat transfer above a hot liquid surface simulating a cup of drink,” *Journal of Food Engineering* 197 (2017), 24–33](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfoodeng.2016.10.014); and [Ferreira, “Forced convection and the coffee cup,” *Physics Education* (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6552/adb6ea).

A simple check is to repeat the pour with the same tea volume, initial water temperature, cup, location, and thermometer.
Run covered and uncovered trials, then a still-air and a gentle-airflow trial.
Record temperature once per minute for ten to fifteen minutes.
If the apparent anomaly does not repeat under matched conditions, it is weak evidence for any special physical event, much less a cultural meaning.

## Interpretive ceiling

This survey supports saying that no attested general meaning was found.
It does **not** support saying that no Chinese person or local community has ever held such a belief.
“Chinese philosophy,” “Chinese tea culture,” regional custom, household lore, Traditional Chinese Medicine, and internet-era superstition are not interchangeable evidence domains.
The question is housed here because the Dao section is the repository's current active home for Chinese-philosophy material, but this initial result establishes no specifically Daoist teaching about the event.

## Search record

Searches were conducted in English and Chinese for combinations of tea, cup, cooling quickly, omen, portent, folk custom, feng shui, and divination, including `茶 很快 凉 预兆`, `茶凉 风水`, `茶占`, and English equivalents.
The search also checked the *Classic of Tea*, a late-Qing etiquette compilation, modern cultural histories of *ren zou cha liang*, a local tea-custom record, and heat-transfer literature.
Low-quality repetition and uncited omen lists were not treated as evidence.

## Open research directions

- Search regional folklore collections and ethnographies by province and language rather than treating “Chinese” as one undifferentiated tradition.
- Check whether a remembered saying concerns the *person leaving*, the *host ceasing to refill*, the *tea's medicinal cold nature*, or the *speed of physical cooling*; these produce different questions.
- Investigate Song tea divination and foam-pattern practices separately, without assuming they interpret temperature.
- Record the location, vessel, tea, weather, social setting, and exact wording from any oral testimony before generalizing it.
- Revisit the section placement if the evidence turns out to be medical, folkloric, Buddhist, or a modern divination practice rather than Daoist philosophy.
