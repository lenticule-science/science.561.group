Wu wei (無為), "non-action," names the *Daodejing*'s central practical claim that the way acts by not (forcibly) acting — chapter 37's 道常無為而無不為, "the way never acts, yet nothing is left undone."

This primitive philosophical sense is distinguished from a specialized downstream application recorded elsewhere in this estate's own corpus (a leadership-discipline reading of wu wei in a management-thought glossary, built on top of this concept rather than restating it), and from wu wei's unrelated romanized homophone 五味 ("the five flavors"), which shares no meaning with it.

## Renderings across published translations

Chapter 37's 道常無為而無不為 is rendered with real variance in how actively the way is read as *doing* anything at all:

- Waley: "Tao never does; Yet through it all things are done." [@waley1934]
- Ames & Hall (2003): a way-making register — "Way-making is really nameless. Were the nobles and kings able to respect this, all things would…" [@ames2003]
- Brian Browne Walker (1996): "Eternal Tao doesn't do anything, yet it leaves nothing undone."
- Ursula K. Le Guin (2009): "The Way never does anything, and everything gets done."
- Victor H. Mair (1990): "The Tao never does anything, yet there is nothing it doesn't do."

Every rendering keeps the paradox's shape (inaction, then total completeness), but they diverge on whether "Tao" is the grammatical subject of an ordinary verb (Waley's "does"), a gerund naming an activity (Ames & Hall's "way-making"), or an entity with a name at all.
