De (德) names what a particular thing has when dao's generative operation is present and active in it specifically — most often rendered "virtue" or "power," and in chapter 51's 道生之，德畜之 it is what "rears"/"nurtures" what dao "gives life to."

De is distinguished from the ordinary English sense of "virtue" as moral goodness: the classical term names an efficacy or potency a thing has through dao, not primarily a moral quality, which is why Waley scare-quotes his own rendering — "the 'power' of Tao" — rather than asserting it plainly.

## Renderings across published translations

Chapter 51's 道生之，德畜之 ("the way gives them life, de rears them") is the clearest single locus for comparing how translations handle de:

- Legge (1891): "All things are produced by the Tao, and nourished by its outflowing operation." [@legge1891]
- Waley (1934): "Tao gave them birth; The 'power' of Tao reared them." [@waley1934]
- Lau (1963): "The way gives them life; Virtue rears them." [@lau1963]
- Mitchell (1988): "The Tao gives birth to all beings, nourishes them, maintains them, cares for them, comforts them, protects them." [@mitchell1988]

Legge does not use "virtue" or "power" at all here, folding de into "its outflowing operation"; Mitchell's paraphrase drops the second clause's distinct subject entirely, so that de disappears as a named term rather than being translated by any single English word. That a major translation can render de by *not* naming it separately from dao is itself evidence this glossary records, not an omission to silently correct.

This glossary does not yet hold a grounded Ames & Hall (2003) rendering of this locus; that gap is recorded rather than filled with an unverified guess.
