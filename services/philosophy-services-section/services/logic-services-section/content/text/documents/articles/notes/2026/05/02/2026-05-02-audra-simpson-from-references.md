Audra Simpson is a Mohawk scholar from Kahnawà:ke, professor of anthropology at Columbia University, and one of the central figures of contemporary Indigenous political theory. Her work develops a Mohawk political-theoretical standpoint on sovereignty, refusal, and the gendered structure of settler-colonial governance, in close conversation with Glen Coulthard, Leanne Betasamosake Simpson, and the broader Indigenous-resurgence and Indigenous-feminist literatures.

## Core ideas

- **Nested sovereignty.** The Kahnawà:ke Mohawk polity exists *inside* — not as a subordinate unit of, but spatially nested within and politically distinct from — the Canadian and US settler states. Nested sovereignty names the analytical and political situation in which an Indigenous polity exercises its own jurisdiction, citizenship, and governance while geographically encompassed by a settler polity that refuses to fully recognize that jurisdiction. The concept reframes the standard sovereignty literature, which assumes mutually exclusive territorial states.
- **Ethnographic refusal.** A methodological extension of the political concept of refusal: the ethnographer (and her interlocutors) can decline to deliver certain knowledge into the settler-academic record. Refusal is not lack of access; it is a stance — an exercise of the community's authority over what gets known, by whom, and in what register. Simpson's work develops refusal as a research methodology that takes the community's political authority seriously rather than treating it as an obstacle.
- **The gendered structure of settler elimination.** Settler-colonial elimination operates centrally through gendered violence — the targeting of Indigenous women's bodies, the legal apparatus that has historically stripped Indigenous women of status when they married outside the community, and the contemporary epidemic of missing and murdered Indigenous women. The settler state cannot be analyzed without analyzing this gendered structure; Indigenous-feminist analysis is therefore not a subdomain of settler-colonial studies but constitutive of it.
- **Recognition as governance.** Simpson's analysis converges with Coulthard's: the politics of recognition operates as a settler-state governance technology, offering acknowledgment in exchange for legibility within settler legal and bureaucratic categories. The nation that refuses recognition retains a freedom that the nation that accepts it forfeits.

## Key works

- *Mohawk Interruptus: Political Life Across the Borders of Settler States* (2014)
- *Theorizing Native Studies* (co-edited with Andrea Smith, 2014)
- "On Ethnographic Refusal: Indigeneity, 'Voice' and Colonial Citizenship" (*Junctures*, 2007)
- "The State Is a Man: Theresa Spence, Loretta Saunders and the Gender of Settler Sovereignty" (*Theory & Event*, 2016)
