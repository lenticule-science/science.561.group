Kim TallBear is an enrolled member of the Sisseton-Wahpeton Oyate, professor in the Faculty of Native Studies at the University of Alberta, and Canada Research Chair in Indigenous Peoples, Technoscience, and Society. Her work brings Indigenous studies into sustained engagement with science and technology studies (STS), focusing on how settler-scientific practice — especially genomics — constructs and contests indigeneity, and on Indigenous-feminist accounts of kinship that exceed both biological and settler-legal definitions.

## Core ideas

- **Settler-scientific indigeneity.** The settler scientific apparatus (population genetics, genealogical-DNA companies, "Native American DNA" tests) constructs indigeneity as a biological category located in individual bodies — and in doing so substitutes a technical, individual, market-mediated identity for the political and relational category of tribal citizenship. *Native American DNA* shows how this substitution serves settler interests by displacing Indigenous nations' own determination of who their citizens are.
- **Tribe as polity, not population.** Indigenous nations are political communities with their own membership rules. The genomic offer to "discover" Indigenous ancestry sidesteps the polity entirely and treats indigeneity as personal heritage. TallBear's analysis insists on the political category against the biological one and on the nation's authority against the consumer's curiosity.
- **Kinship beyond biology.** Drawing on Dakota and broader Indigenous-feminist traditions, TallBear theorizes kinship as relational practice — including kinship with land, water, plants, and other-than-human relatives — rather than as biological pedigree or settler-legal household. This is the foundation of her ongoing work on critical polyamory, on "making kin not babies" in conversation with Donna Haraway, and on Indigenous-feminist accounts of relationship structure.
- **Indigenous standpoint in STS.** Indigenous standpoint is not an add-on to science studies; it reorganizes what counts as evidence, what counts as relation, and what counts as the politics of a scientific practice. Researchers studying Indigenous communities are accountable to those communities, and the methodological consequence is a different shape of research design.
