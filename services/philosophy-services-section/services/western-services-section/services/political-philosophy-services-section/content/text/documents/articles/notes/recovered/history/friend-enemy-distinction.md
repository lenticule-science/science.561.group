# friend-enemy-distinction

The friend-enemy distinction is Carl Schmitt's claim that the political as such is constituted by the
distinction between friend and enemy — that what makes a question *political*, as opposed to moral,
aesthetic, or economic, is its reference to this fundamental opposition. Generated by: the friend-enemy
distinction is the sovereign decision that draws the boundary of the political community by naming its
outside; it is the state's foundational act of marking who belongs and who is the enemy. It comes from
Schmitt's *The Concept of the Political* (1932), and it stands as one of the most influential and most
dangerous ideas in twentieth-century political thought.

Schmitt's argument runs as follows. Each domain of human life, he held, has its own basic
distinction: morality turns on good and evil, aesthetics on beautiful and ugly, economics on
profitable and unprofitable. The political has its own irreducible criterion, and it is the
distinction between friend and enemy. The political enemy is not a personal adversary, a business
competitor, or a moral offender — not someone you hate or disapprove of. The enemy is an existential
other, a group whose mode of existence so negates your own community's form of life that conflict, up
to and including killing, becomes a real possibility. The intensity of the friend-enemy grouping, not
its subject matter, is what makes any conflict political; a religious, economic, or ethnic difference
becomes political precisely when it sharpens to the point of this existential antagonism.

From this Schmitt drew a pointed anti-liberal conclusion. The friend-enemy distinction, he insisted,
cannot be dissolved through liberal procedures — negotiation, compromise, constitutional mediation,
universal humanity. The enemy is not someone you can simply talk into agreement, because the conflict
is over incompatible forms of existence, not over claims that reasons could reconcile. Liberalism's
attempt to abolish the political by appealing to humanity does not eliminate the antagonism, Schmitt
argued, but disguises it: a power that fights its wars in the name of humanity, peace, and progress
denies that it has an enemy at all, and so denies its enemy even the status of a legitimate foe,
treating opponents not as enemies to be defeated but as criminals or monsters to be eradicated. The
decision over who is the enemy belongs, for Schmitt, to the sovereign — which ties this concept
directly to his account of sovereignty as the power to decide the exception.

The concept's history is a cautionary one. It became operational in fascist governance, where Joseph
Goebbels rendered it as a struggle of "light against darkness," and it is no accident that Schmitt
himself became a jurist of the Nazi regime. Yet the structure he described has reappeared across the
political spectrum. Cold War rhetoric inherited it — Reagan's "Evil Empire" reproduced the existential
antagonism while claiming liberal universalism — and liberalism itself, in Karl Popper's "enemies of
the open society," inverted the content while preserving the antagonistic form, treating intolerance
as the existential enemy that tolerance must not tolerate. Schmitt's enduring provocation is the
suggestion that no political order, however liberal its self-description, escapes the drawing of a
line between us and them.

Within this cluster the friend-enemy distinction is the outer edge of the state's authority — the
sovereign act that constitutes a political community by naming its outside. It is intimately bound to
sovereignty (the power to decide the exception is also the power to decide the enemy) and to the
nation-state (whose insistence on one people gives the line between friend and enemy its most violent
modern expression, in the production of internal enemies, minorities, and the stateless).
