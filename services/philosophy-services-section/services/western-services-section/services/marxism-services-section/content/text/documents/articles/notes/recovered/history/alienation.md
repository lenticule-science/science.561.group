# alienation

Alienation describes the estrangement that arises when people lose connection to their labor, its products, their own capacities, and each other. Marx developed the concept in his *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*, the early notebooks in which he reworked Hegel's and Feuerbach's idea of estrangement into a critique of capitalist production. He identified four interlocking dimensions: alienation from the *product* of labor (which belongs to the employer, not the maker), from the *activity* of laboring (which is directed by another), from *species-being* (the distinctively human capacity for free, conscious, creative activity), and from *other people* (who, within the system, become competitors or instruments rather than fellows). Generated by: **wage-labor** — alienation is a structural feature of producing for another under compulsion, not a passing mood or a complaint about disliking one's job.

The structure becomes clear when set against the wage relation. Under wage labor, the worker does not decide what to make, how to make it, or what becomes of what they make. The product confronts its maker as something alien — owned by someone else, sold for someone else's profit, and the more the worker produces, the more wealth stands over against them as the property of another. The labor process itself, which could in principle be an exercise of human capacity, becomes a site of unfreedom: the worker is active in their leisure and passive in their work, "at home" only when not working. And because each worker is set into competition with every other for wages and jobs, the relation to others is hollowed out into rivalry. Marx insisted this was not a psychological grievance that better attitudes could fix but a consequence of a specific historical arrangement of production — change the arrangement and the estrangement changes with it.

What makes alienation a deep concept, rather than merely a description of unpleasant work, is its claim about human nature and loss. For the early Marx, human beings are the kind of creature that realizes itself through purposive, shared, creative transformation of the world — labor, in the broadest sense, is how humans make themselves. Alienation names the severing of this self-realizing activity from the people who perform it: their own life-activity is turned into a mere means to physical survival, sold to another, and returned to them as a wage. The human capacity that should be the medium of freedom becomes the very thing through which unfreedom is organized.

The concept has limits, and the broader tradition has pressed on them. By centering wage labor as the primary form of estrangement, alienation theory taken alone can obscure other forms of dispossession — enslavement, land theft, the unwaged reproductive labor that sustains the wage system — that do not fit the wage relation. Silvia Federici and the theorists of social reproduction have argued that alienation theory cannot, by itself, account for the gendered and racialized labor performed outside the wage that nonetheless sustains it. There is also a long debate within Marxism about the relation between the early "humanist" Marx of alienation and the mature Marx of *Capital*, who reframes much of the same terrain in the more structural vocabulary of exploitation, surplus-value, and commodity fetishism.

As a node, alienation is the experiential and ethical face of the wage relation, the counterpart to surplus-value's economic face. Surplus-value names what is *taken* from the worker; alienation names what is *severed* — the constitutive relations between maker and made, between person and activity, between self and others — by the same arrangement. To name a condition as alienation is to claim that a real connection has been broken by a specific, historical, and therefore alterable organization of production.
