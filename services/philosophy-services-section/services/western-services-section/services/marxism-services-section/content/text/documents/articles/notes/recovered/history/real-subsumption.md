# real-subsumption

Real subsumption is Marx's term for the second of two historical stages by which capital comes to dominate the labor process. The distinction between *formal* and *real* subsumption is developed in *Capital*, Volume I, and most explicitly in the *Resultate des unmittelbaren Produktionsprozesses* — the unpublished "Chapter Six" recovered in 1933 and now usually printed as an appendix to English editions of *Capital*. Generated by: **labor-process** — real subsumption names the moment capital stops merely taking over the labor process from outside and begins reorganizing it from within.

The contrast is the heart of the concept. *Formal subsumption* is the stage in which capital takes over an existing labor process without yet transforming its technical content. The artisan keeps making cabinets the way cabinet-making has always been done; what changes is that the artisan is now a wage-laborer producing for a capitalist who owns the workshop, the materials, and the product. Capital's grip here operates on the *outside* of the work — through the wage, the length of the working day, the disciplinary regime — while the inside of the labor process keeps its pre-capitalist form. The corresponding extraction is absolute surplus-value, won chiefly by lengthening the working day. *Real subsumption* is the stage in which capital reorganizes the labor process itself. Cabinet-making is no longer done as it always was: it is broken into specialized operations distributed across a sequence of workers, mediated by machines designed for that sequence, and disciplined by time-and-motion methods. The work now bears capital's organizational stamp throughout, and the corresponding extraction is relative surplus-value, won by raising the productivity of labor itself.

For Marx the transition from formal to real subsumption is the historical core of *industrial* capitalism. The factory and the machine system, analyzed at length in the chapters of *Capital* on cooperation, the division of labor, and machinery, are real subsumption's institutional embodiments. The tradition that followed identifies several characteristic mechanisms through which it operates: the detailed division of labor, breaking work into specialized partial operations; machinery engineered to fit and require that divided process; cooperation in the workplace, the assembled workforce becoming a productive force in itself; the discipline of clock-time and imposed work-rhythms (the subject of E. P. Thompson's classic essay on time and work-discipline); and the separation of conception from execution, the appropriation of the *design* of work by management. This last is what Braverman identifies as the Taylorist completion of real subsumption.

The political edge of the concept is that real subsumption is what makes the labor process appear *technical* while functioning as *control*. Once capital has reorganized the work itself, the resulting arrangement — the line, the standardized procedure, the machine-set pace — presents itself as simply the efficient way to produce the thing, when in fact it was selected for its capacity to subordinate labor and intensify extraction. This is the methodological inheritance the labor-process tradition takes from Marx: to refuse the presentation of work organization as mere technical necessity and to read it as a move in the antagonism between capital and labor.

As a node, real subsumption is the frame within which scientific management and deskilling are read: scientific management is the originating technical-managerial expression of real subsumption, and deskilling is a consequence of one form it takes. Carlo Vercellone later argued that contemporary cognitive capitalism, where the general intellect becomes directly productive, marks a partial *reversion* toward a formal-subsumption-like configuration — capital appropriating value from cooperative-cognitive activity it can no longer fully reorganize from the inside — making real subsumption the historical reference point against which the immaterial-labor analyses define their novelty.
