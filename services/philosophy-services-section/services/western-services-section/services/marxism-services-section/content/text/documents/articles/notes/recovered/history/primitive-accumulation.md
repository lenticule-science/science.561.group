# primitive-accumulation

Primitive accumulation is Marx's term — in the closing part of *Capital*, Volume I — for the historical processes that created the preconditions for capitalist production: above all, the separation of people from the means of their own subsistence. Capital cannot accumulate by exploiting wage labor until there exists a class of people who own nothing but their capacity to work and must therefore sell it; primitive accumulation is the violent making of that class. Generated by: **capital** in its accumulating mode — primitive accumulation is the frontier process by which capital first constitutes, and then continually renews, the conditions under which exploitation can occur.

In England the paradigm case was enclosure: the forcible conversion of common land into private property, dispossessing peasants who had subsisted on shared fields, pastures, and forests, and driving them into the cities with nothing to sell but their labor-power. Globally it took the form of colonial conquest, the Atlantic slave trade, and the extraction of wealth from colonized peoples and lands. Marx treated these together as capitalism's "so-called original accumulation" and stripped the euphemism from it, calling it the system's original sin — the conquest, enslavement, robbery, and murder that preceded and enabled the apparently peaceful, free, and equal exchange of wages for work. The crucial polemical point is that the contract at the heart of capitalism rests on a prior dispossession that was anything but contractual.

Marx's own account tended to locate primitive accumulation in capitalism's past — a founding stage that, once completed, gives way to the "normal" operation of surplus extraction. David Harvey's influential reformulation, *accumulation by dispossession*, corrects this temporal framing. Dispossession is not a completed prehistory but an ongoing requirement of accumulation. The privatization of public goods, the enclosure of intellectual and informational commons through expanding patent and copyright regimes, the seizure of Indigenous territories, structural-adjustment programs that pry open markets in the Global South, and the financialization of housing and debt are all contemporary forms. Capital does not merely exploit existing workers; it continually manufactures new populations to exploit by destroying their existing means of subsistence.

This is where Marxist and decolonial analyses converge, and where the concept does some of its most important work. Settler colonialism, on this reading, is not a historical event with lingering consequences but an ongoing structure of dispossession that continues to separate Indigenous peoples from their land. Racial capitalism shows that this dispossession has always operated through racial differentiation, marking populations as available for appropriation. Social-reproduction theory adds that the unwaged labor of women — disproportionately women of color — is itself a form of ongoing primitive accumulation, the appropriation of labor without compensation that sustains the wage system from outside it. The Land Back demand follows directly: if capital requires continuous dispossession, and if settler colonialism is its ongoing form on stolen land, then the return of land is not a symbolic gesture but a structural challenge to the conditions of accumulation.

As a node in the cluster, primitive accumulation is what the wage relation presupposes. Wage-labor describes a worker who must sell labor-power; primitive accumulation explains why — because the means of subsistence have been taken — and thereby exposes the unfreedom hidden inside the "free" labor contract. It is the historical and continuing counterpart to surplus-value: where surplus-value names what capital extracts from workers it already has, primitive accumulation names how capital acquires the workers, the land, and the resources in the first place. Its reciprocal opposite is the commons it destroys.
