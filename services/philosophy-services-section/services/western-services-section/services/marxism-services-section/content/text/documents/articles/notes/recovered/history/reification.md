# reification

Reification names the process by which social relations come to appear as fixed, thing-like properties of the world. The German word, *Verdinglichung*, means literally "thing-ification" — the making of a relation into a thing. Georg Lukács developed the concept in *History and Class Consciousness* (1923), in the long central essay "Reification and the Consciousness of the Proletariat," extending Marx's analysis of commodity fetishism from the commodity into a general theory of consciousness under capitalism. Generated by: **commodity-fetishism** — reification is its generalization, the spreading of the commodity's characteristic inversion across the whole of thought and institutional life.

Where Marx showed that the commodity conceals social relations behind a thing-like value, Lukács argued that this concealment is not confined to economic exchange but comes to pervade all thought and all institutions in a society organized around commodity production. Law, bureaucracy, science, journalism, and philosophy each tend to treat dynamic, relational, historical processes as if they were static objects with fixed, measurable properties. A wage rate, a property title, a credit score, a crime rate, a market index — each takes a relation between people (between worker and employer, between community and land, between knower and known, between the governed and the governing) and freezes it into a thing that then appears to stand over against people as an external, given fact of nature. Once frozen, the relation looks natural and inevitable rather than produced and changeable.

Lukács is careful, and the care matters, to distinguish reification from abstraction in general. All thought abstracts; concepts necessarily leave out detail and treat unlike things as alike for some purpose. Reification is a *specific* kind of abstraction — one that erases the relational and historical character of what it describes and substitutes a self-standing thing. The reified concept does not merely simplify; it presents a human, contingent, contestable arrangement as a fixed feature of the world about which nothing can be done. This is why Lukács treats reification as a structure of consciousness with political consequences: a reified world is one in which people confront their own collective products — markets, states, laws — as alien powers governing them, and lose the sense that these were made and could be remade.

A concrete example brings the abstraction down to earth. Consider how unemployment can come to be discussed as a "rate" that "rises" and "falls" like the weather, a thing with its own behavior to be forecast and managed — when what the number registers is millions of particular relationships of dependence, dispossession, and exclusion among real people and the institutions that govern their access to work. The reified form is not false in its measurements, but it presents a historical relation as a natural object, and in doing so makes the relation harder to see as a relation, and so harder to contest.

Lukács inherited this concept partly from Max Weber's account of rationalization and bureaucratization — the spread of calculable, rule-bound, impersonal forms across modern life — and fused it with Marx's value-theory, giving twentieth-century critical theory one of its central terms; it runs forward into the Frankfurt School's analyses of administered society and instrumental reason. As a node, reification is the most general form of the appearance that begins with commodity fetishism: it names the tendency of a commodity-producing society to present its own living relations back to itself as a world of fixed things, and the critical task of seeing through that appearance to the relations and the labor it conceals.
