The warble is a rapid alternation between the [fundamental](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/fundamental.md) and an upper [harmonic](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/harmonic.md) of an aerophone's [bore](./bore.md), produced when the player's breath pressure is held in a specific narrow regime that puts the air-jet at the splitting edge into unstable, oscillating behavior.
The warble is the principal cadential [ornament](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/ornament.md) of the [cedar-flute](../cedar-flute/cedar-flute.md) tradition and a recurring articulative device on the bone whistles (especially the [eagle-wing-bone-whistle](../eagle-wing-bone-whistle/eagle-wing-bone-whistle.md)) — a small but acoustically distinctive timbral event that, on the cedar flute, is so closely associated with the cadential moment that its presence functions almost as a structural marker of phrase- and strophe-ends.

Mechanically, the warble arises from *jet instability*.
At normal breath pressure on a [duct-flute](../duct-flute/duct-flute.md) like the [cedar-flute](../cedar-flute/cedar-flute.md), the air-jet emerging from the wind way strikes the splitting edge cleanly and drives the [bore](./bore.md) into stable vibration at the fundamental.
At substantially higher breath pressure, the air-jet drives the bore into the next [harmonic](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/harmonic.md) mode (the octave for an open-open cylindrical bore), producing a clean [overblow](../overblowing/overblowing.md).
At a precisely intermediate breath pressure — the threshold between fundamental-driving and harmonic-driving regimes — the jet is unstable: it "flips" between striking the edge in a configuration that drives the fundamental and a configuration that drives the second harmonic, at an audio-rate that depends on the bore's dimensions and the player's breath.
The result is a rapid alternation between the fundamental and the octave, heard as a single ornamented tone with a characteristic "wobble" timbre.

The audio rate of the warble — how rapidly the alternation occurs — is in the range of roughly 5 to 15 cycles per second on a typical cedar flute, slow enough that the listener perceives the alternation as a distinct two-tone wobble rather than as a steady third frequency, but fast enough that the warble is heard as a single ornamented event rather than as a sequence of separate tones.
The player has some control over the warble's rate by fine adjustments to breath pressure: lower pressure within the warble-threshold range produces a slower wobble, higher pressure produces a faster wobble.
Skilled players use this rate-control expressively, beginning a cadential warble at a slower rate and accelerating it toward the phrase's terminus.

Musically, the warble's principal use is *cadential*.
The canonical cedar-flute cadence is a descent to the tonic (scale-degree 1) — typically the descending minor third b3 → 1 — with the tonic held and a warble applied to the held tone, which then decays into silence as the breath runs out.
The warble at the cadence serves several functions at once: it marks the phrase's terminal moment audibly (a held plain tonic without warble would not announce the cadence as definitely); it carries expressive content (the warble's intensity and duration are at the player's discretion and admit considerable variation); and it provides a natural decay shape for the phrase (the warble's intensity falls off as the breath weakens, producing the characteristic "fading wobble" that closes the cedar-flute phrase).
The phrase ends not abruptly but in a controlled decay through the warble.

The warble also appears, less prominently, as a *mid-phrase* ornament on certain sustained tones (typically the scale-degree 5 or b7 when held for an extended duration), where it provides expressive coloration without functioning as a phrase-end marker.
The decision to mid-phrase-warble or not is at the player's discretion and is one of the principal sites of [ornament-substitution](../../philB/code/transformers/ornament-substitution.md) variation across strophes of a [courting-song](../courting-song/courting-song.md) performance.

On the [eagle-wing-bone-whistle](../eagle-wing-bone-whistle/eagle-wing-bone-whistle.md) and other bone whistles, the warble is also produced by jet instability — the player's [embouchure](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/embouchure.md) tunes the air-jet to the threshold regime — and serves an analogous cadential/expressive role within the whistle's [envelope-grammar](../../philB/prose/learning/claims/whistle-envelope-grammar.md).
On bone whistles, the warble's two-tone alternation may be between the fundamental and the second harmonic (an octave above) or, in some whistles whose embouchure-and-bore-acoustic combination supports it, between the fundamental and a slightly-detuned neighbor pitch — the latter case producing a "trilled" rather than "octave-wobble" warble.
