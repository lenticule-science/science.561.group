The breath pulse is a brief modulation of breath pressure — typically produced at the diaphragm rather than at the tongue — that articulates one tone from another within a continuous breath, without the player having to interrupt the breath to retake air or to use a sharp tongue-stroke to mark the boundary.
The breath pulse is the principal non-finger articulation device of the [cedar-flute](../cedar-flute/cedar-flute.md) and is also a key technique on the bone whistles ([eagle-wing-bone-whistle](../eagle-wing-bone-whistle/eagle-wing-bone-whistle.md), [crane-bone-whistle](../crane-bone-whistle/crane-bone-whistle.md)), where it provides the rhythmic articulation that distinguishes one whistle-event from the next within a sustained breath.

Mechanically, the breath pulse is a brief contraction of the diaphragm (or a controlled pause-and-resumption of the breath flow at a higher level of the respiratory system) that causes a momentary dip in the breath pressure at the instrument's sound source — the [duct-flute](../duct-flute/duct-flute.md) wind way for the cedar flute, or the [embouchure](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/embouchure.md) for the bone whistles.
The dip is brief — typically 50 to 200 milliseconds — and small enough that the instrument's bore does not drop out of vibration; the sound continues across the pulse, but with an audible dip in intensity and a brief moment of articulation that separates the pre-pulse tone from the post-pulse tone perceptually.

The breath pulse contrasts with two alternatives.
Against *tongue articulation* (the European-classical wind-instrument technique of using a sharp tongue stroke to start each new note, equivalent to the consonant "t" or "d" in vocal pronunciation), the breath pulse is softer and more rolled — the new tone begins with a gentle rise from the pressure dip rather than with a sharp consonant attack.
The cedar-flute tradition does not use tongue articulation; the breath pulse is the entire idiomatic articulation vocabulary for separating notes within a phrase.
Against *re-breathing* (the act of pausing entirely to take in new air between notes), the breath pulse maintains the continuity of the breath — the player does not need to inhale between pulse-separated notes, and a single breath can carry many pulse-separated notes in succession.
The phrase as a whole is bounded by the single-breath capacity ([phrase-bounded-by-single-breath](../../philB/prose/learning/claims/phrase-bounded-by-single-breath.md)), but within that single breath, the breath pulse provides the internal articulation structure.

The breath pulse's musical functions are principally three.
First, *note separation*: where the melody requires two distinct notes to be heard as separate articulations (rather than as one continuous tone), the breath pulse provides the separation.
This is the basic function and applies whenever consecutive notes share a fingering or when the change in fingering is too small to produce a perceptible articulation on its own.
Second, *syllable articulation in instrumentalization*: when the [cedar-flute](../cedar-flute/cedar-flute.md) [instrumentalizes](./instrumentalization.md) a [wiilowan](../wiilowan/wiilowan.md) vocal song, the syllable boundaries of the vocal text correspond to breath-pulse articulations on the flute.
The flute cannot pronounce syllables, but it can mark where syllables would begin in the vocal source, by pulsing the breath at those moments.
This is one of the principal mechanisms by which the vocal source is preserved in flute rendering.
Third, *rhythmic articulation*: the breath pulse provides a way to articulate rhythm within a sustained tone or across a melodic figure, where the player wants to create a sense of pulse without changing pitch — analogous to the role of the [tap](../tap/tap.md) ornament, but available even when the fingering does not admit a finger-controlled articulation.

On the bone whistles, the breath pulse is even more central than on the cedar flute, because the whistles have only one pitch and the entire rhythmic structure of a whistle call is produced by breath-pulse articulation.
A whistle "tune" in the sense of [whistle-rhythm-vocabulary](../../philB/prose/learning/claims/whistle-rhythm-vocabulary.md) is essentially a sequence of breath-pulses applied to the whistle's fundamental, with pulse-spacing producing the rhythmic structure and pulse-shape (sharp or rolled) producing the timbral character of each whistle-event.
