Rubato is flexible rhythmic timing without an underlying regular pulse — the temporal organization in which note durations are determined by the player's musical and physical sense of pacing rather than by a metric grid imposed from outside (by a drum, a regular pulse, or a notated meter).
The term is borrowed from Italian (*tempo rubato* — "robbed time," with the implication that time is "borrowed" from one note and "returned" to another), where it originally described the European-classical practice of subtle metric flexibility within an underlying regular meter.
The term is here extended — as is now widespread in ethnomusicological writing — to designate temporal organization that has *no* underlying regular pulse at all, which is the relevant case for the solo [cedar-flute](../../../../b4/cedar-flute/cedar-flute.md) idiom and for solo unaccompanied [wiilowan](../../../../b4/wiilowan/wiilowan.md) vocal performance.

The Lakotaean cedar-flute idiom is fundamentally rubato.
There is no regular pulse against which note-durations are measured; instead, the temporal organization is *breath-paced* — note durations are determined by the player's breath-control within the [phrase](./phrase.md), and the phrase as a whole is bounded by the player's single-breath capacity.
Within the phrase, a player may sustain a note for a longer or shorter duration depending on the music's expressive needs; may apply [ornaments](./ornament.md) of varying duration; may produce [breath-pulse](../../../../b4/breath-pulse/breath-pulse.md) articulations at varying intervals.
The same phrase, played twice by the same player, will not have identical note-durations — the variation between performances is part of the music's character.

This contrasts sharply with the metric organization of [drum](../../../../b4/drum/drum.md)-anchored ensemble music.
In a [honor-song](../../../../b4/honor-song/honor-song.md) performance, the drum supplies a regular metric pulse, and the [voice](./voice.md) and whistle aerophones must align their articulations to that pulse at structural moments.
The drum-ensemble music is *metric*, not rubato.
The cedar-flute solo idiom and the drum-ensemble idiom therefore occupy different temporal regimes within the same broad musical tradition: the cedar flute is non-metric, drum ensembles are metric.
The [non-metric-temporal-substrate](../../../code/structures/non-metric-temporal-substrate.md) structure formalizes this distinction.

Rubato has specific music-theoretic consequences.
First, the *unit of temporal organization* is the [phrase](./phrase.md) rather than the beat — a phrase is a single unbounded-time melodic gesture, not a measure-counted sequence of beats.
Second, *rhythmic notation* is fundamentally inadequate for rubato music — Western music notation, which assumes a regular metric grid, can only approximate the actual durations of a rubato performance, and reading a rubato performance from notation requires the reader to know the idiom independently of the notation.
Third, *ensemble organization* with rubato music is structurally different from ensemble organization with metric music — two rubato musicians playing together must coordinate by mutual listening and by structural-moment alignment (at phrase-ends, at cadences) rather than by shared pulse, and this is one reason the cedar flute is principally a solo or [flute-voice-duet](../../../code/structures/flute-voice-duet.md) instrument and does not idiomatically join larger ensembles.

The rubato character of the cedar flute idiom is *not* unstructured.
Within a phrase, note-durations have characteristic relative weights: melodic peaks tend to be sustained longer than melodic interior points; cadential tones (the held [tonic](./fundamental.md) at the [cadence](./cadence.md)) tend to be sustained longer than any other tone in the phrase; ornamented tones tend to occupy more time than unornamented tones.
A skilled player's rubato is internally coherent and recognizably idiomatic; an unidiomatic rubato (with note-durations distributed unconventionally) sounds strange even to listeners who could not articulate the convention they are missing.
The rubato of the cedar flute is therefore a *structured* freedom rather than a literal freedom — the player has discretion over note-durations within a tradition that constrains the range of acceptable choices.
