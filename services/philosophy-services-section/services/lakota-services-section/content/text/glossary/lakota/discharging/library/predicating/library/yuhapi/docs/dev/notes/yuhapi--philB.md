# Yuhá / Yuhápi

Yuhá — *to bear*, *to keep*, *to hold-and-maintain* — is the act of witnessing-by-bearing.
It is the form that witness takes in Lakota tradition: the witness does not merely observe; the witness *carries* what is observed, maintaining it as a kept-thing across time.
Yuhápi is the durational form — the ongoing bearing.
In the relational framework, yuhá/yuhápi is the witness-act that fixes recognitions as kept, making them available for further relational work.

## What yuhá does

To yuhá a thing is to *take it up and hold it*.
The act has three components:

1. **Attending**: the bearer's attention is on the borne-thing.
2. **Holding**: the bearer maintains the thing across time, not letting it lapse.
3. **Carrying**: the bearer is responsible for the thing — its continued existence, its proper conduct, its eventual release if release is appropriate.

This is a stronger sense of witnessing than mere observation.
A Western witness *sees and reports*.
A Lakota yuhá-bearer *takes responsibility for what is seen*.
The wičháša wakȟáŋ who witnesses a ceremony does not merely confirm that it happened; they bear the ceremony forward — the relational-substrate effect of the ceremony continues *through* the bearer's holding.

## Wanáǧi yuhápi as the paradigmatic case

The clearest paradigmatic instance is **wanáǧi yuhápi** — the *keeping-of-the-soul* rite, one of the seven rites of the Lakota tradition.
After a death, the soul (*naǧí*) of the deceased is kept by a chosen family member for approximately one year.
The keeper bears the soul: maintains the bundle that holds it, attends to its needs, conducts the proper observances, refrains from contact-pollution, and at the end of the keeping period conducts the release-ceremony that allows the soul to depart toward Wanáǧi Tȟačháŋku (the spirit-trail).

This is yuhápi in its most ritually-formalized form, but the same structure runs in less-formal yuhá:

- A pipe-bearer *yuhá*-s the pipe.
  The pipe is not stored; it is *kept*.
- A medicine-bearer *yuhá*-s the medicines.
  They are not catalogued; they are *carried-with-attention*.
- A name-bearer *yuhá*-s a name.
  The name is not assigned; it is *borne forward*.
- A treaty-bearer *yuhá*-s the treaty.
  The treaty is not archived; it is *kept-as-binding*.

In each case, the act is a *bearing* with continuing responsibility.

## Yuhá as the witness-form of the relational framework

The relational framework names *witness* as the first observation in the chain: the act that testifies to the tension between relating and not-relating, that determines two recognitions coexist without yet determining which carries the act.
The structural role of witness in the framework is to *fix* a recognition so it can become available for further work.

Yuhá does exactly this.
To yuhá a recognition is to *fix it as kept*.
The kept-thing is now available — to be drawn on later, to be invoked in ceremony, to be borne in the polity's continuing relational substrate.
The act of bearing converts a moment-of-observation into a *durable witness-record*.

This explains why so much Lakota relational work involves designated bearers.
The polity is held together not only by ongoing kinship-conduct ([woótakuye](wotakuye.md)) but by the *bearers* who carry the substrate-records that make the conduct intelligible.
The pipe-keepers, the medicine-keepers, the bundle-keepers, the name-keepers, the treaty-keepers are the polity's distributed witness-bearing structure.

## Yuhá and the seven rites

Each of the seven rites involves yuhá in a specific form:

- *Iníkağapi* (sweat-lodge): the wičháša wakȟáŋ *yuhá*-s the purification — bears the cleansing through the rite.
- *Haŋbléčheyapi* (vision-quest): the quester *yuhá*-s the vision if one is given.
- *Wanáǧi yuhápi* (keeping-of-the-soul): the family-keeper *yuhá*-s the naǧí for the keeping period.
- *Wiwáŋyaŋg wačhípi* (sun-dance): the dancers *yuhá* the offering on behalf of the people; the wičháša wakȟáŋ *yuhá* the rite itself.
- *Huŋkápi* (making-of-relatives): the kin-link is *yuhá*-d into existence by the proper conduct of the rite.
- *Išnáthi awíčhalowaŋpi* (preparing-of-a-young-woman): the woman is *yuhá*-d into her standing-as-adult-woman.
- *Tȟapá wakȟáyap* (throwing-of-the-ball): the wisdom-coming is *yuhá*-d by those who reach for the ball.

The seven rites are the witness-bearing morphisms of the relational substrate.
See [seven-rites](seven-rites.md).

## Yuhá and the substrate's wakȟáŋ-character

When yuhá is performed *in proper relation* to wakȟáŋ — by a wičháša wakȟáŋ, in ceremonial conduct, on a wakȟáŋ-bearing thing — the bearing is itself wakȟáŋ.
The bearer becomes a *substrate-extending point of the sacred*: through the bearer, wakȟáŋ-quality persists into the polity's everyday life.
This is why bearer-status is a sacred-political role, not a clerical-archival one.
The bearer's conduct must remain in keeping with what is borne; failure of conduct breaks the bearing.
