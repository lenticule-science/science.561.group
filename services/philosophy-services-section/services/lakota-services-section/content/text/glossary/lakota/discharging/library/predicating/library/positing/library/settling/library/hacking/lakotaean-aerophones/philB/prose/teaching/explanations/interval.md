An interval is the pitch-distance between two tones — the measure of "how far apart" two pitches are, considered as a difference in pitch height (in [cents](../../../../b4/cents/cents.md), semitones, or other pitch-distance units) or as a frequency ratio.
The interval is the unit in which both a scale's structural identity is described (a scale is, formally, an ordered sequence of intervals from a starting tonic) and a melody's motion is described (a melody is, formally, a sequence of pitches each related to the next by an interval).
Without intervals there is no music theory; intervals are the connective tissue that turns a collection of pitches into a structured object.

Intervals are commonly named by their *quality* (perfect, major, minor, augmented, diminished) and their *number* (unison, second, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh, octave) — these names derive from European diatonic music theory but are widely used as a lingua franca across musical traditions for describing intervallic content.
The numerical name indicates the number of scale-positions the interval spans (a third spans three scale-positions counting both endpoints), and the quality indicates the specific size in semitones — a major third is 4 semitones, a minor third is 3 semitones, a perfect fifth is 7 semitones, a major sixth is 9 semitones, a minor seventh is 10 semitones, and so on.

The intervals that organize the [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md), measured from the tonic, are: the *minor third* (1 → b3, 3 semitones), the *perfect fourth* (1 → 4, 5 semitones), the *perfect fifth* (1 → 5, 7 semitones), the *minor seventh* (1 → b7, 10 semitones), and the *octave* (1 → 8, 12 semitones).
The intervals between *adjacent* scale-degrees — what determines the scale's stepwise melodic motion — form the pattern ⟨3, 2, 2, 3, 2⟩ semitones, equivalently ⟨minor third, major second, major second, minor third, major second⟩.
This adjacency-interval pattern is what makes the pentatonic minor a distinct scale rather than a permutation of some other scale; reading the same pattern starting from a different tonic produces a different mode.

Intervals have *acoustic* properties as well as music-theoretic ones.
The perfect intervals (perfect fifth, perfect fourth, octave) correspond to small-integer frequency ratios — 3:2 for the fifth, 4:3 for the fourth, 2:1 for the octave — and are the most acoustically stable intervals, in the sense that their tones combine without producing strong beating dissonance.
The thirds and sixths correspond to slightly more complex ratios (5:4 for the just major third, 6:5 for the just minor third, 5:3 for the just major sixth, 8:5 for the just minor sixth) and are still consonant but with more spectral complexity.
The seconds and sevenths are the most dissonant intervals in the diatonic system.
The [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md) is anhemitonic — it contains no semitone (minor-second) intervals between adjacent scale-degrees — which is part of what makes it stable in unaccompanied solo performance: there are no dissonant minor-seconds within the scale, and any melodic motion within the scale stays in consonant territory.

In the Lakotaean cedar-flute context, the *minor third* between scale-degrees b3 and 1 is the principal cadential interval — the descending b3 → 1 motion is the canonical cadence (see [cadence](./cadence.md)).
The *perfect fifth* between 5 and 1 is the strongest stable interval; melodic ascent to 5 and descent from 5 to 1 (across the intermediate scale-degrees) is a common structural pattern in [courting-song](../../../../b4/courting-song/courting-song.md) phrases.
The *minor seventh* between b7 and 1 is the largest stable interval within the octave; b7 is the phrase's typical peak, descending through 5 and 4 to b3 and 1 across the course of the phrase.
