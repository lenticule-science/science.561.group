# Naca

Načá — a leader.
In the late-19th-century Oglala material recorded in James R.
Walker's *Lakota Society* ([walker1982](../../philA/references/walker1982.md)), the term *naca yuha* is used for the council of senior, respected men — sometimes glossed in English as "those who carry the shirts" — and *wakíčuŋza* (commonly anglicized as *wakichunza*) is used for the four men selected from among the naca to act as the executive *shirt-wearers*: the ones to whom the council delegated the decision-making and policing oversight for the camp during a specific period.

The relationship between these terms is not perfectly settled in the published sources, and Lakota-internal usage varies across oyate and across time.
The structure recorded by Walker (with the caveats below) involves:

- A larger body of senior, respected men — *naca* / *naca yuha* — who form the deliberative council.
- A smaller executive of four — *wakichunza* / shirt-wearers — selected from the naca, who carry painted shirts as visible marks of office and who hold operational authority during their term.
- A still-smaller body that selects the four — *wičháša itȟáŋčhaŋ*, sometimes called the Big Bellies — older respected men who handle the high-level deliberation.

## What authority a naca holds

The naca's authority is not command.
It is the authority of one whose conduct toward the people has shown them to be a leader.
The four virtues — wówačhantognaka (generosity), wóksape (wisdom), wóohitika (bravery), wówačhiŋtȟaŋka (fortitude / perseverance) — are not abstract ideals; they are the conditions of leadership ([marshall2002](../../philB/references/marshall2002.md)).
A naca who fails his relatives in any of them loses standing; the office is not hereditary, and a person does not become a naca because their father was one.
The Oglala material is explicit on this last point: *the concept of a hereditary or a traditional chief does not exist in the Lakota culture*; leadership is a recognition of conduct, not a transmission of office.

## What a naca cannot do

A naca cannot coerce.
The structural mechanism is the [right of withdrawal](../consensus-by-withdrawal/consensus-by-withdrawal.md): a tiyospaye or person who does not consent to a naca-led decision can leave.
The akicita (warrior societies) enforce certain communal decisions — most clearly during the buffalo hunt, where individual hunting could disrupt the collective drive and was checked by the akicita — but the akicita's enforcement is bounded, occasion-specific, and itself answerable to the naca who appointed them.

The naca cannot accumulate wealth at the expense of his relatives.
Generosity is constitutive: a leader who hoards is no longer recognized as a leader.
The structural pattern recurs across many Indigenous polities and is the basis for Clastres' ([clastres1974](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../public-services-section/services/personal-services-section/services/emsenn-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/reference-articles/bibliographies/clastres1974.md)) thesis that the chief-as-servant is a form of state-refusal — a leader who must continually give cannot consolidate command.

## Source caveats

Walker is the most-cited ethnographic source for the term-structure given here, but Walker's compilation was assembled around 1900 from Lakota informants whose recollections referenced the pre-reservation period.
The structure he records is the Oglala structure of a particular time and place.
Other oyate had related but not identical arrangements.
Contemporary Lakota scholarship — Albert White Hat Sr. ([white-hat1999](../../philB/references/white-hat1999.md), [white-hat2012](../../philB/references/white-hat2012.md)), Joseph Marshall III ([marshall2002](../../philB/references/marshall2002.md)) — should be the primary basis for any claim about contemporary practice; Walker is cited for the historical record under that caveat.

## The leader-shirt

The shirt itself was the office.
Painted to symbolize the powers and obligations a wakichunza accepted, the shirt was given at an admission ceremony.
After tribal warfare on the Plains had ended, persons under a shirt-wearer's care contributed a lock of their own hair to adorn his shirt; the Lakota understanding is that hair carries a person's spirit, and its application to the shirt represents the people's trust in the leader.
The shirt is therefore a substrate object — the office is materialized as a thing the people themselves have constituted.
