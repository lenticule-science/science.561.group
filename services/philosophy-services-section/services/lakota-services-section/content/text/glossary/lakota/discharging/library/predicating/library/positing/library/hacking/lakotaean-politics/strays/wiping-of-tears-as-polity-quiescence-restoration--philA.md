# Wiping-of-tears as polity-quiescence-restoration morphism

The [wiping-of-tears](../b4/wiping-of-tears/wiping-of-tears.md) ceremony — Ištámniyaŋ Wičhákičhipakiŋta Pi — is realized in the substrate as a *quiescence-restoration morphism* on the relational settling after a loss.
The morphism resolves the persistent $\top$ on grief-relation cells and the persistent $\bot$ on cells the deceased's death has unsettled, returning the affected substrate to wicozani.

## The structural problem the morphism resolves

A death (or another major loss, at the polity scale) produces two kinds of substrate disturbance:

1. **Persistent $\top$ on grief cells.** A relation cell $\langle \text{the bereaved}, \texttt{stands-in-relation-with}, \text{the deceased}\rangle$ enters a contradicted state: the relation is real (the kin-tie does not vanish at death — ancestor-relations persist) but is also broken (the deceased is no longer in the lived populace).
  The substrate carries the relation at $\top$ until a morphism resolves the contradiction.

2. **Persistent $\bot$ on cells where settlement is due.** The deceased's role in the tiyospaye — their participation in council, their part in ceremony, their contribution to the camp — leaves cells unsettled that should be settled.
  Who now speaks for what they spoke for?
  Who now carries what they carried?
  The substrate marks the unsettlement with $\bot$ until reassignment.

If these disturbances persist, the bereaved (a person, a tiyospaye, in the limit case the polity) is structurally out of wicozani.

## The morphism's substrate effect

The wiping-of-tears morphism, performed approximately one year after the loss (or at an appropriate interval for the polity's grief), produces:

1. **Resolution of $\top$ on grief-cells.** The relation $\langle \text{the bereaved}, \texttt{stands-in-relation-with}, \text{the deceased}\rangle$ is *re-resolved* — not by negating the relation (the relation persists, now as an ancestor-relation) but by *re-classifying* it through closure-rule firing under the morphism.
  The cell $\langle \text{the bereaved}, \texttt{stands-in-ancestor-relation-with}, \text{the deceased}\rangle$ settles at $\mathbf{t}$; the cell over the broken-by-death lived relation settles at $\mathbf{f}$ — both consistently, the contradiction resolved.

2. **Settlement of the $\bot$ cells.** The bereaved's role in the tiyospaye reorganizes around the morphism; cells over the deceased's responsibilities are reassigned, settled, and witnessed.
  The ceremony's communal speaking (in the contemporary form) is part of this substrate work: participants name what they carry from the loss, and the naming settles the cells.

3. **Polity-quiescence restoration.** The aggregate effect is that the bereaved entity — person, tiyospaye, polity — moves from a state of disrupted settling back into wicozani.

## Restorative governance as substrate machinery

This morphism is one of the polity's principal *restorative governance* operations (it sits alongside the predicated-governance object [restorative-accountability](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/sociology-science-services-section/services/work-science-services-section/services/predicated-work-services-section/services/predicated-governance-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/restorative-accountability.md), of which it is a Lakota instance).
The polity does not require a punitive apparatus to maintain itself across loss; it has substrate morphisms that repair the relational structure directly.

The contrast with punitive substrate machinery is sharp.
A punitive operation produces cells of the form $\langle \text{offender}, \texttt{liable-for}, \text{harm}\rangle$ and downstream cells over sanction, separation, and confinement.
A restorative operation like the wiping-of-tears produces cells of the form $\langle \text{relation}, \texttt{repaired}\rangle$ and downstream cells over re-integration of the affected parties into the polity's settling.
Different cells, different closure-rule subsystems, different regimes.

## Polity-scale application

The morphism is performed at the polity scale after collective losses.
Historically: after the massacre at Wounded Knee, after the major battles and dispossessions, after epidemics.
In contemporary practice: after suicide losses in a community, after major accidents, after collective traumas.
The polity-scale application is not a metaphorical extension of the person-scale rite — it is the same morphism operating on a substrate of larger scope.
The conditions for proper performance scale with the scope; a polity-scale wiping-of-tears typically involves multiple wičháša wakȟáŋ and a wider participant set, but it is the same substrate operation.

## What this morphism does not do

The morphism does not erase the loss.
The ancestor-relation cell $\langle \text{the bereaved}, \texttt{stands-in-ancestor-relation-with}, \text{the deceased}\rangle$ persists at $\mathbf{t}$ after the morphism; the relation is permanent.
What the morphism does is *resolve the contradiction* — the bereaved no longer carries a $\top$ on the grief-cell, they carry an ancestor-relation that is itself wakȟáŋ-marked and part of the polity's living substrate.
Grief becomes ancestor-relation; ancestor-relation is sustained by [wanáǧi yuhápi](../../../../indigenous/theories/lakotaean-philosophy/concepts/seven-rites.md) and the other rites that maintain the living-and-ancestor substrate.
