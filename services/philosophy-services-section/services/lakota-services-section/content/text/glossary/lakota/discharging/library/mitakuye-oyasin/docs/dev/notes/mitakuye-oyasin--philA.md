# Mitákuye Oyás'iŋ

Mitákuye oyás'iŋ — "all my relatives" or "we are all related."
The phrase that most concisely *names* the Lakota relational metaphysics.
It is the *invocation* of the relational field — spoken at the close of prayer, at the close of ceremony, in the act of orienting oneself toward what is already the case.
The field itself is [Íŋyaŋ](iyan.md) — the originating-and-emptied source whose self-pouring-out *is* the relations in which every being stands.
Mitákuye oyás'iŋ does not produce the field; it *invokes* it.

## What the phrase does

The phrase has the grammatical structure of an *invocation*: *mi-tȟakúye* ("my relatives," with the first-person possessive prefix) + *oyás'iŋ* ("all").
To speak the phrase is to perform the relational orientation — to declare oneself in relation to all-relatives, to acknowledge the standing-in-relation that is already the case.

This invocation-function matters for the metaphysical reading.
The phrase does not assert a controversial claim that the speaker is defending.
It performs a *recognition* — a re-cognition — of what is already structurally the case in the Lakota cosmos.
The field of relations is there because of the cosmogonic self-emptying of [Íŋyaŋ](iyan.md); the phrase is the speaker's act of *taking up position* in that field.

## The metaphysical claim

The phrase invokes the structural fact that every being stands in kin-relation with every other being.
Humans, animals, plants, the standing-ones (trees), the rooted-ones, the swimming-ones, the four-leggeds, the wingeds, the crawlers, the elements (earth, water, fire, air), the stones, the places, the ancestors, the spirits — all are relatives.
This is not metaphor and not sentimental extension.
It is the substantive Lakota understanding of what is the case: the world *is* a kinship network because the cosmogonic self-emptying of Íŋyaŋ poured the relational substance into every being.

Vine Deloria Jr.'s *God Is Red* ([deloria-vine-jr-1973](../../../../../../sociology/theories/politics/theories/lakotaean-politics/references/deloria-vine-jr-1973.md)) develops the contrast with Western metaphysics.
Western traditions tend to organize reality as a hierarchy of separate substances (God / human / animal / plant / inanimate matter) and to ground morality in the differences between levels.
Mitákuye oyás'iŋ orients into a relational whole and grounds morality in the structure of obligations within the relations — *kin obligations*, not species hierarchy.

## Three scales of relation

The phrase fits within a three-scale articulation of relation in this subtheory:

- **[Tȟakúye](takuye.md)** — the individual relative; one specific kin-link.
- **Mitákuye oyás'iŋ** — the invocation of the totality of relations one stands in.
- **[Íŋyaŋ](iyan.md)** — the cosmogonic source-and-field; the relational substance itself.

Tȟakúye is the unit.
Mitákuye oyás'iŋ is the invocation of the unit-totality. Íŋyaŋ is the substance of which all units are made.
The three together specify how relation works in the Lakota cosmos: each specific kin-link is a tȟakúye; the totality of one's kin-links is what mitákuye oyás'iŋ invokes; the substance that constitutes them all is Íŋyaŋ's emptied-out body.

## What it is not

Several flattenings should be avoided:

- It is not a New Age "we are one" claim.
  Distinct beings remain distinct.
  The point is that they are *related*, not that they are the same.
  Mitakuye oyasin honors the distinction of beings while binding them in relation.
- It is not a generalized "interconnection" platitude.
  The Lakota claim is specific: the relations are *kin* relations, and they carry the specific obligations that kin obligations carry — generosity, attention, support, restraint.
  Generalized interconnection does not specify obligations; mitakuye oyasin does.
- It is not pan-Indian.
  Other Indigenous traditions have related claims (Anishinaabe, Haudenosaunee, Diné, and many others articulate variants), but each tradition's specific articulation matters.
  The Lakota articulation is mitakuye oyasin; it should not be conflated with similar-but-distinct claims in other traditions.

## How it grounds Lakota political life

The Lakota polity is constituted by kinship because Lakota metaphysics holds that kinship is the structure of reality itself.
Ella Deloria's ([deloria-ella1944](../../../../../../sociology/theories/politics/theories/lakotaean-politics/references/deloria-ella1944.md)) sociological observation — that Dakota and Lakota social organization is kinship organization — is not an arbitrary cultural choice.
It is the political enactment of the philosophical claim.
A polity that is structured by kinship is a polity that is *living in* the metaphysics of mitakuye oyasin.

The four virtues — wówačhantognaka, wóksape, wóohitika, wówačhiŋtȟaŋka ([four-virtues](four-virtues.md)) — are the qualities of conduct that sustain kin relations in the wider sense the metaphysics requires.
They are not aspirational ideals; they are the conduct that follows from taking mitakuye oyasin seriously.

## Continuity into the present

The phrase is in continuous use today across the Oceti Sakowin.
It closes prayer in the inipi, at the sun dance, at council, at gatherings of all kinds.
Its presence in contemporary speech is one of the most visible signs that the Lakota relational metaphysics is not historical — it is the operating philosophy of the people now.
