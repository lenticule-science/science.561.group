# Claim. A cedar flute phrase is bounded by single-breath capacity; phrase length, contour, and cadential pacing are direct consequences of the breath's physiological behavior across the exhalation.

**Claim.** A [phrase](../../teaching/explanations/phrase.md) in the [cedar-flute](../../../../b4/cedar-flute/cedar-flute.md) [courting-song](../../../../b4/courting-song/courting-song.md) repertoire is one exhalation: it begins with the breath's onset, unfolds across the breath's sustainable duration (typically 4 to 10 seconds), and ends as the breath weakens through the [cadence](teaching/explanations/cadence.md).
The next phrase begins after a rest in which the player inhales.
The phrase's length, the phrase's descending contour bias, and the phrase's terminal cadential pacing are all direct consequences of the breath's physiological behavior across the exhalation.
This is a *music-theoretic* claim with a physiological derivation, exactly parallel to "Baroque violin phrase length is determined by bow length."

**Argument.**

*Phrase length tracks lung capacity.* A typical [cedar-flute](../../../../b4/cedar-flute/cedar-flute.md) phrase runs between 4 and 10 seconds — the range determined by the player's lung capacity, the breath pressure required to sustain the instrument's tone, and the density of notes within the phrase (denser phrases require more breath per note, and so are shorter than sparser phrases at comparable lung capacity).
Phrases longer than 10 seconds are uncommon and push at the limits of comfortable lung capacity even for trained players.
Phrases substantially shorter than 4 seconds are uncommon except for terminal cadential phrases or in some compact song-types.
The phrase-length distribution is therefore not a free parameter of the music; it is set by the physiology of breathing.

*The descending phrase-contour is breath-driven.* Across the course of an exhalation, breath pressure naturally declines — the diaphragm progressively relaxes, the lung volume decreases, the available pressure at the [duct-flute](../../../../b4/duct-flute/duct-flute.md) wind way falls.
On a duct flute (and on bone whistles), pitch is reasonably stable across a wide breath-pressure range (the [bore](teaching/explanations/bore.md) sounds the fundamental at a range of breath pressures, not just at a single pressure), so the breath-pressure decline does not directly force a pitch change.
But the breath-pressure decline does affect the *expressive character* of the tone — softer, more decayed, less projecting — and the breath-pressure decline correlates with [embouchure](../../teaching/explanations/embouchure.md) relaxation and with the player's overall energy across the phrase.
The net result is that high-energy, high-pitched playing happens early in the breath, low-energy, lower-pitched playing happens late in the breath, and the typical phrase contour is therefore descending: beginning on a high scale-degree (often b7 or 8) at the start of the breath, descending through the scale to the [fundamental](../../teaching/explanations/fundamental.md) at the cadence at the end of the breath.

*The cadence is the breath's terminal moment.* The [cadence](teaching/explanations/cadence.md) of a phrase occupies the end of the breath, when breath pressure is fading.
The cadential gesture — typically a descending motion to the [tonic](../../teaching/explanations/fundamental.md), held with a [warble](../../../../b4/warble/warble.md) — exploits the breath's natural decay: the warble's amplitude fades as the breath fades, providing a natural decay shape for the phrase's terminal tone.
A player who tries to cadence in mid-breath (with significant breath remaining) produces a less natural-sounding cadence — the held tonic doesn't fade naturally, the warble is too forceful, the cadence sounds applied-from-outside rather than emerging from the phrase's own breath-arc.
Conversely, a player who under-shoots the breath (running out of breath before reaching the cadence) cannot complete the cadential gesture, and the phrase ends abruptly rather than with the held-tonic-warble decay.
The cadential gesture is therefore matched to the breath's terminal phase, and the breath's terminal phase shapes the cadential pacing.

*The breath-rest between phrases is the inhale.* The rest between [phrases](../../teaching/explanations/phrase.md) within a [strophe](../../teaching/explanations/strophe.md) is the player's inhale — typically half a second to a second and a half — and is bounded above by the requirement to maintain the strophe's coherence (a too-long rest fragments the strophe) and below by the requirement to take in sufficient air for the next phrase (a too-short rest produces an underbreathed next phrase).
The inter-phrase rest is therefore not a freely-chosen musical parameter; it is the inhalation, set by the player's breath-pace.

*This claim distinguishes cedar-flute music from metric ensemble music.* In a [drum](../../../../b4/drum/drum.md)-anchored ensemble like an [honor-song](../../../../b4/honor-song/honor-song.md) performance, phrases are fitted to the metric pulse: their length is a definite number of drum-beats, their pacing is constrained by the metric grid, and the breath-rest between phrases is fitted to a definite number of rest-beats.
The breath constraint is still physically present (singers and whistle-players still have to breathe), but the phrase organization is metric rather than breath-paced.
The cedar-flute solo idiom, lacking the drum's metric grid, organizes purely by breath, which is why the [rubato](../../teaching/explanations/rubato.md) pacing is fundamental to the idiom rather than being a flexible variation on an underlying meter.

*Music-theoretic consequences.* The breath-bounded phrase is the unit at which the cedar-flute idiom is most naturally analyzed.
Compositional decisions in a [courting-song](../../../../b4/courting-song/courting-song.md) — how to organize the melodic material into phrases, where to place [ornaments](../../teaching/explanations/ornament.md), how to pace the [cadences](teaching/explanations/cadence.md) — are decisions about how to deploy musical content within a sequence of breath-bounded units.
A music theory of the cedar flute that did not center the phrase-as-breath would miss the unit at which the music is actually organized.

**Structures produced.**

- [phrase-archetype-set](../../../code/structures/phrase-archetype-set.md) — the named set of phrase-shapes that appear in the repertoire, with their specific pitch-contours and characteristic cadential pacings.

**Transformers produced.**

None directly — this claim establishes the phrase's defining property and its musical consequences, rather than an operation on phrases.
