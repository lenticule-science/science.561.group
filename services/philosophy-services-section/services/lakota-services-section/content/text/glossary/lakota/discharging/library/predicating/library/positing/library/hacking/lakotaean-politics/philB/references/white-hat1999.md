# White Hat 1999 — Reading and Writing the Lakota Language

**White Hat, Albert, Sr. (1999). *Reading and Writing the Lakota Language*.
Edited by Jael Kampfe.
Foreword by Vine Deloria Jr. Salt Lake City: University of Utah Press.**

## Annotation

Albert White Hat Sr. (1938–2013, Sicangu Lakota, Rosebud Reservation) was the first Native Lakota speaker to publish a Lakota textbook and glossary.
He taught Lakota at Sinte Gleska University (the first tribal-based university in the United States, on the Rosebud Reservation) from 1975 onward and headed the Lakota Studies Department.

The book's distinctive contribution is methodological — as Vine Deloria Jr. writes in the foreword, White Hat "reverses the traditional method of explaining language by showing through examples, anecdotes and lessons the world view and values of the Brule Lakota, how people speak and think."
Lakota grammar is taught alongside Lakota philosophy: the language carries the worldview, and the worldview shows in the language's structures, and neither can be properly learned without the other.

White Hat also documents the impact of acculturation on the Lakota language — how Lakota evolved under non-Indian influences, what was lost, what changed, what persisted.

## Why this is a political reference

The Lakota language itself is a political artifact.
The settler-state's boarding-school system was designed to extinguish it; its survival and revitalization is part of the Oceti Sakowin's continuing political life.
White Hat's work — both his textbook and his role at Sinte Gleska — is part of that political work, and the textbook stands as a primary source on Lakota orthography (the modern standard form most often used in this subtheory), pronunciation, and the language-philosophy integration.

The book is also the primary source for the orthography used here.
Where this subtheory writes *Pȟahá Sápa*, *Očhéthi Šakówiŋ*, *thiyóšpaye*, *omníčiye*, *akíčhita*, the orthographic conventions follow the White Hat / Sinte Gleska / modern standard form.

## Key concepts sourced here

- Lakota orthography (used throughout this subtheory)
- Lakota-language grounding for political-philosophical terms
- Pedagogy that teaches language and worldview together
