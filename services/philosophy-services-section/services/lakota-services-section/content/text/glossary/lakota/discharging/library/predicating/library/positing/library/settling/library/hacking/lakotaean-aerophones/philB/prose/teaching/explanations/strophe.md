# Strophe

A strophe is a complete melodic unit consisting of several [phrases](./phrase.md) that, together, constitute one statement of the song's melodic material — the song's "theme" or "verse" — and that becomes the repeating unit of the song's overall [strophic-form](./strophic-form.md).
Where the [phrase](./phrase.md) is the smallest complete melodic unit (one breath-bounded melodic gesture), the strophe is the next level up: it is the smallest unit at which the song is recognizable *as that song*.
A single phrase, in isolation, is not usually a song; the song begins when a sequence of phrases together establishes a melodic identity, and that identifying sequence is the strophe.

A typical cedar-flute [courting-song](./courting-song.md) strophe consists of two to four [phrases](./phrase.md), arranged in a sequence that establishes the song's melodic identity through phrase-internal contours, inter-phrase relations (parallelism, contrast, motivic development), and a definite terminal [cadence](./cadence.md) at the strophe's end.
The total duration of a strophe is therefore typically in the range of 15 to 40 seconds — several breaths' worth of music — depending on the number and length of constituent phrases and the player's [rubato](./rubato.md) pacing.

The strophe-internal organization of phrases varies by song-type and song-tradition.
A common pattern in [courting-song](./courting-song.md) is a two-phrase strophe of question-and-answer character: the first phrase establishes a melodic figure that does not fully resolve (perhaps ending on scale-degree b3 or 4 with a partial cadence), and the second phrase responds with a related figure that resolves fully to the [fundamental](./fundamental.md) at the strophe's end.
Another common pattern is a three-phrase strophe of statement-development-cadence character: the first phrase introduces a melodic idea, the second develops or varies the idea, the third concludes with a definite cadence.
Longer strophes (four or more phrases) appear in songs of greater elaboration.

The strophe is the unit at which [strophic-variation](../../code/transformers/strophic-variation.md) operates.
A [strophic-form](./strophic-form.md) song does not present an entirely new melodic unit on each repetition — it presents *the same* strophe each time, with [ornament-substitution](../../code/transformers/ornament-substitution.md) and small rhythmic shift producing variation between strophes.
So a typical [courting-song](./courting-song.md) performance might present the strophe four to six times, with each strophe a recognizable variant of the same underlying melodic material.
The strophe-as-presented in any given performance instance is thus a specific instance of the underlying *strophe-type*; the listener hears both the variation across strophes (which provides interest and expressive content) and the persistence of the strophe-type across strophes (which provides the song's identity).

In vocal [wiilowan](./wiilowan.md) performance, a strophe carries a definite text — either lexical (Lakȟóta-language) or vocable (non-lexical sung syllables) or a combination of the two.
Successive strophes of the same song-type may carry the same text (literal repetition), variant texts (different verses of the same song), or vocable variations.
In cedar-flute [instrumentalization](./instrumentalization.md) of vocal song, the text-distinction collapses (the flute cannot pronounce text), and strophe-to-strophe variation is carried entirely by [ornament-substitution](../../code/transformers/ornament-substitution.md) and rhythmic shift.

The choice of how many strophes to perform in a given instance of the song is, in most contexts, at the player's discretion — there is no fixed canonical number for most cedar-flute repertoire.
Vocal-ensemble repertoire in some contexts has more definite conventions (the four-strophes-then-honor-beats convention of many Northern Plains ensemble songs is widely observed), but solo cedar-flute performance is freer.
The performance ends when the player decides it is complete — typically with a final strophe given a slightly more emphatic [cadence](./cadence.md) than the preceding ones.
