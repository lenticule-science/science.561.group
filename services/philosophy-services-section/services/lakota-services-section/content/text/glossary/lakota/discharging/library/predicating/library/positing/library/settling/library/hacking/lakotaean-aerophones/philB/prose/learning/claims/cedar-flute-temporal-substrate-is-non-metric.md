# Claim. The cedar courting flute's temporal substrate is non-metric and breath-paced; phrases are bounded by breath capacity rather than by a fixed pulse.

**Claim.** The cedar courting flute does not impose or follow a regular underlying pulse.
Its temporal substrate is **non-metric**: time is organized at the phrase level (5–15 seconds, bounded by breath capacity) and at the song level (strophic, 3–7 strophes), but not at a sub-phrase metric level.
Tempo within a phrase is rubato — accelerating, decelerating, and dwelling on individual notes are normal expressive devices.

The flute's temporal substrate contrasts sharply with the whistle-with-drum context (see [whistle-drum-heterophony](./whistle-drum-heterophony.md)), which imports the drum's pulse and becomes metric.
The flute in isolation is the only fully non-metric instrument in the Lakotaean aerophone family.

**Argument.**

*Absence of regular pulse in documented recordings.* Recordings of Densmore-era players, Richard Fool Bull, Belo Cozad, and Kevin Locke do not exhibit a regular underlying pulse against which note durations are measured.
Note durations are continuously variable; phrase peaks are sustained for variable durations; cadences slow naturally.

*Phrase-level organization.* Phrases consistently align with a single breath.
Phrase duration varies with the player's breath and with the emotional shape of the piece.
Phrase boundaries are marked by breath rests, not by any periodic boundary.

*Strophic form-level organization.* Songs are typically organized as strophes (complete musical ideas, restated with variation).
The strophe is the highest temporal organization; there is no super-strophic metric scaffold.

*Contrast with metric idioms.* Common-practice Western music, most metric folk idioms, and the drum-led ceremonial song share a regular underlying pulse against which note durations are measured.
The cedar flute does not.
This is a positive musical feature of the instrument's idiom, not an absence to be filled.
