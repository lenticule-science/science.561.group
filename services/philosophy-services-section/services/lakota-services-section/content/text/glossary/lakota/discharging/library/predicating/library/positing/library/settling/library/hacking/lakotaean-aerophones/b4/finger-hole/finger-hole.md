A finger hole is a hole drilled into the side wall of a wind instrument's [bore](./bore.md), positioned so that the player can cover or uncover it with a finger during play.
The hole's musical function is to *shorten the effective air column* when it is uncovered: rather than the air column extending from the [duct-flute](../duct-flute/duct-flute.md) sound source to the foot of the instrument, the air column effectively terminates at the position of the open hole (with some end-correction).
A shorter air column produces a shorter wavelength, hence a higher [fundamental](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/fundamental.md), so uncovering a finger hole raises the sounding pitch.

The exact pitch produced by uncovering a given hole depends on the hole's position along the [bore](./bore.md), the hole's diameter (a larger hole shifts the effective termination closer to its actual position; a smaller hole produces a softer termination farther down the bore), and the cross-section of the bore at the hole's position.
For a cylindrical bore of fixed dimensions, the conventional placement of holes determines which scale-degrees the instrument produces.
The [cedar-flute](../cedar-flute/cedar-flute.md) carries six finger holes whose conventional positions, on the standard Plains instrument, produce the five non-fundamental degrees of the [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md) plus the octave — when the holes are uncovered in sequence from the foot of the instrument upward (the foot-most hole is "hole 1," and so on toward the head).

The fact that the six standard holes of the cedar flute produce specifically the [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md), and not some other scale, is a *historical-musical* fact about hole placement convention, not an acoustic necessity.
A cedar flute with the same bore but holes placed at different positions would produce a different scale.
Other Plains and Northern flute traditions exist with different hole-placement conventions (and hence different scales), and there is some range of micro-variation across cedar-flute makers in the exact placement of holes (which is why the cents-deviations from 12-tone equal temperament vary slightly across instruments).
The choice of hole placement that defines the standard cedar-flute scale is a *theoretical* choice — it is the choice that established the [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md) as the playable material on which the [courting-song](../courting-song/courting-song.md) repertoire is built.

The musical use of finger holes on the cedar flute extends beyond the basic open-or-closed binary that produces the standard scale degrees.
Three further uses appear in idiomatic playing.
First, *cross-fingering*: covering a hole below an open hole (rather than the standard pattern of all holes from the foot to a given position covered) produces a pitch slightly different from the standard scale degree, useful for occasional non-scale tones or for fine tuning.
Second, *partial covering*: covering a hole only partially (leaving a thin crescent open) produces a pitch between the fully-covered and fully-open positions, the acoustic basis of the [finger-slide](../finger-slide/finger-slide.md) ornament.
Third, *rapid covering and uncovering* of a single hole produces a [finger-pop](../finger-pop/finger-pop.md) (a sharp momentary pitch lift), and rapid alternation between covered and uncovered produces a [trill](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/trill.md).

The finger-hole convention is what makes the cedar flute a *scaled* instrument: each hole-state corresponds to a definite scale-degree, and the player's musical task is to move between hole-states in sequences that produce the melodic shapes of the [courting-song](../courting-song/courting-song.md) repertoire.
The [cedar-flute-fingering-chart](../../philB/code/structures/cedar-flute-fingering-chart.md) structure formalizes this mapping from hole-state to scale-degree.
