# Marshall 2002 — The Lakota Way

**Marshall, Joseph M., III.
(2002). *The Lakota Way: Stories and Lessons for Living*.
New York: Penguin / Compass.
(Subsequent editions and a youth edition extend the project.)**

## Annotation

Joseph M.
Marshall III is Sicangu (Brulé) Lakota, raised on the Rosebud Indian Reservation; Lakota is his first language. *The Lakota Way* is a popular but substantively serious presentation of the Lakota virtue tradition: chapters organized around twelve qualities the tradition holds central — bravery (*wóohitika*), fortitude (*wówačhiŋtȟaŋka*), generosity (*wówačhantognaka* / *čháŋteyuke*), wisdom (*wóksape*), respect, honor, perseverance, love, humility, sacrifice, truth, and compassion.
Each chapter pairs a story drawn from oral tradition with reflective commentary that locates the virtue in everyday Lakota life and in the political-relational shape of Lakota personhood.

The book's central methodological move is to present the virtues *as they are taught* — through story, repetition, example, and apprenticeship — rather than as a doctrinal list.
This is itself a substantive claim: the virtues are not propositional ethics; they are dispositional cultivation through narrative inheritance.

## Why this is a political reference

The four virtues most often cited in older sources (woksape, wówačhantognaka, wóohitika, wówačhiŋtȟaŋka) function as the *standards of leadership* in the Lakota polity.
A naca's authority is the recognition of conduct in these virtues; a naca who fails them loses standing.
The political bindingness of leadership is the relational bindingness of these virtues toward one's relatives.
Marshall's treatment is the most accessible contemporary Lakota statement of how this works.

## Related Marshall works

Marshall's other books on Lakota history and political life — including *The Day the World Ended at Little Bighorn* (Viking, 2007), *The Journey of Crazy Horse* (Viking, 2004), and *On Behalf of the Wolf and the First Peoples* (Red Crane Books, 1995) — are companion sources. *The Lakota Way of Strength and Courage* and *Walking with Grandfather* extend the virtue-and-story method.
