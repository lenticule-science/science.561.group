# Paha Sapa

Pȟahá Sápa — the Black Hills.
The mountains in what is now western South Dakota that are, for the Lakota and the wider Oceti Sakowin, the heart of the people's relation to land.
The phrase commonly used is *Wamakognaka E'cante* — "the heart of everything that is."

## Why this is a political concept

Paha Sapa is not a piece of property the Lakota lost.
It is a constitutive relation of the polity.
The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty between the United States and the Oceti Sakowin acknowledged Lakota possession of the Black Hills; the United States subsequently violated the treaty after gold was found in the hills, seized them through the Black Hills Act of 1877, and confined the Lakota to reservations.
In *United States v.
Sioux Nation of Indians* (1980), the U.S. Supreme Court found that the seizure was unconstitutional and awarded the Oceti Sakowin compensation — at the time approximately $106 million, now (with accumulated interest in a Treasury account) well over a billion dollars.

The Lakota have continually refused to take the money.
The refusal is not a tactical position awaiting better terms.
It is a structural political claim: the Black Hills are not a property whose loss can be commuted to a cash settlement.
The relation that the cash payment would settle is not a property relation.
The hills are a relative; the polity's standing as a polity is bound up with their return.

## Refusal as political form

The refusal of the Black Hills compensation award is one of the clearest contemporary cases of the *politics of refusal* analyzed by Audra Simpson ([simpson2014](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/sociology-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/bibliographies/simpson2014.md)) and Glen Coulthard ([coulthard2014](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/sociology-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/bibliographies/coulthard2014.md)).
The refusal refuses the *terms* the settler state offers: that the dispute be settled, that the value be quantified, that the Indigenous polity's relation to land be commensurated with the settler state's property-frame.
To take the money would be to accept that frame; refusal preserves the polity's claim that its relation to Paha Sapa is not a property relation at all.

## Continuity and contemporary politics

The contemporary struggle around Paha Sapa is continuous with the political life of the polity.
Nick Estes ([estes2019](../../philB/references/estes2019.md)) treats Standing Rock — the protest at the Dakota Access Pipeline crossing — and the Paha Sapa struggle together as moments of one ongoing Indigenous resistance: the same polity, the same political form, the same refusal of incorporation into settler-state categories of land and authority.

Mount Rushmore, carved into the eastern face of Pȟahá Sápa, is — from the Lakota political-cosmological perspective — a settler-state inscription on the heart of the people's relation.
The contemporary movement to remove the monument is not merely iconoclastic.
It is continuous with the polity's standing claim to Paha Sapa.

## Note on language

The Lakota name *Pȟahá Sápa* (sometimes written *Paha Sapa*) is the people's name for the place.
Mount Rushmore's actual mountain has a Lakota name distinct from "Mount Rushmore."
Using the Lakota name is itself a small political act in line with the wider refusal of settler-state naming.
