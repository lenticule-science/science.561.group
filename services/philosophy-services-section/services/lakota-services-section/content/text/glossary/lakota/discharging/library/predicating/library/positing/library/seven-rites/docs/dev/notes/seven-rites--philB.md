# The seven rites

The seven sacred rites brought to the Oceti Sakowin by Pte Saŋ Wiŋ (the White Buffalo Calf Woman), in the form most consistently transmitted in the recorded literature through Black Elk's account in Joseph Epes Brown's *The Sacred Pipe* ([brown1953](../../../../hacking/lakotaean-politics/philB/references/brown1953.md)).

The seven, with their conventional Lakota names and brief glosses:

1. **Iníkağapi** — the sweat lodge / inipi.
  Purification, prayer, and renewal in the lodge of stone, water, and steam.
2. **Haŋbléčheyapi** — "crying for a dream" / the vision quest.
  Solitary fasting and prayer to seek vision and guidance.
3. **Wanáǧi Yuhápi** — the keeping of the soul.
  The ceremony by which the spirit of one who has died is kept and then released, traditionally over a year.
4. **Wiwáŋyaŋg Wačhípi** — the sun dance.
  The great annual gathering ceremony of the people; the dancers' offering on behalf of the people.
5. **Huŋkápi** — the making-of-relatives ceremony (see [hunka](../../../../hacking/lakotaean-politics/b4/hunka/hunka.md)).
  The formal binding of two persons (or wider groups) into a kin relation with the obligations of relatives.
6. **Išnáthi Awíčhalowaŋpi** — the preparing-of-a-young-woman ceremony.
  The coming-of-age ceremony for a young woman entering womanhood.
7. **Tȟapá Wakȟáyap** — the throwing of the ball.
  A ceremony in which a young girl throws a sacred ball to participants who reach to receive it, with the ball understood as wakȟáŋ wisdom that comes to those who reach for it.

## Why the seven rites are philosophical content

The rites are not separable from the philosophical claims that ground them.
They are *practices of* the metaphysics.
The inipi enacts the relational structure of mitakuye oyasin — the stones, the water, the steam, the persons in the lodge are all relatives, and the ceremony is the work of attending to those relations.
The sun dance enacts the wičhózani of the people: the dancers' bodily offering is on behalf of the people, and the wholeness of the people is what the ceremony seeks.
The huŋkápi enacts the political extension of kinship that the polity-form requires.
The wanáǧi yuhápi enacts the relation between the living and the ancestors that the metaphysics holds is continuous.

For a tradition in which knowledge is constituted through ceremony rather than only through propositional inscription, the rites are not adjunct religious life.
They are the medium in which the philosophical content is held and transmitted.

## Source caveats

Brown's *Sacred Pipe* is the most-cited record but is mediated (through Ben Black Elk's interpretation, through Brown's editorial work, through Black Elk's late-life perspective which integrated Catholic experience).
Raymond DeMallie's *The Sixth Grandfather* (Bison Books, 1984) is a critical-edition companion to Neihardt's *Black Elk Speaks* and is useful as a comparative resource.
Damian Costello and Michael Steltenkamp have written on the integration of Black Elk's Catholic and Lakota practice, raising questions that contemporary Lakota readers continue to engage.

Authority for the substantive content of the rites — what they are, how they are done, who can lead them — rests with the wičháša wakȟáŋ and other Lakota knowledge holders in living tradition, not with the printed sources.
The printed sources are useful starting points, especially for readers approaching from outside the tradition; the living tradition is the proper authority.
