# Wiping-of-tears

Ištámniyaŋ Wičhákičhipakiŋta Pi — "the wiping of their tears," the ceremony performed approximately one year after a death (or in contemporary practice, after a major collective loss) that ends the formal mourning period and returns the grievers to full participation in the life of the people.
The ceremony's components include the offering of sage water, ceremonial combing of the hair, the literal wiping of tears, and (in contemporary practice) communal speaking by participants about the loss they carry.

The shorter ceremonial form *Wašígila* is also recorded.

## Why this is a political concept

A polity sustains itself across deaths, losses, and breaches; the question is *how*.
The wiping-of-tears is one of the polity's structural repairs.
In the period of formal mourning, the bereaved have a recognized political-social status — they are not expected to take ordinary roles in council, hunt, or ceremony at full participation.
The wiping-of-tears closes that period in a way that does not require the grief to be forgotten, but does return the bereaved to full participation in the polity's ongoing life.

For the polity, this matters at multiple scales.
After a personal loss, the ceremony repairs the affected tiyospaye and its relations to the wider oyate.
After a collective loss — a massacre, an epidemic, a defeat — the ceremony has historically been performed at larger scale to repair the people as a whole.
In contemporary practice, the wiping-of-tears has been adapted to serve communities affected by suicide loss, accident, and the cumulative loss imposed by colonial history; the structural function (a ceremonial close to grief that returns participants to full standing) remains.

## Restorative governance

The wiping-of-tears is one of the Lakota polity's instances of *restorative governance* — political-ceremonial work that repairs harm without producing punishment.
The polity does not need a carceral apparatus to maintain itself across loss and harm; it has structural ceremonies that do that work.
This is not a romantic claim.
It is a specific structural feature, attested in the ethnographic and contemporary record, that distinguishes the Lakota polity-form from polities whose response to harm is concentrated in punitive institutions.

## Continuity into the present

The ceremony is widely performed today, both in its traditional grief-closing role and in adapted forms responding to contemporary collective harms.
It is one of the most visibly continuous instances of Lakota ceremony-as-governance.
