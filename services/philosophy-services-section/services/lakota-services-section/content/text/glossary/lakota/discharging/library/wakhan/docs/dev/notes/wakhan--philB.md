# Wakhan

Wakȟáŋ — a quality, conventionally translated as "sacred," "mysterious," or "holy," though each English gloss is a partial fit.
Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka — "the Great Wakȟáŋ" — names the encompassing sacredness of the whole, often (and confusingly) translated as "the Great Spirit" in older non-Lakota sources.

## Why "Great Spirit" is a mistranslation

Vine Deloria Jr.'s *God Is Red* ([deloria-vine-jr-1973](../../../../../../sociology/theories/politics/theories/lakotaean-politics/references/deloria-vine-jr-1973.md)) is the canonical critique.
Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka is not a Christian-style God — not a person, not a creator separate from creation, not a being who issues commandments and judges souls.
It is the Great Sacredness, the encompassing mystery, the relational whole understood as sacred-in-itself.
To translate it as "the Great Spirit" imports a Christian theological frame that the Lakota tradition does not share and that disfigures the concept.

The mistranslation has deep historical roots: missionary and government translators in the 19th century needed a Lakota referent for the Christian God they were imposing, and Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka was the term they appropriated for the purpose.
The appropriation produced a long-running confusion that contemporary Lakota scholarship continues to work to disentangle.

## Wakhan as quality, not domain

The sacred in the Lakota frame is not a separate domain (church-vs-world, religious-vs-secular) but a quality that things, places, persons, and acts can carry.
A specific place can be wakȟáŋ.
A person — a wičháša wakȟáŋ — can be in standing where they bear and transmit the sacred.
A ceremony can render the participants wakȟáŋ for its duration.
A pipe is wakȟáŋ.

This means the relationship between sacred and everyday is not the relationship between two domains.
Properly attended, the everyday is full of the sacred — the morning, the spring water, the meal, the encounter with another relative.
Improperly attended, ceremony itself can become hollow.
The sacred is a quality of relation, not a region of being.

## Wicasa wakan / winyan wakan

The persons whose standing is constituted by the sacred — the holy people, the medicine people.
A wičháša wakȟáŋ (or wíŋyaŋ wakȟáŋ for women) is one who has done the work — hanblecheya (vision quest), ceremonial apprenticeship, lifelong cultivation — to bear and transmit ceremony.
The role is not self-declared; it is recognized by the people and by other holy people.
There is no church-officer track that produces wičháša wakȟáŋ; the position is constituted by demonstrated relationship with what is wakȟáŋ.

For the polity, the wičháša wakȟáŋ holds a position distinct from the [naca](../../../../../../sociology/theories/politics/theories/lakotaean-politics/concepts/naca.md) and the [akicita](../../../../../../sociology/theories/politics/theories/lakotaean-politics/concepts/akicita.md): not deliberative-executive, not enforcement, but the standing-with-the-sacred that makes council itself bindable through ceremony.
The pipe that binds council decisions is wakȟáŋ; the person who properly presents the pipe is exercising a position the polity recognizes.
