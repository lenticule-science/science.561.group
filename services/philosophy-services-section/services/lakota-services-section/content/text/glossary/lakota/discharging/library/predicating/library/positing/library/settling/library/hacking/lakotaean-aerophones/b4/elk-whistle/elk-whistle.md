The elk whistle is a single-pitch end-blown aerophone fashioned from elk material — most commonly a hollowed antler tine, occasionally a long bone — and voiced in a low to middle register, distinctly below the [eagle-wing-bone-whistle](../eagle-wing-bone-whistle/eagle-wing-bone-whistle.md).
Its tone is mournful, breathy, and broadly imitative of the bull elk's bugling vocalization, the call from which the instrument takes its name and its principal acoustic model.
The elk whistle is one of the smaller-repertoire aerophones of the Lakotaean tradition: it has narrower documented ceremonial use than the [cedar-flute](../cedar-flute/cedar-flute.md) or the [eagle-wing-bone-whistle](../eagle-wing-bone-whistle/eagle-wing-bone-whistle.md), and the music theory it admits is correspondingly narrower — but it has theoretical content nonetheless, organized around the same axes of [timbral envelope](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/ornament.md), [breath-pulse](../breath-pulse/breath-pulse.md), and pitch glide.

Construction varies by the material used.
Antler-tine whistles are bored or naturally hollow, with a small fipple-like edge cut near the blowing end; bone whistles parallel the eagle wing-bone construction at a larger bore size, producing a lower [fundamental](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/fundamental.md).
The wider bore relative to length and the somewhat less-precise edge geometry compared to the eagle wing-bone whistle gives the elk whistle a broader spectral envelope and a more diffuse attack: rather than the eagle whistle's narrow piercing tone, the elk whistle produces a tone with audible breath noise mixed into the fundamental, which is part of its imitative character.

The defining musical feature of the elk whistle is its capacity for a controlled pitch *glide* across the call — the player can bend the pitch downward through the course of a single sustained tone by relaxing breath pressure or adjusting [embouchure](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/embouchure.md), mimicking the descending portion of the elk's bugle.
This is a behavior the eagle wing-bone whistle does not foreground and the [cedar-flute](../cedar-flute/cedar-flute.md) achieves only through deliberate [finger-slide](../finger-slide/finger-slide.md) ornamentation.
On the elk whistle, the glide is intrinsic to the basic sustained-tone articulation rather than an added ornament.
The instrument's primary envelope types are: a sustained tone with descending glide (the canonical "bugle" envelope), a short attack with quick decay (a barking call used in some hunting contexts), and a rolled attack with broad sustain.

Repertoire and use are documented less systematically than for the cedar flute and eagle whistle.
Two principal use-contexts appear in the ethnographic and ethnomusicological record.
First, as a courting signal: a younger version or distant relative of the [courting-song](../courting-song/courting-song.md) function carried out by the cedar flute, the elk whistle serves as an audible signal to a specific addressee, with the elk-call imitation carrying a clear referential meaning grounded in the elk's role as a Plains animal associated with male display.
Second, as a hunting decoy or signaling device, where the imitative function is acoustic rather than musical in the narrow sense — but the boundary between signal and song is not as sharp as in European categories, and the whistle's rhythmic and timbral grammar is shared across the two contexts.

The elk whistle does not appear to play a regular role in [heterophony](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/heterophony.md) with [drum](../drum/drum.md) and [voice](./voice.md) in the way the eagle whistle does.
Its ensemble life, where documented, is solo or in dyadic exchange with another whistle or with a vocalist; its non-ensemble life — as solo signal, decoy, or expressive call — is at least as important to its theoretical character.
