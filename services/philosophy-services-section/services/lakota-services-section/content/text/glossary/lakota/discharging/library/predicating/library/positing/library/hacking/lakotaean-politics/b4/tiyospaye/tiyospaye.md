# Tiyospaye

Thiyóšpaye (commonly written *tiyospaye*) — the extended family group, encompassing multiple households related through descent, marriage, and ceremony, residing and moving together.
The tiyospaye is the basic political and economic unit of Lakota life — small enough that members know one another by name and by relation, large enough to constitute a working camp.

Etymologically, *thiyóšpaye* derives from *thí* (dwelling, lodge) and *óšpaye* (a portion or group split off); it names a group of relatives sharing the dwelling-camp.

## The tiyospaye as political unit

Ella Deloria ([deloria-ella1944](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../public-services-section/services/personal-services-section/services/emsenn-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/reference-articles/bibliographies/deloria-ella1944.md)) is the foundational source: the Dakota and Lakota social world is organized through kinship, and the kinship obligations within and across tiyospaye are the substance of political organization.
A tiyospaye has its own leadership — a respected senior man (often called *itancan* within the tiyospaye, though this term and *naca* are sometimes used interchangeably in the sources) — who coordinates the daily life of the camp, with his authority resting on demonstrated capacity for [generosity, wisdom, bravery, and fortitude](../../../../../../philosophy/theories/indigenous/theories/lakotaean-philosophy/concepts/four-virtues.md).

Multiple tiyospaye together form an [oyate](oyate.md).
The relation is genuine federation: each tiyospaye retains its internal life and the right to withdraw from collective action with which it does not agree (see [consensus-by-withdrawal](../consensus-by-withdrawal/consensus-by-withdrawal.md)).
The oyate's [omniciye](../omniciye/omniciye.md) — the council assembled around the council fire — convenes representatives across the tiyospaye to deliberate matters affecting them all.

## Continuity and adaptation

The tiyospaye form has persisted through the reservation period and into the present, though its operations have been disrupted, surveilled, and re-categorized by United States Indian policy.
The settler-state's reservation-enrollment system and the imposition of nuclear-family-shaped administrative categories work against the tiyospaye's organization, but contemporary Lakota communities continue to live tiyospaye-organized life despite this pressure.
Nick Estes ([estes2019](../../philB/references/estes2019.md)) treats this continuity as central to the contemporary Oceti Sakowin political life.

## Why this is a political concept, not a kinship trivium

Outsider treatments of Lakota society sometimes describe the tiyospaye as a "kinship unit" or "social structure" while treating the oyate's council or the chiefs as the "political institutions."
Ella Deloria's foundational point is that this separation is wrong: the kinship organization *is* the political organization.
The naca's authority is the authority of a senior relative whose generosity and wisdom have made him a relative-to-all in the tiyospaye and oyate.
The akicita's enforcement is the enforcement of obligations that hold *because* the people are related.
Removing kinship leaves no politics behind.

## Sources

Ella Deloria's *Speaking of Indians* ([deloria-ella1944](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../public-services-section/services/personal-services-section/services/emsenn-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/reference-articles/bibliographies/deloria-ella1944.md)) is the foundational source for the kinship-as-organization claim.
