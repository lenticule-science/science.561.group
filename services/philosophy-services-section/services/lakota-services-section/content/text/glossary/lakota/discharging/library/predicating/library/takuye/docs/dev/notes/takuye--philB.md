# Tȟakúye / Wótakuye

Tȟakúye — *relative*, *kin-relation* — is the relational substance that two beings *have in common* in the Lakota cosmos.
It is the condition-form that complements [woótakuye](wotakuye.md) (the activity-form): tȟakúye is what *is the case* between relatives, woótakuye is *what they do*.
In the relational framework, tȟakúye is the durable structure that holds-relation-open over time — the "relative" in the framework's sense of the first stabilized form within the relational field.

## Tȟakúye / Wótakuye distinction

Two closely-related forms appear in the older recordings and contemporary usage:

- **Tȟakúye** — *relative* (noun, used of persons and other beings); also *a kin-relation* abstractly.
  The most common form.
  Walker's informants and DeMallie's reworkings use this form predominantly.
- **Wótakuye** — *kinship*, *family*, the abstract-collective form (with the wó- nominalizer that turns the relational stem into an abstract noun).
  Used in contemporary Lakota teaching for "kinship as a system" or "family as a thing."
  Possibly post-Walker as a frequent term.

For this subtheory: **tȟakúye** names the *unit-of-relation* (one relative, one specific kin-link); **wótakuye** names the *system-of-relations* (the kinship-substance as a whole, the field of all tȟakúye-links).
The distinction tracks the framework's structural distinction between an individual *relative* (one stable relational unit) and *relation* (the field of all such units).

## DeMallie's gloss

The clearest single articulation comes from Raymond DeMallie's reworking of the Walker corpus on Dakota/Lakota kinship: **tȟakúye is "something they have in common"** — *very broad, not restricted to blood or marriage but to relationships of all kinds*.
"To be a good relative is the most important thing that a Dakota can be… being Dakota is being related."

This breadth is structurally important.
Tȟakúye is not narrowly biological.
The relation between persons by descent is tȟakúye; the relation between persons made-kin by huŋkápi is tȟakúye; the relation between a person and the four-leggeds is tȟakúye (those are also relatives); the relation between a person and a place is tȟakúye (Pȟahá Sápa is *tȟakúye* to the Lakota people, not merely *territory*).
The substance is the same — what they have in common — across all these kinds.

## Tȟakúye as the durable relative

In the relational framework, the *relative* is the minimal durable structure within the relational field — the first form that *holds-relation-open across time*.
Relations need to be sustained by woótakuye (kinship-as-act), but they also need a durable form to be sustained *as*.
Tȟakúye is that form.

A specific tȟakúye-link — say, the kin-link between two cousins by descent — is a durable structure that persists across time even when the cousins are not currently engaged in active kinship-conduct.
The link is not the conduct; the conduct is what *animates* the link.
But the link can be there even when temporarily un-animated.
This is what makes reclamation possible: a Lakota person who was separated from kin by boarding school can return to the tȟakúye that always-existed and re-animate it with woótakuye-conduct.
The relation was never severed; it was only un-animated.

This is also why iyúhá-šni does not sever relation: the *withdrawing* refuses-an-engagement, but the tȟakúye remains.
The structural durability of tȟakúye is what makes the right of withdrawal politically viable.
See [iyuha-sni](iyuha-sni.md).

## Tȟakúye and mitakuye oyasin

The phrase [mitakuye oyasin](mitakuye-oyasin.md) — "all my relatives" — is the invocation of the field-of-all-tȟakúye.
The *mi-* prefix is "my"; *takuye* is the relative; *oyásiŋ* is "all."
The phrase points at the totality of relations one stands in.
Mitakuye oyasin is therefore *the invocation of the field of relations one is in*; tȟakúye is *each specific relation within that field*.
The two are related as field-and-unit: mitakuye oyasin is the whole, tȟakúye is each particular standing-in-relation.

In the captain's structural reading: tȟakúye is the *individual relative*; mitakuye oyasin is the *invocation*; Íŋyaŋ is the *field-itself* (the substance of all relations).
The three together specify how relation works at three scales: the unit, the invoked-totality, and the cosmogonic-source.
See [iyan](iyan.md) on Íŋyaŋ as the relational field.

## Tȟakúye as substrate-condition

The framework's claim that the world is structured by tȟakúye is *substantive*, not metaphorical.
The Lakota cosmos is *literally* made of relational substance — each being is what-Íŋyaŋ-poured-out-into-it; each pair of beings stands in the relational substance Íŋyaŋ's pouring-out produced.
To recognize this is not to "treat" beings as relatives; it is to *recognize the case*.
The relations are not added on top of beings; they are what beings are made of.

This metaphysical commitment is what makes the Lakota relational ethic non-optional.
To be ethical with a being is not to choose-to-treat-them-as-a-relative; it is to *recognize that they are* a relative.
The ethical demand is recognition, not generosity-as-supererogation.
The non-recognition of relation is what is anomalous, not the recognition of it.
