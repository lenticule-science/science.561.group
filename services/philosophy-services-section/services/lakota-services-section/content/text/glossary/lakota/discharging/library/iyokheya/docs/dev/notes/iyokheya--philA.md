# Iyókheya

Iyókheya — *to set apart* — is the act of marking-as-distinct that produces the first boundary in the relational field.
It is what Škáŋ enacts on the originating paradox: the moving-principle, rising from Íŋyaŋ's self-emptying, *sets apart* what was undivided.
Iyókheya produces the first recognition — the first thing that is *not* Íŋyaŋ-alone-in-Haŋhépi.
In the relational framework, Iyókheya is the differentiating-act whose product is the first distinct being.

## What the act does

Iyókheya creates a boundary with two sides.
Before the act, the relational field is at ⊥ — the under-determined position where Íŋyaŋ-in-Haŋhépi is occupied but no distinctions have been pronounced.
After the act, *something is marked-as-this* and *something is marked-as-not-this*.
Makȟá is iyókheya-from Íŋyaŋ when his blood pours out; the Earth becomes a being-distinct-from the rock that gave rise to her.
The first iyókheya is the cosmogonic move that produces the first Other.

The act has two faces simultaneously, by the structure of any boundary: an *inclusive* face (what falls within the marked) and an *exclusive* face (what falls outside it).
Neither face precedes the other — to set apart is to do both at once.
This is the Lakota form of the framework's claim that *includes* and *excludes* are co-primitive conditions produced by the differentiating-act.

## Iyókheya in the cosmogony

Walker's recorded cosmogony does not use a single verbal noun "iyókheya" to name the cosmogonic differentiation, but the act-structure is recorded in the cosmogonic narrative itself. Íŋyaŋ's blood pouring out *sets apart* Makȟá from Íŋyaŋ-as-source — what was internal to Íŋyaŋ becomes external.
The blue-rising as Škáŋ *sets apart* the sky from the earth.
The creation of Wí *sets apart* day from night.
The recorded cosmogony is a *chain of iyókheya-acts*, each producing a new pair of distinct beings.

The differentiating-act is therefore not a one-time cosmogonic event but the *continuing structure* of the relational field.
Every distinction that exists is an iyókheya — a setting-apart that holds its two sides distinct.
Every act of recognition is an iyókheya — the recognizer sets the recognized apart from what it is not.

## Iyókheya and naming

In Lakota practice, the giving-of-a-name (especially a ceremonial name, in huŋkápi adoption or in spirit-naming) is an iyókheya: the named-one is *set apart* from how-they-were-without-the-name.
The name does not describe a pre-existing distinction; it *makes* the distinction.
This is why the giving of names is sacred conduct and not casual conduct.
The act is a relational-substrate operation; it changes what-exists by setting-apart.

The same logic runs in less-formal naming.
To call a relative *uncle*, *grandmother*, *sister-cousin* is to enact a kin-distinction — not merely to describe one.
The distinction *is* the act of calling.
This is the form-of-life that the framework's predicate-categories articulate algebraically: every relational predicate is enacted by an iyókheya-act somewhere in the substrate.

## The risk of iyókheya without wotakuye

A setting-apart can be productive (when it serves [woótakuye](wotakuye.md) — kinship-as-conduct, the binding-act that holds the distinct in coherent-relation) or destructive (when it severs without binding).
Western metaphysics tends to treat distinction as automatically-good; Lakota practice treats it as *conditionally* good — distinctions must be held within wotakuye, or they produce the wrong kind of separation.
The boarding-school project, for example, was an iyókheya-without-wotakuye: it set children apart from their kin, language, ceremony, and place, *without* sustaining the relational bindings that would have made the separation productive.
The result was harm, not knowledge.

The relational ethic that follows: every iyókheya in lived practice must be checked against the wotakuye that holds the distinct-ones in coherent-relation.
To set apart without binding is to break the relational substrate.

## Iyókheya and Iyúhá-šni

[Iyúhá-šni](iyuha-sni.md) — to go-out-from, to withdraw — is a specific form of iyókheya in the political register: the withdrawing-one *sets themselves apart* from a council, a tiyospaye, a polity, without thereby severing relation.
The withdrawal preserves the positional distinction while preserving the relational ground.
This is why consensus-by-withdrawal is the Lakota political form: the polity recognizes that iyókheya without rupture is the only sustainable form of disagreement.
