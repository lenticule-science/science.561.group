# Woótakuye

Woótakuye — *kinship-as-act* — is the doing-of-kinship: the lived activity by which kin-relations are sustained, enacted, and renewed.
It is the relating-side of relation.
Where [tȟakúye](takuye.md) names kin-relation as substance ("what we have in common"), woótakuye names kinship as *what we do*.
The relational framework's "relating" — the binding-act that sustains tension between including and excluding — is woótakuye in Lakota.

## What woótakuye does

Kinship is not a status that, once established, persists by itself.
It must be *enacted continuously*.
To be in kin-relation with someone is to *do kinship with them* — to speak the kin-term, to share food, to attend to their well-being, to bear obligations on their behalf, to receive their bearing-on-yours.
The cessation of doing is the cessation of being-in-relation; relation is not a label affixed once but an activity sustained.

This makes Lakota kinship *performative* in a strong sense.
Ella Deloria (Yankton Dakota, *Speaking of Indians*, 1944) is the canonical sociological articulation: "Being Dakota is being related."
But the *being* in that phrase is active — *being-related* is *relating*.
The polity is constituted by *people-relating-to-each-other-as-kin*, not by people-who-happen-to-be-listed-as-kin in some external registry.

## Woótakuye vs. tȟakúye

The distinction:

- **Tȟakúye** — kin-relation as substance.
  "She is my *tȟakúye*" — she is my kin, the relation is the case.
  Walker's informants describe tȟakúye as "something they have in common."
  See [takuye](takuye.md).
- **Woótakuye** — kin-relation as activity.
  "I am doing *woótakuye* with her" — I am enacting kinship, performing the conduct of a kin-relative.

The pair is the Lakota form of the framework's distinction between *relation* (the condition; the structural-link) and *relating* (the act; the sustaining-activity).
Both are present in Lakota tradition; the activity-form is named woótakuye.

In Walker's recordings, **wóčhekiye** — to invoke kinship, to call-on-relation — is the closest single-word attested term for the relating-act, especially in the context of prayer (which the Lakota tradition understands as the *verbal invocation of kinship with a spirit*; see [yuhapi](yuhapi.md) for the related witness-act).
Wóčhekiye is one form of woótakuye; the wider activity-of-kinship encompasses wóčhekiye but also includes the everyday conduct of sharing food, speaking kin-terms, attending to relatives, etc.

## The four virtues as woótakuye-conduct

The [four virtues](four-virtues.md) — *wówačhantognaka* (generosity), *wóksape* (wisdom), *wóohitika* (bravery), *wówačhiŋtȟaŋka* (fortitude) — are the qualities of woótakuye.
Each virtue is a way of *doing kinship well*:

- **Wówačhantognaka** — generosity is woótakuye in the giving-direction.
- **Wóksape** — wisdom is woótakuye in the discerning-direction (knowing how to relate well).
- **Wóohitika** — bravery is woótakuye in the protecting-direction.
- **Wówačhiŋtȟaŋka** — fortitude is woótakuye in the holding-fast direction (continuing to relate well under strain).

The virtues are not separable ethical principles applied to kinship-as-an-external-fact.
They are the *forms of the activity itself*.
To say someone is *acting wolakȟota* is to say they are *doing woótakuye well*.

## Woótakuye as binding

In the relational framework's structure, *relating* is the act that *binds* — that holds inclusion and exclusion in coherent opposition rather than letting them collapse.
Woótakuye is the Lakota form of this binding-act.
Without woótakuye, kin-relations dissolve; with woótakuye, they hold across time and across the strains that life puts on them.

The binding is *asymmetric* with respect to its complement [iyúhá-šni](iyuha-sni.md) — the withdrawing-act.
Only woótakuye can bind.
Iyúhá-šni cannot do binding work; it can only sustain its own pole of the relational tension.
This is the structural reason that polity-maintenance requires *active* kinship-conduct rather than passive non-violation: the binding has to be done, again and again, by those who do it.

## The huŋkápi connection

The huŋkápi ceremony — making-of-relatives — is the *most-formal* enactment of woótakuye.
The rite installs new kin-cells in the relational substrate: two persons (or sometimes whole groups) are bound by the ceremony into a kin-relation they did not have before.
The huŋkápi is *not* a declaration that they will now treat each other as kin; it is the act-itself, the substrate-installation of the kin-relation through ceremonial means.
After huŋkápi, the parties *are* kin in the same structural way that descent-kin are kin — and the woótakuye-conduct that follows is the same conduct.

This is why ceremony is *constitutive* in Lakota tradition, not decorative.
The ceremony does relational-substrate work that no declaration alone can do.
