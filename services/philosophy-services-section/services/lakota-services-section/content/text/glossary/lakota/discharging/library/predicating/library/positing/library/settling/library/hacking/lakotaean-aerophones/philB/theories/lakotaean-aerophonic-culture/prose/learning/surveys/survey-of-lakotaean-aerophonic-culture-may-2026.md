# Survey of Lakotaean Aerophones, May 2026

## Executive Summary

Lakotaean wind instruments form a small but tightly stratified family of aerophones whose distinguishing feature is not acoustical richness but **functional specialization tied to social and ceremonial roles**.
The published record converges on three core types: the **cedar courting flute** (called by Lakota speakers *siyotanka*, *čotaŋka*, or related forms), the **eagle wing-bone whistle** used in the Sun Dance and in Native American Church ceremonies, and a small group of **bird-bone or wood whistles** (elk whistle, crane whistle, grass-dance whistle) used to signal, to call weather, or to mark moments in a dance.
Each type is a different aerophone in the Hornbostel-Sachs sense — a duct flute, an end-blown whistle without a fipple, and various whistles with or without finger holes — but functionally they are organized around **who may play what, when, and to whom**, not around tonal range.

The cedar courting flute is the only Lakotaean aerophone in the published literature that is designed to produce melodies in a defined scale.
It is a two-chamber duct flute with an external block ("bird" or "fetish") that splits the air over a labium; it carries five or six finger holes and is tuned in practice to a pentatonic minor scale.
Field recordings preserved by Frances Densmore at the Smithsonian Bureau of American Ethnology (1907–1918, published as *Teton Sioux Music*, Bulletin 61) and recordings of Richard Fool Bull (Lakota) and Belo Cozad (Kiowa, cognate tradition) made for the Library of Congress establish the documented baseline.
The flute revival from the late 1960s onward — through Kevin Locke, R.
Carlos Nakai, and others — substantially widened performance contexts but did not displace the original courtship function from the source tradition.

The eagle wing-bone whistle is acoustically simple — a short, single-pitch bone whistle, often without a fipple block, sometimes with a wax or pitch plug — but it carries the heaviest ceremonial weight of any aerophone in the Lakotaean inventory.
It is played during the Sun Dance (*Wiwáŋyaŋg Wačípi*) and in Peyote ceremonies of the Native American Church, where it is treated less as an instrument and more as a sacred object whose sound carries prayer to *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka*.
U.S. federal law (Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act; Migratory Bird Treaty Act) creates a regulatory regime in which possession of these whistles is restricted to enrolled members of federally recognized tribes — meaning the artifact category is co-defined by ceremonial use and by an external legal apparatus.

The remaining whistles — elk, crane, grass-dance, war — are sparsely documented but recur consistently in museum records and replicator accounts.
They sit between the flute and the eagle-bone whistle on a *signal–song* axis: they are not melodic instruments, but they are not as restricted as the eagle-bone either.
They tend to carry one or two pitches, are often carved from a single piece of wood or bone, and are used to call rain, to invoke water-spirits, to coordinate movement during dances, or to declare presence on the battlefield.
Their construction is typically simpler than the courting flute (single chamber, no external block) and their cultural restrictions are typically lighter than the eagle-bone whistle.

The biggest near-term research opportunity is therefore not "more flutes" but **the functional taxonomy itself**: a principled framework that distinguishes Lakotaean aerophones by *purpose*, *eligibility-to-play*, *context-of-use*, and *materials-and-spirit* — rather than by Hornbostel-Sachs acoustic category alone.
The published literature mixes ethnomusicological description, museum catalog metadata, replicator construction notes, and contemporary performer commentary, and these sources frequently disagree on names, on construction, and on which contexts permit which instrument.
A unified taxonomy that respects both the acoustic and the social structure is missing.

## Scope and Definitions

This survey uses **Lakotaean aerophone** to mean any wind-driven sound-producing instrument documented in the ethnographic, museum, or contemporary performer record as belonging to Lakota (Teton, Oglala, Sicangu, Hunkpapa, Mniconjou, Sihasapa, Itazipco, Oohenumpa) practice, including instruments cognate with the broader Northern Plains tradition where Lakota use is specifically attested.
I include the related Dakota (Santee, Sisseton, Yankton, Yanktonai) tradition where the terms or instruments cross over.

I exclude: drums (membranophones), rattles (idiophones), vocal music (the courting-song *wiílowaŋ* genre is referenced only as the context the flute instrumentalizes), and pan-Native-American flute revival instruments that are not directly continuous with the Lakotaean source tradition.

The survey covers four interrelated questions:

1. **What aerophones are documented in Lakotaean practice?** A catalog of instruments.
2. **How are they constructed?** Materials, geometry, sound-producing mechanism.
3. **How are they used?** Performance contexts, eligibility, ceremonial function.
4. **What is the relationship between acoustic structure and social function?** This is the central theoretical question and is treated as open.

## Instrument Catalog

### Cedar courting flute

The cedar courting flute is the most fully documented Lakotaean aerophone.
It is a **duct flute** (Hornbostel-Sachs 421.221.12) of the **two-chamber external-block** type now widely called the "Native American flute" or, more precisely, the "Plains-style flute" or "North American Indigenous flute."

**Construction.** A length of cedar (typically western red cedar) is split lengthwise, hollowed in two halves, and glued back together with hide glue, sinew, and buckskin or rawhide wrapping.
Air leaks are sealed historically with buffalo intestine, contemporarily with beeswax or a tighter glue line.
The internal bore is divided into two chambers by an unbored "septum" left during hollowing: a proximal **slow air chamber** receives the player's breath, and a distal **sound chamber** produces tone.
Two holes are bored through the wall above the septum — a **flue exit** at the proximal end and a **sound hole / true sound hole** at the distal end.
An external **block** ("bird," "fetish," "saddle"), carved as a stylized bird, horse, or other figure, is tied over the septum with sinew, leaving a narrow **flue channel** beneath it.
Air leaving the slow air chamber rises through the flue exit, traverses the flue channel under the block, and splits on the **labium** (sharp edge of the true sound hole), exciting a standing wave in the sound chamber.

**Tuning.** Five or six finger holes are bored along the sound chamber.
In ethnographic recordings and in contemporary practice the flute is tuned to a **pentatonic minor scale** (root, minor third, perfect fourth, perfect fifth, minor seventh, octave).
The six-hole variant supplies one additional chromatic degree.
Hole placement is by ear or by traditional finger-width measurement; absolute pitch varies widely between flutes, and "in tune with itself" rather than to a fixed reference is the practical standard.

**Length and range.** Flutes range roughly 30 cm to 75 cm in playing length and typically span about 1.3 octaves of practical melodic range.

**Use.** Almost exclusively for **courting**.
A young man composed or received in dream a courting song; he played it within earshot of a young woman's lodge to declare interest.
Once a courtship was settled, the flute was traditionally set aside.
The flute also enters the dream-songs tradition: composers might be instructed in dream by spirits, and a finished flute could be sold to another player for a significant gift (cited prices include "a fine horse").

**Documentation.** Frances Densmore's *Teton Sioux Music* (1918, BAE Bulletin 61) is the foundational ethnographic source.
Library of Congress recordings of Richard Fool Bull (Lakota) and Belo Cozad (Kiowa, 1941) preserve playing styles from before the mid-20th-century near-extinction of the tradition.
The Kevin Locke / Smithsonian Folklife essay (2021) provides a contemporary Lakota performer's framing.
Smithsonian Music's "Oglala Lakota Flute" entry, the Metropolitan Museum's *siyotanka* of red pipestone (catlinite) — an unusual material choice — and various flute-builder accounts fill in the construction record.

### Eagle wing-bone whistle

The eagle wing-bone whistle is a **single-pitch end-blown bone whistle** (Hornbostel-Sachs 421.111) carved from the wing bone (ulna or radius) of a bald or golden eagle.

**Construction.** A wing bone is cut to length (typically a few inches), the marrow cleaned, one end formed as a mouthpiece, and a sound hole cut along the side of the bone shaft.
A wax, pitch, or wood plug is sometimes set inside to shape the air column; the published descriptions are inconsistent on whether a true fipple block is used.
Decoration is heavy and is part of the instrument: red dots and lines representing the eagle's vision, a downy eagle plume attached to the distal end representing the breath of life, and porcupine quillwork or beading on the binding.

**Sound.** A single high-pitched, piercing tone of approximately constant frequency; the published phrase is "the scream of the eagle."

**Use.** Played primarily in the **Sun Dance** (*Wiwáŋyaŋg Wačípi*), where each pierced dancer carries a whistle and blows it while gazing at the sun and pulling against the buffalo-rawhide thongs.
The whistle's sound is understood to carry the dancer's prayer to *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka* via the eagle as intermediary.
Also used in **Peyote ceremonies** of the Native American Church (where Lakota practice overlaps a Plains-wide ceremonial complex) and, more sparingly, in warrior-society dances.

**Legal and ethical regime.** The Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act and the Migratory Bird Treaty Act prohibit general possession of eagle parts.
The Eagle Feather Law creates an exemption for enrolled members of federally recognized tribes.
The result is that the legal category "eagle-bone whistle" exists at the intersection of ceremonial practice and federal regulation; the instrument cannot be detached from this regulatory regime without distortion.

### Elk whistle (crane whistle)

A wood whistle, often ash, carved from a single piece, with an elongated steamed beak shaped to resemble a shore bird (the published examples cite avocet).
A metal plate or sleeve covers the first hole; a slight air channel beneath the plate directs air across the edge.
Heavy ornamentation: red beaded horse hair, brass tacks for eyes, porcupine quillwork, brass bells.
Produces a "shrill tweak" — likely one or two pitches.

Used to **call rain** in drought and to **invoke the water-spirit's influence over women**.
The dual function (weather + courtship-adjacent intercession) places this instrument in a different functional class than either the courting flute or the Sun Dance whistle.

### Grass-dance whistle and warrior-society whistles

The grass-dance whistle is mentioned consistently in powwow-tradition sources as a specific signaling whistle for the grass dance, one of the older men's dance forms now central to many powwows.
Warrior-society whistles are noted as used "sparingly and with deliberate intentions" in specific society dances.
Construction details are not consistently documented in the open literature; these likely overlap heavily with the eagle-bone or wood-whistle types depending on society regalia.

### Bullroarers, panpipes, ocarinas

The Lakotaean record does **not** support a strong tradition of bullroarers, panpipes, or ocarinas.
Bullroarers are widely attested in other Indigenous traditions (notably Australian Aboriginal) and in some Plains groups in ritual contexts, but the published Lakotaean ethnographic record does not feature them prominently.
The absence is itself worth noting and is included in the open questions section.

## Acoustic Mechanism Summary

| Instrument | H-S class | Air-jet excitation | Air column | Pitches | Player control |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cedar courting flute | 421.221.12 | External block + labium | Single bore, two chambers separated by septum | 5–6 finger-holes; pentatonic minor | Embouchure, finger holes, block position |
| Eagle wing-bone whistle | 421.111 | Sharp edge at sound hole; sometimes plug | Single short bone tube | One pitch | Breath pressure (loudness only) |
| Elk / crane whistle | 421.x (wood) | Metal-plate-covered edge | Single carved wooden tube | One or two pitches | Breath, possibly one hole |
| Grass-dance whistle | 421.x | Variable; not well documented | Variable | One or two pitches | Breath |

The pattern is clear: **only the courting flute is a melodic instrument**.
Every other documented Lakotaean aerophone is a signal-pitch or two-pitch instrument whose performative variable is timing, context, and player identity — not pitch sequence.

## Functional Architecture

The most useful framing the survey can offer is not "Lakotaean aerophones in Hornbostel-Sachs order" but a **functional architecture** organized along three axes:

1. **Eligibility.** Who may make, possess, and play this instrument?
   - Courting flute: any young Lakota man (historically); modern: any practitioner trained in the lineage.
   - Eagle wing-bone whistle: pierced dancers in Sun Dance; Native American Church members; enrolled tribe members under federal law.
   - Elk / crane whistle: holders of specific medicine or song; less restricted than the eagle whistle.
   - Grass-dance whistle: dancers carrying the role.

2. **Context.** Where and when does the instrument speak?
   - Courting flute: outside a young woman's lodge; private; courtship season.
   - Eagle whistle: Sun Dance arbor; Native American Church tipi; sacred and bounded.
   - Elk / crane whistle: drought ceremony; possibly courtship-adjacent.
   - Grass-dance whistle: powwow grass-dance segment.

3. **Addressee.** To whom is the sound directed?
   - Courting flute: a specific young woman; community as witness.
   - Eagle whistle: *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka* directly, via the eagle.
   - Elk / crane whistle: the water-spirit; weather; women.
   - Grass-dance whistle: the dancers; the song-leader; the drum.

This three-axis architecture (eligibility × context × addressee) is the closest the survey can come to a unifying structure.
It is **not** isomorphic to acoustic class.
The courting flute and the elk whistle are both wooden duct-style aerophones, but their eligibility, context, and addressee are different.
The eagle whistle and a grass-dance whistle may be acoustically near-identical short bone whistles, but their eligibility and addressee differ sharply.

## Historical Arc

```
1700s–1850s : Pre-reservation flute tradition embedded in courting practice
1860s–1890s : Plains wars; sun-dance ceremonies suppressed; flute use begins to decline
1907–1918   : Densmore Smithsonian recordings preserve Teton Sioux songs and flute styles
1890s–1960s : Flute tradition nearly disappears; kept alive by elders incl. Richard Fool Bull, Belo Cozad
1934        : Indian Reorganization Act eases some ceremonial suppression
1941        : Library of Congress recordings of Belo Cozad
1970s–1990s : Flute revival via Kevin Locke (Lakota), R. Carlos Nakai (Navajo/Ute), others
1990        : Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act re-frames museum holdings
Present     : Cedar flute is a globally circulated instrument; eagle-bone whistle remains tightly restricted
```

The flute tradition and the eagle-bone whistle tradition followed **different recovery curves**.
The flute became a publicly performed, recorded, taught, and exported instrument.
The eagle-bone whistle did not — it remained inside ceremonial practice and inside the federal regulatory regime, and recordings of it are rare.
