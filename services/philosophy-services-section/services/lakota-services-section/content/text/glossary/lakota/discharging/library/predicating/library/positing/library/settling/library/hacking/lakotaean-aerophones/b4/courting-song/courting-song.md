# Courting Song

The courting song is the principal solo [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) repertoire — a body of [wiilowan](./wiilowan.md) vocal songs, of romantic or addressive character, that the cedar flute renders by [instrumentalization](./instrumentalization.md).
The genre's name in English ethnomusicological writing refers to its historical use-context (musical address from one person to another in a romantic relationship), but its identifying *musical* features are determinate and stand on their own as a music-theoretic category: a courting song is a solo, non-ensemble, [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md), [strophic](./strophic-form.md) melody, bounded by single-breath phrases, with the characteristic descending phrase-contour and [cadence](./cadence.md) vocabulary of the cedar flute idiom.
Songs in this genre form the largest single body of documented Lakotaean aerophone repertoire and are the primary source for any music theory of the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md).

The pitch content of a courting song is the [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md) in its principal mode (Mode I, fundamental-as-tonal-center).
Songs occupy a range of roughly an octave to an octave-and-a-third — from the fundamental up to the octave or just above.
The melodic profile is descending-biased: phrases begin in the upper portion of the range and descend to the fundamental at the cadence.
The cadential vocabulary is small and identifiable; the canonical cadence is the descending minor third b3 → 1, often with held tonic and a terminal [warble](./warble.md) or [breath-pulse](./breath-pulse.md) decay.

The phrase-structure of a courting song is bounded by the player's single-breath capacity — each phrase is one exhalation, with a brief breath-rest between phrases.
Phrases typically run between 4 and 10 seconds in continuous playing time and admit considerable rhythmic flexibility ([rubato](./rubato.md)) within the breath.
A complete courting song is built from several phrases organized into a [strophe](./strophe.md), and the song-form is [strophic](./strophic-form.md): the strophe repeats through the performance, with [ornament](./ornament.md) substitution and small rhythmic variation between strophes producing the characteristic "variations on a theme" texture of the genre.
The total number of strophes performed is at the player's discretion; performances often run from two to six strophes in length.

The [ornament](./ornament.md) vocabulary of courting songs draws on the full cedar-flute repertoire: the [warble](./warble.md), [finger-pop](./finger-pop.md), [finger-slide](./finger-slide.md), [cut](./cut.md), [tap](./tap.md), [trill](./trill.md), and [breath-pulse](./breath-pulse.md).
Ornaments are not interchangeable; specific ornaments belong in specific positions within the phrase.
Cadential ornaments (warble, slide to tonic) are reserved for phrase-ends; mid-phrase ornaments (cut, tap, trill) decorate notes within the phrase; the breath-pulse functions as a syllable-marker and articulation across the phrase.
The [ornament-substitution](../../code/transformers/ornament-substitution.md) transformer is the mechanism by which a player generates ornamental variants of a given phrase, and it is one of the principal sources of variation across strophes.

A courting song's relation to its source [wiilowan](./wiilowan.md) — when the song has a known vocal source — is the principal site of the [instrumentalization](./instrumentalization.md) operation.
Players who know both the vocal song and the flute can render the song faithfully in flute terms, and the mapping is reasonably definite (syllable boundaries become breath-pulses, syllable pitches quantize to flute scale-degrees, vowel-quality contrast becomes ornament-choice contrast).
Some courting songs exist primarily or exclusively in flute form in living tradition and may have a vocal source that is no longer continuously practiced; the music-theoretic content of such songs is fully describable in the flute idiom on its own terms.
