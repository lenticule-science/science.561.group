# Walker 1980 — *Lakota Belief and Ritual*

The first of the three posthumous Walker volumes edited by DeMallie and Jahner.
Contains the substantial portion of Walker's collected material on Lakota theology, cosmology, and ceremonial life.
The most-cited single volume of the Walker corpus for the philosophical content.

## Position in the Walker corpus

This is the cosmological-theological volume. *Lakota Society* ([walker1982](walker1982.md)) is the sociological-political volume; *Lakota Myth* ([walker1983](walker1983.md)) is the narrative-cosmogonic volume.
The three were edited from Walker's notebooks, which had been preserved by the AMNH after his death.

DeMallie and Jahner exercise editorial judgment: they organize Walker's material thematically (Walker's notebooks were not always organized), translate where needed, and sometimes consolidate parallel passages from different informants.
The text is therefore not raw-archival; it is *curated-from-the-archive*.
The curation is responsible and well-documented in the editorial apparatus, but readers should know they are reading edited material, not transcripts.

## Principal informants represented

- **George Sword (Long Knife)**: the most-systematic informant; gives Walker the doctrine of Tóbtób Kiŋ (the four-times-four), the cosmogony of Íŋyaŋ's self-emptying, and the four superior Wakȟáŋ.
  Sword had been a wičháša wakȟáŋ before converting to Christianity and was the longest-serving informant in the Walker project.
- **Finger**: the source of the March 1914 "Conception of Energy" interview on Škáŋ.
  A distinguished shaman.
- **Thomas Tyon**: Sword's longtime collaborator in the project; provided extensive material on ceremonial structure.
- **Little Wound, Bad Wound, No Flesh, Short Bull, American Horse, and others**: variously cited.

## Key passages cited in this subtheory

### Sword on the Tóbtób Kiŋ (the four-times-four; pp. ~50–55 in the DeMallie/Jahner edition)

Sword's systematic theology of Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka:

- The four *Superior Wakȟáŋ* (*Wakȟáŋ Aŋkáŋtu*): Wí (Sun), Škáŋ (Sky/Motion), Makȟá (Earth), Íŋyaŋ (Rock).
- The next four (the *Associates*): Hanwí (Moon), Tȟaté (Wind), Wóȟpe (Falling-Star), Wakíŋyaŋ (Thunder).
- The third tier (the *Subordinate*): Tatáŋka, Hunúŋpa, Tatékiyutaŋ, Wani.
- The fourth tier (the *Wakanlike*).

Sixteen wakȟáŋ aspects in total, structured as four-times-four.
The four superior are functionally the most central; the others are aspects-or-associates of the four superior.
See [tobtob-kin](teaching/explanations/tobtob-kin.md).

### The cosmogony of Íŋyaŋ's self-emptying

Sword's recording, paraphrased in DeMallie's reworking:

> In the beginning was Íŋyaŋ, who had no beginning, for he was there when there was no other, only Haŋ, the Darkness. Íŋyaŋ was soft and shapeless.
> Like all powers, he was wakȟáŋ; like all powers, he had no other to exert his power upon.
> But Íŋyaŋ wanted to create another entity over which he could exert his power.
> He knew that to do so he must give up some of his power.
> So Íŋyaŋ opened his veins.
> His blood was blue, and from his blood emerged Makȟá, the Earth.
> The power that had been in his blood rose up as Škáŋ, the Sky. Íŋyaŋ himself shriveled and became hard and dry, the rock that he is now.

This is the recording that grounds [iyan](teaching/explanations/iyan.md), [iyan-hanhepi](teaching/explanations/iyan-hanhepi.md), and [skan](teaching/explanations/skan.md).

### The Finger "Conception of Energy" interview (1914)

Reprinted in this volume from the 1917 paper.
Direct quotes:

- Finger: "**The shamans use the word Škáŋ; the common people say Takúškaŋškaŋ.**" The same being, two registers — wakȟáŋ-language and everyday-language.
- Walker: "I have been told that Škáŋ is the sky."
- Finger: "**Yes. Škáŋ is a Spirit and all that mankind can see of Him is the blue of the sky.
  But He is everywhere.**"
- Finger: "**Wí is Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka Kiŋ and Škáŋ is Naǧí Tȟáŋka, the Great Spirit.**"
- Finger (on the giving of *naǧí*): Škáŋ "takes from the stars a *naǧí* (ghost) and gives it to each babe at the time of its birth."

See [finger-conception-of-energy](finger-conception-of-energy.md) for the full reconstructed interview text.

### Ceremonies of the Heyoka (pp. ~155–165)

Walker's recording of the heyókha vocation.
The Wakíŋyaŋ-dream produces the heyókha; the dreamer is obligated to enact the contrary.
The list of qualifying visions: snow bird, swallow, horse, dog, night hawk, frog, dragonfly.
The contrary conduct is the social discharge of the vision's power.
See [heyokha](teaching/explanations/heyokha.md).

### Wanáǧi yuhápi (keeping-of-the-soul)

Walker's recording of the rite.
The bearer keeps the *naǧí* of the deceased for approximately one year; the rite concludes with a release-ceremony.
The most-elaborated form of yuhápi (witness-by-bearing) in Lakota ceremonial life.
See [yuhapi](teaching/explanations/yuhapi.md).

## Editorial caveats

DeMallie and Jahner are generally careful, but two concerns are worth noting:

1. **Selection and synthesis.** Walker's notebooks contain parallel and sometimes-contradictory recordings from different informants.
  The editors consolidate; readers should consult the introduction for the editors' criteria.
2. **Translation.** Some material is translated from Lakota by Walker or by the editors.
  The translations are competent but not infallible; where the precise Lakota wording matters, the Walker notebooks themselves (held in the AMNH archive) are the deepest source.
