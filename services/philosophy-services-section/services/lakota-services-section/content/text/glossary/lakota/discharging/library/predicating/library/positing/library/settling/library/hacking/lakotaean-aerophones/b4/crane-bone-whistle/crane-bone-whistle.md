The crane bone whistle is a single-pitch end-blown aerophone fashioned from the hollow long bones of a crane — typically the wing bones (ulna or radius) of a sandhill crane in the Northern Plains range.
Like the [eagle-wing-bone-whistle](../eagle-wing-bone-whistle/eagle-wing-bone-whistle.md), it is a fixed-pitch instrument whose musical content lives in [timbral envelope](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/ornament.md), [breath-pulse](../breath-pulse/breath-pulse.md) rhythm, and ensemble function rather than in melodic pitch variation.
Compared to the eagle wing-bone whistle, the crane bone whistle is typically of comparable or slightly larger bore and length, producing a [fundamental](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/fundamental.md) in a similar high register but with a softer, less penetrating spectral character — the crane bone's wall is thinner and the resonance has somewhat shorter sustain.
The instrument has narrower documented use in the surviving record than the eagle whistle and the [cedar-flute](../cedar-flute/cedar-flute.md), but it has clear historical attestation in Plains contexts and contemporary continuation in some ceremonial and craft traditions.

The construction parallels eagle wing-bone whistle construction in its main steps: bone cleaning, the cutting of a small lateral edge near the blowing end to function as the splitting edge, optional plugging at one end to tune the [fundamental](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/fundamental.md), and a finished mouthpiece arrangement that the player engages with [embouchure](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/embouchure.md).
Because the crane bone is somewhat lighter and more brittle than the eagle wing-bone, surviving instruments often show wear and repair patterns specific to the material, and the makers' choices about plug placement and edge geometry are noticeably more variable across surviving examples.
This means crane bone whistles, as a class, exhibit greater pitch and timbral variance from instrument to instrument than eagle wing-bone whistles do.

Musical use in the documented record clusters into two contexts.
The first is ceremonial, where the crane whistle has appeared in specific song-and-dance contexts whose details belong to the [lakotaean-aerophonic-culture](../../theories/lakotaean-aerophonic-culture/_index.md) sub-theory but whose music-theoretic content parallels the eagle whistle's role: short envelope articulations punctuating specific structural moments in a song bound by [drum](../drum/drum.md) and [voice](./voice.md).
The second is signal — the whistle's piercing voice carries across distance and has been used for distance communication and warning in some Plains contexts.
As with the [elk-whistle](../elk-whistle/elk-whistle.md), the line between musical use and signaling use is not sharply drawn in the Lakotaean tradition: the same instrument and the same articulation grammar serves both.

The crane bone whistle does admit a useful [warble](../warble/warble.md) — the rapid alternation between fundamental and an upper [harmonic](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/harmonic.md) — and the somewhat lighter and less penetrating fundamental allows the warble to function as a strong ornamental device without losing the underlying pitch.
Where the eagle whistle's piercing fundamental can sometimes overwhelm a warble (the warble registers as a "shake" on the fundamental rather than as a distinct alternation), the crane whistle's softer fundamental lets the warble's two-tone structure be more clearly heard.

Within the ensemble, where used, the crane bone whistle plays a role essentially identical in structure to that of the eagle whistle: a single high voice in [heterophony](../../philB/prose/teaching/explanations/heterophony.md) with the [drum](../drum/drum.md), aligned to drum [pulse](../breath-pulse/breath-pulse.md) via [pulse-import](../../philB/code/transformers/pulse-import.md), articulating specific structural moments.
Whether a given ensemble convention calls for an eagle whistle, a crane whistle, or another high-register whistle is in part a question of the song-context and in part a question of what is locally available — a contextual matter rather than a music-theoretic one.
