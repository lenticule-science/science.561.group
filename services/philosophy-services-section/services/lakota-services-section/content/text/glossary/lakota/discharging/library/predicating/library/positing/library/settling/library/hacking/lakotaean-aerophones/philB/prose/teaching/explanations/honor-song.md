# Honor Song

The honor song is a class of ceremonial ensemble songs in the Lakotaean tradition — a [voice](./voice.md)-and-[drum](./drum.md) song-form whose principal musical role for the aerophone theory is as the ensemble context in which the [eagle-wing-bone-whistle](./eagle-wing-bone-whistle.md) and other ceremonial whistles characteristically appear.
Honor songs occupy a different musical territory from the solo [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) [courting-song](./courting-song.md): they are ensemble rather than solo, [drum](./drum.md)-anchored and metric rather than [rubato](./rubato.md), and oriented toward a vocal melodic line accompanied by drum and articulated by whistle, rather than toward a self-sufficient instrumental melody.

The ceremonial and dedicatory functions of the honor song — to whom, on what occasion, by what convention — are properly the subject of the [lakotaean-aerophonic-culture](../../theories/lakotaean-aerophonic-culture/_index.md) sub-theory.
This entry's concern is the *musical* content of the form: pitch material, phrase organization, ensemble texture, and the role of the whistle within it.

The pitch material of an honor song is, in the song-types that this theory of aerophones touches, predominantly the [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md) in its principal mode, though the broader honor-song repertoire includes other scale collections.
The vocal line carries the principal melody, typically in a high-register male vocal style with falsetto access (see [voice](./voice.md)) and, on specific structural moments, an octave-above female vocal entry.
The [drum](./drum.md) provides the metric pulse, typically a steady single-beat pattern with characteristic "honor beat" accents inserted at specific structural moments of the song (the moments when the drum strokes are notably stronger or doubled to mark a phrase-end or section-end).

Phrase organization in an honor song is [strophic](./strophic-form.md) — the song consists of a strophe that repeats through the performance — but the strophe-internal phrase structure is fitted to the metric pulse of the drum rather than to single-breath rubato.
Phrases are typically of regular length (a fixed number of drum-beats), and the singer's phrasing adheres to the metric grid at structural moments even while admitting some breath-paced flexibility internally.
A complete performance runs typically four times through the strophe (a convention in many Northern Plains ensemble song-forms), with the dancer or whistle-bearer able to extend the performance by signaling for additional strophes (see [grass-dance-whistle](./grass-dance-whistle.md) for the parallel mechanism in the Grass Dance, which honor songs share in some forms).

The whistle's role within an honor song is determined by the song's structural moments.
A whistle articulates at specific points — typically at honor beats, phrase-ends, and section-ends — with a definite envelope-type for each role (a short sharp articulation for an accent, a sustained-with-warble articulation for a structural highlight).
The whistle does not play continuously through the song and does not carry melodic content (it has only one pitch); its function is *temporal-structural* — it marks the song's organization audibly above the vocal-drum texture.
The result, in music-theoretic terms, is a [heterophony](./heterophony.md) of three streams: voice (melodic content), drum (metric pulse), and whistle (structural articulation), each operating in its own register and timbral territory but sharing the metric ground that the drum provides.

The honor song genre is the principal site, in the Lakotaean aerophone tradition, for the music-theoretic content that single-pitch instruments carry: timbral envelope grammar, rhythmic articulation vocabulary, and ensemble-functional placement.
A theory of single-pitch instrument music that did not address the honor song context would be missing the principal practical site of single-pitch aerophone music in the tradition.
