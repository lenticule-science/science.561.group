*Wiílowaŋ* (also rendered *wiilowan*, *wíičhokoyake*, or simply "song" in English ethnomusicological literature) is the Lakȟóta term for vocal song — the basic unit of the Lakotaean melodic tradition, and the form from which the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) repertoire is derived by [instrumentalization](./instrumentalization.md).
A wiílowaŋ is, in music-theoretic terms, a [strophic](./strophic-form.md) vocal composition: a melodic line organized in [phrases](./phrase.md), each phrase bounded by a breath rest, and the phrases organized into a strophe (a complete melodic unit) that repeats — often with ornament substitution and small rhythmic variation — through the course of a performance.
The text of a wiílowaŋ may be a Lakȟóta-language text, a sequence of vocables (non-lexical sung syllables), or a mixture of the two; the relationship between text and melody is treated below.

The pitch content of a wiílowaŋ is the [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md) in its principal mode (Mode I, with the fundamental as tonal center), as carried in the vocal tradition that this theory documents.
Other pitch sets appear in the broader Lakotaean and Northern Plains vocal corpus — including hexatonic and heptatonic collections in some song-genres — and a complete theory of *all* Lakotaean vocal song-types would need to address that variation.
For the purposes of this aerophone theory, the wiílowaŋ types of immediate interest are those that the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) can and does [instrumentalize](./instrumentalization.md), and these are predominantly the [pentatonic-minor-scale](./pentatonic-minor-scale.md) song-types — including [courting-song](./courting-song.md) but also some lyric and reflective song-types in the same scale family.

The phrase organization of a wiílowaŋ is breath-bounded: each phrase is one exhalation, with the next phrase beginning after a breath rest, and the durations of phrases (and inter-phrase rests) are organized rhythmically by the singer rather than against an external metric grid.
The wiílowaŋ in its solo or small-group vocal form is therefore non-metric ([rubato](./rubato.md)); in [drum](./drum.md)-anchored ensemble forms (which include many specific song-genres that this theory of *aerophones* does not directly address) the same general phrase-structure is fitted onto a metric pulse.
The cedar flute's [instrumentalization](./instrumentalization.md) of wiílowaŋ preserves the non-metric phrase-bounded-by-breath structure of the vocal source.

The melodic shape of a typical wiílowaŋ phrase is descending — beginning on a high tone (often the b7 or 8) and descending stepwise or by characteristic leaps through the scale to the fundamental, where the phrase concludes on a [cadence](./cadence.md) (typically the descending minor third b3 → 1 or the descending fourth 4 → 1).
Multiple phrases in a single strophe descend in this pattern across different starting heights, producing a stepped-descent overall contour for the strophe.
This descending-contour bias has a physiological grounding (the breath weakens through the phrase, the embouchure and vocal tract relax) and a musical grounding (the fundamental is the tonal center to which phrases resolve).

Text-and-melody relations in wiílowaŋ are non-trivial.
Texted phrases align syllables to notes (often one syllable per note, though melisma occurs); vocable phrases align nonlexical syllables to notes in a more freely-musical way.
The treatment of text in the [instrumentalization](./instrumentalization.md) onto the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) is one of the central music-theoretic questions of the tradition: a flute cannot pronounce a syllable, but it can mark a syllable boundary by [breath-pulse](./breath-pulse.md), and it can render a vowel-quality distinction as a difference in [ornament](./ornament.md) selection.
