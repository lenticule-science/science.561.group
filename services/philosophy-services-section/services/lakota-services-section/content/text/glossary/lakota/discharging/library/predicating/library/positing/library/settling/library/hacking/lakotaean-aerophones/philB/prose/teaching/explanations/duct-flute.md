# Duct Flute

A duct flute is an aerophone in which the player's breath is conducted through a fixed wind way (the "duct") onto a sharpened edge inside or just outside the instrument's tone chamber, where the resulting jet excites the air column and produces a sustained tone.
The duct flute mechanism stands in contrast to the *transverse* flute mechanism (in which the player blows across an open hole at an angle, and the player's lips supply the splitting edge directly) and to the *reed* mechanism (in which a vibrating reed, not an air-jet against an edge, supplies the periodic excitation).
The [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) of the Lakotaean tradition is a duct flute, and the [eagle-wing-bone-whistle](./eagle-wing-bone-whistle.md) and other end-blown whistles are *not* duct flutes in the strict sense (they use the player's [embouchure](./embouchure.md) to direct breath against an edge, without a fixed duct) — though they share the air-jet-against-edge sound-production principle and are sometimes grouped with duct flutes as "edge-tone" aerophones.

The defining structural property of a duct flute is the *fixed* geometry of the wind way and the splitting edge.
The player does not need to find or hold an [embouchure](./embouchure.md) to produce the tone — the instrument supplies the geometry.
This has substantial musical consequences.
First, the duct flute has a low entry barrier: a beginner can produce a clean fundamental tone immediately, where the bone whistles and (especially) the transverse flutes require considerable [embouchure](./embouchure.md) skill to sound at all.
Second, the duct flute admits very fine control over breath quantity (because the breath path is fixed, the player can attend entirely to the air's velocity and pressure rather than to its direction), which makes [breath-pulse](./breath-pulse.md) articulation and continuous-tone modulation idiomatic on the instrument.
Third, the duct flute's spectral character is constrained by the fixed geometry — the tone color is reasonably stable across the range and across players, in contrast to the bone whistles, where the [embouchure](./embouchure.md) gives the player some control over spectral character.

The [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md) realizes the duct mechanism in a distinctive Plains form.
The wind way is internal to the instrument as a slow-air chamber at the head end; an external block (the carved bird or animal figure traditional to Plains construction) sits over a rectangular cutout in the top of the instrument, directing the air across the splitting edge of the tone chamber's upper wall.
The block can be removed and replaced (and is bound to the body with leather or sinew rather than glued), which makes the duct adjustable in a way that internal-duct flutes (such as European recorders) typically are not — a player can fine-tune the breath response of a cedar flute by repositioning the block.
The carved character of the block also gives the cedar flute its visual identity, but the block's function is acoustic.

The duct mechanism is what makes the cedar flute idiomatic for [rubato](./rubato.md), single-breath-bounded melodic playing.
The fixed geometry allows the player to begin a phrase from silence with a clean attack at very low breath pressure (the jet establishes itself reliably across a wide pressure range), to articulate tones within the phrase by [breath-pulse](./breath-pulse.md) without needing to retake breath, and to bring the phrase to a soft conclusion as the breath weakens — without the embouchure changes that would be needed on a transverse flute or bone whistle to track that pressure curve.
The cedar flute's principal music-theoretic idioms — single-breath phrases, breath-paced rubato, breath-pulse articulation, terminal warble or fade — are direct consequences of the duct flute's acoustic affordances.
