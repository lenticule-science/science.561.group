# Deloria 1973 — God Is Red

**Deloria, Vine, Jr. (1973). *God Is Red: A Native View of Religion*.
New York: Grosset & Dunlap.
(Revised editions: 1992, Fulcrum Publishing; 30th-anniversary edition 2003, Fulcrum.)**

## Annotation

Vine Deloria Jr. (1933–2005) was Standing Rock Sioux, the son of an Episcopal priest, a law-trained scholar, longtime professor at the University of Colorado, and one of the most consequential American Indigenous intellectuals of the twentieth century.
His earlier *Custer Died for Your Sins* (1969) is the political-polemical work that established him as a public Indigenous voice; *God Is Red* is the philosophical-theological work that developed the substrate of that politics.

The central argument: religions can be classified by their orientation in time versus space.
Western religions (Christianity especially) are *temporally* oriented — they tell stories about events in time, organize a developmental history, and read the sacred as moving forward through events.
Indigenous religions of the Americas are *spatially* oriented — they are bound to specific places, the sacred is *of* the land, and the religious life is conducted in and through relation to particular sites.
Sacred relationships to land are not metaphor or sentimental attachment; they are constitutive of the religion.

The political consequences are direct.
Disputes over Indigenous rights, Deloria argues, are fundamentally rooted in conflicting spiritual relationships to land — and therefore cannot be resolved by adjudicating them in the property-frame the settler state offers, because that frame already presupposes the temporal-religious orientation in which land is severable from people and reducible to title.

## Why this is a political reference

For the lakotaean-politics subtheory, *God Is Red* provides the foundational treatment of why the Lakota relation to land — most explicitly to [Paha Sapa](../../b4/paha-sapa/paha-sapa.md) — is not a property relation.
The refusal of the Black Hills compensation award rests on the philosophical claim Deloria articulates: the relation cannot be commuted to cash because the relation is not of the kind that cash can settle.

Deloria's wider corpus — *Behind the Trail of Broken Treaties* (1974), *The Metaphysics of Modern Existence* (1979), *Red Earth, White Lies* (1995), *The World We Used to Live In* (2006), *We Talk, You Listen* (1970) — extends the analysis. *We Talk, You Listen* is the foundational treatment of Indigenous politics-as-itself rather than as a minority politics within American politics.
