# Drum

The drum is not an aerophone and is therefore not itself a subject of this theory's main analysis, but it is the principal ensemble partner of the whistle aerophones — [eagle-wing-bone-whistle](./eagle-wing-bone-whistle.md), [crane-bone-whistle](./crane-bone-whistle.md), [grass-dance-whistle](./grass-dance-whistle.md) — and so the theory makes regular reference to it.
This concept entry exists to fix what the term *drum* refers to in this theory and what musical content it contributes to the ensemble texture, so that claims and structures that reference the drum have a single definite referent to link to.

The Lakotaean drum in its principal ensemble form is a large two-headed frame or barrel drum (Lakȟóta: *čhaŋčheǧa*) — a hollow wooden body with rawhide heads on both faces, struck by hand-held padded sticks.
The drum is typically large (in the range of 60 cm to over a meter in diameter for "big drum" ensemble use), played by multiple drummers sitting around it and striking the head in unison, and tuned by the choice of head material, head tension, and ambient humidity rather than by precise pitching to a specific frequency.
Smaller hand drums also appear in the tradition for solo and small-group use, with structurally similar (but smaller-scale) musical content.

The drum's musical contribution to the ensemble is *pulse* — a regular metric stroke pattern that establishes a recurring temporal grid against which the [voice](./voice.md) and the whistle aerophones articulate their lines.
The drum's pulse is what makes the ensemble's temporal substrate *metric*, in distinction to the non-metric breath-paced substrate of the solo [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md).
Where the cedar flute floats free of any regular pulse, the drum-anchored ensembles run on regular periodicity, and the whistle aerophones in those ensembles align their articulations to that periodicity by what this theory calls [pulse-import](../../code/transformers/pulse-import.md).

The drum's pulse pattern varies by song-type.
Many Northern Plains song-types use a single repeated beat at a steady tempo with occasional accent strokes ("honor beats") inserted at specific structural moments.
Other song-types use a more elaborate pattern — a strong beat followed by a softer one, or a steady beat with regular accent insertions, or patterns specific to particular dances such as the Grass Dance, the Round Dance, and others.
The detailed inventory of drum patterns is a subject for a parallel theory of Lakotaean percussion rather than this theory of aerophones; this entry simply names the drum and identifies the kind of contribution it makes.

For the purposes of the aerophone theory, two of the drum's properties matter most directly.
The first is the regular metric pulse it provides, which the whistle aerophones use as the temporal grid for their articulations (and which the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md), in its solo contexts, does not).
The second is the drum's strong low-frequency, broad-spectrum sonic profile, which sits in a register and timbre complementary to the high-frequency narrow-band whistle aerophones — the two together cover a wide spectral range and produce the characteristic stratified texture of Lakotaean ensemble music.
