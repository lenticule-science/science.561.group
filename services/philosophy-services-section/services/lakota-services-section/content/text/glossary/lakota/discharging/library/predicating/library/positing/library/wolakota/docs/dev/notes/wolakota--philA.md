# Wolakota

Wolakȟota — the Lakota way.
The name for being-Lakota as a *practice*, not as a possessed identity.
The full sense includes: living in proper relation with one's relatives, conducting oneself by the four virtues, participating in ceremony, speaking Lakota where one can, and — broadly — living the philosophy of [mitakuye oyasin](mitakuye-oyasin.md) in everyday conduct.

The related phrase *lakȟól wičhoȟ'aŋ* ("the way of Lakota doing-things," the Lakota lifeway) is sometimes used.
Wolakȟota and lakȟól wičhoȟ'aŋ overlap; the first names the way more abstractly, the second the lived practice of it.

## Practice, not possession

A consequential feature: wolakȟota is something one does, not something one is by descent.
A Lakota person who fails the four virtues, who treats relatives badly, who hoards rather than gives, is acting against wolakȟota — even though they remain Lakota by birth.
A non-Lakota person can act in keeping with wolakȟota (this is rare and conditional but not impossible; the [hunka](../../../../../../sociology/theories/politics/theories/lakotaean-politics/concepts/hunka.md) ceremony's adoption-into-relatives makes this formally possible) without thereby becoming Lakota in any complete sense.
The practice and the descent are distinct, and the tradition is clear that conduct can fail descent.

The point is not that descent does not matter.
Descent matters very much — being born into the people, into a tiyospaye, into the kin-relations from the start is the ordinary path into wolakȟota.
The point is that descent without practice is not enough.
The lifeway has to be lived to be the lifeway.

## Wolakota as standard

The concept functions, in contemporary Lakota speech, as a standard.
To say of an act or a person *that is wolakȟota* / *šni wolakȟota* (not-wolakȟota) is a substantive evaluation: this conduct holds the relations together; this conduct breaks them.
The standard is internal to the tradition — it is the tradition evaluating its own practice — and it is also strong: the tradition takes a clear stance on what counts and what does not.

## Continuity through disruption

The concept matters because the Lakota tradition has been under continuous settler-state pressure for a century and a half: boarding schools, language suppression, religious suppression, displacement, dispossession.
Wolakȟota's status as a lifeway-in-practice means it can be reclaimed even when transmission has been disrupted: a Lakota person re-learning the language, returning to ceremony, taking up the virtues, is returning to the lifeway.
The descent does not need to be re-established (it was never lost); the practice does.

## Sources

The concept is developed across Lakota writing rather than concentrated in a single canonical text.
Joseph Marshall III ([marshall2002](../../../../../../sociology/theories/politics/theories/lakotaean-politics/references/marshall2002.md)) presents wolakȟota implicitly throughout *The Lakota Way* by treating the virtues as practices to be cultivated.
Albert White Hat Sr. ([white-hat1999](../../../../../../sociology/theories/politics/theories/lakotaean-politics/references/white-hat1999.md), [white-hat2012](../../../../../../sociology/theories/politics/theories/lakotaean-politics/references/white-hat2012.md)) develops it as the integration of language, ceremony, and conduct.
