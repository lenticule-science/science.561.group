# Akicita

Akíčhita — the warrior societies.
Distinct named societies (the Kit-Fox / Tokála, the Strong Hearts / Čhaŋté T'iŋza, the Crow Owners / Khaŋǧí Yuhá, and others; the exact roster and membership criteria vary by oyate and across time) whose role in the polity is enforcement of communal decisions during specific occasions — most clearly the communal buffalo hunt, where individual hunting could disrupt the collective drive and was checked by the akicita on duty.

## What the akicita do — and do not do

The akicita are emphatically *not* a standing police force in the modern sense.
Their enforcement role is occasion-specific.
During the communal hunt, during a camp move, during a major ceremony, the akicita appointed by the [naca](../naca/naca.md) hold authority to enforce communal discipline — to prevent premature hunting, to keep the camp orderly, to compel participation in necessary collective tasks.
Outside those bounded occasions, the akicita are warriors and society members; they hold no general policing authority.

The structural pattern is important.
The akicita's enforcement is *delegated* by the naca for a specific occasion and recalled when that occasion ends.
The enforcement does not float free of the council that authorizes it.
This is a distinct polity-form feature: enforcement authority is occasion-bounded and council-delegated, not held continuously by a separate institution that develops its own interests over time.

## Why this matters for state-refusal

Pierre Clastres ([clastres1974](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../public-services-section/services/personal-services-section/services/emsenn-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/reference-articles/bibliographies/clastres1974.md)) argues that the consolidation of permanent armed enforcement under autonomous command is a key step in state-formation.
The Lakota akicita arrangement structurally prevents this: enforcement exists, it is recognized, and it works — but it is bounded, delegated, and dissolvable.
The akicita cannot become a praetorian guard whose interests diverge from the people because the akicita's authority is constituted as on-loan from the council and returns there when the occasion ends.

This is not because the Lakota lacked the capacity to develop a standing armed institution.
It is because the polity-form structurally refuses it.
The akicita's bounded role is one of the mechanisms by which the Lakota polity stays a polity of relatives rather than becoming a state with subjects.

## Akicita societies

Each akicita society has its own ceremonial life, songs, regalia, membership rites, and history.
They are not merely functional offices; they are full social institutions with their own ceremonial standing in the polity.
Membership in a particular akicita society — Tokála (Kit-Fox), Čhaŋté T'iŋza (Strong Hearts), and others — was a recognized form of political and ceremonial belonging.

For the historical roster and detailed practice, the ethnographic record (Walker's *Lakota Society* [walker1982](../../philA/references/walker1982.md), with the usual cautions about non-Lakota mediation) and contemporary Lakota writing should be consulted.
This entry sketches the structural role; the substantive content of each society's life is the proper domain of the societies themselves.

## Contemporary form

Some akicita societies persist.
Some are dormant or have been revived.
The akicita-as-structure (occasion-specific, council-delegated communal enforcement) also persists in less visible form in contemporary Lakota communal practice — at ceremony, at gatherings, at moments of communal cause (Standing Rock is the recent paradigmatic case).
Nick Estes ([estes2019](../../philB/references/estes2019.md)) provides the contemporary continuity-frame.
