# Grass Dance Whistle

The Grass Dance whistle is a single-pitch end-blown aerophone used by the lead dancer in the Grass Dance, a Northern Plains warrior society dance whose specific historical lineage and ceremonial detail belong to the [lakotaean-aerophonic-culture](../../theories/lakotaean-aerophonic-culture/_index.md) sub-theory but whose musical role is sufficiently distinct from the other whistles of the tradition to warrant its own concept entry.
The dance-whistle's defining property, musically, is that it is not voiced by a musician in the song-ensemble but by a dancer in the dance — and its phrasing therefore is keyed not to melodic line or song-form alone but to dance-step structure, with the whistle marking specific transitions in the choreography that the dancer is in the best position to anticipate.

Construction follows the general single-pitch end-blown pattern: a hollow tube — historically bone (eagle, crane, or other bird wing-bone), occasionally wood or, in more recent practice, brass or other metal — with a small edge near the blowing end and an [embouchure](./embouchure.md)-engaged mouthpiece.
The dance-whistle's [fundamental](./fundamental.md) sits in a high register comparable to the [eagle-wing-bone-whistle](./eagle-wing-bone-whistle.md) and the [crane-bone-whistle](./crane-bone-whistle.md) — the high register is in fact common to all the whistles whose role includes ensemble cut-through against [drum](./drum.md) and [voice](./voice.md).

The dance-whistle's musical content is concentrated in two specific functions, each tied to its dancer-borne role.

The first is the *song-change call*: at the conclusion of a song's regular cycle through its strophes, the lead dancer can call for an additional repetition of the song by blowing the whistle at a specific structural moment in the song — typically just before the song would otherwise end.
The drum group responds by continuing the song into another cycle rather than concluding.
This is a music-theoretic event with definite consequences for [strophic-form](./strophic-form.md): a Grass Dance song's number of strophes is not a fixed property of the song but a property of the song-as-performed in interaction with the whistle's calls, and the whistle is the mechanism by which that interaction is carried.

The second is the *step-change accent*: brief, sharp whistle articulations marking specific moments in the dance choreography — turns, drops, accents — that may or may not coincide with song-structural moments.
These articulations function in the manner of a percussive accent rather than a melodic addition, and their timbral character is therefore typically a short sharp envelope without [warble](./warble.md) or extended sustain.

The Grass Dance whistle's relation to the rest of the ensemble is therefore distinctive: rather than being a regular voice in the song-texture (as the [eagle-wing-bone-whistle](./eagle-wing-bone-whistle.md) often is in honor-song contexts), it is an *interrupting* or *structurally-active* voice whose articulations have grammatical consequences for the song's unfolding.
This makes the dance-whistle the clearest case in the Lakotaean aerophone family where a single-pitch instrument carries form-determining musical authority rather than ornamental or accompanimental function.
