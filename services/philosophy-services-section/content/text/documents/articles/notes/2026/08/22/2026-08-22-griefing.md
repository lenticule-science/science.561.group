Griefing is play whose object is other players' distress. The definitional literature immediately runs into a wall that is itself the finding: Grimmelmann, quoting Hunter and Lastowka's draft "Virtual Crimes," reports the working definition as "any activity that genuinely bothers most players of the game in question" — and observes that this "renders futile the task of developing any general doctrine of virtual criminal law," because whether an act is griefing depends on the community's reception of it, not on the act. Foo and Koivisto's taxonomy ("Defining Grief Play in MMORPGs," 2004 — reported here at second hand, via citing literature rather than the paywalled original) sorts the phenomenon into harassment, power imposition, scamming, and greed play, with the distinctive move of admitting *unintentional* grief: harm inflicted as a side effect of self-interested play, without sadistic intent. Bartle's killer type (see [player-types](../../../../../../triage/player-types.md)) is the phenomenon's oldest portrait: what the killer needs is real distress from a real person — "the more massive the distress caused, the greater the killer's joy at having caused it" — which is to say griefing is parasitic on the porosity of the [magic circle](../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/magic-circle.md): it only works because feelings cross the boundary.

Enforcement inherits the definitional problem and adds a structural one. Grimmelmann's observation: in a virtual world "the set of unpleasant and wrongful acts players might wish to deter is identical to the set of unpleasant and effective sanctions available as deterrence," and no punishment can exceed the value the offender places on staying — "she can always respond by quitting." The Mr. Bungle case proved the point at the origin: after LambdaMOO's community deleted its first griefer, he re-registered under a new name and reoffended; Dibbell's verdict was that "his punishment, ultimately, had been no more or less symbolic than his crime." There is no expulsion from a world that has no outside, only the shedding of a name. What actually polices persistent worlds, Grimmelmann argues, is the guild: player institutions with weak formal powers and outsized social authority — "whoever understands the nature of guilds will understand the nature of community and community norm-enforcement in modern virtual worlds."

The boundary of the category is drawn differently in different worlds, and some worlds legislate the grief in. EVE Online's operator declares player crime out of jurisdiction — scamming "is not only allowed, it is encouraged and rewarded by the game mechanics," per CCP's own community team — producing what Marcus Carter calls a ruthless game, one where "players are lawfully afforded the opportunity to act in ways that have great, negative consequences on other players." Ian Brooks's ethical analysis of EVE betrayal turns on the observation (via Craft) that roleplayers and non-roleplayers in the same world disagree about where the game's boundary sits — they are, in effect, not playing the same game — so consent to "it's just part of the game" cannot be assumed uniform across the population. Griefing, in the end, is less a kind of act than a standing dispute about the location of the circle, adjudicated case by case by whatever institutions the [god-problem](../../../../../../triage/god-problem.md) has allowed to grow.
