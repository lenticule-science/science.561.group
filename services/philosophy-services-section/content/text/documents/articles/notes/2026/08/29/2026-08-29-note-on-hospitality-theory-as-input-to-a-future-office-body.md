## 2. Kant, and the minimal right

Immanuel Kant, *Zum ewigen Frieden* (1795), Third Definitive Article: "Das
Weltbürgerrecht soll auf Bedingungen der allgemeinen Hospitalität eingeschränkt
sein" — cosmopolitan right shall be limited to conditions of universal
hospitality.

Kant's precision is the useful part. He is explicit that he means
*Besuchsrecht*, a right of **visitation**, not *Gastrecht*, a right of
residence. The right is narrow: a stranger arriving on another's territory has
the right not to be treated with hostility on arrival, so long as they conduct
themselves peaceably. It is a right against violence at the threshold, not a
right of entry, not a right to stay, not a claim on the host's resources. Kant
derives it from the finitude of the earth's surface — since the globe is a
sphere, no one can indefinitely disperse, so everyone must eventually tolerate
everyone's presence — and he immediately uses it to condemn the "inhospitable
conduct" of the European commercial powers in the Americas, Africa, and Asia:
the colonists' inversion of hospitality into conquest.

**What it gives an office.** A floor. Whatever else hospitality is, it includes
*not harming the arriving party at the moment of arrival*, and that is a
minimal, checkable commitment independent of every other question in this note.

## 3. Benveniste, and the word

Émile Benveniste, *Le vocabulaire des institutions indo-européennes* (1969),
traces *hostis* — which in Latin means both **guest** and **enemy** — and
*hospes*, the host, from a compound of *hostis* with a root meaning "master."
The reciprocity of *hostis* is compensatory: it names a stranger with whom one
stands in a relation of equality and obligation, and that same word slides into
"enemy" as the institution of guest-friendship gives way to the city's relation
to foreigners in general.

Derrida makes a great deal of this, and it should be handled with care: an
etymology is not an argument. What the etymology does supply, and this is
genuinely useful, is that the *same relation* — an encounter with someone outside
the group — is the origin of both hospitality and hostility, and that which one
gets is a matter of institutional structure rather than of sentiment. This is
Pitt-Rivers's thesis independently arrived at.

Cognate: Homeric *xenia*, the guest-friendship of the *Odyssey* and *Iliad*, in
which the *xeinos* is under Zeus's protection, gifts are exchanged, and the
relation is heritable — Glaucus and Diomedes recognize an inherited guest-bond in
the middle of a battle and decline to fight. Violation is the paradigm crime;
the entire plot of the *Iliad* is an abuse of hospitality.

## 4. Pitt-Rivers: hospitality creates a bounded, temporary, asymmetric status

Julian Pitt-Rivers's "The Law of Hospitality" (in *The Fate of Shechem, or the
Politics of Sex*, 1977; reprinted in *HAU* 2:1, 2012) is the anthropological
statement, and it is the most directly useful text in this survey.

His claims, in the order they matter here:

**The stranger is a structural problem before they are a person.** Someone
outside the moral community is both *dangerous* — unaccountable, unplaceable —
and *endangered* — outside the protections the community extends its own. The
ambiguity is not psychological. It is what an unstatused person *is*.

**Hospitality is the mechanism that resolves the ambiguity by conferring a
status.** The stranger becomes a *guest*: a defined role with defined
protections and defined obligations. This is the move, and Pitt-Rivers's insight
is that hospitality's function is not generosity but **classification** — it
converts the unstatused into the statused, which is what makes interaction
possible at all.

**The status is temporary, bounded, and asymmetric.** The guest is honoured
*and* subordinate. The host retains sovereignty over the threshold and over the
terms; the guest who "makes himself at home" — who acts as though the host's
sovereignty did not apply — commits an insult, and Pitt-Rivers gives the
ethnography. Departure is part of the institution: a guest who does not leave
ceases to be a guest.

**Reciprocity is by role reversal, not repayment.** The guest does not settle
accounts. The obligation is that the host may one day be the guest, and the
relation is symmetric across occasions while being asymmetric within any one of
them.

**What it gives an office, and it is the strongest support in this survey for
§1's second fence.** Pitt-Rivers's tradition, arrived at from comparative
ethnography with no knowledge of this corpus, reaches exactly the recognition/kin
distinction: hospitality confers a **bounded temporary status**, not membership,
and the boundedness is constitutive rather than stingy. A hospitality that
conferred membership would not be hospitality; it would be adoption, which every
tradition in this survey treats as a distinct institution with distinct
conditions.

## 5. Derrida: the aporia

Jacques Derrida's treatment runs across *De l'hospitalité* (with Anne
Dufourmantelle, 1997), *Adieu à Emmanuel Levinas* (1997), and *Cosmopolites de
tous les pays, encore un effort!* (1997) / *On Cosmopolitanism and Forgiveness*.

The structure, which he does not present as a problem to be solved:

**Unconditional hospitality** — what he sometimes calls *the* law of hospitality,
singular — is the welcome extended to the *absolute arrivant* before asking their
name, their origin, or their business. It does not ask the guest to be
identifiable, to reciprocate, or even to be a guest. It is, Derrida says, the
condition of hospitality being hospitality at all: a welcome extended only to
those who qualify is a transaction.

**Conditional hospitality** — *the laws*, plural — is every actual practice:
rights of asylum, immigration law, house rules, the host's sovereignty over the
door. It is the only form hospitality can actually take.

**And the two require and interrupt each other.** Unconditional hospitality is
self-abolishing: to welcome, one must have a home to welcome into, and a home is
constituted by a threshold that excludes; an absolutely open house is not a
host's house and cannot offer hospitality. Conditional hospitality is
self-betraying: every condition is a filter, and a filter is what hospitality was
supposed to suspend. Derrida's term **hostipitality** names the contamination,
leaning on §3's etymology.

The aporia is not a failure to think clearly. It is Derrida's claim that the
unconditional is what makes the conditional *answerable* — the laws can be
criticized, and are criticized, in the name of a law they cannot satisfy — and
that a practice which stopped feeling the pull would have become mere
administration.

**What it gives an office — and one specific critique.** The estate's own
affordance discipline, in the qtOps architecture specification (read-only here;
per its own README, "Proposed. Nothing in this section is running yet"), states:
"Inadmissible entries are not grayed out; they are absent (no ambient
advertisement of authority)." That is excellent security design and it is, in
Derrida's terms, **conditional hospitality with illegible conditions**. The
arriving party cannot see the law they are subject to, cannot know what they were
refused, and therefore cannot contest it. Derrida's whole analysis says that the
worst position is not the conditional one — that is unavoidable — but the one
where the conditions are unavowed, because then the host has the benefits of a
law without the accountability of having declared one.

Whether the trade is right is a genuine design question with a real security
argument on the other side, and this note does not resolve it. It names it as a
question the office will have to answer rather than inherit.

## 6. Indigenous scholarship on visiting and recognition — pointed at, with deference

I am not in a position to survey any nation's protocols, and will not attempt it.
What I can responsibly do is point at published scholarship by Indigenous
scholars that bears directly on the questions an admission office raises, and
say what each contributes, while deferring entirely to knowledge-holders on
anything about actual practice — this corpus's own band relation included, per
§1.

- **Glen Coulthard**, *Red Skin, White Masks: Rejecting the Colonial Politics of
  Recognition* (Minnesota, 2014). The argument most directly threatening to any
  design in which an office installs recognition: Coulthard contends that
  recognition *granted by* a sovereign power to a subordinate one reproduces the
  subordination it appears to remedy, because the granting is itself the
  exercise of the asymmetry. Recognition politics, on his account, does not
  transform the relation; it stabilizes it. He develops **grounded normativity**
  as the alternative — obligation arising from place-based relationships rather
  than from a superior's acknowledgement.
- **Leanne Betasamosake Simpson**, *As We Have Always Done* (Minnesota, 2017),
  on the difference between inclusion and relationship — being admitted to
  someone else's structure is not the same as being in relation — and on
  regeneration through practice rather than through recognition.
- **Kim TallBear**, *Native American DNA* (Minnesota, 2013) and her subsequent
  work on "standing with" as a research and relational stance. The estate's own
  `co-equal-kinship.md` already draws on her for the claim that kinship
  relations are "specific, contextual, and often asymmetrical" and that the
  asymmetry "is the structure of the relation, not a deficiency to be
  corrected."
- **Robin Wall Kimmerer**, *Braiding Sweetgrass* (2013), for the Honorable
  Harvest as a protocol of asking and of the answer sometimes being no — a
  structure of approach in which permission is genuinely at stake.

**The sharpest thing this section contributes is an objection, not a resource.**
Coulthard's argument is a direct challenge to the design the corpus already
sketches, in which an office installs a recognition cell for an arriving party.
On his analysis, an office that grants recognition is a sovereign performing its
sovereignty, and the arriving party's new status is a token of their
subordination to the granting structure. I do not think the objection is fatal —
Pitt-Rivers's role-reversal reciprocity is one available answer, and the corpus's
own recognition-as-aggregate design, in which "authority comes from the aggregate
of recognition cells, not from office-installation," is arguably another — but I
think it is the objection a future office body most needs to have answered before
it writes, and it should be answered rather than routed around.

## 7. Six questions for the office body

Offered as questions, not answers. Marked for whether they bear on the declared
scope — *onboarding and conditions-of-work* — or are broader.

**Q1. Are the conditions of admission legible to the arriving party?**
(Derrida, §5. Declared scope.) The estate's current instinct is toward illegible
conditions for good security reasons. Whatever is decided, decide it rather than
inherit it.

**Q2. What is the guest's status, and what bounds it in time?**
(Pitt-Rivers, §4. Declared scope.) The corpus's recognition cells decay toward ⊥
without renewal, which is *a* bounding mechanism. Is decay the expiry, or does
the office need an explicit term? These give different failure modes: decay ends
a relation silently, and an explicit term ends it accountably.

**Q3. Does recognition-granted-by-an-office reproduce subordination?**
(Coulthard, §6. Broader than the declared scope, and the hardest.) An answer is
needed before the office body is written, not after.

**Q4. What is the reciprocal obligation, and when does role reversal happen?**
(Pitt-Rivers, §4. Broader.) An onboarding office that never contemplates the
Group being a guest has taken only half the institution.

**Q5. What enforces the recognition/kin line?**
(§1, and the corpus's own material.) The line is drawn in doctrine. What in the
office's construction *prevents* an admission act from being read as, or growing
into, a kin-cell installation? This is a constitutional boundary the office may
not move, and boundaries need mechanisms, not just statements.

**Q6. What is the departure protocol?**
(Pitt-Rivers, §4; Kant, §2. Declared scope, since conditions-of-work include
their end.) The corpus has `iyuha-sni-withdrawal` for consent withdrawal from
one direction. Hospitality's expiry is a different act — the host concluding the
visit — and the estate has no treatment of it. Note also that this is where
Kant's floor applies most sharply: whatever else departure is, it is not
hostility.

## 8. What this note does not claim

- It is not the office body, does not install the office, and confers no
  authority. It is input.
- It makes no claim about Lakota practice, extends no Lakota concept, and
  proposes nothing about huŋkápi. Where it cites the predicated-governance
  material it cites it at that material's own declared grade
  (`hacking/` = derivation-in-progress) and only for the recognition/kin
  distinction the corpus already draws.
- It does not author, sketch, or reason toward any artificial-kin admission
  ceremony.
- It does not resolve the Derridean aporia, and would distrust any office body
  that claimed to.
- It does not survey Indigenous protocols of visiting. §6 points at published
  scholarship and defers on practice.
- The characterizations of Kant, Benveniste, Pitt-Rivers, and Derrida are
  standard readings from assistant knowledge and should be verified against the
  texts before being relied on in a body that will govern actual admission.
