A dark pattern, in games, is design turned against its player. The founding paper — Zagal, Björk and Lewis, "Dark Patterns in the Design of Games" (2013; its definition reported here via directly-quoting secondary literature, the original being persistently unfetchable) — defines dark game design patterns as design "used intentionally by a game creator to cause negative experiences for players which are against their best interests and likely to happen without their consent," and sorts them into three families: patterns that extract time (grinding, appointment play — see [asynchronous-play](../../../../../../../../services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/asynchronous-play.md)), patterns that extract money (pay-to-skip, and the monetized currencies of the [virtual-economy](../../../../../../../../services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/virtual-economy.md)), and patterns that exploit the player's social network (the "social pyramid schemes" of the Facebook-game era that Bogost's Cow Clicker satirized). Karlsen's empirical follow-up (2016) complicates the exploitation story: comparing WoW's appointment cadences against FarmVille 2's, he finds players "may find satisfaction... through patient earning rather than skills... and may in the process embrace design that has as its main purpose to stimulate loyalty" — the pattern's darkness is not reliably felt as dark.

The strongest thing in this literature is the critique of it. Deterding, Stenros and Montola's "Against 'Dark Game Design Patterns'" (2020) makes three objections. First, the concept is "ontologically incoherent": it makes the subjective states of designers and players — intent, harm, absent consent — necessary conditions for darkness, yet proceeds to declare material design features (loot boxes, grinding, pay-to-skip) dark per se, a "performative self-contradiction." Second, the ethics is unstated: the original gestures at "the interaction with a system as a contract," implying an unacknowledged contractualist frame while eliding consequence, excellence, and care as alternative vantage points. Third, and most damningly, the specific patterns flagged reveal "a bias against a particular historical formation of digital games, namely then-ascendant freemium casual and social network games" — the taxonomy quietly canonizes 1990s–2000s AAA design as the neutral baseline, so that "the designation of a particular pattern as 'dark' effectively bottoms out in the authors' personal intuitions and preferences." They concede the original's sensitizing questions — surfacing transparency as a value and regret as a player experience — are worth keeping.

What survives the critique is the loop the concept was pointing at. A live persistent world is steered by an operator who observes continuously and adjusts continuously (see [virtual-economy](../../../../../../../../services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/virtual-economy.md)), under business models that, in the telemetry literature's own words, require "constant monitoring and evaluation of the player population behavior in order to drive revenue." The dark-patterns argument is the field's conscience about that loop: the worry that the operator's steering optimizes engagement against the player's interest, and that the player's consent at the [magic circle](../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/magic-circle.md)'s threshold — the lusory agreement to be bound — cannot cover adjustments made to the arena after admission, invisibly, and in response to the player's own measured behavior. The debate over whether any given pattern is "dark" is unresolved precisely because darkness, like [griefing](2026-08-22-griefing.md), is a claim adjudicated between communities and frameworks rather than a property of the mechanism — which is what Deterding and colleagues' objection amounts to, even though they do not put it that way.
