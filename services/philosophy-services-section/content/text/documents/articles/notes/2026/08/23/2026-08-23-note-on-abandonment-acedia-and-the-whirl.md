## 0. Abstract and plan

The critical-theoretic analysis of hope, grace, and abandonment has a theological register that is older, more developed, and in several places more precise than its secular successors — and the traffic runs both ways: the theological vocabulary illuminates contemporary pathologies (burnout, doomscroll despair, the take-economy) that secular idioms describe less exactly.
This note works the register.

§1 treats hope's two failure modes as Aquinas analyzed them — despair and presumption, the vices opposed to hope — arguing that both are *premature closures* of what is constitutively open, and that Pieper's *status viatoris* (the wayfarer condition) is the doctrine of inhabiting the not-yet.
§2 treats acedia, from Evagrius's noonday demon to Aquinas's sorrow-at-the-good to Pieper's decisive inversion: sloth is not laziness but the restlessness that cannot rest — workaholism's root, not its opposite.
§3 argues the secular return: Byung-Chul Han's burnout society read as **political Pelagianism** — a social order that has structurally abolished grace, in which nothing is unearned, standing is replaced by continuous performance, and rest is delegitimated — with depression as its signature illness, exactly where the acedia tradition would predict.
§4 reads the platform intellectual's economy of takes as despair and presumption industrialized, and the classical symptoms of acedia as a clinical description of feed-life; it also recovers lament as the tradition's alternative to the take, and melancholy as hope's honest register under deferral.
§5 turns to abandonment proper: the cry of dereliction and Moltmann's theology of divine godforsakenness; then the tradition's astonishing move — the conversion of abandonment from curse to practice — in de Caussade's self-abandonment and, at its most embodied, the Mevlevi sema: the whirl as sanctified vertigo, voluntary reversible self-loss whose annihilation (*fanā*) is completed by return (*baqā*).
§6 places the pentagonal and quintessential material: the tradition, from Aristotle's fifth element onward, has persistently figured the divine as that which does not settle to a place but *circulates* — a history-of-ideas resonance stated as such, not as numerology.
§7 synthesizes; §8 answers objections; §9 gives directions.

## 1. Hope and its two collapses

### 1.1 Aquinas's analysis

In the *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, qq. 17–21), Aquinas treats hope as a theological virtue whose object is a future good, difficult but possible to attain — *bonum futurum arduum possibile* —
and then does something structurally remarkable: he identifies **two** opposed vices, not one.
**Despair** (*desperatio*, q. 20) abandons the good as unattainable: it treats the open future as settled loss.
**Presumption** (*praesumptio*, q. 21) claims the good as already secured: it treats the open future as settled possession.
Both are analyzed as corruptions not primarily of feeling but of *judgment about the open* —
and Aquinas's placement of despair among the gravest sins (graver in one respect, he argues, than unbelief or hatred of God, because it removes the ground of all further motion toward the good) registers exactly what a structural analysis would predict:
the premature closure of the open is not one error among many but the error that forecloses repair.

Stated generally, and this is the section's thesis:
**despair and presumption are the two premature settlements of what is constitutively undetermined — the same closure, performed in opposite directions — and hope is not a third judgment but the virtue of *withholding* the closure: the disciplined inhabitation of the open.**

### 1.2 Pieper: the wayfarer condition

Josef Pieper's *On Hope* (1935) draws out the anthropology beneath the analysis.
The human being, for Pieper, exists in *status viatoris* — the condition of the wayfarer, the not-yet —
and this is not a deficiency awaiting removal but the very structure of creaturely existence: to be a creature is to be underway.
Despair and presumption are then two forms of one refusal — Pieper calls both an *anticipation* of fulfillment's verdict:
presumption anticipates the arrival, despair anticipates the failure, and both thereby *desert the way* — abolishing the wayfarer condition rather than enduring it.
Hope, conversely, is the virtue proper to the way as way:
"the confidently patient expectation" that stays in the not-yet without collapsing it.
The structure could not be more exact:
the tradition's two sins against hope are the two directions in which the undetermined can be falsely settled,
and its central virtue is the trained capacity to hold the undetermined open —
a capacity, the tradition insists, that is not native equipment but grace-assisted discipline, which is why it is a virtue and not a temperament.

## 2. Acedia: the noonday demon

### 2.1 Evagrius and the desert

The desert monastic literature of the fourth century contains the first clinical description.
Evagrius Ponticus (*Praktikos*, c. 383) describes the "noonday demon" — *acedia* — as the most oppressive of the eight tempting-thoughts:
it makes the sun seem motionless and the day fifty hours long;
it drives the monk to the window, again and again, to look for visitors;
it instills hatred for the place, hatred for the life, hatred for the work;
it paints vivid pictures of *other* places where life would be easier, other vocations that would suit better;
it suggests, with perfect reasonableness, that one can serve God anywhere — so why here?
The phenomenology is precise and instantly recognizable: restlessness that cannot settle to its own task, compulsive checking of the horizon, fantasy of elsewhere, disgust with the given, the noon hour when the morning's energy is spent and the day's end is nowhere in sight.
Cassian (*Institutes*, book X) systematized it for the West and noted its two characteristic discharges: sleep, and *busy flight* — the acedic monk becomes suddenly, urgently occupied with anything other than his cell.

### 2.2 Aquinas: sorrow at the good

Aquinas's treatment (ST II-II, q. 35) gives acedia its formal object, and the formality is the insight:
acedia is *tristitia de bono spirituali* — **sorrow at spiritual good** — sadness directed at the divine good itself, and specifically at the good *as one's own*: the shrinking of the soul from what would fulfill it, because of the demand the fulfillment carries.
It is classed as a sin against the *joy* of charity — against the enjoyment of the highest relation —
and thus, in the terms of the wider analysis, acedia is the **grace-refusal affect**:
not doubt that the good exists, but sorrow that it applies to oneself; the gift perceived as burden; the holding experienced as weight.
This is what distinguishes acedia from mere depression in the tradition's own eyes and gives it moral (which is to say: addressable) structure — it is a *stance toward the gift*, and stances can be trained.

### 2.3 Pieper's inversion

Pieper's *Leisure, the Basis of Culture* (1948) performs the modern rescue of the concept by inverting its folk identification.
Sloth, he argues, is **not laziness**; the acedic person is characteristically *busy*.
Acedia is the restlessness of the self that cannot consent to be what it is —
and "the incapacity for leisure" is its social form: leisure, in Pieper's analysis, is not idleness but the *receptive* stance — celebration, contemplation, the Sabbath posture of accepting the world and one's existence as given, i.e., as grace.
The acedic flight into work is flight from precisely this reception.
Whence Pieper's diagnosis of modernity, two generations before the burnout literature:
a civilization of "total work" is not the opposite of a slothful civilization but its *fulfillment* — acedia armed with a work ethic —
and its inability to rest is not a scheduling problem but a theological one: rest is reception, reception presupposes gift, and a world that admits no gift can only keep working.

## 3. The secular return: Han's burnout society as political Pelagianism

### 3.1 Han's diagnosis

Byung-Chul Han's essays assemble the contemporary evidence.
*The Burnout Society* (2010): the disciplinary society of prohibition has yielded to the **achievement society** of limitless positivity — "Yes, we can" — whose subject is no longer obedient but *self-exploiting*: entrepreneur of himself, auctor and victim in one, working himself out under no master but the project.
Its signature illnesses are accordingly no longer the neuroses of prohibition but "infarctions" of positivity: depression, burnout, attention collapse — the exhaustion of a self required to be its own ground.
*Psychopolitics* (2014): under neoliberal technique, freedom itself becomes the instrument of capture — the compulsion to self-realize, self-optimize, self-brand is experienced *as* freedom, which is what makes it inescapable ("the exploited are not workers but entrepreneurs of themselves"; auto-exploitation outperforms allo-exploitation precisely because it feels free).
*The Disappearance of Rituals* (2019) and *The Scent of Time* (2009) supply the temporal half: rituals are "time-architectures" — stabilizing recurrences that make time habitable — and their liquidation leaves the dyschronic time of pure sequence, in which nothing concludes, nothing is celebrated, and rest has no form to occur in.

### 3.2 The theological reading

Set Han's diagnosis beside §§1–2 and the pattern names itself.
A social order in which *nothing is unearned* — in which standing is never secure but must be continuously performed; in which the self is its own project and its own ground; in which rest lacks form and reception lacks legitimacy — is, in strict theological grammar, **Pelagianism as political economy**:
the doctrine that everything must be achieved, established not as a heresy but as a labor market.
The consequences follow the old map with disquieting fidelity:
where grace is structurally abolished, acedia returns as the ambient condition (Pieper's total-work society; Han's tired self) —
and the achievement-subject's depression is the noonday demon's modern parish:
the same restless paralysis, the same sorrow at a good (now: one's own life) experienced as demand, the same flight into busy-ness, the same fifty-hour noon.
The reading is not a pious annexation of Han — Han himself repeatedly invokes the contemplative, the festival, and the Sabbath as the lost counter-structures —
but it sharpens his account at one point:
what the burnout society has liquidated is not "negativity" in general but *the unearned* in particular — standing, gift, rest, forgiveness —
and its illnesses are the specific illnesses which the tradition that theorized the unearned predicted for its absence.

## 4. The platform intellectual, the take, and the lament

### 4.1 Acedia's media form

Evagrius's symptom-list transfers to the attention economy with almost no translation:
the monk driven repeatedly to the window is the writer driven repeatedly to the feed;
*horror loci* — hatred of the cell — is the inability to remain with one thought long enough to finish it;
the vivid fantasy of the other place where work would flow is the platform's structural promise;
the busy flight into any-task-but-the-task is the take itself — urgent, adjacent, endless.
The revival literature has drawn the parallel (Norris, *Acedia & Me*, 2008; Snell, *Acedia and Its Discontents*, 2015): acedia is the desert's name for what the feed industrializes.

### 4.2 The take-economy as despair and presumption, industrialized

§1's two vices provide the genre analysis.
The economy of commentary runs on *certainty*, because certainty is what circulates:
its two staple products are the doom-take — the future settled as loss, with the poster's authority staked on the settlement — and the solution-take — the future settled as secured, five easy mechanisms, the thread that fixes everything.
These are despair and presumption as *content formats*:
premature closure in both directions, produced at volume, because the one thing the format cannot carry is the wayfarer's stance — confident patience in the open is unshareable, unmonetizable, and reads as having no take at all.
The intellectual formed by the format is thereby trained *against* hope in the technical sense:
their professional instrument is the closure reflex, exercised publicly, on every open thing, at speed —
a formation the desert tradition would recognize at a glance and could name.

### 4.3 Lament, and melancholy as hope's register

The tradition also holds the alternative genre, and its recovery is overdue.
The lament — a third of the Psalter; analyzed by Brueggemann as the practice whose loss cripples a community's speech —
is *complaint within relation*: grief and protest addressed *to* the Holder, at full strength ("How long?"), without closure in either direction — neither the doom-take's verdict nor the solution-take's fix, but the open wound kept open *in the covenant*.
Lament is what the take cannot be: sorrow that stays in relation and stays in the not-yet.
And this locates melancholy correctly.
There is a sorrow proper to hope itself — the ache of the good sensed under deferral; Advent's structure; the *Sehnsucht* the Romantics and their theological readers treated as evidence rather than illness —
and a tradition-literate diagnostics would distinguish it sharply from acedia:
acedia is sorrow *at* the good (the gift refused); hope's melancholy is sorrow *toward* the good (the gift longed for).
The first flees the cell; the second keeps vigil in it.
A person analyzing their own melancholy as a mechanism of hoping has the tradition's full support: longing is hope's honest feeling-tone under the wayfarer condition, and its suppression — cheerfulness enforced as proof of faith or of brand — is presumption's minor key.

## 5. The theology of abandonment, and its chosen form

### 5.1 The cry

At the center of the Christian narrative stands an abandonment: *Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani* — the crucified quoting Psalm 22's opening, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"
Moltmann's *The Crucified God* (1972) made this cry load-bearing for modern theology:
godforsakenness is not the outside of the divine life but is taken *into* it — God undergoes abandonment — with the consequence that no human abandonment is beyond the divine experience, and the abandoned are precisely where God has been.
Whatever one's confession, the structural move deserves notice:
the tradition does not deny abandonment's reality or explain it away; it *relocates* it — inside the deepest relation — so that even dereliction occurs within a holding it cannot see.
And the narrative's own sequence completes the arc: the psalm of dereliction is followed, in the Lukan tradition, by the commendation — "into your hands I commit my spirit" —
imposed abandonment answered by chosen surrender: the same unheld position, modality transformed.

### 5.2 Abandonment as practice

That transformation — abandonment converted from fate to act — is a developed spiritual technology with classical manuals.
De Caussade's *Abandonment to Divine Providence* (18th c.) makes self-abandonment the whole of the spiritual life: the surrender of self-management, moment by moment, to what the present offers, on the ground that the offering hand is trustworthy — "the sacrament of the present moment."
The Islamic frame carries it in its very name — *islām*, surrender — with *tawakkul* (trust-reliance upon God) as the corresponding virtue.
The structural point, common across the traditions:
**chosen self-abandonment presupposes a Holder** —
it is the limit-case of trust, not of despair; the unheld position entered voluntarily *because* the deeper holding is believed unconditional;
which is to say the practice is only possible under maximal grace, and is unintelligible — indeed indistinguishable from self-destruction — where no ground is trusted.
The difference between the mystic's abandonment and the abandoned person's condition is, once again, entirely modality: consent, and the believed return.

### 5.3 The whirl

The Mevlevi *sema* — the turning ceremony of the dervishes descending from Rūmī — is the practice at its most embodied, and its structure rewards exact attention.
The ceremony is a *form*: fixed dress heavy with meaning (the camel-hair hat the ego's tombstone, the white skirt its shroud), fixed stations, a sheikh who anchors the floor, music and salutation in prescribed sequence — a strong frame, within which the turner spins:
right hand open to heaven, left turned to earth, the body a conduit;
the turning itself a *dhikr*, a remembrance, entering the vertigo deliberately.
The theological grammar of what happens there is the pair *fanā* and *baqā*:
annihilation — the self's dissolution in the divine — followed by *subsistence*: the return, the self given back, transformed, to the world and its duties.
Read against everything above, the sema is the tradition's complete statement in a single practice:
**voluntary, framed, reversible self-abandonment — vertigo entered as worship, with the return built into the rite** —
the unheld position sought at its absolute limit (loss of self, not merely of standing) under the absolute holding,
dizziness itself — the body's premonition of dissolution, which play theory catalogues as *ilinx*, the vertigo-play humans seek in spin and fall — sanctified as the very medium of the encounter.
The whirl is what abandonment looks like when grace is total and consent is complete;
its existence demonstrates that the unheld position is not evil in itself — it is the *modality* that makes abandonment a wound, and the same position, framed and chosen and returnable, is among the oldest doors to God.

## 6. The fifth that circulates

A last, older stratum deserves its place, stated carefully as history of ideas and not as numerology.
In Aristotle's cosmology (*De Caelo*), the four sublunar elements each possess a natural *place* and a linear motion toward it — earth down, fire up; their physics is a physics of settling, and rest-in-place is their completion.
The heavens required a different account: their motion is eternal and circular, completing nothing, settling nowhere —
and so Aristotle posited a fifth element, the *aether* (later the *quintessence*), whose *nature* is circulation:
no natural place, no coming-to-rest, its perfection not position but unending motion.
The scholastics inherited the scheme; the divine associations accreted for two millennia — the quintessence as the celestial, incorruptible, circulating substance beyond the four that settle.
The resonance with everything above is plain and old:
**the tradition persistently figures the divine, and the highest life, as that which does not settle to a place but circulates** —
the whirl rather than the position, the fifth beyond the four, motion-as-perfection where the elements' perfection is rest.
The pentagonal thread that runs through sacred geometry — the five-fold symmetry that no periodic lattice can host, the pentagram's endless self-regeneration, the golden ratio's non-repeating remainder — belongs to the same figure:
five as the order that cannot be settled into a grid, and therefore, across many symbol-systems, the number hung on spirit, life, and the divine.
No inference is drawn from the number to the theology;
what is claimed is only the *consistency of the figuration*, across physics, geometry, and mysticism:
where the settleable is creaturely, the circulating is holy — and the dervish turning in the fifth's own motion is the figure made flesh.

## 7. Synthesis

The theological register assembles into one coherent structure, matching the critical-theoretic one at every joint while exceeding it in practices:

- The creature's condition is the **not-yet** (*status viatoris*): constitutively open, underway, unheld-in-part by design.
- Its proper virtue is **hope**: the trained inhabitation of the open; its two ruining vices are the premature closures, **despair** and **presumption**.
- Its proper rest is **grace-reception** — leisure, Sabbath, festival: the acceptance of the unearned; its refusal is **acedia**, sorrow at the gift, whose modern civilization-scale form is the total-work, burnout, achievement society — Pelagianism as political economy, with despair and presumption industrialized as its discourse.
- Its honest sorrow is **lament and longing** — grief in relation, hope's minor key — as against the take's closures and cheerfulness's presumption.
- And its limit-practice is **chosen abandonment** — surrender, tawakkul, the sema's whirl — the unheld position entered in trust, with return, under total holding: proof, enacted for centuries, that abandonment's horror was never in the position but in the modality, and that the position itself, held rightly, borders the holy.

## 8. Objections and replies

**O1. This is religious nostalgia dressed as analysis; the secular reader loses nothing by ignoring it.**
*Reply.* Every structural claim in §§1–4 is separable from confession and most are independently stated by secular authors (Han's whole corpus; the burnout literature; the acedia-revival essays): the two-closure analysis of hope's failure, the busy-restlessness account of sloth, the grace-abolition reading of achievement society, the genre-analysis of takes. What the theological sources add is precision (formal objects, e.g. *tristitia de bono*), priority (the descriptions predate the phenomena's secular names by fifteen centuries), and *practices* (§7's list) — which the secular literatures diagnose the absence of without supplying.

**O2. The treatment is Christian-centric.**
*Reply.* The note's practical center of gravity is deliberately plural — the sema and tawakkul (Islam), the Sabbath and lament (Judaism; Heschel and Brueggemann behind them), alongside the Latin scholastics — and the structural claims are stated tradition-neutrally. A fuller comparative treatment (Buddhist analyses of the settled/unsettled; Indigenous ceremonial forms of held openness) is flagged as a direction rather than gestured at thinly.

**O3. Reading depression as acedia moralizes illness.**
*Reply.* The note marks the distinction explicitly (§2.2): the tradition itself separates the stance (addressable, trainable) from the affliction, and §4.3 adds a further protective distinction (hope's melancholy versus acedia) that *defends* a class of sorrow against pathologization. The claim is not that depressed people sin; it is that a society structured to abolish the unearned manufactures, at scale, the condition the tradition described — an indictment of the order, not the ill.

**O4. The Pelagianism charge caricatures both Pelagius and neoliberalism.**
*Reply.* "Political Pelagianism" is a structural label, flagged as such: it names an order in which standing must be continuously achieved and nothing is granted — the operative point being the *shape* (no unearned holding) and its predicted sequelae, not a thesis in patristics. The historical Pelagius is owed more nuance than the label; the label is nonetheless the tradition's own shorthand for exactly this shape, which is why it illuminates.

**O5. §6 flirts with the numerology it disclaims.**
*Reply.* The section's only claim is a documented consistency of figuration — settling assigned to the elements, circulation to the divine, across named sources — offered as intellectual history. Nothing is derived from five-ness; the disclaimer is load-bearing and the argument would survive the section's deletion, which is the correct test.

## 9. Directions

1. **A comparative phenomenology of held openness**: lament, dhikr, Sabbath, and their analogues in other traditions, analyzed as practices that train the wayfarer stance — the anti-closure disciplines — with the two-vice schema as the comparative grid.
2. **The take-genre study**: operationalize §4.2 — code commentary corpora for closure-direction (doom/solution) versus held-open forms, and test the economy's selection pressure against hope's register directly (circulation metrics by genre).
3. **Acedia and the feed, clinically**: the Evagrian symptom-cluster as a candidate construct for problematic-use research — restless task-flight, horror loci, elsewhere-fantasy — distinct from, and testable against, existing attention-pathology constructs.
4. **The sema as frame-practice**: a careful study of the ceremony's structure (frame, titration, return) alongside the secular literatures of vertigo-play and recoverable self-loss, as the tradition's most complete worked example of chosen abandonment.
5. **Han and the scholastics**: a proper monographic treatment of the correspondence sketched in §3 — total work as acedia (Pieper), achievement society as grace-abolition, ritual-loss as the liquidation of time-architectures — which no literature known to this note has yet written as one argument.
