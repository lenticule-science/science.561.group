Active nihilism is Friedrich Nietzsche's term for the form of nihilism that responds to the collapse of metaphysical foundations not with exhaustion or despair (passive nihilism) but with the active destruction of old values and the creation of new ones. It is nihilism as a force, not as a condition — the hammer, not the rubble. Nietzsche's image in *Twilight of the Idols* (1889) — "philosophizing with a hammer" — captures the stance: the old idols are hollow, and the hammer's tap reveals them as such. Active nihilism is the willingness to tap.

The distinction between active and passive nihilism appears most explicitly in Nietzsche's notebooks compiled posthumously as *The Will to Power*. Passive nihilism is the exhaustion that follows the loss of meaning: the old God is dead, and the passive nihilist does not replace what has been lost. They continue within the empty forms — going to church without belief, following moral rules without conviction, pursuing goals whose justification has evaporated. Passive nihilism is the cultural condition of decadence: a civilization that has outlived its values but cannot produce new ones.

Active nihilism, by contrast, is the deliberate destruction of the old values as a precondition for the creation of new ones. The active nihilist recognizes that the foundations were always a construction — that "God" was a human creation, that moral law was a historical product, that the "true world" was a fable. This recognition is liberating because it reveals the human capacity to create values. If the old values were made, then new values can be made. Active nihilism is the moment of transition: the old table of values has been smashed, and the new one has not yet been written.

For Nietzsche, the danger is not nihilism itself but the failure to move through it. A culture that cannot complete the passage from passive to active nihilism remains trapped in decadence — performing the motions of a dead tradition without the conviction that gave them meaning. The will to power — not as domination but as the creative self-overcoming that produces new forms of life — is what active nihilism clears the ground for. The Übermensch is not the nihilist but the one who comes after, once the nihilist's work of demolition is done.
