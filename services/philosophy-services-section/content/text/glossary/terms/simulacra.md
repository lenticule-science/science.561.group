Simulacra, as theorized by Jean Baudrillard in *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981), are [signs](../../linguistics/topics/semiotics/terms/sign.md) that bear no relation to any reality — copies without originals. Baudrillard proposed four successive stages of the image's relationship to reality. In the first, the image reflects a basic reality — it is a faithful representation. In the second, the image masks and perverts reality — it misrepresents what exists. In the third, the image masks the absence of reality — it conceals the fact that there is nothing behind the representation. In the fourth and final stage, the image bears no relation to reality whatsoever. It has become its own pure simulacrum, circulating without reference, producing meaning only through its own internal logic of circulation.

Baudrillard argued that contemporary media culture operates primarily in the third and fourth stages. The question is no longer whether a representation is true or false but whether the distinction between true and false still applies. When [signs](../../linguistics/topics/semiotics/terms/sign.md) produce other [signs](../../linguistics/topics/semiotics/terms/sign.md) without anchoring in experience or material conditions, the entire framework of representation collapses. What remains is simulation: a self-sustaining system of [signs](../../linguistics/topics/semiotics/terms/sign.md) that refers only to itself.

emsenn references Baudrillard's framework in "Marked speech, borrowed grammar" to describe how [meme grammar](../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/communication-science-services-section/services/linguistics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/meme-grammar.md) reaches the fourth stage. When borrowed syntax circulates without context, memory, or responsibility — when a rhetorical pattern is reproduced purely because it is recognizable, not because it carries meaning — the [sign](../../linguistics/topics/semiotics/terms/sign.md) has detached from any referent. It operates as simulacrum: the form persists but the relation to what it once represented has dissolved entirely.
