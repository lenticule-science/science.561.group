**False** is the semantic property a [proposition](../../../../../../humanities/theories/math/theories/logic/terms/proposition.md) has when what it claims to be the case is not the case. Aristotle's formulation in *Metaphysics* Γ — "to say of what is that it is not, or of what is not that it is, is false" — gives the classical sense, and remains the reference point against which non-classical accounts position themselves. Falsity is not merely the absence of truth; it is the positive ascription of a contrary, and on most accounts it stands in a structured relation to the operations of negation and refutation.

## False as the negation of true

In classical logic, falsity is exhaustively defined as the negation of truth: *p* is false if and only if not-*p* is true. This makes falsity derivative — there is no further fact of the matter than the failure of truth. Gottlob Frege (1892) gave this its sharpest expression by treating *the True* and *the False* as the two abstract objects that sentences refer to: every declarative sentence picks out one or the other, and negation maps one to the other.

The simplicity of this definition depends on [bivalence](../../western/theories/logic/theories/classical/terms/bivalence.md) — the assumption that every proposition is either true or false. Where bivalence is rejected, the relation between truth and falsity becomes more interesting.

## Falsification and the demarcation of science

Karl Popper (1959) made falsity a methodological category as well as a semantic one. A scientific hypothesis is one for which some possible observation would render it false; falsifiability is what distinguishes scientific claims from metaphysical ones. The asymmetry Popper insists on is that no finite body of confirming observations can establish the truth of a universal claim, but a single counterexample can establish its falsity. Whether or not one accepts the demarcation thesis in the strong form, the asymmetry between confirmation and refutation has remained a structural feature of how the natural sciences treat their hypotheses.

## Non-classical falsity

**Truth-value gaps.** Stephen Kleene (1938) introduced a three-valued logic in which propositions can be *undefined* — neither true nor false. Gaps appear naturally for propositions that fail to refer (the present king of France), for vague predicates at the borderline (a man with exactly one less hair than would make him bald), and for self-referential cases that produce no determinate verdict. In a gap-tolerant logic, falsity is *not* the negation of truth: the negation of the undefined is also undefined.

**Truth-value gluts.** Graham Priest (1979) and the dialetheist tradition argue that some propositions are *both* true and false. The motivating cases are paradoxes (the Liar: "this sentence is false"), inconsistent legal codes, and inconsistent empirical theories that are nonetheless reasoned with productively. In a paraconsistent logic, the inference *from a contradiction, anything follows* is rejected, and falsity coexists with truth at certain propositions without trivializing the system.

**Bilattice falsity.** Nuel Belnap (1977) made explicit the structure required when a reasoner may have, for the same proposition, evidence in favor, evidence against, both, or neither. The four values arrange along two orthogonal orderings: a *truth ordering* (none < true; none < false) and a *knowledge ordering* (none < both; true < both; false < both). On this picture, *false* is not the negation of *true* but a sibling truth-value with its own knowledge-order behavior. *Both* is not a contradiction to be eliminated but a register of conflicting evidence to be reasoned with.

## Falsity and refutation

In a constructive setting (Brouwer, Heyting), a proposition is false when one has a constructive proof of its negation, not merely when no proof of it exists. The intuitionist refusal of double-negation elimination — *not-not-p* does not entail *p* — is the formal mark of this stance: showing that a proposition cannot be false is not the same as showing it is true. The refutation of *p* is a positive achievement, not a default.

This has practical consequences in computer-checked proof and in [research](../../../../sociology/theories/work/terms/inquiry.md) more broadly: failing to confirm a hypothesis is not the same as refuting it; refutation is its own deliverance.

Under the [relationality](../_index.md) commitment, **false** cannot be defined merely as the negation of [true](true.md), because the underlying ontology does not provide bivalent propositions to negate. False must be located in the structure of derivation itself: it is the negative position on the truth axis of [settlement](settlement.md) — the case where the iteration that would compose relational presence has produced denial, or where the relation is structurally absent from the system of relations.

This makes *false* a positive datum, not a default. A relation is not false because nothing has affirmed it; it is false because the derivation that would have established it failed to go through, or because the active denial of it has been reflexively affirmed in turn.

## Two readings: absence and active denial

The relationality formalism distinguishes two ways a relation can be false:

- **Absence reading.** The reflexive act that would compose the relation has not been performed; no derivation establishes the relation; it is not present in the system. This is closest to the intuitionist *not yet established* — the relation lacks affirmation but is not necessarily under positive denial. Absence-falsity is asymmetric with truth: a relation can be absent without its negation being affirmed.
- **Active denial reading.** The negation of the relation has been reflexively affirmed: the system contains a derivation that the relation does *not* hold. This is the case that aligns most closely with classical falsity, but it remains distinct because the affirmation of the negation is itself a positive derivational act, not a lack of derivation.

The two readings come apart in cases where the system has neither affirmed the relation nor affirmed its negation. Under classical bivalence such cases collapse — the relation is true or false, no third option. Under relationality they remain distinct: an absent relation is not the same datum as an actively denied one. *Absent* lives at the [absurd](absurd.md) end of the knowledge axis; *actively denied* lives at the [determined](determination.md) end. False, properly speaking, names the truth-axis position; whether it sits at absurd or determined on the knowledge axis is independent.

## False is not the symmetric pair of true

Classical bivalent logic treats *false* as the structural mirror of *true*: the negation operation maps each to the other, and double negation returns the original. In relationality this symmetry is not assumed. Three asymmetries are structural:

- *Both can hold.* A relation may be both affirmed and denied — both true and false — when the system contains evidence in both directions. The classical contradiction-eliminating move is not available; the system reasons paraconsistently. *Both* is the [determined](determination.md) corner with full evidence on both sides.
- *Neither can hold.* A relation may be neither affirmed nor denied — neither true nor false — pending derivation in either direction. *Neither* is the [absurd](absurd.md) corner with no evidence on either side.
- *The denial is itself an act.* In classical logic, *not-p* is an operation on the proposition *p*. In relationality, denying a relation requires its own reflexive derivation: a denial is positive in the sense that nothing produces it for free.

The four-cornered structure — true-only, false-only, both, neither — is the natural treatment of these asymmetries. It corresponds to what bilattice logics in the [logic](../../logic/terms/false.md) tradition independently arrived at via different routes, and to the math-side [predication fiber algebra](../../../../math/theories/voidmath/terms/predication-fiber-algebra.md) which the framework adopts as its formal articulation.

## False and refutation

A relationality-flavored notion of *refutation* falls out cleanly: to refute a relation is to perform the reflexive derivation that affirms its negation, not merely to fail to affirm the relation itself. This matches the constructive intuition (refutation as positive achievement, not as the residue of failed verification) and the methodological intuition that emerges in research practice — the failure to confirm a hypothesis is structurally distinct from its refutation. The first is *absurd-aligned* (no evidence has been registered); the second is *determination-aligned* (the negation has been positively witnessed).

## False and the math layer

Mathematically, *false* corresponds to the truth-order bottom $\mathbf{f}$ in the [predication fiber algebra](../../../../math/theories/voidmath/terms/predication-fiber-algebra.md). A claim's settlement value at a [predication cell](../../../../math/theories/voidmath/terms/predication-cell.md) sits somewhere in the bilattice; its truth-axis position is the projection onto the truth order, with $\mathbf{f}$ as the denying pole. The truth axis $\leq_t$ runs from $\mathbf{f}$ (false) to $\mathbf{t}$ (true), independent of the knowledge axis $\leq_k$ (which runs from $\bot$ absurd to $\top$ determination).
