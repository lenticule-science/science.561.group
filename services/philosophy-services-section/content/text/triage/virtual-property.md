Virtual property is Joshua Fairfield's term (Boston University Law Review, 2005) for a category of code that behaves like property and should, he argues, be governed by property law rather than by license agreement. His definition is a triple: "They are rivalrous. If one person owns and controls them, others do not. They are persistent. Unlike the software on your computer, they do not go away when you turn your computer off. And they are interconnected. Other people can interact with them." The persistent world's objects — the character, the house, the hoard — satisfy all three, which is exactly what a boxed game's save file does not. Fairfield's institutional argument is pointed: contract (the EULA) only allocates costs between its two parties, while property law manages externalities on third parties; and courts rather than legislatures should build the doctrine, because "when an identifiable, small, and wealthy group (like the holders of intellectual property) seeks legislative action against a disorganized, inchoate, and dispersed majority, the former can be confidently expected to prevail in the legislature."

Lastowka and Hunter's "The Laws of the Virtual Worlds" (California Law Review, 2004) supplies the wider frame: virtual worlds analyzed through real legal categories — property, crime, and the rights of "cyborgs" (avatar-and-controller units) against "wizards" (operators). Their reading of LambdaMOO's famous abdication (see [god-problem](./god-problem.md)) is deflationary in an instructive way: the wizards' withdrawal "was not as absolute as it may have seemed," because they retained the power to implement ballot outcomes as code — "no matter what they claimed, [they] were still wizards." They also preserve the earliest precedent: Habitat's operators in 1986 recoding theft and murder away in response to player complaints — in-world legislation by patch, two decades before anyone called it that. Lessig's *Code* gives the exit-rights corollary: because MOO characters are "earned rather than bought," members "have the right to exit, but in the sense that Soviet citizens had the right to exit — namely, with none of the assets they had built."

The stakes of the category are the stakes of persistence itself. Property presupposes a tomorrow: a claim that survives the session, the reboot, and the owner's absence is only meaningful in a [persistent world](./persistent-world.md), and once such claims exist they leak value across the world's boundary — Castronova's Norrath measurements and the whole real-money trade (see [virtual-economy](./virtual-economy.md)) are the empirical face of what Fairfield formalized. Koster's "Declaring the Rights of Avatars" (2000) drew the political conclusion early, modeling a bill of rights on 1789 on the premise that a relationship without a termination date accrues obligations; the unresolved question — still unresolved — is whether the god of the [god-problem](./god-problem.md) can be made to honor claims held against it in a world it can rewrite by patch.
