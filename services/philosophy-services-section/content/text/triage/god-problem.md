The god problem is James Grimmelmann's name (in "Virtual Worlds as Comparative Law," 2004) for the constitutional defect of every persistent world: the designer-operator is a sovereign no one can depose. Unlike a Lockean state, "there is no way to depose the designers of a game." Grimmelmann catalogues the restraints that actually function in the absence of revolution: market exit (quitting, which works as a continuous signal but happens outside the game's own interface — "'Quit' is not really a command in the game interface"); an international-law analogy in which players resemble foreign investors who cannot repatriate capital, only sell their position; and constitutional self-binding, where operators legitimate themselves by publishing in advance the narrow domains where they will intervene. The problem exists because a [persistent world](./persistent-world.md) cannot end disputes by ending; it must grow standing institutions instead, and every such institution sits under an authority that owns the physics.

The founding case ran the whole arc in four years. In December 1992 LambdaMOO's archwizard Pavel Curtis posted "LambdaMOO Takes a New Direction": the wizards would withdraw from "the discipline/manners/arbitration business" and become pure technicians — the society had "left the nest." Months later the Mr. Bungle incident (see [deep-play](./deep-play.md)) forced the vacuum closed: after a chaotic meeting settled nothing, one wizard unilaterally deleted the offender, and the wizards then instituted a petition-and-binding-ballot democracy — the first sustained legislature grown inside a running game. Lawrence Lessig's verdict in *Code* is the load-bearing line: the move to democracy "was not just an achievement. It was also a failure. The space had failed... to engender values in its population sufficient to avoid just the sort of evil Bungle had perpetrated... From a space self-regulated to a space regulated by self." In May 1996 the wizards reversed themselves ("LambdaMOO Takes Another Direction"): "The line between 'technical' and 'social' is not a clear one, and never can be" — reserving an explicit veto over any passed ballot. The population then ratified the self-coup by ballot, 321 to 111 with 272 abstaining: a legislature voting on the legitimacy of its own executive's reclaimed absolutism, with the count preserved as a primary document.

The pattern generalizes and the sovereigns know it. EVE Online's Council of Stellar Management — a player-elected advisory body — states in CCP's own white paper that real democratic accountability "cannot be emulated" because the world "relies on the technical support of CCP"; the same document declares player crime out of scope entirely ("under no circumstances will the legislator interfere"), which is what licenses EVE's tolerated-scam jurisprudence and drives players to build private institutions like EVE University's volunteer counterintelligence department. Empirically the growth of law with age appears to be a regularity: Frey et al.'s study of 80,000 online communities (Minecraft servers, subreddits, private WoW servers) through Ostrom's institutional framework finds formalization rising with community age, size, and engagement across all three platforms, with communities converging on weak norms over strong requirements. A world without an ending accretes institutions the way it accretes [griefing](./griefing.md) precedents and property claims ([virtual-property](./virtual-property.md)) — continuously, and always under a god.
