Philosophical traditions of Indigenous peoples. These are not pre-philosophical or proto-philosophical — they are philosophical traditions with their own epistemologies, ontologies, and ethical frameworks.

The library's grounding in Lakota epistemologies means Indigenous philosophy is not a peripheral addition but a structuring commitment. The formal architecture (the relational universe) is built on relational ontology arrived at through Indigenous thought, not on Western substance metaphysics.

The philosophy/_index.md declares `contains: [..., indigenous, ...]` and this directory occupies that slot. The intermediate exists to host the philosophical traditions of Indigenous peoples — each tradition held under the custodial authority of the people whose tradition it is — without collapsing them into a single "Indigenous philosophy" category that flattens real distinctions.

## Why an intermediate at all

The library's philosophy domain is organized by tradition of origin (buddhist, caribbean, western, etc.). "Indigenous" is itself a contested category: it refers to a vast and diverse set of peoples whose philosophical traditions are at least as different from one another as Buddhist and Western philosophy are. Treating "Indigenous philosophy" as if it were a single tradition would replicate exactly the homogenization that contemporary Indigenous scholars push back against (Vine Deloria Jr. is foundational here; see his critique of pan-Indian flattening in *God Is Red* and *We Talk, You Listen*).

The intermediate `indigenous/` exists as an organizational convenience — Indigenous philosophical traditions belong together in the library's tree because they share certain structural features (relational ontologies, place-based metaphysics, kinship-cosmological organization, oral-traditional transmission) and because they share a political situation (operating under settler-colonial pressure, generally less represented in academic philosophy than Western traditions) — but each child subtheory speaks for itself.

## What is here

- [`lakotaean-philosophy/`](theories/lakotaean-philosophy/) — the philosophical tradition of the Lakota people, authored under the captain's vested authority as a Lakota person.

## What might come here later

Slots are open for the philosophical traditions of other peoples, under the proper custodial authority of those peoples. Possible future subtheories include traditions of: Anishinaabe / Ojibwe, Diné / Navajo, Haudenosaunee, Inuit, Kanaka Maoli, Aboriginal Australian peoples, Māori, Mapuche, Quechua and Aymara, and many others. No such subtheory should be authored without the proper custodial standing.

## How to read this intermediate

The intermediate is thin by design. The substantive philosophical content lives in each tradition's subtheory; this index just locates the slot. The convention for adding a new child here: the child has a substantive `_index.md` authored under the proper authority, declares its sources, and treats its philosophical content on its own terms — not through the lens of another tradition (Indigenous or otherwise).

## Methodological notes for readers

A few orientations that recur across Indigenous philosophical traditions and that may help readers from other traditions:

- **Place is constitutive.** Many Indigenous philosophies are *place-based*: the philosophical content is bound to specific land, sites, and ecological relations. Vine Deloria Jr. ([deloria-vine-jr-1973](../../../sociology/theories/politics/theories/lakotaean-politics/references/deloria-vine-jr-1973.md), in the lakotaean-politics references) developed this point against the Western tradition's tendency to treat philosophical content as temporally and spatially generalizable.

- **Kinship is metaphysical.** Many Indigenous philosophies treat kinship as extending beyond humans — to animals, plants, places, and the wider cosmos. This is not metaphor in those traditions; it is the substance of how the world is structured.

- **Ceremony is method.** Knowledge is constituted and transmitted through ceremony, not only through propositional inscription. Shawn Wilson's *Research Is Ceremony* (Cree author, methodologically applicable across traditions) is one accessible articulation of this.

- **Authority is custodial.** Knowledge in Indigenous traditions is held by specific knowledge holders with the standing to hold and transmit it; it is not a public commons available for arbitrary citation. This is why authority for the children of this intermediate is custodial rather than open.
