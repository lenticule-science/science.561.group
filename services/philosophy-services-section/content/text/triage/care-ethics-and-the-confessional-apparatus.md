This document explains, drawing closely on the primary sources, why there is an irreducible structural conflict between care ethics — as developed by Nel Noddings, Joan Tronto, Virginia Held, and Carol Gilligan — and the confessional apparatus described by Michel Foucault. The conflict is not a matter of bad intention, insufficient skill, or failure to think carefully enough. It is built into the architecture of modern institutions, and it cannot be resolved by the practitioners who are caught within it. The document focuses on carceral settings (prisons, jails, detention, probation) as the sharpest site of collision, but the analysis applies wherever care-giving occurs inside an institution that requires subjects to produce themselves as knowable through supervised self-disclosure.

---

## I. The Caring Relation: What Noddings Requires

Nel Noddings begins *Caring: A Feminine Approach to Ethics and Moral Education* (1984) by locating the ethical primitive not in a principle, a duty, or a calculation of consequences, but in a concrete encounter between persons. The "ethical ideal," in her account, is built from accumulated experiences of caring and being cared for, and it motivates the effort to sustain the caring relation even when natural feeling is absent or depleted. The relation has a structure, and that structure is load-bearing — the ethical force of care depends on instantiating it correctly.

The structure has two poles: **the one-caring** and **the cared-for**. Two qualities constitute the caring relation at the pole of the one-caring.

### Engrossment

The first is *engrossment*: a particular mode of attention in which the one-caring receives the cared-for's reality on the cared-for's own terms.

> "When I care, I really hear, see, or feel what the other tries to convey... I allow the other's reality to really sink in."
> — Noddings, *Caring* (1984), p. 30

Noddings distinguishes engrossment sharply from empathy in the ordinary psychological sense. Empathy, as standardly understood, is projective: I put myself in your position and imagine what I would feel. Engrossment is receptive: I let your situation present itself as given and orient my attention toward it without filtering it through my own experience. The carer's categories, projects, and prior assumptions are backgrounded — not suppressed, but temporarily reorganized around what the other presents.

This receptive quality is why engrossment fails in specific and recognizable ways. The social worker who "knows" what a client needs before the client speaks, the rehabilitation counselor whose assessment form determines the shape of the encounter before anything is said — these practitioners have substituted projection and procedure for reception. They may be technically competent and even emotionally engaged. But engrossment, in Noddings's sense, has not occurred.

> "The engrossed carer sets aside her own projects, plans, and needs in order to receive what the other is trying to convey."
> — Noddings, *Caring* (1984), p. 33

Engrossment is not self-abnegation. The carer's consciousness persists; it is organized around what the other presents, not dissolved into it. This is what makes engrossment sustainable — the carer remains a subject, capable of responding, rather than disappearing into the other's reality.

### Motivational Displacement

The second constitutive quality is *motivational displacement*: the shift from pursuing one's own projects to acting on behalf of what the cared-for needs. Where engrossment is the receptive phase, motivational displacement is the active response.

> "My motive energy flows toward the other and perhaps, though not necessarily, toward his ends. I do not relinquish my ethical agency in this; I act with special regard for the particular other."
> — Noddings, *Caring* (1984), p. 33

Noddings is careful to distinguish this from Kantian duty. For Kant, the morally praiseworthy act is one performed *from duty*, especially when inclination pulls the other way. Noddings inverts this: the act performed purely from duty — without genuine engrossment, without motivational displacement — is a degraded form of care. It may be the right action, but it is not the caring relation. The distinction matters because an institution can require practitioners to perform the actions associated with care (assessment, referral, counseling, advocacy) while making genuine engrossment structurally impossible.

### Completion by the Cared-For

The caring relation, for Noddings, is not unilateral. It requires completion by the cared-for.

> "The caring relation requires the engrossment and motivational displacement of the one-caring and the recognition of this caring by the cared-for."
> — Noddings, *Caring* (1984), p. 69

This is a crucial structural feature. Care is not something done *to* someone; it is something accomplished *between* people, and its accomplishment is assessed from the cared-for's side. A person who has been processed by an institution — assessed, classified, referred, supervised — has not necessarily been cared for, even if every procedural step was followed correctly.

## II. Tronto: The Political Structure of Care

Joan Tronto's *Moral Boundaries: A Political Argument for an Ethic of Care* (1993) extends Noddings's phenomenology of the caring relation into a political analysis of how care is organized and what that organization does. Tronto's key contribution is the four-phase model, which makes visible how caring can be split apart in ways that systematically serve the interests of the powerful at the expense of both carers and cared-for.

The four phases and their corresponding moral qualities:

**Caring about** (attentiveness): noticing that care is needed. This sounds simple, but Tronto argues it is a political achievement. Privilege produces structured inattention: those who benefit from existing arrangements do not see the needs that others must meet.

> "Attentiveness, the first moral element of care, means that we must become aware of others' needs... Inattentiveness is a moral failure, not simply a cognitive mistake."
> — Tronto, *Moral Boundaries* (1993), p. 127

**Taking care of** (responsibility): assuming obligation to respond to the identified need. This is where the split between noticing and acting is institutionalized. Organizations and individuals routinely express concern while systematically delegating or deferring actual responsibility.

**Care-giving** (competence): the actual work of meeting the need. This is the phase concentrated in the hands of low-wage workers, predominantly women of color, migrants, and people without professional credentials. Its devaluation is structural, not accidental.

**Care-receiving** (responsiveness): assessing whether care was actually received from the perspective of the cared-for.

> "We must consider the perspective of the cared-for to assess whether the care-giving process has been successful... The response of the cared-for is always relevant to an assessment of whether care has been provided."
> — Tronto, *Moral Boundaries* (1993), p. 134

This fourth phase is where the conflict with institutional demands becomes sharpest. An institution defines adequacy from its own perspective: did the practitioner follow protocol, complete the assessment, make the required referrals? The cared-for's own experience of the encounter — whether they felt received, whether the care met their actual need — may not be tracked at all. Tronto's point is that when responsiveness is absent or systematically discounted, the caring relation collapses into something else: management, supervision, administration. The form of care is preserved while its substance is evacuated.

In *Caring Democracy* (2013), Tronto added a fifth phase, **caring with**, requiring solidarity and trust — the political condition in which everyone participates in both giving and receiving care. This phase is structurally blocked in carceral settings, where the power differential between carer and cared-for is institutionally mandated and legally enforced.

### Care as Political Concept

Virginia Held's *The Ethics of Care* (2006) draws out the political dimension explicitly:

> "Care ethics recommends the development of persons in caring relations rather than merely of individuals as separate selves. Persons in relations of care are better understood as interdependent than as either dependent or independent."
> — Held, *The Ethics of Care* (2006), p. 14

The relational ontology that care ethics proposes — persons as fundamentally interdependent rather than autonomously self-sufficient — is in direct tension with the liberal individualism presupposed by legal and penal institutions, which understand incarcerated persons as bearers of individual responsibility for individual acts.

## III. The Confessional Apparatus: What Foucault Identifies

Foucault's analysis of confession spans three major works: *Discipline and Punish* (1975), *The History of Sexuality, Volume 1* (1976), and the Collège de France lectures published as *On the Government of the Living* (1979–80). Together they identify a technology of subject-production that is structurally incompatible with the caring relation as Noddings describes it.

### The Shift from Spectacle to Discipline

*Discipline and Punish* traces how Western punishment shifted, between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries, from *sovereign power* — the public spectacle of torture, execution, and branding — to *disciplinary power* — the normalized surveillance of imprisoned bodies. The guillotine did not replace the scaffold for humane reasons. It replaced it because discipline proved more effective as a technology of social control.

> "Generally speaking, punishment is no longer a body to be destroyed, but a soul to be reformed."
> — Foucault, *Discipline and Punish* (1977), p. 16

The prison is not primarily a place of punishment. It is a machine for producing knowledge about its inmates and using that knowledge to normalize their behavior. Classification, observation, record-keeping, case management, parole assessment, therapeutic programming — these are not incidental to the prison. They are its operating mechanisms.

The panopticon — Jeremy Bentham's architectural design for a circular prison in which a central tower can observe all cells at all times, while prisoners cannot know whether they are currently being watched — is, for Foucault, the architectural figure of disciplinary power.

> "Hence the major effect of the Panopticon: to induce in the inmate a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power. So to arrange things that the surveillance is permanent in its effects, even if it is discontinuous in its action."
> — Foucault, *Discipline and Punish* (1977), pp. 200–201

What the panopticon produces is not merely controlled behavior. It produces a *self-surveilling subject*: a person who internalizes the gaze and monitors their own conduct as if permanently observed. The transformation from external constraint to internal self-governance is the signature achievement of disciplinary power. And this self-governance is not liberation — it is the most efficient form of control.

### The Examination as Power/Knowledge

The mechanism through which disciplinary power produces its knowledge is *the examination*.

> "The examination combines the techniques of an observing hierarchy and those of a normalizing judgment. It is a normalizing gaze, a surveillance that makes it possible to qualify, to classify and to punish."
> — Foucault, *Discipline and Punish* (1977), p. 184

Every institutional interaction in a prison that produces information about an inmate is, in this sense, an examination: the intake assessment, the psychiatric evaluation, the educational assessment, the vocational assessment, the risk-classification interview, the parole hearing. Each is simultaneously an exercise of institutional power and a method of producing knowledge that flows back into the apparatus that administers the inmate.

The examination is not neutral with respect to truth. It does not observe pre-existing facts; it constitutes its objects. The "high-risk offender," the "treatment-resistant inmate," the "model prisoner" are not descriptions of people who existed before the classification. They are produced through the practices of observation and record-keeping.

### The Confessional Technology

*The History of Sexuality, Volume 1* identifies a parallel mechanism operating through speech rather than through visible observation: the incitement to discourse, of which sacramental confession is the historical template.

Foucault's target is what he calls the *repressive hypothesis* — the popular view that Victorian society suppressed sexuality through silence and prohibition. His counterargument is that modernity has been characterized not by the suppression of self-disclosure but by its proliferation. Modern institutions do not silence subjects about their inner lives; they require subjects to speak at length about them.

> "Western man has become a confessing animal... one confesses one's crimes, one's sins, one's thoughts and desires, one's illnesses and troubles; one goes about telling, with the greatest precision, whatever is most difficult to tell."
> — Foucault, *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1978), p. 59

The confession is not merely a transfer of information from the speaking subject to the receiving authority. It is a *production of the subject as a particular kind of truth-bearer*. Three features define it.

First: **speech to a recognized authority**. The priest, the physician, the judge, the therapist, the parole officer — each occupies a position empowered to receive and evaluate the disclosure. Self-disclosure without a recognized receiving authority is not confession in this sense.

Second: **truth-production rather than truth-revelation**. The confession does not merely report facts; it constitutes the confessing subject as the kind of person who has these particular things to confess. The penitent who discloses sins becomes the sinful subject; the inmate who narrates their crime and psychological history becomes the classified offender; the therapy participant who articulates their trauma becomes the traumatized subject as a case-object.

Third: **power/knowledge function**. The speech feeds an apparatus that uses it to administer the speaker. Absolution, diagnosis, sentence, classification, treatment plan — each is a power-effect that proceeds from the confessional speech. The subject participates in their own administration through the very act of speaking.

> "The obligation to confess is now relayed through so many different points, is so deeply ingrained in us, that we no longer perceive it as the effect of a power that constrains us; on the contrary, it seems to us that truth, lodged in our most secret nature, 'demands' only to surface."
> — Foucault, *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1978), p. 60

This is Foucault's sharpest insight: the confessional apparatus is invisible as power precisely because it presents itself as liberation. The subject experiences the demand to speak as the natural expression of their authentic self. This is why the apparatus is so resistant to critique from within: those who operate it believe they are helping, and those subjected to it often experience resistance to disclosure as pathology rather than as a reasonable response to a power structure.

The later Collège de France lectures (*On the Government of the Living*, published 2014) extend this analysis through the concept of *avowal* (*l'aveu*) — the broader category of which sacramental confession is the most-studied case. Avowal includes any speech-act by which a subject self-positions before an authority: declarations of identity, professions of belief, testimony about one's own state.

> "Avowal designates a verbal act by which the subject affirms who he is, binds himself to this truth, places himself in a relationship of dependence with regard to another, and modifies at the same time his relationship to himself."
> — Foucault, *On the Government of the Living* (2014), p. 19

Every institutional encounter in a prison that requires the inmate to characterize themselves — their state of mind, their intentions, their history, their progress — is an avowal in Foucault's sense. This includes encounters with practitioners who are genuinely trying to help.

Chloë Taylor's *The Culture of Confession from Augustine to Foucault* (2013) provides the historical complement: the gradual emergence, from Augustine through Aquinas through Reformation Protestantism through modern psychiatry, of what she calls "the confessing animal" — a form of subjectivity for which self-disclosure to an authority is constitutive of being a self at all.

> "The confessing subject does not simply have a self that precedes confession; the self is partially constituted through confession, through the ongoing practice of articulating one's inner life to another."
> — Taylor, *The Culture of Confession* (2013), p. 4

## IV. The Structural Collision

The caring relation (Noddings) and the confessional apparatus (Foucault) collide because they make incompatible requirements of the same encounter.

### Care requires engrossment; the apparatus requires classification

Noddings's engrossment means receiving the other's reality on their own terms: attending to the particular person before you, not to the category into which they will be sorted. Motivational displacement means acting from their actual, presented need.

The confessional apparatus requires the opposite orientation: the practitioner attends to the subject in order to produce information that will classify them within a pre-existing framework (risk score, treatment category, diagnostic code, case type). The practitioner is not receiving the person; they are producing a case-object.

These are not merely different emphases. They require different modes of attention that cannot be held simultaneously. A practitioner who is conducting a risk assessment cannot simultaneously be engrossed in the person before them, because engrossment means letting the other's reality come forward without filtering it through the categories the assessment requires.

### Care requires responsiveness; the apparatus requires upward accountability

Tronto's fourth phase — responsiveness — locates the assessment of whether care occurred at the cared-for's perspective: did this person receive care? The apparatus locates the assessment upward: did the practitioner follow protocol, complete the required documentation, produce a classifiable outcome?

These accountabilities point in opposite directions. A practitioner who is genuinely responsive to the cared-for may have to deviate from protocol in order to meet the actual need. A practitioner who is institutionally accountable must follow protocol even when following it fails the person. Institutional survival selects for upward accountability; the caring relation requires downward accountability. In most carceral settings, only one of these is enforceable.

### Care requires the cared-for's recognition; the apparatus produces the subject who has been processed

Noddings's insistence that care requires completion by the cared-for — that the cared-for must recognize the care as care — is blocked by the structural power differential of incarceration. The incarcerated person who participates in a therapeutic program, who submits to a psychiatric evaluation, who narrates their history to a case manager, is in a coercive situation. Their "participation" feeds the apparatus regardless of whether they experience it as care. The classification proceeds whether or not they recognize the interaction as caring. The apparatus does not need their recognition; it needs their disclosure.

### Why disclosure is required for care at all

The reason this is a genuine dilemma rather than a straightforward rejection of institutional practice is that care ethics also requires knowing the person. Engrossment requires attending to their actual situation; motivational displacement requires knowing what they actually need. You cannot care for a person you know nothing about. Some disclosure is necessary for care to be possible.

The confessional apparatus exploits exactly this requirement. It presents the demand for disclosure as the natural precondition of care: "I need to know your history before I can help you." From within the encounter, this is indistinguishable from genuine care-seeking. The mechanism operates precisely because it attaches to the legitimate epistemic requirement of care.

The difference — between disclosure that serves the caring relation and disclosure that serves the apparatus — is real but difficult to operationalize in practice. Disclosure that serves the caring relation: what the carer learns is used only to respond to the person's presented need, is not routed into classification systems, does not feed the apparatus that administers the person. Disclosure that serves the apparatus: what the person says produces them as a classified subject whose record will outlast the encounter and constrain them in future interactions with the institution.

In carceral settings, the second form is structurally mandated. There is no conversation with an incarcerated person that does not feed the apparatus — through mandatory reporting requirements, case notes, risk assessment updating, parole documentation. The practitioner cannot choose to receive someone's disclosure in the caring mode and guarantee that it will not be routed into the administrative apparatus. The routing is not under the practitioner's control.

## V. Why the People in Tension Cannot Resolve It

The most important analytical move — and the one most consistently missed by practitioners who encounter this conflict — is understanding why the structural problem cannot be resolved through individual effort, improved technique, or better intentions.

### The apparatus is not constituted by practitioners' intentions

Foucault is explicit that disciplinary power does not operate primarily through the intentions of the individuals who exercise it.

> "The carceral system combines in a single figure discourses and architectures, coercive regulations and scientific propositions, real social effects and invincible utopias, programmes for correcting delinquents and mechanisms that reinforce delinquency... Discipline 'makes' individuals; it is the specific technique of a power that regards individuals both as objects and as instruments of its exercise."
> — Foucault, *Discipline and Punish* (1977), p. 294

A practitioner who genuinely cares, who is well-trained in care ethics, who makes every effort to engross in the particular person before them, is still embedded in institutional structures that route whatever they learn into the classificatory apparatus. The practitioner does not control the case management system, the mandatory reporting requirements, the risk assessment algorithms, the parole board's use of clinical notes. The apparatus operates through them whether they endorse it or not.

This is not a failure of the practitioner. It is a structural feature of the institution. The care ethics literature recognizes this clearly. As Tronto writes:

> "Care is not simply a matter of the intentions of individuals, however important intentions may be... To adequately care requires an examination of the social context in which care occurs."
> — Tronto, *Moral Boundaries* (1993), p. 111

### The knowledge-production system survives individual practitioners

The power/knowledge apparatus does not depend on any particular practitioner for its operation. Classifications, diagnostic categories, risk scores, and case records outlast the individual encounters through which they were produced. A practitioner who leaves the institution does not take the records with them. A person who was diagnosed, classified, and scored in one encounter carries that classification into every subsequent encounter.

This means that even if an individual practitioner managed to conduct every single interaction in a mode of genuine care — attending to the person, not the category; accountable to the person's experience, not the protocol — the apparatus would continue to operate around them. The person they cared for yesterday will arrive at tomorrow's parole hearing with the records produced by every previous interaction, including the caring one, and those records will be used by the apparatus regardless of the caring intent with which they were produced.

### The norm-conflict is politically blocked

The structural conflict between care ethics (restorative, relational, responsive to the particular person) and the carceral apparatus (retributive, classificatory, accountable upward to the institution) cannot be resolved at the level of individual practice because the authority to resolve it does not rest with practitioners.

Changing the mandatory reporting requirements, revising the risk assessment instruments, restructuring the institutional accountability system — these are political acts that require authority the practitioner does not hold. The legal framework that mandates the apparatus was established by legislatures and courts. The institutional procedures were established by administrators. The professional licensing requirements were established by credentialing bodies. None of these are accessible through good practice alone.

This is not an argument for passivity. It is an argument for accuracy about what can and cannot be accomplished from within the practitioner role. A practitioner who believes they can resolve the structural conflict through personal virtue is not more helpful — they are less helpful, because they will mistake failure of the structure for personal failure, or worse, they will cover over the structural problem with a performance of caring that obscures the apparatus rather than challenging it.

### The incitement to discourse exploits the care-relationship

Foucault's most uncomfortable insight for care practitioners is that the confessional apparatus sustains itself in part by being genuinely helpful. The therapeutic relationship is real. The care that flows through institutional care-giving is real. The problem is that the same relationship that makes care possible also makes subject-production possible, and the apparatus cannot be extracted from the care without destroying the care.

> "The obligation to confess is not born of the relationship between a knowing subject and an unknown object; it is inscribed in a diffuse relationship between power and pleasure: the pleasure of an analysis that discovers, and the power that discovers the pleasure."
> — Foucault, *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1978), pp. 71–72

This is why the confessional apparatus is experienced as liberation from within: it *is* liberating, in certain respects, while simultaneously subjugating. The person who finally articulates their history to a therapist who genuinely listens may experience real relief, real recognition, real care. And their articulation has produced a case-record, fed a risk assessment, and contributed to a classification that will constrain them. Both things are true simultaneously. The apparatus exploits the real value of the care to sustain the extraction it requires.

## VI. What the Literature Points Toward Without Resolving

The literature does not provide a resolution, because there is none available at the level of individual practice. What it does provide is a clearer account of the terrain.

Foucault's late work on the *care of the self* (*epimeleia heautou*) — developed in volumes 2 and 3 of *The History of Sexuality* and the Collège de France lectures on *The Hermeneutics of the Subject* — gestures at non-confessional modes of self-formation drawn from Greco-Roman sources. The Stoic practice of self-examination was not disclosure to an external authority but a reflexive practice conducted for the self's own transformation. Whether anything like this can be sustained within a carceral institution — where the self-examination will inevitably be observed, evaluated, and used by the apparatus — is not answered by Foucault. He identifies the alternative mode; he does not claim it is available under all institutional conditions.

Peter Brooks's *Troubling Confessions* (2000) raises the problem from the legal side: the legal system's intense investment in obtaining confessions — its preference for the confessing subject over the externally-proven-guilty subject — reflects a deep structural requirement that goes beyond evidentiary efficiency. The confessing subject is more useful to the apparatus than the merely-proven-guilty one because the confession produces the guilty subject as an interior truth-bearer, not merely as an external fact. This means that the appetite for disclosure in the carceral setting is not a contingent feature that could be reformed away; it is a structural requirement of how the institution constitutes its authority.

Carol Gilligan's original formulation in *In a Different Voice* (1982) — which identified care ethics as a different moral voice rather than a deficient one — positioned the caring orientation as an alternative to the justice orientation of dominant liberal moral theory. The carceral system is built on the justice orientation: rule-governed, impartial, concerned with rights and procedures rather than with relationships. Gilligan's insight that these are genuinely different orientations — not reducible to each other — is the ground-level explanation for why practitioners trained in care ethics experience the carceral institution as structured to resist what they are trying to do. They are not wrong. It is.

The abolitionist literature (Ruth Wilson Gilmore, Angela Davis, Mariame Kaba) draws the conclusion that the structural conflict is not resolvable within the carceral institution at all — that the institution must be dismantled and replaced with institutions organized around care, mutual aid, and community accountability rather than punishment and classification. This is the political consequence of the structural analysis. It does not tell the practitioner working inside the institution tomorrow what to do. But it tells them what kind of problem they are facing.

## Summary

The tension between care ethics and the confessional apparatus in carceral settings is structural, not contingent. It arises because:

1. The caring relation (Noddings) requires engrossment — receiving the other on their own terms — and completion by the cared-for. The confessional apparatus requires the opposite: the subject speaks to an authority who classifies them within a pre-existing framework, and the classification proceeds independent of the subject's experience of the encounter.

2. Responsible care (Tronto) requires responsiveness — accountability to the cared-for's experience of care. Institutional care requires upward accountability — compliance with protocol, documentation, classification. These point in incompatible directions.

3. The apparatus does not operate through practitioners' intentions. It operates through the structures in which they are embedded: mandatory reporting, case records, risk assessments, institutional accountability. Practitioners who care well still feed the apparatus.

4. The authority to change the structural conflict lies outside the practitioner role, at the level of political and legal change that practitioners individually cannot accomplish.

5. The apparatus sustains itself in part through genuine care. The therapeutic relation is real; the help is real; and the subject-production is also real. These cannot be separated from within the encounter.

The people caught in this tension — practitioners trying to care and institutions requiring classification — cannot resolve it through better practice because the conflict is not located at the level of practice. It is located at the level of institutional structure, which is a political problem, not a technical one.
