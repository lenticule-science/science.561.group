# Two lines: the Medicine Line and the Gadsden line

This note sets the [Medicine Line](../../../../../../glossary/terms/medicine-line.md) — the 49th parallel, as Lakota, Blackfoot, Assiniboine, Métis, and Cree people named it — beside the Gadsden line at Lochiel, Arizona, documented in this corpus's [companion history](./2026-08-22-note-on-lochiel-history.md) and its glossary entry on [La Gran Línea](../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/social-and-economic-geography-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/la-gran-linea.md). The two lines share a treaty-year proximity — the Gadsden Purchase in 1853-54, the Convention of London fixing the 49th parallel as astronomical boundary in 1818 and the 1872-74 survey that finally marked it on the ground — and neither was drawn with the consent, or even the knowledge, of the nations it crossed. What makes the pairing useful for this corpus is not the resemblance. It is that the two lines' documented histories run this corpus's [exclusion-powers](../../../../../../glossary/terms/exclusion-powers.md) argument in opposite directions, on two independent evidentiary bases compiled by different research passes on the same day. A thesis attested once is an interesting reading of one case. Attested twice, with the polarity flipped and the mechanism unchanged, it starts to look like structure.

## 1. Two fictions, both operative

Neither line is where its own treaty says it is, and both governments on both lines have chosen the marker over the mathematics.

The Gadsden line's clearest documented flaw is Monument 127, placed by William Emory's 1855 survey near what became Lochiel **4.41 miles** from the boundary's own treaty-intended coordinates — an error the Mexican commissioner Francisco Jiménez raised in real time on June 15, 1855 and Emory dismissed outright, and that the 1891-96 Barlow-Blanco resurvey had the technical capacity to correct and chose instead to leave standing. The Medicine Line's comparable flaw is diffuse rather than singular: modern satellite geodesy (the Digital Chart of the World dataset, as reproduced in standard reference treatments — attested-secondary, the dataset itself not read directly for this corpus) finds the 1872-74 monument line running on average 270 meters south of the true 49th parallel, with local deviations ranging from about 810 meters south to 590 meters north along its roughly 860-mile prairie run — an artifact of 1870s astronomical instruments that could not correct for the Earth's departure from a perfect ellipsoid, not a single overruled objection like Jiménez's. The scale of the two errors is not comparable — Monument 127's single, large, discovered-and-dismissed displacement is roughly seven times the average magnitude of the northern line's diffuse instrument drift — but the governing choice is identical on both lines: both pairs of states have formally agreed to treat the *located monuments*, not the treaty's astronomical abstraction, as the legal boundary. The error was never fixed on either line. It was declared authoritative.

That both lines are, in this specific and measurable sense, fictional — a parallel of latitude and a set of treaty coordinates that the physical markers do not actually trace — while remaining fully load-bearing as instruments of exclusion is the first plank of this corpus's [property-as-disposition](../../../../../../glossary/terms/property-as-disposition.md) argument: a border's power to admit or expel was never a property of its geodetic accuracy. Both lines prove this the same way, independently, because both governments' own surveyors handed them the proof and both governments filed it away rather than act on it.

## 2. Opposite polarities

Where the two lines diverge is in which direction their exclusion-powers point, and this is the pairing's real evidentiary weight.

The Medicine Line's power, for the specific years this corpus has documented, pointed away from the people crossing it and toward the state pursuing them. After the June 1876 destruction of Custer's column at the [Greasy Grass](../../../../../../glossary/terms/greasy-grass.md), Sitting Bull's Húŋkpapȟa and allied Lakota bands crossed into the North-West Territories beginning in the winter of 1876-77, with Sitting Bull's own band arriving at Wood Mountain in May 1877; General Alfred Terry's peace commission could only ask him, at Fort Walsh on October 17, 1877, to come south voluntarily, because no lawful path existed for the U.S. Army — an army that had just annihilated a third of a regiment — to cross a boundary its own government fully recognized. No American soldier ever crossed the line to take him. The negative case, documented in the medicine-line glossary term and the companion notes on [the medicine line's differential grip](./2026-08-22-note-on-the-medicine-line.md), makes the point more precisely than the positive one: Chief Joseph's Nez Perce, fleeing the same summer toward the same sanctuary, were cornered at the Bear Paw Mountains and surrendered on October 5, 1877, by most tellings roughly forty miles short of the boundary. Joseph never crossed, and the line did nothing for him at all. The Medicine Line's power belonged strictly to the fact of crossing, not to intention, direction of flight, or proximity — which is exactly why it counts as a documented instance of an exclusion-power running, for once, against the state that built the apparatus rather than for it.

The Gadsden line's exclusion-powers, across its whole documented history and especially in its 2025-26 present, point the other way: at the people the line was drawn through. The Tohono O'odham Nation's ancestral territory — which ran from the Gila River to the Sea of Cortez — was bisected with no consultation in 1853-54; the Nation's 62-mile boundary saw a federal judge deny its injunction against wall construction on August 13-14, 2026, reading a 1907 reservation-strip provision as permitting the very barrier the Nation sued to stop. The San Rafael Valley segment of that 2026 build carries a $309.46 million contract and a federal waiver of 34 laws under §102 REAL ID Act authority — an apparatus with no analogue anywhere on the 49th parallel, where the physical marking remains, in 2026, what it was in 1874: a mowed twenty-foot vista and a stone cairn. Both lines are dispositional objects in this corpus's technical sense — their power to admit or expel is switched on and off by the state holding the apparatus, not fixed by the ground — but one apparatus was, for four specific years, aimed at the pursuer, and the other has been aimed at the pursued for its entire documented existence and is being actively re-armed as this note is written.

## 3. The crossing-rights asymmetry

The two lines also differ in what, if anything, was ever written down to protect Indigenous crossing, and the difference is textual, not just historical.

Article III of the 1794 [Jay Treaty](../../../../../../glossary/terms/jay-treaty-border-crossing-right.md) named Indigenous passage across the northern line as a right the treaty's own drafters had to carve out, eleven years after the boundary it addresses was first described and eighty years before the 49th parallel itself was surveyed on the ground. That right survived only in damaged form — the Supreme Court's *Karnuth v. United States ex rel. Albro*, 279 U.S. 231 (decided April 8, 1929), held the treaty's general free-passage guarantee abrogated by the War of 1812 — and Congress restored the U.S. side of it by statute, not treaty interpretation: 8 U.S.C. § 1359, enacted in 1952, preserves the crossing right for persons who "possess at least 50 per centum of blood of the American Indian race," a settler blood-quantum arithmetic laid over Indigenous peoplehood, but a legal entitlement nonetheless, one a U.S. immigration officer is obligated to honor rather than free to withdraw. Canada has never recognized a reciprocal right; Canadian courts treat *Karnuth*'s abrogation as closing the question on that side of the line entirely, and a status Indian or other Indigenous person crossing north enters under the same immigration framework as anyone else.

Nothing comparable was ever written into the Gadsden Purchase or Guadalupe Hidalgo for the O'odham, Yaqui, Kumeyaay, or any other nation the southern line divided. What exists in its place is the Tohono O'odham Nation's Enhanced Tribal Card, a WHTI-compliant travel document issued under a memorandum of agreement between the Nation and DHS/CBP — an administrative accommodation DHS chose to grant, revocable and renegotiable at the agency's discretion, resting on no treaty text and no statute comparable to § 1359. The asymmetry is not merely that one line's Indigenous crossing right is better enforced than the other's, though it is: Blackfeet members report inconsistent U.S. enforcement of Jay Treaty rights even where the treaty's language is plain, and Métis citizenship cards are not accepted as standalone proof on either side. The asymmetry is categorical. One line carries a named, if damaged, textual concession the affected nations can litigate and Congress has had to legislate around. The other carries a filing labeled "accommodation" that the agency which wrote it can also unwrite — [settlerism-as-filing-system](../../../../../../glossary/terms/settlerism-as-filing-system.md) doing, on the Gadsden line, exactly the work a filing labeled "treaty right" would do, except that the first filing has no floor under it and the second, however unevenly honored, does.

## 4. Grandmother's Land, and a grandmother tree

Two facts, kept separate here rather than merged: in Lakota, Canada is Uŋčíyapi Makȟóčhe, "Grandmother's Land," Uŋčí (grandmother) applying to Queen Victoria on a kinship pattern this corpus's [exile note](./2026-08-22-note-on-the-exile-in-grandmothers-land.md) traces back at least to the 1862 Dakota War refugees, fifteen years before Sitting Bull's own crossing — an attested-secondary usage, corroborated across multiple sources, that named a specific hope: that a polity entered under a grandmother's kinship idiom owed the protection a grandmother owes a grandchild, food and standing included. Canada did not honor that hope; the exile note documents the refusal at length. In late June 2026, at Lochiel on the Gadsden line, four old cottonwoods facing removal for border-wall construction began to be called "grandmothers" by participants in an Indigenous women youth-led solstice ceremony, per the companion Lochiel history's organizer attribution (Kate Scott, Madrean Archipelago Wildlife Center) — a naming this note has found no documentation tying to a specific nation's word or ceremony, Lakota or otherwise, and states as such rather than assuming a lineage the sources do not support.

These are not the same word, the same people, or a demonstrated line of transmission across 150 years, and this note does not claim they are. What is factual, and worth naming without inflating it into more than it is, is the recurrence of the pattern itself: two Indigenous-inflected namings, a century and a half apart, on two different settler borders, both reaching for kinship — specifically an elder-female kinship term — to describe a tree or a territory understood as offering the kind of protection a state was refusing to guarantee by law. Whether that recurrence reflects a genuinely shared Indigenous grammar for naming refuge under threat, or is simply what "grandmother" does in English when applied to something old and protective, is a question this note leaves open rather than resolves; either reading is compatible with the facts as sourced, and only the second requires no further evidence.

## 5. What this comparison establishes, and what it does not

It establishes, on two geographically and chronologically independent bases, that a settler-drawn line's power to exclude is disposition, not geography: both lines are measurably not where their treaties say, both have been formally re-anchored to the flawed marker rather than the true coordinate, and both remain fully operative as instruments regardless. It establishes that the same dispositional mechanism can be energized in opposite directions by the states that hold it, which is the stronger claim — a mechanism that only ever ran one way could be redescribed as that direction's own special cause rather than as a general property of borders as such. And it establishes, via the Jay Treaty asymmetry, that when a settler state does write a floor under Indigenous crossing, the northern line is where it happened and the southern line is where it did not, which is itself evidence for the corpus's claim that these powers are chosen and held, not naturally occurring.

What it does not establish is that the Medicine Line's four years were egalitarian or costless to the Canadian side's own interests. Canada's welcome was never full sovereignty restored: the [North-West Mounted Police](../../../../../../glossary/terms/north-west-mounted-police.md) entry's own framing is exact on this point, describing the force's conduct at Wood Mountain and Fort Walsh as "a more patient, better-administered route" to the same destination the U.S. cavalry reached by force, not a different destination. Ottawa's refusal to grant a reserve, its pressure on Superintendent Walsh to encourage Sitting Bull's departure, and his 1880 reassignment once that pressure produced no result were all policy choices serving a Canadian interest: avoiding both the cost of provisioning several thousand foreign refugees and the diplomatic risk of appearing to harbor a hostile force against a power Canada had no wish to antagonize. And what ended the exile was not a failure of the line but the success, on schedule, of a starvation policy: the northern buffalo herds largely failed to reach Canadian territory by autumn 1878, Canadian policy supplied no ration in their place — a choice, since Canada was provisioning treaty nations elsewhere in the same territory under the same numbered-treaty system at the same time — and Sitting Bull's remaining people were starved back across a line no soldier had ever managed to cross, surrendering at Fort Buford on July 19, 1881. The refuge was real. It also had a clock on it that Ottawa controlled and never had to announce. The [Wood Mountain Lakota First Nation](../../../../../../glossary/terms/wood-mountain-lakota.md) — the thirty-seven founding families who stayed rather than go home in 1881 — waited fifty-three years, until an Order-in-Council on August 5, 1930, for even provisional recognition, which is the same filing-system patience running past the crisis and into the following century.

## What the second case does to the theory

A single documented case of an exclusion-power inverting its usual direction is a striking anecdote; this corpus already had one, in the four years the Medicine Line held against the U.S. Army. What the Gadsden-line research adds is not a second inversion — it is the opposite polarity, the more common one, independently derived by different researchers working from different sources on the same day, converging on the same mechanism: a geodetically fictional line, ratified over its own known error, whose power to admit or expel tracks the state's convenience rather than the ground. That the Medicine Line ran toward refuge and the Gadsden line runs toward exclusion is not a contradiction between the two cases; it is the theory's prediction confirmed twice, because a genuinely dispositional power should be capable of pointing either way depending on who holds it and against whom, and these two lines are exactly that test performed by history rather than by the corpus. The Jay Treaty's textual floor under one line's crossing right, and its absence under the other's, plus the honest accounting of the Medicine Line's own limits — a NWMP that administered rather than abolished the same claim, a refuge with a starvation clock built into its ending — keep this from reading as a tidy morality tale in either direction. What it establishes instead is what the corpus needed a second, independent case to establish: that the border's dispositional character is structure, attested now on two lines with opposite polarities, and not a property this corpus discovered once and has been reading back into everything since.
