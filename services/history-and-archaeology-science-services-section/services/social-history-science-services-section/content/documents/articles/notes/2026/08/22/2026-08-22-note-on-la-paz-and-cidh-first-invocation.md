# La Paz and CIDH: two unused (or once-used) levers, precisely mapped

Research pass 2026-08-22, Sonoran round, stream four. Task: map the La Paz Agreement machinery and the IACHR precautionary-measures process as potential first-invocation friction points, with status tags. Verification statuses: confirmed (direct read/multiple convergent sources) / attested-secondary (single or unconfirmed-chain source) / gap.

## Framework 1 — La Paz Agreement / Border 2025

**Status: confirmed institutional structure; confirmed never invoked against border-barrier construction (absence-in-search, not a documented negative).**

**The machinery, precisely** (confirmed, EPA official pages, cross-checked against the walls-versus-water and Mexican-legal-frame notes already in this corpus):

- **Origin**: signed Aug 14, 1983, La Paz, entered into force Feb 16, 1984. Covers a 100-km zone each side of the line.
- **National Coordinators**: EPA (US side) and **SEMARNAT** (Mexican side) — leadership, oversight, overall program management; meet at least annually with Policy Workgroups and Regional Coordinators.
- **Policy Workgroups** (current structure, descended from the original six annexes): four thematic groups — **Air, Water, Land, Emergencies** — each with an EPA co-chair and a **SEMARNAT co-chair**. These groups "consider review and comments from US and Mexican States, US Federally recognized Tribes, **Mexican indigenous and Afro-Mexican communities**," and take input from NGOs and the public.
- **Regional Coordinators**: four regional bodies; the one that matters here is **Arizona/Sonora/Tribes** — one state co-chair, one federal co-chair per country (US side: EPA Regional Administrator; Mexican side: a SEMARNAT representative). They produce biennial Action Plans.
- **Task Forces**: created and managed by the Regional Workgroups; convene local governments, tribes, academia, NGOs, and the public to identify priority issues and run pilot projects. Historically active as the "Border 2020 Arizona/Sonora Regional Workgroup Task Force" (predecessor cycle) — the same structure under the current Border 2025 name.
- **Public input channel**: **Public Plenary meetings**, where EPA and binational program partners "share updates with the community and solicit comments and insights from the public" — the documented, standing venue where a Mexican indigenous community, ejido, ayuntamiento, or NGO could formally place the San Rafael crossings on record.

**Named Mexican-side contacts** (SEMARNAT Arizona/Sonora regional co-chair, specific individual) were not resolved this pass — EPA's contact-sheet PDFs (Arizona-Sonora Regional Representatives; Region 9 Border Team Contacts Sheet) were listed but not machine-readable in this session; **gap**, next-step retrieval item.

**The "never invoked" finding, held**: no instance was found — in EPA's Border 2020/2025 program documents, in the Mumme & Collins institutional retrospective, or in general search — of the La Paz Agreement or its Border 2020/2025 successor programs being used as a channel to raise concerns about border-barrier/fence/wall construction, at any point across the Secure Fence Act (2006), the 2017–2021 wall program, or the 2025–26 San Rafael project. This matches and extends the finding already logged in `note-on-the-mexican-indigenous-and-legal-frame.md` (no CILA statement on the 2025–26 wall found; CILA's own bulletin archive bot-walled and unconfirmed either way). **Two adjacent binational Mexican-federal channels — CILA (1944 Treaty, Art. 2, jurisdiction over "works on the boundary") and SEMARNAT/La Paz (environmental cooperation, Policy Workgroups) — both show the same shape: real jurisdictional or procedural hooks, and no confirmed record of anyone pulling them for a border wall, ever.**

**The procedural path, as it exists on paper** (attested-secondary — reconstructed from the program's own description of its process, not from a worked example of it being used this way):
1. A Mexican federal entity (SEMARNAT itself, as co-chair) or a border-zone actor (ayuntamiento, NGO, indigenous community) raises the San Rafael crossings — flow obstruction, wash damming, water/land impacts — at an **Arizona/Sonora Regional Workgroup Public Plenary** or through the **Land** or **Water Policy Workgroup** (both have direct thematic fit: the walls-versus-water note's hydraulic-obstruction findings and the La Noria note's root/canopy-in-Mexico fact both map cleanly onto "land" and "water" as defined categories).
2. Because Policy Workgroups explicitly canvass "Mexican indigenous and Afro-Mexican communities" for review and comment, a submission from or endorsed by the **Consejo Supremo de Ancianos** or the Santa Cruz ayuntamiento would sit squarely inside the program's stated intake categories.
3. La Paz/Border 2025 has no enforcement teeth and cannot stop construction — its function is to put a binational, documented record in front of SEMARNAT and EPA, which is itself the friction: SEMARNAT would then hold formal knowledge, on the binational record, of a project no Mexican federal body has yet been shown to have reviewed.

## Framework 2 — IACHR / CIDH precautionary measures (medidas cautelares)

**Status: confirmed general mechanism; confirmed directly on-point precedent (2017, same Consejo, same border area, same threat).**

**The mechanism, precisely** (confirmed, CIDH Reglamento Art. 25 and CIDH's own published guidance, cross-checked across independent secondary summaries after the OAS site itself returned 403 to automated fetch this session):

- **Legal basis**: Reglamento de la CIDH, Artículo 25. The Commission may act "a iniciativa propia o a solicitud de parte" — on its own initiative or **at the request of a party** — meaning a petition does not require the state's cooperation to originate; **any interested party** — alleged victims, their representatives, an NGO, legal counsel — may file.
- **Criteria** (all three required): **gravedad** (serious impact on a protected right), **urgencia** (imminence of the risk), **daño irreparable** (harm affecting rights not susceptible to repair or restoration — i.e., destroying the tree, or blasting a sacred site, is definitionally the kind of harm this criterion is built for).
- **Procedure**: request goes to a study phase; as a general rule it must first be brought to the knowledge of the State before being granted; the Commission (in session, or the President/Vice-President between sessions, consulting other members through the Executive Secretariat) decides. States are typically given ~10 days to respond to the transmitted communication and, once a measure is granted, ~15 days to report on implementation.
- **Timeline — no fixed deadline**: CIDH's own published guidance states explicitly that it **cannot set a fixed timeframe** for a final decision; response time depends on the matter's evolution, the volume of requests, and complexity, with priority triage by gravity/urgency. **Base rate, for calibration**: in 2019, CIDH approved 74 of 3,212 petitions reviewed (~2.3%); since 2006, only 53 precautionary measures total have issued for petitions filed regarding people in the United States. This is a real, live channel — and a long-shot one; both facts should travel together to any principal being handed this option.
- **Jurisdiction over the US**: the US has not ratified the American Convention but remains subject to CIDH petitions under the **American Declaration of the Rights and Duties of Man** as an OAS member state — confirmed by the existence of the precedent below, filed and processed against US federal action.

**Precedent 1 — directly on point (2017, attested-secondary, multiple convergent Spanish-language sources — La Jornada, El Financiero, RegeneraciónMX)**: In March 2017, facing the first Trump administration's wall program, the **Consejo Supremo (Consejo Supremo de Ancianos) of the Tohono O'odham** — described in these sources as "cuerpo autónomo... con autoridades tradicionalmente reconocidas por el gobierno mexicano," and named elsewhere in this session's research as the specific Mexican-side traditional authority (spokesperson/translator Matías Valenzuela) whose bypass by CFE's Plan Sonora process was itself already logged as a finding — decided to petition CIDH to halt wall construction across their 120-km territory. The mechanism used: the Consejo sent its request to Mexico's **Secretaría de Gobernación** (then Miguel Ángel Osorio Chong) and to **Jaime Martínez Veloz**, head of the **Comisión para el Diálogo con los Pueblos Indígenas** — asking the Mexican government to prepare the **medida cautelar** the Pápago people would then present to CIDH. Ethnic governor Alicia Chuhuhua (Pozo Prieto) is the named quoted official. **This is the same institution, the same border segment's Indigenous nation, and the same procedural channel (CIDH medida cautelar via Mexican-federal-government preparation) that would be in play for a San Rafael Valley invocation today.** No outcome/decision record for the 2017 request was found this pass (gap — the request appears to have been prepared/announced but a subsequent CIDH ruling was not located; worth a direct pull of CIDH's Registro de Peticiones before citing this as "granted" or "denied" to any principal).
- **Precedent 2 — same nation, current wall, no CIDH component found (2026, confirmed)**: The Tohono O'odham Nation sued DHS in US federal court (D.D.C.) in June 2026 over the current wall program; Judge Richard Leon denied a preliminary injunction and left the case under review as of the last-found reporting (Mongabay ES, July 2026; Native News Online). Sacred sites and burial grounds are the stated basis. No CIDH filing was found associated with this 2026 US-side litigation — the tribe's current fight runs through US courts, not CIDH, as far as located.
- **Precedent 3 — sacred-site pattern, other countries (confirmed, CIDH's own indigenous-rights rapporteurship listings, cross-checked)**: CIDH has granted precautionary measures explicitly protecting Indigenous sacred sites before — the **Maya community of Sitio El Rosario-Naranjo** (Guatemala, July 14, 2006, archaeological zone and sacred site for Maya spiritual practice) and the **Lof Paichil Antriao community, Mapuche people** (Argentina, Apr 6, 2011, protecting access to a sacred site, the Rewe, from harassment/obstruction). These establish that "a sacred site under threat" is itself a recognized fact pattern the Commission has acted on, independent of the border-specific 2017 case.
- **Precedent 4 — infrastructure-vs-sacred-site request, no confirmed outcome (2016, attested-secondary)**: the Standing Rock, Cheyenne River, and Yankton Sioux tribes filed a 25-page CIDH precautionary-measures request (Dec 2, 2016) over the Dakota Access Pipeline, citing sacred sites and absence of free, prior, and informed consent. No confirmed grant/denial outcome was located this pass (gap) — cited here only as a template for what such a filing looks like in practice (co-filed by multiple tribes with legal-clinic support), not as a result to point to.

## What this gives the operation

Both frameworks are real, both are procedurally open to the actors this operation already supports, and both currently sit unused for this wall (La Paz: confirmedly never invoked for any border barrier, ever; CIDH: invoked once, in 2017, by the exact same Consejo, against an earlier iteration of the exact same threat, with the outcome undetermined). The La Paz path is slow, toothless, and binational-record-building; the CIDH path is a long-shot (2.3% base rate) but carries the strongest single available precedent — the same principal already walked through this exact door once. Per doctrine, neither path is ours to choose for anyone: this note hands emsenn the mapped machinery and the 2017 precedent as evidence to offer, not a recommendation to make.
