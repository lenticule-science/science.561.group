# The medicine line's differential grip: who it stopped, and who it never touched

Compiled 2026-08-22 alongside the [medicine-line glossary term](../../../../../../glossary/terms/medicine-line.md) and this same date's two companion notes — [the exile in Grandmother's Land](./2026-08-22-note-on-the-exile-in-grandmothers-land.md), which carries the full Sitting Bull chronology and its disputed head counts, and [the line and the other nations](./2026-08-22-note-on-the-line-and-the-other-nations.md), which carries the comparative Dakota/Blackfoot/Cree/Métis crossing history in depth. This note does not re-derive either. Its subject is narrower and, for the corpus's purposes, more load-bearing: the 49th parallel was, for most of its four-hundred-mile prairie run, not fenced, not walled, and barely marked — twenty-eight astronomical stations and scattered iron posts, earth mounds, and stone cairns, often line-of-sight apart, laid down by the joint North American Boundary Commission between 1872 and the last marker at Waterton Lake on August 8, 1874 (attested-secondary, multiply corroborated across boundary-commission histories; see also this corpus's [la gran línea](../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/social-and-economic-geography-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/la-gran-linea.md) entry on the Commission's sister survey to the south). A line that thin cannot, as a physical object, explain why it stopped the U.S. Army in 1877 and did nothing at all to stop whiskey traders in 1869-74, wolf hunters in 1873, the Commission's own surveyors in 1872-74, or the Métis brigades that had been crossing it seasonally for a generation before it existed. The explanation is not in the ground. It is in who each crosser was to the two states on either side of it — which is exactly the corpus's [property-as-disposition](../../../../../../glossary/terms/property-as-disposition.md) argument, tested here against a boundary with no fence to hide the argument's abstraction behind.

## The historiography, briefly distinguished

Four works anchor this corpus's treatment of the term, and they are not interchangeable. Beth LaDow's *The Medicine Line: Life and Death on a North American Borderland* (2001, Routledge) is a transnational social history of the Montana-Saskatchewan borderland across a full century, not a Sitting Bull biography; her argument is that this ground refutes the triumphalist settlement myth for everyone who tried to live on it, Indigenous and settler alike, and she uses Sitting Bull's exile and Chief Joseph's failed flight as opening bookends to a story that continues through the region's twentieth-century dry-farming boom and bust. Wallace Stegner's *Wolf Willow* (1962, Viking) is a different genre entirely — a personal memoir of boyhood in Eastend, Saskatchewan (the book's "Whitemud"), with a chapter titled "The Medicine Line" that renders the boundary as lived, remembered landscape rather than as an analytic object; it corroborates the term's currency and texture rather than its causal history. Robert Utley's *The Lance and the Shield* (1993, Henry Holt) and Joseph Manzione's *"I Am Looking to the North for My Life": Sitting Bull, 1876-1881* (1991, University of Utah Press) are the standard scholarly biographical and diplomatic-archival treatments respectively — Manzione's monograph, built specifically on U.S. and Canadian government correspondence, is the fullest account of the three-government standoff the exile produced. Ernie LaPointe's *Sitting Bull: His Life and Legacy* (2009, Gibbs Smith) is the family-authorized account, by Sitting Bull's great-grandson, and this corpus follows house method in privileging it wherever it and the settler-authored scholarship diverge. None of these five books has been read cover to cover for this note; claims drawn from them are marked **attested-secondary**, consistent with the citation-status discipline applied throughout this corpus.

## What stopped: the U.S. Cavalry, and only the U.S. Cavalry

The mechanism that stopped the Army was not physical, it was diplomatic, and it is documented in the diplomatic record rather than in any fence line. **Attested-secondary**, drawn from a Foreign Relations of the United States compilation (FRUS 1878, document 209) summarizing NWMP Commissioner James Macleod's own account: Macleod told Sitting Bull's people on their arrival that they would not be recognized as "British Indians" — i.e., granted status or land — but that they also "must not cross the line with hostile intent," a formula that names the exact shape of the protection on offer: refuge, conditioned on the border not being used as a raiding base back into U.S. territory. That condition is what made the boundary usable by the two states as a diplomatic instrument rather than a battlefield: as long as the Lakota did not raid south, the U.S. Army's crossing north to seize them would have been not a policing action but an act of war against a sovereign power, something no Army officer in this period was authorized to risk. This is why General Terry's October 17, 1877 commission at Fort Walsh could only *ask* Sitting Bull to come south voluntarily, and why, when he refused, the commission simply left — the same Army that had annihilated Custer's column sixteen months earlier had no lawful path across a boundary its own government fully recognized.

The clearest proof that the line's power belonged to the crossing, not to the country beyond it, is a case the corpus's glossary entry states briefly and this note can source more fully. Hiŋmatóowyalahtqit — Chief Joseph — led the Nez Perce on a roughly 1,170-mile fighting retreat toward Canada through the summer of 1877, aiming for exactly the sanctuary Sitting Bull already held. The U.S. Army under General Nelson Miles cornered the band at the Bear Paw Mountains in northern Montana on September 30, 1877; after a five-day siege, Joseph surrendered on October 5, 1877 — by most tellings roughly forty miles short of the boundary (attested-secondary; the specific distance is reported as "about 40 miles" or "42 miles" depending on source and is not independently surveyed here). Twelve days later, at Fort Walsh, Sitting Bull was telling Terry's commission that the United States had no claim on him at all. Same army, same summer, same pursuit logic, opposite outcomes — because one band crossed and one did not. The line conferred nothing on intention, exhaustion, or proximity. It conferred everything on the single fact of physical crossing.

## What never stopped: traders, wolfers, surveyors, and the buffalo brigades

Every other kind of crossing the boundary saw in this same decade moved across it with no comparable friction at all, because none of it triggered the interstate mechanism that stopped the Army. **Attested-secondary**, corroborated across Fort Whoop-Up institutional histories (Galt Museum; Canadian Encyclopedia): American traders out of Fort Benton, Montana, built the whiskey-trading post that became Fort Whoop-Up in 1869 well inside British territory, on Blackfoot (Niitsítapi) land, and ran an entirely unpoliced trade — whiskey, guns, and manufactured goods for buffalo robes — north of the line for five full trading seasons with no Canadian government presence to stop them. The trade's violence culminated in the Cypress Hills Massacre of June 1, 1873, when a mixed party of American wolf hunters, traders, and Métis cart men killed at least thirteen Assiniboine people in a dispute that began as a dispute over stolen horses (attested-secondary, Canadian Encyclopedia, Parks Canada). That massacre, not the abstract idea of an unpoliced border, is what finally produced enforcement: the North-West Mounted Police Act had already passed on May 23, 1873, ten days before the killings, but the massacre gave Ottawa the political urgency to actually deploy the force, which marched west from Fort Dufferin in July 1874 — 275 of an authorized 300 men, with 150 eventually garrisoning the newly built Fort Macleod (attested-secondary, multiple March West histories converging on these figures). The whiskey posts closed within that same season, not because the boundary suddenly became solid, but because a police force finally existed on one side of it to enforce Canadian law against the traders operating there — an enforcement of domestic law inside Canadian territory, not a closing of the border to American crossing as such.

The survey that fixed the line's exact position was itself a continuous cross-border operation. **Attested-secondary**: the joint North American Boundary Commission's British and American parties worked from a shared base camp at Fort Dufferin and wintered together at Willow Bunch in 1873-74, moving supplies, personnel, and Métis guides back and forth across the very meridian they were fixing, because no boundary existed yet to observe. The Commission hired Métis hunters and guides specifically because Red River Métis buffalo brigades already knew the country the survey needed to cross — brigades that had been running Red River carts from Manitoba through the Cypress Hills into Montana Territory as "hivernants," wintering wherever the herds required, for decades before 1872, with no boundary in practice to observe at all (attested-secondary, Manitoba Historical Society; see the companion "other nations" note for the fuller Métis history). The people who built the line's physical markers, in other words, crossed the ground the markers would eventually claim to fix more freely than anyone the line was later used to stop.

## Native nations crossing in both directions

This corpus's companion note on the other nations documents the comparative detail in full; the mechanical point worth isolating here is that the line's power ran in different directions for different Indigenous nations depending on the relationship each had to the two settler states, not on any uniform "Indigenous" relationship to the boundary. For the Lakota fleeing an army that had just destroyed their war leadership, the line was protective in exactly the way described above. For the Blackfoot Confederacy, whose territory the line bisected outright, the boundary was instead an obstacle imposed on an existing, continuous pattern of movement — buffalo hunting, ceremony, and kin visiting that had run north-south across the Cypress Hills-to-Yellowstone range for generations and that treaty-making (Lame Bull's Treaty, 1855, on the U.S. side; Treaty 7, September 22, 1877, on the Canadian side) subsequently cut into four separately governed nations rather than protected. For the Plains Cree under Mistahi-maskwa (Big Bear), crossing south into Montana from roughly 1876 to 1882 to hunt the last herds was a subsistence strategy the line did not obstruct in practice — but it also did not protect: American authorities treated Cree hunting parties as foreign incursions rather than refugees, and Big Bear's people found no equivalent of Walsh's welcome on the American side. The line, that is, was never a single object with one meaning for "Native nations" generically; it was a differently energized disposition for each nation depending on which state each was fleeing, which state each needed something from, and which state was prepared to recognize the crossing as anything other than a nuisance.

## Seasonal and enforcement realities

The Mounted Police force that eventually policed this boundary was minute relative to the ground it covered, and its capacity to act was sharply seasonal. The 1874 authorized strength was 300 men for a territory that would eventually be organized into the whole of the North-West Territories; of the 275 who marched west that summer, only 150 ended up garrisoning Fort Macleod, with smaller detachments at other posts including, from 1875, Fort Walsh in the Cypress Hills (attested-secondary, per the March West sources above). Set against the roughly 4,000-5,000 Lakota and allied people camped in the Wood Mountain area alone by mid-1877 (a figure the companion exile note treats at length, with its own caveats about which count covers which population), the ratio of police to policed population, let alone to the open prairie separating them, made continuous physical enforcement of the boundary itself impossible even where the NWMP wanted to attempt it. What enforcement existed was concentrated at a handful of posts — Fort Walsh, Wood Mountain, Fort Macleod — reachable only by travel that itself was seasonally constrained: winter blizzards on the open plains made extended patrol movement dangerous to impossible for weeks at a stretch, and the same seasonality that had always governed Plains life (buffalo herds moving north in spring and south in autumn, hivernant camps forming and breaking with the hunt) continued to determine when and where anyone, settler or Indigenous, was actually near the line at all. The boundary's "enforcement," in short, was not a continuous physical presence but an intermittent, weather-and-herd-dependent institutional fact, real at Fort Walsh in October when Terry's commission needed it to be real, and essentially notional across the hundreds of miles of prairie where no post existed and no season made travel likely.

The seasonal logic cuts the other way as well, and this is the detail that actually ended the Lakota's four years of protection: the boundary's diplomatic solidity against the U.S. Army never extended to a guarantee of subsistence, and subsistence was itself seasonal and collapsing. The northern buffalo herds that had still been reaching Canadian territory in 1876-77 largely failed to appear by the autumn of 1878 (attested-secondary, consistent across the sources gathered for the companion exile note), and Canadian policy supplied no ration in their place — a choice, not an incapacity, since Canada was provisioning treaty nations elsewhere in the same territory under its numbered-treaty obligations at the same time. The medicine line kept working exactly as advertised through every one of the Lakota's four Canadian winters: no American soldier ever crossed it to take Sitting Bull. What ended the exile was not the line failing, but the line's protection turning out to cover exactly one thing — freedom from military pursuit — and nothing else, while the seasonal collapse of the buffalo removed the only other thing that mattered.

## Connection to the corpus

The medicine line is this corpus's cleanest demonstration that an exclusion-power's strength has nothing to do with physical infrastructure and everything to do with which apparatus stands behind it and against whom that apparatus is willing to act. A line with twenty-eight survey stations across four hundred miles stopped an army that had just won a war, absolutely and for four years, while the same line did nothing whatsoever to the traders who built a five-year illegal liquor economy across it, the wolfers whose 1873 massacre is what actually got it policed, the surveyors who crossed it at will to build it, or the Métis brigades who had crossed it for a generation before it existed. That is [property-as-disposition](../../../../../../glossary/terms/property-as-disposition.md) with the physical alibi stripped away: there is no fence here to mistake for the source of the power, only the bare fact that the U.S. Army's restraint was a policy the American state chose to honor against another state, and chose not to extend to a policy of feeding the people that restraint protected. And it is [settlerism-as-filing-system](../../../../../../glossary/terms/settlerism-as-filing-system.md) running, for once, in the direction this corpus's contemporary Gadsden-line chapters rarely find: for four years, the line was more permeable to people fleeing a settler army than to the army itself, an inversion of the asymmetric permeability this corpus documents at the twenty-first-century wall, where capital, cattle, and strikebreakers cross freely southbound while people crossing northbound meet a $46.5 billion apparatus built for exactly the opposite asymmetry. The medicine line did not disprove that later pattern; it is its exception, and the exception is precisely dated — it lasted only as long as neither settler state's filing system had a category, starvation included, that could reach across it. Once Canada's refusal of a reserve met the buffalo's collapse, the same border that no cavalry could cross closed on Sitting Bull's people anyway, from the inside, without a single soldier setting foot on Canadian soil.
