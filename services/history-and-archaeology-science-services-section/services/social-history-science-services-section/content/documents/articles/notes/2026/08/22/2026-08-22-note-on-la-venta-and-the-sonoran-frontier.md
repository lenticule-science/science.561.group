# La venta and the Sonoran frontier

Research pass 2026-08-22, Sonoran round, stream one. Verification statuses preserved from the research: primary-verified / attested-secondary / unpinned.

## La venta de La Mesilla

Mexican sources almost uniformly say ***venta*** — sale — not purchase. The framing carries the historiography: it centers Mexican agency and culpability (Santa Anna disposing of sovereign territory to fund "Su Alteza Serenísima's" court) where the American "Gadsden Purchase" centers acquisition. The corpus's Gadsden entries should note both names and what each does. The domestic reaction — a second humiliation stacked on 1848, sharpened by Santa Anna spending the proceeds on extravagance rather than the promised army — feeds directly into the Plan de Ayutla as Mexican accounts tell it (CNDH commemorative material names the sale among the Plan's grievances; attested-secondary). Even at the time opinion wasn't monolithic: the pro-Santa Anna *El Universal* defended the sale in April 1854 as rational compensation for "tierras baldías." A common Mexican popular misconception conflates the Mesilla sale with the far larger 1848 loss — a documented historiographical slippage worth preserving. The Mexican boundary commission's own account (SciELO academic source): the Sonora-section survey was headed from October 1854 by **Francisco Jiménez** — the same commissioner whose objection at Monument 127 Emory dismissed. Gap flagged: no canonical modern Mexican monograph on the 1853 treaty was located this pass; Josefina Zoraida Vázquez and Ana Rosa Suárez Argüello are the follow-up targets (unpinned).

## Sonora, 1850s–70s

Post-war Sonora was a depopulating, violence-wracked frontier run by rival elite networks — Gándara vs. Pesqueira — whose civil wars entangled with the Reforma and the French Intervention; **Ignacio Pesqueira** dominated from 1857 to 1876. Recent Mexican scholarship (Padilla Ramos and colleagues, SciELO-published) explicitly corrects the older "historia de bronce" liberal-triumph narrative: the factions were pragmatic networks, and **Yaqui, Mayo, and Ópata leaders used the rivalry instrumentally for their own ends** rather than being its objects (attested-secondary, academic). The new boundary was experienced locally as a fracture that *facilitated* raiding — pursuit halted at the line while raiders crossed — a Mexican-side mirror of the Medicine Line's selective permeability, this time with Mexico on the stopped side.

## Caborca

The Crabb filibuster died at **Caborca, April 1–8, 1857**: ~200 initial defenders growing to ~1,100, Crabb and nearly all his men executed, Crabb decapitated (attested-secondary; force figures Wikipedia-grade, treat as approximate). Two facts the corpus must hold prominently: **the defenders included Papago (O'odham) auxiliaries**, and the commemorative legend centers **Francisco Xavier, a Pápago fighter whose fire arrows destroyed the filibusters' powder store** — the same people the line splits and the wall now crosses fought as Sonora's defenders against American invasion, and Sonoran state memory celebrates an O'odham man as the hero of the gesta. Caborca received its "Heroica" title only in **April 1948** (attested-secondary; gazette not read) — a 20th-century commemorative act, structurally parallel to the 1939 de Niza monument at Lochiel: both sides of the line built their border-origin memory in the same interwar-to-postwar commemorative wave, one canonizing a doubted friar, the other canonizing successful anti-filibuster defense. The annual April 6 commemoration continues; even within Sonora the heroic framing has documented dissenters.

## The scalp-bounty regimes

The darkest thread, and squarely on-thesis. **Sonora**: bounties from September 1835 (100 pesos per Apache man's scalp, age 14+), raised 1850 (150/dead man, 100/captured woman), raised again **under Pesqueira in 1870 to 300 pesos** because prior rates were "no bastante estímulo" (attested-secondary from a peer-reviewed SciELO article — the load-bearing citation: "Las gratificaciones por cabelleras," 2016). **Chihuahua**: the 1849 "Ley de Cabelleras" — passed over **Governor Ángel Trías's veto**, who called it "cruel e inhumana," by legislative override (the policy was contested among Chihuahua's own elite, and lost); 100 pesos/man, 50/woman, later 200 and 250 for a live captive; 17,896 pesos paid out in 1849 alone. The contractors were often American mercenaries — James "Santiago" Kirker, James Johnson, John Joel Glanton — and the documented massacres (Sierra de las Ánimas 1837; **Galeana, July 7, 1846**, ~130–150 peaceful Chiricahua killed at a staged feast, scalps paraded through Chihuahua City) fell on peaceful, treaty-seeking, and non-Apache people, because **scalps are unverifiable by ethnicity** — the bounty regime is the corpus's most literal case of the filing system paying out on category-membership with no verification instrument at all, and killing whoever could be filed. The Sonoran policy failed even on its own terms (9 scalps collected in 1870 against 105 Sonoran deaths from raids that year) and persisted anyway. Mexican academic historiography calls it "la vil industria de vender cabelleras"; contemporary Mexican public discourse increasingly says **genocide** (cite as discourse evidence, not fact-source). And the date that stops the breath: **Chihuahua's law was formally repealed in September 2023** — 174 years on the books, removed after Apache-descendant advocacy (attested-secondary; the state congress repeal documents are the primary-source lead).

## Cananea, from the side where it matters most

In Mexican memory Cananea is not an incident at Greene's property; it is a **named precursor of the Revolution**, grouped with Río Blanco and the PLM's founding, its dead commemorated as the **Mártires de Cananea** — annual union commemorations at the Mausoleo a los Mártires de 1906 at Cananea and at Mexico City's Monumento al Obrero, INAH museum treatment, the works. The armed American crossing is remembered with the polarity our corpus predicts: Governor **Izábal's consent to the crossing is treated as a scandalous breach of national sovereignty** that damaged his historical reputation (interpretive framing unpinned to a specific Mexican-authored quote; bare facts attested). Academic corrective flagged: Cárdenas García's 1998 "una reinterpretación" (*Estudios Sociológicos* 16:46) contests the simple precursor narrative — **its actual thesis is unread (403-blocked) and must not be characterized beyond that until fetched**. Casualty figures (23 vs ~33) remain a genuine source-spread; state as range.

## What this stream does to the corpus

1. Every Gadsden-side entry gains its Mexican name and frame: la venta, not just the Purchase.
2. Caborca gives the O'odham corpus a dimension the U.S.-side sources never surfaced: O'odham as celebrated *defenders of Sonora* against American invasion, four years after the line was drawn through them.
3. The scalp-bounty regime belongs beside the Code of Indian Offenses and Prevention Through Deterrence in the corpus's set of category-violence instruments — bounty-per-scalp, rations-withheld-per-ceremony, desert-per-crossing — with the 2023 repeal as the reminder that these instruments outlive their centuries.
4. The commemorative symmetry (Heroica 1948 / de Niza monument 1939) extends the myth-infrastructure thread binationally.
