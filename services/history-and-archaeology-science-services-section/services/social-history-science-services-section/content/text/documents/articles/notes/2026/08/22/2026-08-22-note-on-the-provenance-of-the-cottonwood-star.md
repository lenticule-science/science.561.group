# The Provenance of the Cottonwood Star

Compiled 2026-08-22 as a companion to the [note on Yoeme riparian lifeway](./2026-08-22-note-on-yoeme-riparian-lifeway.md), which did for Lochiel's cottonwoods what this note does for a claim about the Lakota and theirs: separate what is long-published and specifically attributed from what circulates unattributed. The claim under examination — repeated across nature-education, tourism, and museum-adjacent writing — is that the cottonwood, cut crosswise, reveals a five-pointed star, and that this star is, to the Lakota, the morning star, one reason the cottonwood is the Sun Dance tree. The botanical fact of the pith star is real and treated in the [botany companion note](../../../../../../../../../../../natural-science-services-section/services/biological-science-services-section/services/botany-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-the-cottonwood-pith-star-and-the-willow-negative.md). The attribution is what this note traces.

## What the named sources actually say

The three canonical attributed ethnographic sources on the Sun Dance cottonwood give three different reasons — none of them the star.

**Densmore, *Teton Sioux Music* (Bureau of American Ethnology Bulletin 61, 1918), verified against the archive.org full text:** "It was stated that cottonwood was preferred for the sacred pole and for all the articles of wood used in the Sun dance, because the white down of the cottonwood seed resembles the downy eagle feathers used in the ceremony. If a cottonwood could not be obtained, elm was selected, because the elm is the first tree to blossom in the spring." Densmore's named consultants on cottonwood ritual objects — Matȟó-kuwá (Chased-by-Bears), on the offering stick as the arrow that killed the buffalo and the picket stake of a captured horse (p. 118), and Weasel Bear, on cottonwood for the spirit post in the ghost-keeping ceremony (p. 82) — say nothing of a star.

**Walker, *The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies of the Oglala Division of the Teton Dakota* (1917), verified via the sacred-texts mirror:** the chapters on scouting, marking, capturing, and raising the Sacred Tree are prescriptive ritual protocol — girth, fork height, red paint, four-color painting of the pole — with no stated symbolic rationale for the species at all.

**Black Elk, in Brown's *The Sacred Pipe* (1953):** "Perhaps you have noticed that even in the slightest breeze you can hear the voice of the cottonwood tree; this we understand is its prayer to the Great Spirit, for not only men, but all things and all beings pray to Him continually in different ways." (Verified against Wikiquote's sourced entry, not against a scanned page; the archive.org copies were lending-restricted during compilation.) The reason is the wind-voice — the same register as the Yoeme Talking Tree material in the companion note — not the star.

**Goodman, *Lakȟóta Star Knowledge: Studies in Lakȟóta Stellar Theology* (ed. Seeger, Sinte Gleska University Press, 1992)** is the source most often gestured at online for the cottonwood star. The book itself was not obtainable during compilation. The most substantial accessible scholarly engagement with it — Suzanne Kite (Oglala Lakota), "What's on the earth is in the stars; and what's in the stars is on the earth" (*American Indian Culture and Research Journal* 45:1, 2021), quoting Goodman at pp. 18, 19, 21, 27, 31, 32, 33, 35 — centers the star-map/earth-map mirroring, the Kapemni twisting-vortex figure, and the tipi as microcosm, with named voices Stanley Looking Horse and Mr. Running. The word "cottonwood" does not occur anywhere in Kite's article. Absence from one seventeen-page engagement is not absence from the book — this stays open until someone reads Goodman page by page — but the most star-focused Lakota-institutional source cannot presently be shown to carry the claim.

## The likely locus: an unattributed sentence in 1970

The earliest published instance located is Jeffrey Zelitch, "The Lakota Sun Dance," *Expedition Magazine* (University of Pennsylvania Museum) 13:1, 1970 — an eyewitness account of the 1969 Sun Dance at Spring Creek, Rosebud Reservation. The structure of the passage matters. A direct quotation from the officiating medicine man, Pete Catches (Petaga Wakan), ends; then, with no quotation marks and no citation, the article continues:

> The cottonwood has special meaning for the Lakota: its bark is the only kind which could be fed to horses; when cut diagonally the wood shows a five-pointed star, the morning star of light and wisdom; the leaf shows the pattern of a moccasin and also, when folded, of a tipi. As waga can, "rustling tree," it prays, singing in the wind, to Wakan-Tanka.

— after which the text resumes with another "(Petaga Wakan)"-attributed quotation. The star, moccasin, tipi, and horse-bark sentence is the non-Native academic author's own expository prose sandwiched between attributed statements, and it carries no footnote (the article footnotes Densmore elsewhere, for prayer and song texts — not here). Verification confirmed this structure against the archived article text directly. Whatever Zelitch's unrecorded sources were, the sentence as published is unattributed — and it is the sentence the later popular literature repeats.

Two further displacements are on record. The star-birth story — stars born in the earth, drawn by the sound of water into cottonwood roots, traveling up the tree, released into the sky when wind snaps a twig — circulates (e.g., UW Botanic Gardens' Miller Library page, "I learned about the star shape inside cottonwood twigs from a Lakota story") with no named Lakota teller; that same page's actual citations are a story by Deb Mowry of the Montana Natural History Center and Robert Michael Pyle's 1993 *American Horticulturist* essay, which tells the motif as **Arapaho**. A City of Fort Collins Natural Areas education document is titled "Cottonwood Trees and the Stars: From the Plains Indians: Cheyenne and Arapaho" (title verified; body text could not be extracted). The pattern is consistent with a Cheyenne/Arapaho-documented motif drifting under the more marketable label "Lakota" in nature writing — stated here as a pattern in the located sources, not a proven genealogy. Meanwhile the Akta Lakota Museum's own Sun Dance page, which cites Brown and Mails, mentions the cottonwood only as the tree the holy men select — no star.

Even the word is unsettled. The widely-circulated "wáǧačhaŋ" (glossed "rustling tree" by Zelitch, "peel-off wood" elsewhere) traces online to a Dakota-territory plant blog and may be Dakota rather than Lakota; the credentialed ethnobotanical listing — Linda S. Black Elk with named cultural consultant Wilbur D. Flying By, Sr., *Culturally Important Plants of the Lakota* (Sitting Bull College, 1998; entry #239 in the version filed as expert testimony, SD PUC Docket HP14-001), read directly — gives čhaŋyáȟ'u, šaká čháŋ, and waȟčhíŋča for *Populus deltoides*, and does not contain "wáǧačhaŋ" at all (transliterations carry OCR uncertainty; Buechel's dictionary was not checked and is the natural referee).

## Four and seven, not five

Documented Lakota sacred numerology runs on four and seven. The confederacy's self-name, Očhéthi Šakówiŋ, is built on šakówiŋ, seven (the numeral five, záptaŋ, carries no comparable weight in any located source). The star quilt tradition — the morning star rendered in fabric — is an **eight**-pointed star: "The eight points represent the four stages of life — infancy, youth, maturity, and old age — plus the four cardinal directions" (International Quilt Museum, verified verbatim). Walker's *Lakota Belief and Ritual* is reported to contain a section titled "The Numbers Four and Seven," though the table of contents could not be directly verified (print-disabled access only). In Brown's *The Sacred Pipe* the ritual counting runs to "the six directions" as a set phrase — four winds plus sky and earth — with the center introduced afterward as a further, unenumerated point; popular retellings do count "the fifth direction is above," so an ordinal five occurs in passing, but no located source gives five a resting significance the way four and seven have one. And in every located source that mentions the pith star, the "five" is purely descriptive — it names the point-count so the shape can be matched to the morning star — never a number woven into the four/seven ritual system. This is an absence-based finding across the sources we could access, which is weaker than a positive statement of exclusion, and is flagged as such.

## Willow, briefly

Two willow items belong to this note's attribution ledger rather than the botany note's. The inípi (sweat lodge) frame is documented as bent willow — commonly sixteen withes forming a roughly five-foot-high dome eight to ten feet across (Talamantez's encyclopedia treatment, citing Powers's *Oglala Religion*, Bucko's *The Lakota Ritual of the Sweat Lodge*, and Walker; Bucko's monograph itself was not directly accessible, and construction details vary across communities). And čhaŋšáša, "red willow," the ceremonial smoking bark: botanically *Cornus sericea* (red osier dogwood), not a *Salix* at all — stated plainly by a Lakota/Dakota-territory ethnobotanical practitioner and corroborated by every other located source; the English "willow" is a settler misnomer (one retail source offers the folk-etymology that French *osier* was mistaken for willow in winter; unverified). A claim about "willow" in Lakota ceremony can thus be about a dogwood — one more reason attribution work has to run all the way down to the plant.

## What this note does and does not establish

It establishes: that the three canonical attributed sources give eagle-down, ritual protocol, and wind-voice — not the star; that the star sentence's earliest located published form is unattributed non-Native exposition (Zelitch 1970); that the star-birth story's only tribally-specified published telling located is Arapaho (Pyle 1993); that documented Lakota number symbolism centers four and seven; and that the Lakota name for the cottonwood is itself contested in the sources. It does **not** establish that no Lakota person or community holds the cottonwood star as the morning star — an oral tradition can be real and simply unrecorded in the literature we can reach, and Zelitch stood next to Pete Catches at Spring Creek and may have been repeating what he was told. Deciding what the tree means belongs to Lakota people — Victor Douville (Sinte Gleska University, who worked with Goodman gathering elder testimony) is the obvious living scholarly lead, and Goodman's book, Buechel's dictionary, Mails, and Bucko in full text are the obvious document leads. Until then, the honest citation for "the cottonwood shows the morning star" is: *Zelitch 1970, unattributed; widely repeated since.*
