# The Zanja title and the piecemeal strip

Research pass 2026-08-22. This stream set out to test whether the confirmed grant reached the international line. Answer: probably not — and the mechanism the question was probing turns out to be documented, general, and already proven in the field once.

## The grant, precisely

- Origin corrected and detailed: Manuel Bustillo applied for the 4 sitios in 1821; survey and appraisal ($60/sitio with permanent water, $30 dry — $210 assessed); **Ramón Romero** bought at auction for 1,200 pesos (Jan 1822) on behalf of Santa Cruz presidio residents; Mexican title issued **May 15, 1825**.
- The U.S. adjudication ran through the rival claimant: **Alfred A. Green** petitioned the CPLC in 1893 for the enlarged reading (~152,890–160,000 acres, "four leagues square"). The Supreme Court's affirmance of the four-sitio confirmation is ***United States v. Green*, 185 U.S. 256 (decided April 28, 1902)** — the four sitios "measured in a square from the center established by the Mexican surveyor," ~200 cordels in each direction from "the place called San Rafael de la Zanja." (**Date correction**: prior notes, following Hadley & Sheridan, gave the Supreme Court ruling as 1900 and the CPLC decree as Jan 1899. The reported decision is 1902; the 1899 decree date is plausible but unconfirmed — resolve at the NARA CPLC case file before external citation.)
- **Boundary finding**: the confirmed grant "lies in the valley center **just north of Lochiel**"; the modern State Natural Area / easement land lies "**south of the land grant, and borders Mexico**." So the four-sitio square appears to stop short of the line, with Lochiel and the border strip on land outside it. (Secondary-source finding — the official plat, offline, settles it.)

## The treaty's property promise, precisely

The Gadsden Treaty protects property **by incorporation**: its **Article V** applies Articles VIII, IX, XVI, and XVII of Guadalupe Hidalgo to the ceded territory "as fully and as effectually as if the said articles were herein again recited." The "**inviolably respected**" language is Guadalupe Hidalgo **Article VIII**. Implementation history: Congress created no forum for these claims until the **1891 CPLC Act** — 37 years of unconfirmed title — and the courts then construed the promise to the strict Mexican-law minimum. *Green* is itself the worked example: "inviolably respected" cashed out as one-ninth of the claim.

## The strip is piecemeal by its own terms — and has lost before

- **Proclamation 758's own text** reserves only "all *public* lands" and expressly excepts "all lands which are at this date embraced in any legal entry or covered by any lawful filing, selection or rights of way duly of record." The reservation is documented as running "in piecemeal fashion, interspersed by private and state land" (Cochise Regional News, on this very segment); nationwide, federal/tribal land covers only about a third of the border's length.
- **The mechanism has already produced a hole in the fence**: multiple independent secondary syntheses reproduce, near-verbatim, an account of a **New Mexico landowner whose ownership predated the Roosevelt easement** — the 60-foot right-of-way did not extend across his property, fencing was delayed, and a **1.2-mile gap existed** until CBP acquired the land through **negotiated settlement**. Verification pass 2026-08-22: the passage is *not* in GAO-15-399 (full text checked — that report never mentions Roosevelt; the research agent mis-cited it) nor in GAO-09-896 (checked). The anecdote is consistently reported (Cato; Alta, "Who Owns the Border?"; Albuquerque Journal 2019) but its primary source is **not yet pinned** — candidates: GAO-17-331, CRS border-barrier reports. Treat as well-attested secondary, pending the primary, before any external transmission. Note the ending matters: the gap closed by *negotiated purchase* — i.e., pre-1907 title forced the government to buy, not to bulldoze.

## Who owns the ground at the line today

- The 1999 transaction split the ranch: **3,557 acres in fee to Arizona State Parks** (the headquarters/Natural Area) and a **17,574-acre conservation easement** — under which the land **remains privately owned**, State Parks holding only the easement, the ranch still operating. It is this easement/Natural Area complex, not the old grant footprint, that **borders Mexico**.
- **Lochiel townsite is privately owned** — customs house, church, cemetery reported as belonging to "a family that has called the town home for generations" — while Border Road itself runs atop the Roosevelt strip as a public road.
- **The clearing exceeds the strip by DHS's own account**: reporting has DHS telling the Sierra Club's borderlands coordinator the project builds beyond the 60 feet — a **150-foot swath** reaching into **Coronado National Forest** — with IIRIRA/Secure Fence authority invoked for the excess.
- **No condemnation filing found** (D. Ariz., Rule 71.1) naming the easement landowner, TNC, State Parks, or Lochiel-area owners for this segment — web search is not authoritative here; **PACER is the decisive check**. If the 150-foot swath crosses easement or private land and no acquisition paperwork exists, the posture is the 2022 container-wall posture with the roles recast: a builder on someone else's land.

## What this does to the scope memo

The memo's "existence question" narrows and sharpens into three concrete questions: (1) parcel-by-parcel, whose land does the 150-foot swath cross between the Patagonia Mountains and Coronado NM — private-under-easement, state fee, townsite-private, or federal? (2) does any acquisition/condemnation paperwork exist for the non-federal crossings? (3) does the conservation easement's recorded text (Santa Cruz County Recorder) bar or constrain such clearing, giving State Parks or TNC an enforcement right? The strip's piecemeal character and the GAO precedent supply the legal frame; the county GIS, the recorded easement, and PACER supply the answers.
