# ILO 169 and the amparo mechanism

Research pass 2026-08-22, Sonoran round, legal-frame stream (companion to
`note-on-the-mexican-indigenous-and-legal-frame.md`, which this note does not
repeat). Statuses: **primary-verified** (direct read of the primary
instrument/ruling text) / **attested-secondary** (direct read of a reputable
secondary source, or concordant multi-source search synthesis) / **unpinned**
(single source or unresolved discrepancy — flagged, not asserted). This is a
factual/legal-landscape record. It identifies what legal tools and precedents
exist; it does not recommend that any principal use them, and no strategy for
the Consejo Supremo de Ancianos or any other Indigenous body is authored here.

## Mexico's ratification of ILO Convention 169

**Attested-secondary, concordant across sources**, with one internal
discrepancy flagged: the Mexican Senate approved Convention 169 on **July 11,
1990**; the approval decree was published in the *Diario Oficial* on **August
3, 1990**; the instrument of ratification was deposited with the ILO on
**September 5, 1990**; and — because ILO conventions enter into force twelve
months after registration of ratification — the Convention entered into force
for Mexico on **September 5, 1991** (the DOF publication of the Convention
text itself is separately dated January 24, 1991). Mexico is repeatedly
reported as **the second country in the world to ratify, after Norway, and
the first in the Americas** (concordant: UNAM's *Nación Multicultural*
"100 preguntas" project, direct-fetched; CNDH's own C169 pamphlet, title
confirmed via search but PDF not machine-readable this pass — flag; an INEA
social-media post giving the same September 5, 1990 deposit date). The exact
day of deposit (Sept 5, 1990) versus a competing single-source claim of
ratification "August 13, 1990" was not fully reconciled — **unpinned** on that
one sub-date only; the Senate-approval date (July 11, 1990) and the
entry-into-force date (Sept 5, 1991) are solid across sources.

## The constitutional and jurisprudential frame

- **Article 2, Apartado B, fracción IX of the Constitution** (added by the
  2001 indigenous-rights reform) obligates federal, state, and municipal
  authorities to consult indigenous peoples in the elaboration of national and
  entity development plans and to incorporate their proposals where
  applicable — **attested-secondary**. Scholarship and the SCJN itself
  describe this constitutional text as **narrower than ILO 169** — the 2001
  reform was the domestic settlement that followed the San Andrés Accords'
  non-implementation, and its consultation clause is procedurally thinner than
  Convention 169's Articles 6, 7, and 15 (attested-secondary, SciELO synthesis
  below).
- **ILO 169 as directly applicable / self-executing**, independent of any
  secondary Mexican statute: the SCJN has treated Convention 169's
  consultation obligation as binding on its own terms, applying a
  **"significant impact" standard** — consultation is mandatory whenever a
  legislative, administrative, or authorization act may substantially affect
  indigenous peoples' rights or territory — **attested-secondary**, drawn from
  a SciELO synthesis article ("El derecho a la consulta de los pueblos
  indígenas: una propuesta de sistematización para el caso mexicano",
  2021) that reviews the case line directly. This is the doctrinal reason
  Indigenous communities have repeatedly won amparo protection over
  consultation **before** Congress ever passed implementing legislation.
- **Inter-American standards incorporated by reference**: Mexican courts,
  starting with the Yaqui ruling below, cite the Inter-American Court's
  *Sarayaku v. Ecuador* and *Saramaka v. Suriname* consultation standards
  alongside ILO 169 — attested-secondary (search synthesis, UNDP and SCJN
  *Centro de Estudios Constitucionales* pages).

## The amparo mechanism, generally

Amparo is the constitutional-protection lawsuit under Articles 103/107 of the
Constitution and the Ley de Amparo, available against **norms, acts, or
omissions of authority** that violate human rights recognized by the
Constitution or by treaties to which Mexico is party (Ley de Amparo, Art. 1 —
**primary-verified**, statute text). Relevant structure:

- **Indirect amparo** (*amparo indirecto*) is filed before a federal
  district judge and is the vehicle used against administrative
  authorizations, legislative omissions, and ongoing conduct; **direct
  amparo** runs against final judicial/administrative resolutions. Ley de
  Amparo Art. 108, fracción IV expressly contemplates "la norma general, acto
  **u omisión** que de cada autoridad se reclame" as a formal element of the
  indirect-amparo complaint — omission is a directly reclaimable category, not
  a workaround — **primary-verified**, statute text.
- **Suspension** (provisional and definitive) is the interim remedy: a judge
  can halt the challenged act — construction, an authorization's effects —
  while the underlying amparo is litigated. This is the mechanism that has
  produced actual construction pauses in the precedents below, independent of
  the final merits ruling.
- **Standing**: Ley de Amparo Art. 1 covers anyone asserting a subjective
  right or an individual or **collective** legitimate interest suffering real
  and current harm — the collective-interest category is the doorway
  Indigenous communities (and, doctrinally, their traditional governing
  bodies) use as *quejosos*.

## Amparo por omisión, specifically

"Amparo por omisión" is not a separate statute but the application of the
ordinary indirect-amparo vehicle to an authority's **failure to act** where
action was legally required. Two distinct patterns appear in the record:

1. **Omission by an administrative authority to consult** before authorizing
   a project — this is what produced the Yaqui and Juchitán rulings below:
   the omission was SEMARNAT's/the consultation-holder's failure to conduct
   (or to properly conduct) prior consultation before granting environmental
   or project authorization.
2. **Legislative omission** — Congress's failure to enact a law it is
   constitutionally obligated to pass. The SCJN's Segunda Sala, in **Amparo
   en Revisión 1144/2019**, found exactly this: a complete legislative
   omission in never having issued the General Law on Prior, Free, Informed,
   Culturally Appropriate, and Good-Faith Consultation. Chronology
   (attested-secondary, search synthesis of the case docket and concordant
   commentary — the primary engrose PDF was located at
   `www2.scjn.gob.mx/juridica/engroses/2/2019/2/2_267374_4719_firmado.pdf`
   but returned as a non-machine-readable binary stream this pass, so the
   date below is not primary-verified): indirect-amparo demand filed **May
   29, 2018**; case admitted for review by the SCJN president **January 28,
   2020**; Segunda Sala resolution dated **June 10, 2020** (some secondary
   summaries round this to "2019" from the docket number, which reflects the
   filing year, not the decision year — flagged, resolve at the primary PDF
   before external citation). Holding: the omission itself violates the
   petitioners' rights, and Congress was ordered to legislate, with the
   Court warning of escalating enforcement (fines, referral for
   non-compliance) if it did not — **as of a June 2026 status check, the
   general law had still not been passed**, five-plus years after the
   ruling (attested-secondary, ECIJA legal-alert synthesis and
   Litigio Estratégico Indígena's public statement).

On procedural mechanics for an omission-based filing specifically — whether
the 15-day general filing deadline (Ley de Amparo Art. 17) is tolled or
renewed for a continuing omission — this pass did not pin a specific tesis;
the doctrinal literature (titles located: "Amparo por omisión," *Inventio*
UAEM; "El amparo ante la omisión legislativa a partir de la reforma en
materia de derechos humanos," Zenodo) treats continuing omissions as
distinct from single completed acts for limitations purposes, but the
specific controlling tesis was not directly read this pass — **unpinned**,
flagged for a follow-up pull before any filing-deadline claim is relied on.

## Documented precedents: infrastructure without consultation

**Yaqui / Acueducto Independencia — the lead Mexican case.** *Amparo
631/2012*, decided by the SCJN **May 8, 2013** (clarification issued August
8, 2013) — **attested-secondary, direct read** of ESCR-Net's case-law
summary, which quotes the ruling directly. Holding: the environmental
authorization for the aqueduct was declared ineffective pending proper prior
consultation with the Yaqui pueblo "according to their habits and customs,"
citing ILO 169 and the Inter-American standards (*Sarayaku*/*Saramaka*) —
the first time the SCJN grounded a domestic ruling in those Inter-American
consultation standards. The ruling ordered suspension of construction/
operation during consultation. **Implementation record, already carried in
the sibling note**: the consultation was never completed and the aqueduct
continued operating "irregularly" as of April 2026 — apology/order as
filing, extraction as practice, the same pattern this corpus has already
recorded for the Plan de Justicia Yaqui.

**Eólica del Sur, Juchitán, Oaxaca.** 1,166 members of the Binnizá
(Zapotec) community obtained a **definitive suspension** from the Juzgado
Séptimo de Distrito de Oaxaca in **September/December 2015**, halting
construction of what would become Latin America's largest wind park, on
grounds that the 2014–2015 consultation was procedurally defective (paid
attendees, coerced show-of-hands assembly) — attested-secondary, multiple
concordant Mexican outlets (Sin Embargo, Expansión, CDH Tepeyac). The SCJN
later rejected the specific amparos challenging the consultation's validity
and the park was ultimately built (132 turbines) despite the documented
procedural complaints — the case is therefore evidence both that the
suspension remedy works as a **delay** mechanism (construction was halted
for a period) and that it does not guarantee a final win on the merits.

**Maya Train (Tren Maya).** A pattern of repeated amparo suspensions across
multiple tramos, attested-secondary across concordant Mexican press
(La Jornada, Milenio, Reporte Índigo, Forbes México, CEMDA, CECCAM):
an indirect-amparo suit filed **January 6, 2020** by Ernesto Martínez
Jiménez (CEIPX, Campeche) obtained a provisional suspension of construction
(though not of the consultation process itself); the collegiate tribunal of
the 31st circuit ratified it; a further suspension was granted **March 4,
2020** to Maya Peninsular and Ch'ol communities; and the Tribunal Colegiado
del Decimocuarto Circuito (Mérida) maintained a **definitive suspension of
Tramo 7** as of 2024, in *amparo directo 1519/2021*, filed by Xpujil/
Calakmul Indigenous petitioners over a consultation they characterized as
simulated — designed and run unilaterally, without genuine prior or
detailed information. Multiple tramos, multiple years of litigation: the
Maya Train record is the clearest example in Mexican practice of amparo
suspension being used **repeatedly, over years, against a single
federal megaproject**, specifically over consultation adequacy.

**Monsanto transgenic soy, Campeche/Yucatán (Maya beekeepers).** SCJN
review numbers 198/2015, 499/2015, 500/2015 — attested-secondary (SciELO
synthesis, not independently cross-verified this pass) — establish the same
consultation-before-authorization principle outside the wall/energy/rail
infrastructure category, applied to a federal planting permit affecting Maya
beekeeping communities. Flagged as the weakest-sourced of the precedents
listed (single synthesis source, not corroborated against a second outlet
this pass).

**The general pattern, stated once:** every precedent above follows the same
shape — an authority (SEMARNAT, SENER/CFE, SCT/Fonatur, SAGARPA) authorizes
or advances a project; the consultation either did not happen or is alleged
to have been defective; an Indigenous community or its members file indirect
amparo naming that omission or defect; a district judge grants provisional
or definitive suspension, which pauses construction while the case
proceeds; and the final merits outcome is decoupled from the suspension —
sometimes the community wins outright (Yaqui), sometimes the suspension
delays for years before the project proceeds anyway (Juchitán), sometimes
litigation is still open after half a decade (Maya Train). **The suspension,
not the final ruling, is the mechanism that produces delay** — consistent
with this operation's stated objective.

## The Mexican-side O'odham angle: confirmed, and not yet applied to the wall

**Confirmed independently this pass**, cross-checking the prior note's
citation: IMER Noticias (direct-fetched this pass, "Despojo y falta de
reconocimiento: las violaciones del Plan Sonora al pueblo Tohono O'odham")
quotes **Matías Valenzuela**, spokesperson/translator for the **Consejo
Supremo de Ancianos del Pueblo Tohono O'odham**, stating that CFE and
SENER negotiated the Plan Sonora megasolar project's transmission lines —
which cross salt flats the Council holds as historically and ceremonially
significant — through a self-described "traditional governor" based in
Arizona, after the Council itself rejected the project at a third
negotiating meeting in Sonoyta/Caborca. This is the same bypass the sibling
note already recorded (**re-confirmed, not re-derived**), now against a
second, independently fetched primary-adjacent source (IMER Noticias),
raising it from single-source to attested-secondary with two concordant
outlets.

What this note adds: the **legal tools above exist independently of whether
anyone has invoked them for the wall**. As the sibling note already found,
**no O'odham-Sonora legal claim through Mexican channels — amparo or
otherwise — was located for the wall itself**; the documented Mexican-side
Indigenous response to the wall (the November 2025 "La Bota" protest) is
civil presence, not litigation. The Plan Sonora bypass establishes that the
Council has both standing-relevant grievance history and a documented
demand ("we don't want that") against a different federal infrastructure
project running through the same consultation failure pattern this note
catalogs — Yaqui, Juchitán, Maya Train. Whether that pattern has any
application to the wall, and whether the Council or its counsel wish to
pursue it, is exclusively theirs to determine; this note records only that
the pattern and the tools are documented and available for their counsel's
own evaluation.
