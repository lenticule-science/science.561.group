# The aperture: a theory of the door in the line

This note was proposed in chat on 2026-08-22, in the same session that produced the Gadsden-line and Medicine Line research documented in this corpus's companion notes — [the Gadsden line at Lochiel](./2026-08-22-note-on-lochiel-history.md), [two lines](./2026-08-22-note-on-two-lines.md), and [the medicine line's differential grip](./2026-08-22-note-on-the-medicine-line.md) — and the glossary terms [exclusion-powers](../../../../../../../glossary/terms/exclusion-powers.md), [property-as-disposition](../../../../../../../glossary/terms/property-as-disposition.md), and [settlerism-as-filing-system](../../../../../../../glossary/terms/settlerism-as-filing-system.md).
It was occasioned by a direct question put to the research session: is there any theory about what it would look like to open the Medicine Line as a door, put a hole in it, so grandmother can reach the southern rivers?
The sketch that followed was offered as a candidate glossary term (`aperture`, contrasting with `exclusion-powers`) and as a possible full theory essay, sitting beside `property-as-disposition.md` and `settlerism-as-filing-system.md` as a third, generative rather than diagnostic, leg of that cluster.
Neither was ever written; the conversation moved on to operationalizing Mexican-side and binational support work instead, and the offer was not revisited.
This note recovers the sketch in full from the session transcript and expands it into the corpus's standing prose register, so that the idea is not lost to the fact that its own author forgot to file it.

## 1. The starting point: the dual of an exclusion-power

The corpus's standing finding, attested twice and in opposite polarities in [two lines](./2026-08-22-note-on-two-lines.md), is that a border is a bundle of [exclusion-powers](../../../../../../../glossary/terms/exclusion-powers.md): dispositional, not geographic, switched on and off by whoever holds the apparatus, pointed at whoever that holder chooses.
If that diagnosis is right, then a dual object exists everywhere the border does.
For every power to exclude, there is a possible **door** — a place where the line is bound to let a specified relation through, not because the line has weakened, but because the same dispositional logic that lets a holder aim exclusion at one party can, in principle, be made to aim admission at another.

The theory's central claim is that this is not merely possible in principle.
It has already happened, more than once, and every instance the corpus has on record was made not by petition or permission but by a force the line's holders could not afford to stop.
Four such instances are already documented in this corpus, independently of one another and for independent reasons; this note's contribution is naming what they have in common.

## 2. Four doors already on the record

**Water holds a door.**
The San Rafael Valley wall's own construction plan leaves its floodgates open for the full monsoon season, a mitigation policy documented for the San Rafael project itself and discussed in this corpus's [walls-versus-water](./2026-08-22-note-on-walls-versus-water.md) note: "leave the gates open during monsoon season, when powerful flows could otherwise topple the wall."
The Santa Cruz River at Lochiel is gauged (USGS 09480000, record 1949–2023) to a flood of record of **12,000 cfs**, reached twice, on October 9, 1977 and August 15, 1984, with repeat events above 4,800 cfs as recently as August 21, 2022.
No statute grants the river a crossing right and none could revoke one; the wall's builders open a door for it because a debris-laden flood of that magnitude will open one regardless, and the apparatus that excludes people has no comparable power over water.

**Law holds a door, but only on the northern line.**
Article III of the 1794 Jay Treaty is a treaty-textual hole cut into the boundary that became the Medicine Line, codifying a right of Indigenous nations "dwelling on either side of the said boundary line, freely to pass and repass," as documented in this corpus's [Jay Treaty border-crossing right](../../../../../../../glossary/terms/jay-treaty-border-crossing-right.md) entry.
That the right survives today only in damaged form — abrogated in *Karnuth v. United States*, 279 U.S. 231 (1929), and restored on the U.S. side alone by 8 U.S.C. § 1359's 50-percent-blood-quantum arithmetic, with no Canadian reciprocal and no southern-line equivalent at all — does not undo what its existence proves.
A legally constructed, litigable door is possible; a state's own treaty-drafters have built one before, even if what they built was immediately compromised by the same settler grammar it was meant to except itself from.
Its total absence on the Gadsden line, as the [two lines](./2026-08-22-note-on-two-lines.md) note documents from the exclusion side, is therefore a design choice and not a natural limit on what a border can be made to carry.

**Capital holds doors everywhere, and always has.**
The [Cananea strike](../../../../../../../glossary/terms/cananea-strike.md) entry documents 275 armed American civilians crossing south into Sonora on June 1, 1906, under Arizona Ranger Captain Thomas Rynning, at a foreign governor's invitation and against a domestic governor's objection, to help suppress a labor action against an American-owned mine on Mexican soil.
No wall, waiver, or inspection regime has ever stood in the way of capital, its owners, or the armed men who protect it moving in whichever direction capital's interest points; the same asymmetry is legible in the 2026 present, where 34 federal laws were waived to build wall aimed at people moving north while nothing comparable has ever been required to move capital south.
This is not a door built by design so much as a door that was simply never built to be a wall in that direction to begin with — but it establishes, alongside water and law, that the line's permeability is a variable the corpus can already show being set differently for different parties.

**Kinship holds a door, and this is the finding the whole season's research turns on.**
This corpus's [Lochiel](../../../../../../../glossary/terms/lochiel.md) entry documents that on July 27–28, 2026, construction crews felled three of four old Fremont cottonwoods at Lochiel despite an earlier CBP promise not to fell them during migratory-bird nesting season; the next day, activists installed a platform sixty feet up the surviving tree, which organizers had already begun calling one of "the grandmothers" after a June solstice ceremony.
As of the most recently verified reporting (August 19, 2026), the sit continues into its third week, binational since mid-August, and — this is the load-bearing fact — there has been no attempt to fell the occupied tree.
A single tree, held by people in ceremony and in a platform, is stopping a $309.46-million, thirty-four-law-waived construction contract from finishing its work at that specific point on the line.
This is not metaphor.
It is the corpus's own most recent field observation, and it is a door standing open right now, made of nothing but bodies, commitment, and a tree neither side has yet been willing to cut down while people are in it.

## 3. The theory statement

**An aperture is a relation strong enough that the line's holders cannot afford to sever it, made durable by being welded to a force the line already accommodates.**

Two lessons follow from setting the four cases beside each other, and they point in different directions on the corpus's own two lines.
The northern lesson is that a kinship-aperture *can be given legal architecture*: the Jay Treaty is proof of constructibility, even though its surviving form is a damaged, U.S.-only statutory floor with no Canadian reciprocal and no equivalent anywhere on the southern line.
The southern lesson is that a kinship-aperture *can be materialized by ceremony and presence alone*, with no legal architecture whatsoever: the Grandmother tree-sit is doing exactly this, right now, with nothing but people willing to stay in a tree.

The synthesis question is the theory's actual payload, and this note does not answer it, because the session that produced the theory did not either: **what would it take to make the Grandmother's door permanent** — to open the Medicine Line, and its southern cousin, so that a specified relation crosses as a matter of standing right, the way Article III (however imperfectly) made Indigenous crossing a matter of treaty text, rather than as a matter of who currently happens to be sitting in the branches?

## 4. Why this leans on Lakota ontology rather than settler political theory — with a caveat this corpus owes itself

In English, cutting a hole in a border reads as subtraction: a barrier is removed, and what is left is an absence, a gap.
The session that produced this theory reached for a different grammar, drawn from the same corpus's treatment of the Sun Dance cottonwood: a tree becomes a sacred pole not by having something taken from it but by a community *entering into committed relation* with it, which the session's working shorthand rendered as a distinction between wáǧačhaŋ, the cottonwood as species, and čhaŋwákȟaŋ, the cottonwood raised as ceremonial center — an addition of relation, not a subtraction of tree.
Applied to the border, the same grammar reframes the aperture: the door is not a gap in the wall, but a relation the wall is compelled to hold, an addition of standing rather than a removal of barrier.

This corpus owes itself an honest caveat here, and the caveat sharpens rather than weakens the theory's own epistemic standards.
The session's shorthand treated the wáǧačhaŋ/čhaŋwákȟaŋ distinction as an established piece of the corpus.
It was not, and a later same-day research pass in this corpus, [the provenance of the cottonwood star](./2026-08-22-note-on-the-provenance-of-the-cottonwood-star.md), found the terminology considerably less settled than the session assumed: the credentialed ethnobotanical listing it consulted (Black Elk with Wilbur D. Flying By, Sr., *Culturally Important Plants of the Lakota*, Sitting Bull College, 1998) gives čhaŋyáȟ'u, šaká čháŋ, and waȟčhíŋča for *Populus deltoides* and does not contain "wáǧačhaŋ" at all, and that note locates no attested source for "čhaŋwákȟaŋ" as a distinct term either.
The underlying ontological move — a pole made sacred by entered relation, not by subtraction — is well attested in the corpus's Sun Dance sourcing (Densmore 1918, Walker 1917, Black Elk via Brown 1953) independent of the specific two-word pairing the session used to name it.
The aperture theory should therefore be read as resting on that underlying, better-attested move, with the wáǧačhaŋ/čhaŋwákȟaŋ vocabulary flagged as an unverified working label from the originating session rather than a settled corpus term, pending the kind of direct check against Buechel's dictionary or a Lakota-language authority that the provenance note itself recommends and did not complete.

## 5. Why this is a distinct contribution, not a restatement

This is explicitly not a restatement of the corpus's existing dispositional-border thesis, which is a diagnostic theory: borders exclude by disposition, not geography, a claim already proven twice, once on each line with opposite polarities, in [two lines](./2026-08-22-note-on-two-lines.md).
The aperture theory takes the same dispositional-power framework and turns it the other way: instead of cataloguing how a line excludes, it asks how a relation can be built into something the line is compelled to accommodate.
As the session framed the move at the time: it turns the corpus "from a diagnosis of how lines exclude into a theory of how they can be made to hold."
