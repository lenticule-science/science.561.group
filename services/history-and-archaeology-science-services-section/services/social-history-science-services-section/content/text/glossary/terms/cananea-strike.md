On June 1, 1906, Mexican miners at William Cornell Greene's Cananea Consolidated Copper Company struck over a two-tier wage system that paid Mexican laborers $1.50 for the same work American laborers were paid $5.00 to do — segregation running straight through the payroll, in a mining town on Mexican soil owned by an American who also ran roughly 600,000 acres of cattle empire straddling the Arizona-Sonora line, including the San Rafael de la Zanja grant he had bought from the Cameron syndicate in 1902–03. Greene's response was to call for help across the border. What arrived was 275 armed American volunteers who crossed into Mexico under Arizona Ranger Captain Thomas Rynning, at the invitation of the Sonoran governor and against the orders of the Arizona territorial governor. Estimates of the dead range from roughly 20 to 33; sources differ on the exact count, but not on the shape of the event.

The strike's significance outruns its body count. Historians widely read Cananea as a prefiguring shock of the Mexican Revolution that broke out four years later in 1910 — an early, visible instance of the exploitation, foreign ownership, and state violence that the Revolution would name as its targets. It sits alongside the Río Blanco textile strike (1907) as one of the two labor eruptions most commonly cited as the Porfiriato's cracking points. Pancho Villa is reported to have raided the Greene ranch for horses in the years that followed and to have crossed the international line at Lochiel — the same stretch of border, put to a different use than the Rangers' crossing south.

What makes Cananea load-bearing for this knowledge base rather than a footnote to Greene's biography is what the border did and did not stop that day. The line was already, in 1906, an apparatus that filtered by category rather than by geography: it did nothing to stop 275 armed American civilians from crossing south to help suppress a labor action against an American-owned company on Mexican soil, at a foreign governor's invitation and over a domestic governor's objection. Compare the asymmetry a century later — a wall waived past 34 federal laws to stop northward movement, while capital, its owners, and (when it suits capital) armed men have never needed comparable permission to move south. Cananea is the border's asymmetric permeability made visible: proof, on the record and by name, that the line was never built to be a wall in both directions.
