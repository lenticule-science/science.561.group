Lochiel is a place that has been named at least three times by people who never lived there permanently. The settlement began as **La Noria** — "the waterwheel," a Mexican-era ranching hamlet built around a well on what would become the San Rafael de la Zanja land grant. When Rollin Richardson bought the grant in 1880, the post office took the name **Luttrell**; when the Cameron syndicate's San Rafael Cattle Company acquired it from him in 1883, the post office was renamed again, in 1884, to **Lochiel** — the name it has carried since. The line surveyed through the valley in 1855 had, by the 1880s, split the original settlement outright: the Mexican side of the border kept the name La Noria and still carries it today as a small Sonoran village, while the American side became Lochiel. One town, one well, two names, because a line drawn thirty years after the well was dug decided which half got to keep the old one.

The name Lochiel did not originate in Arizona and was not chosen for anything particular to this valley. Colin Cameron and his brother Brewster, the syndicate's principals, were sons of a Danville, Pennsylvania farmer who happened to share a name — Simon Cameron — with the far more famous Senator Simon Cameron, Lincoln's first Secretary of War; [H&S 1995] note the family connection to the senator's network without being able to close the exact link. What is traceable is the senator's own property: his Harrisburg-area holdings already included an estate and an iron company both called "Lochiel," themselves named for Achnacarry, the historic seat of Clan Cameron in Lochaber in the Scottish Highlands. The Arizona name is a relay with three hops — a cleared Highland glen, to a Pennsylvania ironmaster's nostalgic estate name, to a cattle syndicate's post office in a valley four thousand miles from either — carrying grief forward as a deed rather than a memory.

At its peak the American-side settlement held roughly four hundred residents, two smelters, three saloons, and five stores; a school opened in 1918 and had run out of students by 1920; the post office closed in 1911. The port of entry itself, one of the smaller crossings on the Arizona-Sonora line, closed in 1983 — a Nogales International retrospective and a September 1983 photograph of the last customs agent on duty corroborate the date, though no official closure order has surfaced and a commonly repeated "safety reasons" explanation should be treated as uncorroborated rather than as settled fact. What remains today — schoolhouse, teacherage, church, cemetery, customs house — stands on private land, a handful of structures outlasting the port, the post office, and most of the population they once served.

Lochiel's most visible monument commemorates a claim its own builders' best evidence does not support. In 1939 the National Youth Administration — a Depression-era jobs program, staffed with local teenagers — built a monument to Fray Marcos de Niza inside the statewide Coronado Cuarto Centennial commemorations, dedicated that April 15 with the governors of both Arizona and Sonora attending. Its inscription declares de Niza the first European west of the Rockies, passing through this exact spot on April 12, 1539. Both halves of that claim are contested in the scholarship: the route through this valley is a minority reconstruction (Wilson 1987) against the majority view that de Niza followed the San Pedro instead (Sauer 1932, the reading [H&S 1995] also favor); and de Niza's underlying report of golden Cíbola was doubted even in his own century, later scholarship (Hallenbeck) calling him "a lying monk" who may have turned back well short of his claimed destination and embellished the rest. The state built a permanent monument, with two governors present, to a claim that may be geographically wrong about a report that may have been fabricated.

The valley's cottonwood grove needed no such monument and got no such protection. Four Fremont cottonwoods estimated at two hundred to two hundred fifty years old — an estimate, not a ring count, but plausible for exceptional trees at a perennial headwaters site — stood at Lochiel as a living, datable archive of two centuries of floods, contemporaries of the 1825 land grant itself. In June 2025 a $309.46 million contract to Fisher Sand & Gravel began roughly twenty-seven miles of double bollard wall through the valley, under a waiver that set aside thirty-four federal laws. On July 27–28, 2026, construction crews uprooted three of the four trees despite an earlier CBP promise not to fell them while migratory birds were nesting; a local resident who blocked the machinery at the fourth tree was arrested and released without charges. The next day, activists installed a platform sixty feet up the surviving tree — organizers had already begun calling the grove "the grandmothers" after a solstice ceremony that June — in what the Center for Biological Diversity called the first tree-sit aimed at stopping U.S. border wall construction. As of the most recent verified reporting (August 19, 2026), the sit continues into its third week, binational since mid-August, with no resolution and no attempt to fell the occupied tree.

Held together, Lochiel's history is the settlerism-as-filing-system argument in miniature: a name imported through three relays of ownership and imperial nostalgia, a monument raised with state ceremony to a claim its own sources doubt, a town whose survival or dissolution tracked not its residents' choices but a Pennsylvania syndicate's balance sheet and a federal port-closure decision nobody bothered to document — set now against a grove that required no naming, no monument, and no survey to be exactly what it is, and that the same filing system has just moved to erase. The tree-sit is the first time in this settlement's hundred-and-forty-year recorded history that the thing being defended is the older, truer claim, and the thing doing the erasing is the one with the paperwork.
