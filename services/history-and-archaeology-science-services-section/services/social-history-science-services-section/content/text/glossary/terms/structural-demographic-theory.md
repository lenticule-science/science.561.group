**Structural-demographic theory**, developed by [Peter Turchin](../../../../../../../../content/text/documents/articles/biographies/peter-turchin.md) from earlier work by Jack Goldstone, holds that complex agrarian and industrial societies undergo recurring "secular cycles" of roughly two to three centuries, driven by the interaction of four structural pressures: population growth outrunning the carrying capacity of the existing economic and political order; **elite overproduction**, in which more aspirants compete for a fixed or slowly growing number of elite positions than the system can absorb, intensifying intra-elite conflict; **popular immiseration**, falling real wages and declining living standards for the general population; and state fiscal distress, as a state caught between rising costs and a resistant or shrinking tax base loses capacity to manage the other three pressures.

The theory is distinguished from purely economic business-cycle models by its multi-generational timescale and its explicit tracking of elite competition, rather than aggregate output, as a primary driver of instability. In a 2010 *Nature* commentary, Turchin projected rising US political instability on a trajectory peaking around 2020, based on measured trends in elite overproduction and wage stagnation since the 1970s — a forecast widely cited as prescient given the convergence of pandemic disruption, mass protest, and the January 6, 2021 Capitol events.

Structural-demographic theory is read alongside [diminishing returns on complexity](diminishing-returns-on-complexity.md) as a second quantitative account of societal stress, applied to political instability within a society rather than to that society's overall complexity-versus-energy-subsidy balance.
