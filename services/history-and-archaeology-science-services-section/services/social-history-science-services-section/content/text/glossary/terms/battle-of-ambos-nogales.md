Nogales, Arizona and Nogales, Sonora were one town split by the Gadsden line, crossable on foot down the middle of International Street, until a single afternoon in 1918 made that crossing lethal by default. The border at Nogales had already been fenced once before, briefly: in August 1915, Sonora Governor José M. Maytorena — mid-revolution, asserting control against rival factions — ordered an eleven-wire fence strung between the two towns; it came down again after about four months, and for the next three years the line reverted to open ground. It was Mexican initiative, not American, that first tried to wall this town, and it did not hold.

The 1918 battle began as a border-inspection dispute — a Mexican civilian shot crossing into Sonora — that a Mexican customs guard's return fire escalated within minutes into a firefight between U.S. Army units (including Buffalo Soldiers of the 10th Cavalry) and Mexican federal troops and armed civilians on the Sonora side, compounded by wartime nerves (the U.S. was five months from the Armistice) and rumors of German agitation. As the shooting spread, Nogales, Sonora's mayor, Félix B. Peñaloza, tied a white handkerchief to his walking stick and ran into the street pleading with the crowd to stop; he was shot down by fire from the American side and died within minutes. President Venustiano Carranza named his death in his address to the Mexican Congress days later. Counts vary by source, but the range given is up to 129 Mexican dead against 4 American dead, with roughly 300 wounded on both sides combined — a lopsided toll from a fight neither government had ordered.

The peace that followed is what made the battle load-bearing rather than a one-day incident: Mexican General Plutarco Elías Calles and U.S. General DeRosey Cabell agreed that fall to install a permanent barrier — a two-mile, six-foot barbed wire fence, built at a reported cost of $4,000-5,000 depending on the source — the first fence to permanently divide two towns on the U.S.-Mexico border. Where the 1915 fence had been a wartime improvisation, taken down again within months, the 1918 fence stayed: proof that it took actual bloodshed, not merely the treaty line's existence, to convert a shared street into a hardened boundary.

The battle sits inside this glossary's hardening sequence at a specific point: after the USDA's 1909-11 cattle-tick quarantine fence at Otay Mesa — the first federally funded border barrier anywhere, built against livestock disease rather than people — and before Border Patrol's founding in 1924. Read in order, the sequence says something about what the border was first built to keep out: disease, then a town's own population after they had already killed each other over a shooting, decades before "border security" against migration became the stated rationale at all. The mayor killed approaching under his own flag of truce is the sequence's starkest image of the line's operating logic: an apparatus that does not reliably distinguish threat from surrender, because its exclusion-power was never calibrated to either.
