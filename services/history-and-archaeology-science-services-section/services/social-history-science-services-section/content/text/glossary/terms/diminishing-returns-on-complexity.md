**Diminishing returns on complexity** is [Joseph Tainter](../../../../../../../../content/text/documents/articles/biographies/joseph-tainter.md)'s theory, developed in *The Collapse of Complex Societies* (1988), that societies invest in increasing sociopolitical complexity — bureaucracy, specialization, infrastructure, military capacity — to solve the problems they face, and that this investment carries increasing costs per unit of complexity added while its marginal returns decline. When the energy subsidy available to a society can no longer cover the cost of maintaining its accumulated complexity, and no cheaper new energy source is available to extend the subsidy, collapse — a rapid, large-scale simplification — becomes the economically rational adaptive response rather than an aberrant catastrophic failure.

Tainter developed the argument through the Western Roman Empire, the Chacoan society of the American Southwest, and the Lowland Classic Maya, in each case identifying the point at which further investment in complexity stopped paying for itself. The theory is cited by exact quotation in the house's own research planning ("Societies collapse when their investments in social complexity and their energy subsidies reach a point of diminishing marginal returns," Tainter 1988), used there to read the cognitive infrastructure of past civilizations — libraries, philosophy schools, legal codes — as funded by scale and extraction efficiency rather than rising per-capita energy return, and therefore vulnerable to collapse alongside the extraction apparatus that funded them.

[John Michael Greer](../../../../../../../../content/text/documents/articles/biographies/john-michael-greer.md)'s [catabolic collapse](catabolic-collapse.md) builds directly on this theory, adding an account of why the resulting decline proceeds unevenly rather than as a single threshold event.
