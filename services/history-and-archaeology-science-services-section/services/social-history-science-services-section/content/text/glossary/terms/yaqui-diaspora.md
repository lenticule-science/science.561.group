Yaqui diaspora names the northward flight of the Yoeme people out of Sonora and across the Gadsden line into Arizona, driven by four decades of Mexican state violence: the destruction of José María Leyva Cajeme's autonomous Yaqui polity in 1887; the guerrilla war led by Juan Maldonado Tetabiate after the collapsed 1897 Peace of Ortiz; and, from 1899 onward, the Porfirio Díaz government's deliberate policy — Hu-DeHart (1984) calls it explicitly what contemporary Mexican officials called it, a "final solution" — of mass deportation. Between roughly 1903 and 1911, an estimated 8,000 to 15,000 Yoeme men, women, and children were shipped to the henequen plantations of Yucatán and to Oaxaca, where forced labor, malnutrition, and disease killed most of them; the January 1900 Battle of Mazocoba alone sent 800 prisoners into that pipeline after 400 Yaqui dead. Crossing north was not incidental to this history — it was the alternative to it.

The refuge line ran along the Southern Pacific corridor the Purchase had been bought to build: the first documented Yoeme arrival in Arizona is a named individual, Juan Flores "Molonko," working the Santa Fe railway out of Nogales in 1884, and rail work supplied both the pretext and the means for the migrations that followed. By 1908 hundreds had settled permanently; the 1910 Revolution's disruptions pushed more north through the 1920s. What resulted was not one settlement but a constellation, each holding a piece of a dispersed nation: Barrio Libre and Pascua Village in Tucson; Yoem Pueblo and Wiilo Kampo near Marana; Guadalupe, founded 1906 outside Phoenix; and smaller communities near Eloy, Somerton, and Scottsdale. By 1940 an estimated 3,000 Yoeme lived across these Arizona villages, most working as migrant farm labor, all of them noncitizens in the country whose border they had crossed to survive the country they'd left.

Federal recognition arrived slowly and only after decades of the Tucson communities organizing on their own behalf. Anthropologist Edward Spicer — whose *The Yaquis: A Cultural History* (1980) remains the standard scholarly synthesis, and who co-edited Muriel Thayer Painter's *With Good Heart* (1986), her account of four decades observing Pascua Village ceremony — was drawn into that advocacy in the 1930s through Thamar Richey's committee to protect Yoeme families from deportation back across the line they had just crossed. Postwar, Yaqui veteran Anselmo Valencia led the Pascua Yaqui Association's petition campaign; Congressman Morris Udall secured 202 acres for a reservation in 1964, and Senator Dennis DeConcini's bill became Public Law 95-375 on September 18, 1978 — the Pascua Yaqui Tribe's federal recognition, ninety-plus years after the first refugees crossed at Nogales.

This is why the Yaqui diaspora belongs beside the Lochiel research note's account of the same line (see the [note on Lochiel history](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-lochiel-history.md)): it is the line's third demonstrated mode, alongside the 1906 Cananea crossing of armed Americans moving south to break a strike and the 2018 deportation of enrolled O'odham from ancestral land moving north. The Gadsden line has been, in documented fact, a capital corridor, an exclusion instrument, and — for the Yoeme fleeing a state's declared intent to eliminate them — a refuge. A single boundary sorting bodies into different regimes depending on who is crossing and why is asymmetric permeability's clearest case: the line does not gate a place, it gates a category, and which category gets through changes with who is asking. It is also worth stating plainly against the current moment: the same strip of land the 2025-26 wall project is bisecting at gunpoint expense was, within living family memory for many enrolled Pascua Yaqui members, the ground a people crossed to stay alive.
