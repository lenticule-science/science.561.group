The Code of Indian Offenses was the administrative regime through which the United States criminalized Native religious practice. It began with Interior Secretary Henry Teller's letter of March 30, 1883, naming "the old heathenish dances, such as the sun-dance" as obstacles to be compelled out of existence; Commissioner Hiram Price implemented it as rules establishing Courts of Indian Offenses — three Native judges appointed by the Commissioner, operating under the local Indian agent's authority, i.e., not tribal courts but agency instruments wearing tribal faces.

The code criminalized the Sun Dance, scalp dance, war dance, and medicine practice by name, along with plural marriage, funerary property destruction, and gift-giving ceremony. The penalty structure is the historically load-bearing detail: for sacred dances, **withholding of rations** — ten days for a first offense, escalating — on reservations where rations were survival. The mechanism of religious suppression was administered hunger.

The regime governed from 1883 until Commissioner John Collier's Circular No. 2970 (1934) — "no interference with Indian religious life or ceremonial expression will hereafter be tolerated" — ended it administratively, though missionary and agency suppression persisted informally for decades. Statutory protection arrived only with the American Indian Religious Freedom Act (August 11, 1978), itself a policy statement without a private right of action, as *Lyng* (1988) and *Employment Division v. Smith* (1990) later demonstrated.

For this glossary's line of argument: the code is a clean instance of exclusion-power selectively energized — the same state that would not enforce its own treaty obligations administered starvation to stop a dance around a cottonwood. Ceremonies revived openly from the 1930s–70s onward, under federal observation, and are living practice today; the 2026 defense of cottonwoods at the Lochiel border wall (see the 2026-08-22 Lochiel history note) sits inside living memory of the ban.
