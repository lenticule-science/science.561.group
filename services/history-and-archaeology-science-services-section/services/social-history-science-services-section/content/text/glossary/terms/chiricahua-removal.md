In September 1886, after Geronimo's surrender at Skeleton Canyon, the United States did not simply imprison the fighters who had held out against it — it deported the entire Chiricahua people, including the Apache scouts who had served the Army in the campaign against Geronimo. Between April and November 1886 the government shipped roughly 500 Chiricahua and Warm Springs (Chihenne) Apache east as prisoners of war: seventeen warriors, ninety-nine other men, and nearly four hundred women and children. Families were split on arrival — men to Fort Pickens, women and children to Fort Marion — a separation the government maintained for two years before consolidating the group, first at Mount Vernon Barracks, Alabama (1887–88), then, in 1894, at Fort Sill, Oklahoma. The captivity that began in Florida did not end until 1913: twenty-seven years, the longest prisoner-of-war status ever held by any group in the United States, under a legal fiction — "prisoner of war" — that applied to children born decades after the war it named had ended.

The toll was demographic as much as political. Tuberculosis and the general conditions of internment cut the Chiricahua population roughly in half, from about 500 at the point of surrender to 261 survivors by 1913. On April 2 of that year — an event the descendants still call "the Parting" — an act of Congress ended prisoner-of-war status and let each family choose a destination: about 180 people relocated to the Mescalero Apache Reservation in New Mexico, joining Mescalero and Lipan Apache communities there, while roughly 80 people in about twenty families remained in Oklahoma, resettled on individual allotments around the towns of Apache and Fletcher. That second, smaller group organized decades later as the federally recognized Fort Sill Apache Tribe (1976); the larger group's descendants are today enrolled largely within the Mescalero Apache Tribe. Both nations are, in the fullest sense, Chiricahua nations — the 1913 split was an administrative accident of relocation logistics, not a division the people chose.

Before any of this, Chiricahua bands — along with Western Apache bands — held the mountain ranges surrounding the San Rafael Valley as seasonal range: the valley floor itself shows no permanent Apache settlement, any more than it shows permanent Sobaipuri settlement (see [Sobaipuri](sobaipuri.md)), but [H&S 1995] read it as shared-use country worked from the Huachucas, the Patagonias, and the ranges the current border wall now bisects. This use long predates the 1825 San Rafael de la Zanja land grant and the Cameron ranching operation that followed it, and it long predates the 1853–54 line itself (see [Gadsden Purchase](gadsden-purchase.md)) — the Chiricahua were moving through this exact terrain a generation before Emory's surveyors set the first monument.

That prior presence is why the Chiricahua descendant nations are the valley's other dispossessed party — the one nobody consults. The Tohono O'odham Nation has standing that current law, however inadequately, still recognizes: a federally recognized reservation whose boundary the border wall physically crosses, and a 2026 lawsuit (denied injunctive relief that August) built on exactly that adjacency. The Fort Sill Apache Tribe and the Mescalero Apache Tribe have no comparable claim on file, because the 1886 removal severed the thing a modern consultation process looks for: a reservation, or a treaty, tying a federally recognized nation to this specific ground. Their relationship to the San Rafael ranges is real, well documented, and older than the ranching empire whose intactness the current wall fight defends — and it survives today only as the seasonal-use record in a Forest Service land-use history, not as a line on a map that triggers a government-to-government consultation letter. The 1886 removal did not just deport people; it filed their claim to this specific landscape somewhere the current permitting apparatus does not look, twice over, once by deportation and once again by the accident of where the survivors were later allowed to resettle.
