Grandmother's Land — rendered in the round's files as **Uŋčíyapi Makȟóčhe** (orthography per the corpus's exile note; verify against the New Lakota Dictionary before external use) — was the Lakota name for Canada during and after the exile years: the country of the Grandmother, Queen Victoria. The name did the same kind of work the corpus keeps finding in Lakota naming practice: it read a political jurisdiction as a *kinship relation*. Crossing the [Medicine Line](./medicine-line.md) did not put you in a rival state's territory; it put you under a grandmother's roof — with everything that implies about obligation, shelter, and the terms on which shelter is given.

The name's aptness and its limits are both historical facts. The Grandmother's country did shelter Sitting Bull's people for four years (1877–1881) when the U.S. Army could not follow, and Dakota refugees from 1862–63 before them. It also refused them a reserve and rations, and waited out the buffalo collapse — refuge on a starvation clock Ottawa controlled, as the exile note documents. A grandmother who will not feed you is still exercising a relation; the name held either way, which is part of what makes it precise rather than sentimental.

The corpus treats the recurrence of grandmother-naming — Grandmother's Land in the 1870s, the "grandmother" cottonwoods named at a solstice ceremony at Lochiel in 2026 — as a **real but undocumented resonance, not a lineage** (the tree-naming's attribution does not tie to any specific nation's word; the two-lines synthesis note states this discipline). What the pair does establish, without overreach, is a pattern in Indigenous naming on both of this corpus's lines: where settler law names things as jurisdiction and property, the naming practices the corpus documents keep answering with kin.
