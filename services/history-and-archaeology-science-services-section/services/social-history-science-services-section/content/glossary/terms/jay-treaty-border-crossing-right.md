Article III of the 1794 Jay Treaty — the Treaty of Amity, Commerce, and Navigation between Great Britain and the United States — provides "that it shall at all times be free to His Majesty's subjects, and to the citizens of the United States, and also to the Indians dwelling on either side of the said boundary line, freely to pass and repass, by land or inland navigation, into the respective territories and countries of the two parties," and to carry on trade without paying duty on their own goods. Negotiated eleven years after the border it addresses was first drawn, the clause names what the border's own draftsmen already knew: the 49th parallel and its eastern counterpart ran through nations that had never agreed to be divided by it, and safe passage for those nations was written into the treaty as a named exception to the line's authority, not an oversight the line later had to accommodate.

The right survived the treaty that created it only in damaged form. The Supreme Court held in **Karnuth v. United States ex rel. Albro, 279 U.S. 231 (decided April 8, 1929)**, that Article III's general free-passage guarantee — for British subjects and U.S. citizens alike — had been abrogated by the War of 1812 under the ordinary rule that treaties of this kind do not survive a war between the signatories unless expressly revived at the peace. *Karnuth* left genuine and lasting confusion over whether Indigenous passage rights specifically survived that abrogation along with everyone else's, or stood on separate footing given the boundary line's own origin in territory none of the affected nations had ceded. Congress resolved the U.S. side of that confusion by statute rather than treaty interpretation: **8 U.S.C. § 1359**, part of the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, provides that "nothing in this subchapter shall be construed to affect the right of American Indians born in Canada to pass the borders of the United States, but such right shall extend only to persons who possess at least 50 per centum of blood of the American Indian race" (primary-verified against the U.S. Code text). The implementing regulation, 8 C.F.R. Part 289, applies the same threshold and excludes a qualifying person's spouse or child, or a member by adoption, unless that individual independently meets the 50-percent standard. The arithmetic is worth naming exactly as the statute states it: not tribal enrollment, not descent from a signatory nation, but a blood-quantum fraction, set by Congress in 1952 and never Indigenous-authored, that a U.S. immigration officer at the border is the one empowered to apply.

Canada has never recognized a reciprocal right. Canadian courts have repeatedly held that the Jay Treaty has no force in Canadian law — it was never implemented by Canadian legislation, and *Karnuth*'s abrogation finding is treated north of the border as closing the question entirely rather than leaving Indigenous passage as a live exception (attested-secondary, consistent across multiple legal-aid and law-review treatments of the border, including reporting collected by the Senate of Canada's Standing Committee on Aboriginal Peoples). A status Indian or other Indigenous person crossing north into Canada does so under the same immigration framework as any other traveler — Canadian citizenship or Indian status under Canadian law, not descent recognized under the Jay Treaty, is what governs the right to enter, live, or work. The asymmetry is not a technicality: it means a Canadian-born Indigenous person who can document 50 percent American Indian blood has a statutory right, in U.S. law, to cross south and remain, work, and reside in the United States that no equivalent U.S.-born Indigenous person holds crossing north into Canada. First Nations organizations have issued travel advisories over the gap, and there are documented cases of ICE detaining or interrogating Indigenous travelers holding valid Canadian status documentation despite their statutory U.S. entitlement.

No equivalent treaty right exists anywhere on the southern border. The **Tohono O'odham Nation's Enhanced Tribal Card**, issued under a memorandum of agreement between the Nation and DHS/CBP as a WHTI-compliant travel document, lets enrolled members cross for kinship, subsistence, and the Magdalena pilgrimage into O'odham lands in Sonora — but it is an administrative accommodation DHS chose to grant, revocable and re-negotiable, resting on no treaty text comparable to Jay Treaty Article III. The Kickapoo Traditional Tribe of Texas holds a narrower documented arrangement of similar administrative character. Where the Jay Treaty's Article III was written into the founding instrument of the northern border itself — an exception the line had to carry from its own creation — nothing analogous was ever written into the Gadsden Purchase or Guadalupe Hidalgo for the O'odham, Yaqui, Kumeyaay, or any other nation split by the southern line.

For this corpus, the Jay Treaty crossing right is load-bearing evidence for the argument that the two borders encode Indigenous mobility asymmetrically by design, not by neglect. Both lines are the same kind of object — a settler-drawn [dispositional](./property-as-disposition.md) [exclusion-power](./exclusion-powers.md), authorized to admit or refuse regardless of what it materially marks. But only the northern line carries a named, if damaged, textual concession to the nations it divided; the southern line carries none, and what stands in its place — the Enhanced Tribal Card — is [settlerism-as-filing-system](./settlerism-as-filing-system.md) at its plainest: a filing labeled "accommodation" doing the work a filing labeled "treaty right" would do, except that the first filing can be amended or withdrawn by the agency that wrote it and the second, however unevenly honored, cannot. Even the honored half of the asymmetry is telling: 8 U.S.C. § 1359's 50-percent-blood arithmetic is a settler category laid over Indigenous peoplehood exactly where the [Code of Indian Offenses](./code-of-indian-offenses.md) laid administrative categories over Lakota ceremony — the right survives, but only in the shape the filing system was willing to hold it in.
