On **June 1, 1873**, a party of American wolfers and whiskey traders attacked an Assiniboine (Nakoda) camp in the Cypress Hills, on the Canadian side of the 49th parallel, killing somewhere between **13 and roughly 30 people** — the source spread is genuine and the corpus states it as a range rather than a false precision (verified). One wolfer died. The killers had crossed the Medicine Line without hindrance, as American traders had been doing throughout the **Fort Whoop-Up whiskey trade (1869–1874)**, which ran liquor north into Blackfoot territory for years with no Canadian government presence to stop it. Attempts to prosecute failed — extradition was refused and later Canadian proceedings produced no convictions (attested-secondary).

The timing is the historically load-bearing fact: the **North-West Mounted Police Act had passed ten days earlier, May 23, 1873**, and the massacre converted a paper force into an urgent deployment — the March West of July–October 1874. The [NWMP](./north-west-mounted-police.md) exists, institutionally, because American violence crossed a line that stopped armies but never stopped traders, wolfers, or whiskey.

For this corpus, Cypress Hills is the sharpest single instance of the [Medicine Line](./medicine-line.md)'s selective permeability — the same asymmetry the southern corpus documents at [Cananea](./cananea-strike.md), where 275 armed Americans crossed south to break a strike. On both lines, in both centuries, armed private Americans crossed freely in the direction of profit while the state's stopping-power pointed elsewhere. The line filters by category, not geography; Cypress Hills is the northern proof.
