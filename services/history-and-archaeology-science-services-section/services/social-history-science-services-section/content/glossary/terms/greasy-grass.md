The people who fought there call it the Battle of the Greasy Grass, for the river the Lakota name — rendered in Standard Lakota Orthography as Pȟežísla Wakpá, and in older popular transliteration as "Pezi Sla" or "Peji Sla Wakapa" — describes literally: grass left slick to the touch by heavy morning dew. (Lakota orthography itself is not single-standard; the Lakota Language Consortium's SLO, Albert White Hat's system, and Violet Catches' Thakíni Iyapi each render the sounds differently, and this entry follows the LLC convention while noting the variance rather than erasing it.) The U.S. Army named the same fight for the river the Crow called the Little Bighorn. Both names are attested-secondary here — drawn from published Lakota-language sources and NPS's own site materials — rather than primary-verified against a fluent speaker, and readers should treat the orthography as approximate for that reason.

**Context: the Great Sioux War of 1876.** The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 had guaranteed the Black Hills (Pahá Sápa) and a large surrounding territory to the Great Sioux Reservation in perpetuity. Gold was found in the Black Hills in 1874 — during a U.S. Army reconnaissance expedition led by George Armstrong Custer himself, in violation of the same treaty — and by 1875 prospectors were pouring in faster than the Army pretended to be able to stop them. In November 1875, President Grant met with General Philip Sheridan and Interior Department officials and decided, in effect, to let the invasion stand and force the issue the other direction: the Interior Department ordered all Lakota and Northern Cheyenne bands living off the reservation, in the unceded territory the 1868 treaty itself protected, to report to their assigned agencies by January 31, 1876, or be declared hostile and turned over to the Army. Sitting Bull's and other bands' winter camps were, by the government's own reckoning, hundreds of miles from any agency in the dead of a Plains winter; the deadline was not a request for compliance so much as a manufactured pretext for war. When it passed unmet, the Army launched a three-pronged summer campaign under Generals Alfred Terry, George Crook, and John Gibbon, with Custer's 7th Cavalry attached to Terry's column.

**The encampment.** Through the spring and early summer of 1876, Lakota (Hunkpapa, Oglala, Sicangu, Miniconjou, Sans Arc, Blackfeet Sioux), Northern Cheyenne, and some Arapaho bands converged along the Rosebud and then the Little Bighorn, drawn by Sitting Bull's leadership and by a Sun Dance vision in which he saw soldiers "falling into camp like grasshoppers." Sources spread widely on the encampment's size, and this entry states the spread rather than picking a number: the National Park Service's own account for the battlefield gives roughly 7,000 people and 1,500-2,000 warriors; other tallies built from lodge counts (Captain Weir's observation of roughly 1,800 lodges, at a rough average of two fighting-age men per lodge) imply figures at the higher end of that warrior range or above it; contemporary participant accounts, including the frontier guide Mitch Boyer's remark to Custer that it was the largest village he had encountered in thirty years among these nations, corroborate the encampment's exceptional scale without fixing a number. What is not in dispute is that it was, by a wide margin, the largest single gathering of Plains nations of the era — and that Custer's intelligence badly underestimated it.

**The fight, June 25-26, 1876.** Custer divided his roughly 600-man regiment into three battalions and attacked the southern end of the encampment at midday on June 25, expecting the village to scatter as it had at the Washita in 1868. It did not. Major Marcus Reno's battalion was driven back across the river with heavy losses; Captain Frederick Benteen's battalion, alerted by messenger, joined Reno on a defensible bluff and held through a two-day siege. Custer's own five companies, roughly 210 men, rode north along the bluffs and were engulfed and destroyed — the "Last Stand" — by warriors led in the field by Crazy Horse, Gall, Two Moons, and others, in an action lasting perhaps an hour. Ernie LaPointe's family account, drawing on oral history passed down as Sitting Bull's great-grandson, records that Sitting Bull himself, past the age expected of front-line combatants, spent the fight in prayer on a nearby rise rather than in the charge — a detail standard military-history accounts built from soldier testimony have no access to, and one of the reasons Lakota-authored sources matter for this battle specifically.

**Casualties.** U.S. losses across the two-day engagement are put at 268 killed and around 55 wounded (six of whom later died), including four Crow and at least two Arikara scouts attached to the regiment; every man in Custer's immediate battalion, roughly 210 soldiers, died. It remains the U.S. Army's worst defeat in the Plains wars. Native losses are far less precisely documented, largely because the encampment moved on rather than leaving a body count for Army investigators — estimates run from roughly 30 to 100 warriors killed, with additional non-combatant deaths from artillery fire on the village's edge; this spread is itself attested-secondary, built from later interviews and Army estimates rather than a contemporary tally on the winning side.

**The aftermath: the victory that produced the exile.** The battle was a decisive Lakota-Cheyenne-Arapaho victory, and it changed nothing about the outcome the United States was prepared to accept. News of Custer's death reached the East during the nation's Centennial celebrations and produced not reconsideration but escalation: reinforcements poured into the field, and the Army spent the following winter hunting down and burning the same bands' villages one by one — Crazy Horse's people at the Battle of Wolf Mountain in January 1877, others surrendering piecemeal through the spring rather than face another winter without lodges or food. Congress moved even faster than the Army. On August 15, 1876 — seven weeks after the battle — it passed an Indian Appropriations Act rider, known among the Lakota as the "sell or starve" rider, cutting off all further rations to the Sioux until they ceded the Black Hills and renounced their unceded hunting territory. A commission under George Manypenny took a prepared cession document to the agencies in September 1876 and secured signatures from roughly 10 percent of adult Lakota men — far short of the three-fourths supermajority the 1868 treaty itself required for any land cession — and Congress enacted that "agreement" into law on February 28, 1877, stripping the Black Hills and other territory, more than seven million acres in total, out of the Great Sioux Reservation. A century later, in *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians* (1980), the U.S. Supreme Court found the taking a plain violation of the Fifth Amendment, writing that "a more ripe and rank case of dishonorable dealings will never, in all probability, be found in our history," and ordered compensation — which the Oceti Sakowin nations have refused ever since, on the position that the Black Hills were never for sale; the judgment fund, left untouched and compounding in a federal trust account, was reported at roughly $1 billion by 2010-2011 and in the $1.3-2 billion range through the 2010s, with no precise, officially disclosed current balance located for this entry — the [Fort Laramie Treaty (1868)](./fort-laramie-treaty-1868.md) entry treats this figure at greater length and flags it **attested-secondary but stale**, since a April 2025 report states the Oglala Sioux Tribe itself pushed Interior to deny a FOIA request to unseal the fund's present value. Crazy Horse surrendered at Camp Robinson in May 1877 and was killed there that September; Sitting Bull led several thousand followers across the border into Canada rather than surrender, beginning the Wood Mountain and Fort Walsh years covered in this glossary's [North-West Mounted Police](./north-west-mounted-police.md) entry, and did not return to surrender at Fort Buford until July 1881.

For this corpus's line of argument, the Greasy Grass fight is the cleanest case on record of a structural point the corpus makes about [exclusion-powers](./exclusion-powers.md): the power an arrangement holds to remove or destroy does not depend on winning any given encounter, because the arrangement is built to absorb a battlefield loss and convert it into an administrative one. The Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho warriors at the Greasy Grass won completely — annihilated a third of a regiment, killed the officer whose name the battle now carries in English — and the material outcome, within eight months, was the largest land seizure in the Sioux Wars' history, enacted not by the Army that lost but by a Congress that had not fired a shot. That is [settlerism-as-filing-system](./settlerism-as-filing-system.md) operating at its most exposed: the battlefield outcome got filed as "hostility" requiring "pacification," the ration supply got filed as leverage rather than treaty obligation, and a cession signed by roughly one in ten eligible men got filed as a nation's consent. The victory did not fail to stop the seizure; the seizure was the structure's direct answer to the victory, passed into law within seven weeks of the news reaching Washington, by a Congress that had lost nothing at the Little Bighorn and paid nothing for what it took. Winning the battle, inside that structure, was never going to be enough — and the corpus's recurring question, what exclusion-power fires regardless of the field result, has no cleaner instance than a starvation rider that answered the U.S. Army's worst defeat of the era with the era's largest single land seizure.
