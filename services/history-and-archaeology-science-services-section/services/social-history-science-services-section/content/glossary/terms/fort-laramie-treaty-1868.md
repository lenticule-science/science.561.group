The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 was signed at Fort Laramie, Dakota Territory, beginning April 29, 1868, between commissioners of the United States (including Generals William T. Sherman, William S. Harney, Alfred H. Terry, and C. C. Augur, alongside civilian commissioners Nathaniel G. Taylor, John B. Sanborn, and Samuel F. Tappan) and leaders of the Oceti Šakówiŋ — the Lakota bands including the Húŋkpapȟa, Oglála, Sičháŋǧu (Brulé), Mnikȟówožu (Miniconjou), Itázipčho (Sans Arc), Sihásapa (Blackfeet), Oóhenuŋpa (Two Kettle), and Yanktonai — together with Arapaho leaders. Over the roughly six months the treaty circulated for signature, 156 Lakota men and 25 Arapaho men signed it; the U.S. Senate ratified it February 16, 1869, and it was proclaimed February 24, 1869. It ended Red Cloud's War, in which Lakota forces had closed the Bozeman Trail forts, and its terms remain in force as binding U.S. law today — no subsequent instrument has lawfully superseded it.

## Terms

Article 2 established the Great Sioux Reservation: all of present-day South Dakota west of the Missouri River, including the Black Hills, set aside "for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Lakota, closed to non-Indian settlement or passage without Lakota consent. Article 16 designated the Powder River Country to the north and west — the unceded territory — as land where the United States agreed no white person would settle or pass without Lakota permission, and committed to abandoning the Bozeman Trail forts (Fort Phil Kearny, Fort C. F. Smith, and Fort Reno), which it did within months. Article 11 preserved Lakota hunting rights along the Republican River and north of the North Platte "so long as the buffalo may range thereon in such numbers as to justify the chase." Article 12 — the provision most consequential for the treaty's later history — specified that no future cession of any part of the reservation held in common would be valid against the Lakota unless signed by at least three-fourths of the adult male population.

Sitting Bull, among other prominent leaders, declined to sign the treaty at all, reportedly on the ground that Lakota land was not his to sell (see [Sitting Bull](../../../../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/knowledge-services-section/services/research-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/sitting-bull.md)) — a refusal later cited by Lakota advocates as evidence that Lakota consent to the reservation framework itself, not only to its later violation, was partial and contested from the outset.

## The 1877 seizure

Gold was discovered in the Black Hills in 1874, following an expedition led by Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer that itself violated Article 2's guarantee of undisturbed Lakota occupation. Rather than enforce the treaty against the resulting influx of prospectors, Congress in 1876 sent a commission to obtain Lakota cession of the Black Hills and the unceded hunting territory; the resulting document was signed by roughly ten percent of adult Lakota men — far short of Article 12's three-fourths requirement. Congress nonetheless enacted the Act of February 28, 1877, which unilaterally declared the cession in force, seizing the Black Hills and ending recognized Lakota hunting rights in the unceded territory without the treaty's own required consent.

## United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians (1980)

The Lakota pursued compensation for the 1877 taking for decades — first before the Indian Claims Commission (created 1946), then before the Court of Claims after Congress waived res judicata in 1978 to let the claim be reheard on its merits. On June 30, 1980, the Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Harry Blackmun joined by seven of eight participating justices (Justice William Rehnquist alone dissenting), affirmed that the 1877 Act effected a taking of Lakota property without just compensation in violation of the Fifth Amendment. The Court set compensation at $17.1 million — the Black Hills' fair market value in 1877 — plus $450,000 for gold illegally extracted, plus five percent annual interest compounding from the date of the taking, yielding a total award of approximately $105 million. Blackmun's opinion quoted the Court of Claims' own finding beneath it: "A more ripe and rank case of dishonorable dealings will never, in all probability, be found in our history."

The Great Sioux Nation — the descendant tribes of the 1868 treaty parties — has never accepted the award. Because acceptance would legally extinguish the underlying claim to the land itself, tribal councils have voted repeatedly to refuse the money rather than treat the Black Hills as a completed sale. Left untouched, the fund held in trust by the Department of the Interior has continued to accrue interest: reporting placed it at roughly $1.006 billion by 2010–2011, with subsequent journalistic estimates in the $1.3–2 billion range through the 2010s. **Citation status: attested-secondary but stale** — no precise, officially disclosed current balance was located for this entry; a April 2025 report (Buffalo's Fire) states that the Oglala Sioux Tribe itself pushed the Department of the Interior to deny a Freedom of Information Act request seeking to unseal the fund's present value, meaning the most recent authoritative figure is, as of this writing, not public.

## Living law

The treaty's Article 12 supplies the corpus's clearest documented instance of a written consent-threshold being formally acknowledged as violated by the same government that imposed it, and then left unremedied rather than reversed: the Supreme Court's 1980 ruling establishes as a matter of federal law that the Black Hills were taken unlawfully, yet the land itself was never returned, and the government's own proposed remedy — cash — is precisely the transaction Article 12 was written to prevent without three-fourths consent. Standing Rock Sioux Tribe, Oglala Sioux Tribe, and other treaty-descendant governments continue to invoke the 1868 treaty in ongoing litigation and organizing (including opposition to the Dakota Access Pipeline, which the tribes argued crossed treaty and unceded territory), treating it not as a historical artifact but as currently binding law whose violation is likewise current.

The 1868 treaty is this corpus's founding instance of [property-as-disposition](./property-as-disposition.md) operating at treaty scale: Article 12 was written as an exclusion-power held by the Lakota themselves — a three-fourths threshold meant to make future dispossession require their own consent — and the 1877 Act shows that power being overridden by unilateral legislative fiat the moment it became inconvenient to honor. The refused $105 million award is this corpus's clearest instance of [settlerism-as-filing-system](./settlerism-as-filing-system.md) run in reverse: the government's filing system can convert land into a monetary line item, but it cannot make the Lakota accept that filing, and the fund's frozen, now-undisclosed balance is the filing system's own record of a debt it filed as closed while the party it is owed to keeps it open. Read alongside the Medicine Line research this term supports, Article 12's ignored consent-threshold is the same asymmetry the corpus tracks at the 49th parallel: written procedural constraints on U.S. power hold exactly as long as honoring them costs nothing, and the treaty text survives — as living law, cited in court to this day — precisely because law-as-filing does not require that the filing be honored, only that it be kept on record.
