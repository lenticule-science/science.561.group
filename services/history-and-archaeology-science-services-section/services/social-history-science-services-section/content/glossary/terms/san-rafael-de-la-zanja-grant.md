The San Rafael de la Zanja grant was issued May 15, 1825, to Ramón Romero — who had won the survey at auction for 1,200 pesos on October 5, 1821 — "and associated residents" of the Santa Cruz presidio: four square leagues, roughly 17,400 acres, held communally by somewhere between 18 and 30 parcioneros, conditioned on continuous occupation. It was a commons from the start, not a private estate, and it predates the Gadsden line by nearly thirty years. If the field-estimated age of the Lochiel cottonwoods holds, the grove itself germinated in the same decade the grant was issued — grant and grove as contemporaries, both older than the border that now runs through them.

Permanent Mexican occupation broke around 1843, after an Apache raid at La Boca de la Noria killed as many as thirty rancheros. The grant passed through American hands after the line arrived: Rollin Richardson bought it in 1880 and sold it in 1883 to the Cameron syndicate — the San Rafael Cattle Company, 300 shares sold at $500 apiece to Pennsylvania investors, organized by Colin and Brewster Cameron. What the Camerons bought on paper was four leagues. What they spent the next twenty years asserting was sixteen — a 152,899-acre claim against a confirmed 17,474. The documented tactics of that campaign include the 1885 Salcido house-burning prosecution (which the press read as land-grabbing), an arson and attempted-murder allegation from neighbor John Jameson, fence enclosures a federal judge ruled illegal in 1887, and Colin Cameron's own description of the squatters contesting him as "worse than Apache Indians" — the commons's former logic recast as a menace to be fenced out. The U.S. Supreme Court settled the acreage question (*United States v. Green*, 185 U.S. 256, decided 1902; Hadley & Sheridan date the decisive ruling 1900 — an unresolved source discrepancy pending the CPLC case file): Justice White is quoted as holding that "no duty rests on this government to recognize the validity of a grant to any area of greater extent than was recognized by the government of Mexico." Cameron kept ranching roughly nine times his confirmed acreage anyway, by cattle-as-occupation, until he sold to William Cornell Greene in 1902–03 for $1.5 million.

Hadley and Sheridan's 1995 land-use history draws the finding that gives this grant its place in the argument, in a section titled "The Ecological Legacy of Colin Cameron" [hadleysheridan1995]. Arizona's cattle numbers rose from 5,132 head in 1870 to an official 720,940 by 1891 — with contemporary stockmen privately estimating closer to 1.5 million, double the books — and the 1891–93 drought that followed killed 50 to 75 percent of herds territory-wide, triggering the arroyo-cutting that gutted the Santa Cruz watershed. The San Rafael Valley was substantially spared. Not because Cameron managed it gently, but because his illegal monopoly excluded the fragmented, competitive overstocking that destroyed neighboring ranges: a single owner asserting exclusive control over land he did not legally hold produced, as an unintended by-product, the conservative stocking that neighboring open-access ranges lacked. Hadley and Sheridan state the irony directly — "his actions may have kept the San Rafael Valley from becoming as degraded as other desert grassland ranges in southeastern Arizona" — and Greene and the Sharp family (owners from 1903 to 1999) continued that centralized management afterward.

This is the grant's double load in the knowledge base's line of argument. It is a case study in property-as-disposition: what the Camerons held was never a title matching their claim but a standing capacity to exclude, asserted past its legal warrant for two decades and made to stick by force, litigation, and cattle on the ground rather than by right. And it is the clearest instance in this corpus of monopoly-as-conservation — the intactness now being defended at Lochiel (grove, grassland, jaguar corridor) is downstream of concentrated, often illegal, settler landholding, not of any commons persisting. The 1825 commons the Camerons enclosed is gone; what survived into the San Rafael Ranch State Natural Area (established 1999) is the private monopoly's residue, inherited by whoever holds exclusion-power next — currently the wall.
