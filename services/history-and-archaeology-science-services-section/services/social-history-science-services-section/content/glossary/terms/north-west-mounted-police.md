The North-West Mounted Police was created by the Mounted Police Act, which the Canadian Parliament passed on May 23, 1873, authorizing an initial force of 150 men (soon raised to 300) under Prime Minister John A. Macdonald's stated mandate: "the preservation of peace and the prevention of crime" across the vast North-West Territories the Dominion had just acquired from the Hudson's Bay Company. Colonel George Arthur French served as its first Commissioner.

**Cypress Hills.** The immediate trigger was the Cypress Hills Massacre of June 1, 1873. A party of American wolf hunters and whiskey traders — including Thomas W. Hardwick and John Evans, operating out of Abel Farwell's trading post near Battle Creek in what is now southwestern Saskatchewan — had lost horses overnight and, after a night of drinking, accused an Assiniboine camp of some fifty lodges under the headman Little Soldier of the theft. The traders and a group of Métis freighters attacked the camp with repeating rifles from cover in a coulee; the Assiniboine, armed mostly with older muskets and bows, could not answer the fire. Estimates of the dead run from roughly 13 to around 30 Assiniboine men, women, and children (sources are not in agreement on an exact count, and this entry states the spread rather than a false precision); one wolfer, Ed Legrace, was killed on the other side. Alexander Morris, Lieutenant Governor of Manitoba, used the reports reaching Ottawa to press Macdonald for immediate action, warning that unpoliced American traders operating with impunity north of the border risked both further bloodshed and, not incidentally, American justification for annexing the territory outright. Recruitment, already authorized ten days before the massacre, was accelerated in its direct wake; by late September 1873 the government had commissioned officers for what would become the field force.

**The March West.** In summer 1874 the NWMP conducted its founding expedition: roughly 275 officers and men, with about 310 horses, well over 100 oxen and carts, and a trailing herd of cattle, marched some 800-900 miles west from Fort Dufferin, Manitoba, toward the Belly and Bow river country of what is now southern Alberta, aiming specifically at Fort Whoop-Up, the most notorious of the American whiskey-trading posts. The march itself was badly planned and brutal on men and animals alike — the historian William Baker's assessment, "a monumental fiasco of poor planning, ignorance, incompetence, and cruelty to men and beasts," is the sober read against the march's later romanticization as a founding epic. When the force finally reached Fort Whoop-Up in October 1874, guided in its final approach by the Métis-Kainai scout Jerry Potts, they found it empty of whiskey — the traders had already fled or dispersed — and the NWMP built Fort Macleod nearby as its first permanent post in the region.

**Whiskey suppression and the Sitting Bull years.** Over the following two years the force did substantially collapse the organized whiskey trade around Fort Macleod, working with local Kainai (Blood), Piikani, and Siksika leadership, including Crowfoot, whose cooperation was pragmatic as much as trusting. Superintendent James Morrow Walsh established Fort Walsh in the Cypress Hills in 1875, and it was Walsh — commanding a garrison that at its peak numbered only a few hundred men — who became the NWMP's point of contact when several thousand Lakota under Sitting Bull crossed the Medicine Line into Canadian territory beginning in late 1876 and through 1877, in the immediate wake of the [Battle of the Greasy Grass](./greasy-grass.md) and the U.S. campaign that followed it. Walsh built a genuine working relationship with Sitting Bull, met the Lakota's material needs as the NWMP's limited resources allowed, and — remarkably, given the numeric imbalance, with Lakota outnumbering his force many times over — kept the peace without a shot fired on Canadian soil for the better part of five years. The Canadian government's own position toward the refugees was consistently narrower than Walsh's conduct on the ground: Ottawa never recognized the Lakota as entitled to a reserve or to treaty status, repeatedly pressed Walsh to persuade Sitting Bull to return south, and by 1880 had transferred Walsh away from the post — Macdonald having come to suspect him "lukewarm" in that persuasion — keeping him on leave in Ontario until after Sitting Bull's departure. What ended the Lakota's stay was not Canadian coercion in any direct sense but the collapse of the buffalo herds on the Canadian plains, exhausted by overhunting on both sides of the line; faced with starvation and denied a reserve of their own, Sitting Bull led his remaining followers back across the border to surrender at Fort Buford, Dakota Territory, on July 19, 1881.

**What the force actually was.** The NWMP combined policing, military, and judicial functions in one centrally directed body answerable to Ottawa — a structure historians have variously called "benevolent despotism" (Ronald Atkin) and "legal tyranny" (John Jennings), both descriptions gesturing at the same reality: a force that applied Canadian law to Indigenous nations with genuine rigor against white settlers who broke it (which built the "fair Mountie" reputation) while extending no comparable deference to Indigenous nations' own law, sovereignty, or land claims. The NWMP negotiated the presence for and helped enforce Treaty 7 with the Blackfoot Confederacy in 1877, trading a reserve system and annuities for the surrender of the plains. After the 1885 North-West Resistance, the force became the enforcement arm of the pass system: an administrative regime with no basis in the Indian Act or any statute, under which Indigenous people leaving a reserve without written permission from an Indian agent could be detained and returned by NWMP officers under vague vagrancy pretexts — a restriction on movement historian F. Laurie Barron and others have documented as illegal even by the government's own later admission, in force informally for decades. By the time the force was renamed the Royal North-West Mounted Police in 1904 and absorbed into the Royal Canadian Mounted Police in 1920, it had been the primary instrument through which the Dominion converted treaty promises into reserve confinement.

This is the record against which this corpus's frame should be tested rather than assumed: that the difference between the NWMP and the U.S. cavalry was procedural, not structural. The record supports it, with the qualification built in rather than smoothed over. Procedurally, the two forces were genuinely different institutions doing genuinely different things at the Medicine Line in the 1870s: the U.S. Army pursued declared total war against mobile winter encampments, while the NWMP — a few hundred men, centrally disciplined, empowered to arrest whiskey traders and apply law evenly enough to earn Crowfoot's and even, for a time, Sitting Bull's qualified trust — kept a genuine, comparatively bloodless peace on its side of the line through the very years the U.S. Army was burning villages on the other. That is a real and not a cosmetic difference, and Walsh's individual conduct at Wood Mountain and Fort Walsh does not reduce to cynicism. But the structure both forces served converged on the same outcome by different procedures. Canada never recognized the Lakota's presence as anything but a temporary, unwelcome problem to be managed until it resolved itself; it extended no reserve, no treaty, no permanent standing — only Walsh's personal decency and an official policy of quiet pressure to leave, which succeeded once the buffalo did what Ottawa would not directly compel. Read against the treaty confinement the NWMP itself built and then policed with the pass system beginning in 1885, the force's founding mission and its later career sit on the same line as the U.S. cavalry's: the [exclusion-power](./exclusion-powers.md) to determine who may move, settle, hunt, or remain was held by the settler state in both countries, and the NWMP's comparative gentleness in the Sitting Bull years reads less as a different destination than as a more patient, better-administered route to it — the orderly filing clerk's version of the same [settlerism-as-filing-system](./settlerism-as-filing-system.md) the cavalry ran by force. The Medicine Line stopped bullets from a pursuing army for five years; it never stopped the settler-colonial claim on the land the Lakota were sheltering on, and the force built to police that line, however fairly it enforced Canadian law among white traders, was never positioned nor willing to answer that claim in the Lakota's favor.
