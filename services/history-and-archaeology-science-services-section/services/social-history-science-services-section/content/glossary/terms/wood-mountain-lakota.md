The Wood Mountain Lakota First Nation is the only registered Lakota (Teton Sioux) government in Canada, holding a reserve — Wood Mountain 160, some 2,376 hectares of prairie near Wood Mountain, Saskatchewan, close to present-day Grasslands National Park — that exists because thirty-seven founding families chose to stay when the rest of their nation went home. That choice was made in 1881, when [Sitting Bull's exile in Grandmother's Land](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-the-exile-in-grandmothers-land.md) ended: starved out by the buffalo collapse and Canada's refusal of a reserve, Sitting Bull led most of the remaining Hunkpapa and allied bands back across the line to surrender at Fort Buford, but a remnant — accompanying earlier arrivals from the Lakota bands that had crossed into the Wood Mountain and Cypress Hills country from 1876 onward — did not go. They stayed on land they had no treaty for, in a country that had spent four years refusing to recognize them, because the alternative was a U.S. Army that had just finished a war against them.

What followed was three decades of exactly the exclusion Ottawa had signaled all along it would apply: nothing. **Attested-secondary** (Utley; the University of Saskatchewan's *Indigenous Saskatchewan Encyclopedia* entry on the community): the families supported themselves through subsistence hunting, gathering, and wage labour on surrounding ranches and farms, unrecognized as a band and outside the treaty system that, however inadequately, structured the Canadian government's obligations to every other Indigenous nation in the territory. A temporary reserve was not created until **October 29, 1910** — twenty-nine years after the families arrived — and even that remained provisional for another two decades: formal recognition came only by Order-in-Council on **August 5, 1930**, fifty-three years after Sitting Bull's own band first crossed the border. The Wood Mountain Lakota have never signed a numbered treaty; Canadian federal administration nonetheless lists the band, per Wikipedia's synthesis of the government's own First Nation Profile record, under Treaty 4 — a filing convenience attached to a nation that was never a party to it. The band today (registered Band No. 388, under the File Hills Qu'Appelle Tribal Council, headquartered in Assiniboia) reports a registered membership in the low hundreds — figures located for this note range from 209 to 355 depending on year and source, with only a handful of members (single digits to roughly a dozen, again depending on the year of the count) resident on the reserve itself and the large majority living off-reserve, a demographic pattern the *Indigenous Saskatchewan Encyclopedia* entry attributes in part to marriage into the surrounding non-Indigenous community over five generations. These population figures are reported here as **attested-secondary** and should not be treated as current-year precise; band membership rolls change, and no figure newer than a 2022 aggregate was located for this note.

Wood Mountain's relatives did not vanish south of the line in 1881 — they went to Standing Rock, to the agency Sitting Bull himself was eventually confined to and killed near in 1890, and the two communities descend from the same bands that camped together at Wood Mountain before the exile split them. The Lakota nation the Medicine Line divided in 1881 is, in this specific and documented sense, still divided by it: one recognized nation with a small reserve and a 1930 Order-in-Council on the Canadian side: the border's paperwork long since caught up with a fact on the ground it took thirty years to write down, still short of treaty; several recognized nations, including Standing Rock, on the American side, holding the larger population and the deeper documentary record because the U.S. government, unlike Canada's, had a coercive apparatus already built to receive returning Lakota rather than a policy of simply waiting them out.

For this corpus, Wood Mountain is the Medicine Line's own version of the pattern the [Tohono O'odham Nation](./tohono-oodham-nation.md) demonstrates on the Gadsden line: a single nation, split by a boundary that arrived after the nation itself did, now administratively legible as two separate polities to two separate settler states, each of which reached its present recognition of the people on its side of the line on its own schedule and by its own logic, decades apart, with no coordination between them and no restored channel connecting the halves. Where the O'odham's split runs through 1854's cession and the 2026 wall's live construction, Wood Mountain's runs through an 1881 exile and a 1930 Order-in-Council that a filing system took half a century to issue — proof that [settlerism-as-filing-system](./settlerism-as-filing-system.md) does not require an active bulldozer to do its work. Thirty years of simply not filing the Wood Mountain Lakota as anything at all was itself the operative [exclusion-power](./exclusion-powers.md): no reserve, no treaty, no standing to complain, and a border between the family that stayed and the family that went home that neither Ottawa nor Washington built, and that neither has ever proposed to unbuild.
