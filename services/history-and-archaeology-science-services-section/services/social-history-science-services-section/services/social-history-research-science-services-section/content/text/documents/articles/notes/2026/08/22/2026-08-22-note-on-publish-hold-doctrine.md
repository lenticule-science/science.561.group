A research note on a live direct action is not written for one audience. It is written for the historian who wants a clean timeline, and it is written — whether intended or not — for the prosecutor building a case, the agency writing an incident report, and the opposing contractor's counsel preparing discovery. Those readers do not need to be invited; a sufficiently organized public document invites them by existing. The publish/hold doctrine is this service's answer to that fact: it does not soften the writing, it sorts the writing — some categories of information get documented as exhaustively as the sources allow, and one category does not get documented in the public record at all, regardless of how well-sourced it might be.

Document the state and its contractors without restraint. Contract awards, modifications, and their dollar figures; the waivers that set aside statutory review and the specific laws named in each notice; the promises officials make in writing — a CBP letter committing to a nesting-bird check before felling is exactly the kind of document this doctrine exists to preserve, because its existence is what makes the promise's breach legible later; court filings, dockets, and rulings; the corporate history of the contractors themselves, including prior federal suits against their work. None of this requires anonymization. It is the opposite of movement information: it is the record the state and its contractors would produce about themselves if compelled to, assembled before compulsion is needed. A dossier this complete is itself a form of accountability infrastructure, and building it is squarely inside the mission.

Archive movement ephemera the same way, for a different reason: not to expose it, but to keep it from disappearing. Published dispatches, banner photographs, press statements already released by organizers or by outlets like the ones cited in the companion research note — these are already public, and the internet is bad at keeping public things public. Independent media disappears, social posts get deleted or platform-purged, a photo hosted on one server today is a dead link in eighteen months. Archiving what has already been voluntarily published, with capture dates and stable local copies, is custodial work: it protects the movement's own record of itself from rot, without adding anything to that record that the movement did not choose to say.

What this service does not do, ever, in the public-facing record: document movement logistics — supply chains, rotation schedules, entry and exit routes, communication methods; document identities — names, descriptions, vehicle plates, home regions, anything that narrows who was present when withholding that information was itself the documented practice; or reconstruct operational detail from fragments (a banner's font matched to a printer, a background matched to a specific camp layout) in the name of thoroughness. Thoroughness is not a neutral virtue here. At Lochiel, participants have withheld their names citing domestic-terrorism-charge fears — a fear this note's evidence base treats as founded, not paranoid, citing the charging strategy used after Weelaunee/Stop Cop City as the precedent organizers themselves invoke. A research service that reconstructs what its subjects have deliberately concealed is not neutral fact-finding; it is doing enforcement's work for it, for free, under the banner of scholarship.

The theoretical ground for this split is the legibility critique this repository already carries. [Legibility](../../../../../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/black-philosophy-services-section/services/abolition-services-section/services/state-repression-services-section/content/glossary/terms/legibility.md), after Scott, names the state's general project of making informal, relational practice into standardized, administrable fact. The companion essay on citing for containment sharpens the point for exactly this genre of writing: a clean, well-cited, coherent account of a movement does not just describe the movement to outsiders, it becomes infrastructure that institutions — including carceral ones — can build on. A clear sentence can be quoted, co-opted, and neutralized in ways an unclear one cannot; the same organizational discipline that makes a research note useful to future historians makes it useful to a grand jury. The doctrine is not a claim that documentation is bad. It is a claim that documentation has a target, and the target has to be chosen on purpose — the state and its contractors, who owe the public an account of what they did with public money and public land, not the people resisting them, who owe the public nothing and are owed protection instead.

Oral history sits at the doctrine's most sensitive edge and gets its own rule: it is collected only under explicit consent and an agreed security protocol — what can be quoted, what can be paraphrased without identifying detail, what stays off the record entirely, and who reviews the final text before it is archived, if anyone is named or namable at all. Default to pseudonym or role-description ("a resident who blocked the machinery," not a name already in the public record only because an arrest report made it so) unless the source has affirmatively asked to be named and understands what that means downstream. Consent here is not a one-time checkbox; it is revisitable, because a security situation that looks stable in August 2026 may not still look stable when a case moves through court in 2028.

The doctrine's practical output, at Lochiel specifically: the contract awards, the waiver notice and its 34 laws, the June 18 CBP letter, and the Aug 13–14 O'odham ruling belong in the permanent record with full citation. The camp's rotation system, its resupply method, and its participants' names do not belong in this service's record at all — not redacted, not vaguely gestured at, simply absent, on the same principle that makes the state's promises present.
