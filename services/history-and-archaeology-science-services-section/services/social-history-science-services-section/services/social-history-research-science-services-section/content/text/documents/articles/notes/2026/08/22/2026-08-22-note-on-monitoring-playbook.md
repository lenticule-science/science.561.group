The Lochiel research is not a closed file; construction, litigation, and the tree-sit are all ongoing as of the evidence layer's Aug 19, 2026 cutoff, and a note that stops updating quietly becomes a note that misleads. The monitoring playbook is the standing-watch method that keeps it current: a short list of primary-source systems checked on a schedule, plus a working target list for records the public systems do not surface on their own.

USASpending.gov is the anchor for the money. The two live awards for this project — 70B01C25F00000328 ($309.46M, the ~27-mile San Rafael double wall, June 2025) and 70B01C26F00000405 ($1.495B, "Tucson 2," ~19 miles of primary/secondary wall plus 136 miles of detection technology, Dec 2025) — get checked periodically for modifications: change orders, obligated-versus-awarded drift, extensions, or a new award appearing under the same solicitation family. A contract modification is frequently the first public signal that a construction timeline, scope, or contractor relationship has changed, well before it shows up in press coverage.

The Federal Register is the anchor for the legal authority behind the project: DHS Secretary Noem's waiver notice under §102 of the REAL ID Act, which set aside 34 federal laws for this segment, is the kind of instrument that can be amended, superseded, or joined by a further waiver notice for an adjacent segment without much notice elsewhere. A standing watch on Federal Register filings citing §102 waiver authority for the Tucson sector is how a second such notice would be caught early rather than discovered after the fact in secondary reporting.

Court dockets are tracked directly rather than through news summaries of them, on two live fronts: the Tohono O'odham Nation's case, where Judge Richard Leon's Aug 13–14, 2026 denial of injunctive relief is not the end of the matter while an appeal remains under consideration and unfiled as of the evidence cutoff; and the Center for Biological Diversity's constitutional challenge to the waiver-delegation power itself, which reached summary judgment for the government in March 2026 but could still see an appeal. Docket-level tracking catches filing dates, judge assignments, and the actual text of rulings — details that shift or get simplified in the retelling.

Humane Borders' mapped-death data and the Pima County Office of the Medical Examiner's recovery counts are the corridor's mortality baseline, checked against the evidence layer's July 2026 figure (19 bodies recovered, the deadliest month in two years, against historically low apprehensions) to see whether the deterrence-funnel pattern — deaths decoupled from crossing volume — is holding, worsening, or reversing as construction and monsoon conditions change.

Four FOIA targets sit on the working list because no public system surfaces them automatically. First, CBP's own nesting-bird survey records for the felled cottonwoods and the text of its June 18, 2026 letter promising no felling during active nesting — the documents that would resolve the still-open dispute between CBP's "no active nests" position and Sierra Club's day-before video. Second, the clearing width actually authorized and used under the Roosevelt Reservation strip at this segment, since the Nation's case and the general 150-ft swath figure both turn on exactly how that authority is being read on the ground. Third, records of the Lochiel port of entry's 1983 closure, since the evidence layer found no official closure record and had to rely on a Nogales International retrospective and a dated photograph — a records request to NARA's Record Group 36 (Records of the U.S. Customs Service) is the next step toward a primary document rather than a secondary account of one. Fourth, and unresolved as an open question rather than a filed request yet: whether any cuttings were taken from the three felled cottonwoods before or during removal for propagation or eventual replanting, a practice with precedent in decentralized native-plant nursery work documented elsewhere in this corpus's movement-history material — worth asking Sierra Club, CBD, or the Madrean Archipelago Wildlife Center directly before assuming the answer is no.

Refresh cadence: the fast-moving items — tree-sit status, court filings, contract modifications — get checked roughly every one to two weeks while the situation remains active, tightening to daily around known decision points (a ruling, an appeal deadline, a contract milestone) and loosening once a front goes quiet. The slower items — ethnographic sourcing, chain-of-title research, the monument-error history — do not need a cadence at all; they get revisited when a specific new source surfaces, not on a schedule.

The method's worked example is the contract count itself. Coverage of the San Rafael project cites three dollar figures — $309.46M, $334M, and $1.495B — and a naive read could take that as evidence of three contracts, or grow suspicious of the discrepancy. Going to USASpending directly resolves it in minutes: two awards exist, 70B01C25F00000328 and 70B01C26F00000405, and the $334M seen in some coverage is a rounding variant of the first award's figure, not a third contract. Settler accounting has a documented habit, elsewhere in this same corpus, of inflating the numbers that justify power and losing the numbers that indict it — the cattle count that ran at half of reality in 1891, Operation Wetback's inflated removal figures. A discrepancy in press coverage of contract dollars is not automatically that pattern, but it cannot be told apart from it without going to the primary document — which is the entire argument for this playbook over trusting coverage.
