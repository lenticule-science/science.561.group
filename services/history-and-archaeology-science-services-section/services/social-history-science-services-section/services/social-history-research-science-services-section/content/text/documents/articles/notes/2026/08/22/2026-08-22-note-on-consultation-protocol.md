This service works across an asymmetry it does not get to opt out of: emsenn writes as a Lakota researcher, but most of the ground this corpus covers — the San Rafael Valley, the O'odham and Apache history of the borderlands — is not Lakota ground, and Lakota positionality is not a passkey into O'odham or Yaqui knowledge. The consultation protocol exists to keep that distinction operative in practice rather than collapsing it into a general "Indigenous perspective" that would flatten specific peoples into an interchangeable category — the same flattening move critiqued elsewhere in this corpus as legibility's own method.

The first stop, on any question that touches a specific nation's land, history, or practice, is that nation's tribal cultural-resource office or its designated historic preservation officer — not the last resort after published sources run out, but the first call, made before the note is drafted, so their answer can shape the research rather than correct it after the fact. Where an office declines to engage, does not respond, or states that a matter is not for outside discussion, that is itself the answer, and the note says so rather than filling the gap with a published source of lower authority than a "no."

Attribution runs to named sources, never to generic "belief." "The Tohono O'odham believe" or "in Lakota tradition" launders a specific person's statement, made in a specific context, into an ambient cultural fact nobody can check and everybody can misuse. The evidence layer for this corpus's first project practices the alternative throughout: Verlon Jose is quoted by name and date ("Over my dead body will a wall be built," January 2017), not paraphrased into "O'odham leaders believe"; the Nation's institutional film carries its own title as a documented claim, not a folk saying. When a claim about sacredness or practice cannot be traced to a named person, office, or publication — as with the tree-sit activists' assertion that the Lochiel cottonwoods are sacred to the Tohono O'odham and Yaqui, for which the evidence layer found no tribal statement — the note records the claim as an assertion by its actual source and flags the absence explicitly, rather than upgrading it to fact by repetition.

Long-published ethnography is usable, but not for free. Joseph Epes Brown's *The Sacred Pipe* (1953) and John Neihardt's *Black Elk Speaks* (1932) are this service's citable sources for Sun Dance practice because no closer source is publicly available, and both are used with their extraction history stated on the page: two non-Native interlocutors recorded and shaped Black Elk's account for non-Native readerships, and Raymond DeMallie's *The Sixth Grandfather* (1984) — publishing the original stenographic transcripts — documents how far Neihardt's editing moved from what was actually said. Citing Brown and Neihardt without that caveat would let a mediated, decades-old text stand in for a living religion's own account of itself. Larry Evers and Felipe Molina's *Yaqui Deer Songs / Maso Bwikam* (1987) is held up in this protocol as the better model precisely because Molina, a Yaqui deer singer, is co-author rather than subject — the difference between knowledge recorded about someone and knowledge published with them, credited and consenting.

Where consultation happens, compensation and editorial review are offered as standard practice, not contingent on a source asking for them — payment for time, and the right to review how their words and their nation's history appear before publication, including the right to request changes or withdrawal. This is not owed because the relationship is transactional; it is owed because the alternative default, in which universities and independent researchers alike have long treated Indigenous knowledge-holders' time as free and their review as optional, is exactly the extraction pattern this service is trying not to reproduce.

Living ceremony is treated as living, not as archival material. The Sun Dance survived a federal ban enforced by ration withholding (1883–1934) and remained without secure statutory protection until AIRFA in 1978; it is practiced now, by living people, under conditions this corpus is not positioned to describe from the inside. Where the evidence layer discusses Sun Dance practice, it does so through named, dated, published sources about historical practice — never as a claim to describe what is done today, and never treating a 1953 transcription as a complete or current account of a tradition that has kept moving since. The same rule extends to O'odham and Apache practice at Lochiel: what the evidence layer knows, it knows from public statements and named ethnography; what it does not know, it states as unknown, and leaves for the offices and the people whose knowledge it actually is.
