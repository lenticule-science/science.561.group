# From treaty noncession to statutory taking: documentary sequences concerning the Black Hills, 1868–1980

**Lenticule Science · History and Archaeology · Working paper · October 12, 2026**

## Abstract

This paper analyzes a bounded sequence of United States documentary and legal acts affecting the Black Hills, Ȟe Sápa, from the 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty through the 1980 Supreme Court decision in *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians*. The immediate question is how a treaty rule limiting the validity of cession came to coexist with federal actions that enabled mining, conditioned subsistence appropriations on relinquishment, enacted a statutory taking, and later recognized a compensable injury without returning land. Primary instruments and legal judgments are distinguished from subsequent historical interpretations and the present positions of Tribal nations. Article XII of the 1868 treaty required the signatures of at least three-fourths of interested adult men for a valid treaty cession; the Supreme Court reports that the 1876 agreement obtained signatures from about one-tenth. The Act of August 15, 1876 made future subsistence appropriations conditional on cession and other compliance, and the Act of February 28, 1877 carried the purported agreement into law. In 1980 the Supreme Court affirmed that the 1877 Act effected a taking requiring just compensation. The evidence supports a sequence of coercive federal policy decisions and subsequent legal conversion of land into a compensable taking. It does **not**, by itself, establish a general causal law linking all mineral extraction to debt finance or demonstrate that a monetary remedy constituted consent. Contemporary 2026 federal-land return efforts and the withdrawal of a Pe' Sla drilling plan demonstrate that the issue remains active, but are not retrospective proof of any single nineteenth-century motive. This is a working documentary analysis requiring additional Tribal archival perspectives and expert review before submission.

**Keywords:** Black Hills; Ȟe Sápa; Fort Laramie Treaty; 1876 appropriations; 1877 Act; treaty cession; Sioux Nation; historical jurisdiction; land return; legal remedy.

## 1. Research question and source position

**Question.** By what identifiable documentary and institutional steps did U.S. policy move from a reservation guarantee and a specified consent condition to federal possession and monetary compensation concerning the Black Hills?

The inquiry is **not** whether the Black Hills were "really worth" the financial award. That would make a market valuation the premise of a dispute about the authority to turn land into exchangeable property. The documentary question instead examines successive legal forms and their stated conditions.

The document sets a strict attribution boundary. U.S. government treaties, executive correspondence, statutes and court decisions are primary evidence **of U.S. actions, assertions and legal reasoning**. They do not, by themselves, state Lakota, Dakota, Nakota, or other affected peoples' histories, obligations, consensus or sovereign legal interpretations. Tribal statements and community records are separate sources with their own authority and dates. The term "Sioux Nation" appears in treaty and case names; it is not assumed to replace the relevant nations' self-description.

## 2. Source-selection and comparison method

The initial corpus contains five source groups:

1. **Treaty:** the [National Archives transcript of the Fort Laramie Treaty of April 29, 1868](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/fort-laramie-treaty), especially Articles II, XI, XII and XVI. Its transcript is read for the treaty's express provisions, not presumed oral understanding.
2. **Federal policy statement:** the contemporaneous [1875 Report of the Secretary of the Interior](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Report_of_the_Secretary_of_the_Interior/1875), retaining the need to verify its transcription against an original government printing. That report suggested making future subsistence appropriations conditional upon relinquishment of the Black Hills and rights of way. For the administration's position, the [1876 President's annual message preserved by the U.S. Office of the Historian](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1876/message-of-the-president) is a primary statement rather than proof of the speaker's motives.
3. **Statutes and litigation:** the August 15, 1876 appropriations rider and February 28, 1877 Act as reproduced and judicially discussed in [*United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians*, 448 U.S. 371 (1980)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/448/371), with [official reporter metadata](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/USREPORTS-448/USREPORTS-448-371/context); the earlier [Court of Claims opinion, 601 F.2d 1157 (1979)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/ct-cl/1979/601-f2d-1157.html) is an additional account of documentary evidence and its judicial evaluation. The 1980 case is a primary court judgment, but the court's historical narration remains a judicial reconstruction.
4. **Historical secondary account:** the [National Park Service analysis of Grant's Indian policy](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/president-ulysses-s-grant-and-federal-indian-policy.htm). It supplies an independently articulated chronology, including November 3, 1875 and the end of efforts to exclude miners.
5. **Contemporary scope checks:** the [June 9, 2026 LANDBACK/NDN statement on Tribal resolutions](https://landback.org/2026/06/all-nine-south-dakota-tribes-pass-resolutions-supporting-developing-legislation-to-return-federal-lands-lakota-nation-united-in-effort-for-black-hills-federal-lands-return/) and [June 10 KOTA reporting](https://www.kotatv.com/2026/06/10/black-hills-are-not-sale-south-dakota-tribes-unite-behind-federal-land-return-proposal/); the [May 8, 2026 NDN report that Pete Lien & Sons withdrew its Pe' Sla exploratory drilling plan](https://ndncollective.org/breaking-pesla-protectors-win-drilling-operation-plan-withdrawn-community-organizing-ceremony-lawsuit-occupation-of-site-leads-to-victory/) and contemporaneous [Associated Press reporting](https://apnews.com/article/bfae28435544f44b40d45a443f7bc4d7).

**Comparison rule.** Record what each instrument said or did; then record how the next federal actor described or changed that condition. Do not treat a later judgment as if it were an earlier actor's mental state. Separate evidence of an act, an explanation offered by the actor, an inferred mechanism, and a normative assessment. Dates are event dates when demonstrable, not dates of our present access.

This is **documentary analysis**; no interview, ethnographic participation, statistical causal inference, or exhaustive archival search has been conducted.

## 3. Treaty conditions precede mineral discovery

**Article II** set aside a described district, including the Black Hills, for the people named in the treaty and restricted unauthorized non-Indian passage, settlement or residence. **Article XII** declared that a treaty for cession of commonly held reservation territory would have no validity or force against the signatories unless signed by at least three-fourths of the adult male Indians occupying or interested in it. **Article XI** reserved qualified hunting rights beyond the defined reservation, while imposing other constraints concerning roads, railroads and conflict. **Article XVI** separately addressed unceded territory.

These provisions are not interchangeable. It would be an error to claim that Article XII required a three-fourths vote of all Indigenous people, of all people living in the present nine South Dakota Tribes, or of modern Tribal governments: it was a specific nineteenth-century treaty rule with an exclusionary adult-male formulation. It would also be an error to read Article XI's hunting provisions as one unlimited right in all places and times.

The significance is a **documented constraint known to the United States before the 1874 Black Hills gold reports**. A mineral discovery did not alter the words of Article XII. A later federal law could assert domestic legal control but is not the same event as obtaining the treaty signatures required for consensual cession.

## 4. Illegal entry, selective enforcement and the 1875 purchase failure

The 1874 military expedition and subsequent gold reports intensified trespass into treaty land. The National Park Service recounts attempts to remove prospectors, an unsuccessful federal proposal to buy the Hills, President Grant's November 3, 1875 strategy meeting, and the Army's cessation of serious efforts to bar miners thereafter.

A more contemporaneous and revealing source is Grant's December 1876 annual message. Grant acknowledged that white miners had violated treaty stipulations while seeking gold and that the government initially removed intruders, but defended its failure to continue exclusion with the claim that enough miners entered that effective removal would induce military desertion. This records the **administration's justification**. It does not independently establish that exclusion was impossible, nor prove that the executive decision was forced by objective economic necessity.

This distinction matters: *trespass existed* and *the government chose a particular enforcement policy* are jointly documented; *there was no alternative* is not thereby demonstrated. The relationship between miners' organized demands, state enforcement and subsequent legal title is historically testable rather than a timeless feature of every frontier.

## 5. Subsistence appropriations became conditions on relinquishment

The Secretary of the Interior's 1875 report suggested that future supplies could be made contingent upon surrender of the goldfields and access rights. It described a fiscal comparison between annual subsistence payments and a hypothetical capital sum, but that was **the Secretary's policy argument**, not proof that subsistence was gratuitous or that treaty relations had a market price.

On **August 15, 1876**, Congress enacted an appropriations provision saying that future subsistence appropriations would depend on surrender of off-reservation hunting rights and the Black Hills and on an arrangement said to promote self-support. The Court of Claims' 1979 review describes the commission's ensuing negotiations as structured by that congressional condition. The Supreme Court's 1980 judgment recites the same linkage.

The record therefore supports a narrow and serious conclusion: U.S. federal policy **conditioned a means of subsistence upon assent to relinquishment**. This is observable in the language of a policy proposal, enacted appropriation and subsequent commission process. It should not be euphemized as a free-market exchange. Whether particular leaders experienced the threat in specific ways must be reconstructed from their own testimony and archives as well as court descriptions; it should not be invented from the federal record alone.

## 6. Treaty cession and statutory taking were different acts

The 1876 commission's purported agreement received signatures from only a minority: the Supreme Court's 1980 syllabus puts the number at about **10 percent of the adult male Sioux population**, not the three-fourths specified by Article XII.

On **February 28, 1877**, Congress enacted legislation implementing the agreement and terminating prior treaty protections for the lands and rights at issue. That statute changed the law the federal state applied; it did not retroactively create the consent specified by the treaty. The Supreme Court later analyzed the statute as a taking under federal constitutional law.

Three statuses must be separated:

- **Treaty-consensual cession:** a mode of relinquishment subject to Article XII's condition; the 1876 signatures did not satisfy it.
- **Unilateral federal enactment:** Congress's 1877 exercise of power, which enabled federal possession and new arrangements even without the treaty's consent threshold.
- **Later judicial remedy:** the 1980 constitutional judgment that the taking required compensation.

No transition between these can be silently described as a voluntary sale, and the fact that a remedy is calculated in money does not prove a people authorized the earlier taking or accepted money as restoration.

## 7. The 1980 judgment recognized a taking, not a land return

The Supreme Court decided *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians*, **448 U.S. 371**, on **June 30, 1980**. It affirmed the Court of Claims' conclusion that the 1877 Act effected a taking of tribal property requiring just compensation, including interest. The opinion addressed the prior claims litigation and congressional restoration of judicial review.

The decision is evidence that **the federal judiciary recognized liability in constitutional compensation terms**. It did not hold that the United States had achieved the treaty signatures; it also did not issue a judgment transferring the Black Hills back. The distinction between finding a compensable taking and providing land restitution is the precise legal difference under study.

This paper does not calculate today's accrued compensation or claim a uniform view of every Tribal government concerning remedies. The widely reported refusal to accept the offered monetary settlement is a separate political and sovereign claim that must be documented with specific nation/party/date records before quantitative or universal statements are made. A court's power to award does not itself determine the receiving community's view of justice.

## 8. Contemporary continuation: proposals and project withdrawals are not restitution

On **June 9–10, 2026**, reports from LANDBACK/NDN and KOTA stated that all nine South Dakota Tribal nations had supported developing legislation to return **federally held** lands in the Black Hills to the Great Sioux Nation / Oceti Sakowin. The proposal was **in early drafting**, excluded privately owned land, and expressly did **not** settle the larger 1868 treaty claims. Therefore "nine tribes supported developing land-return legislation" is warranted; **"Congress enacted the return" is not**.

On **May 8, 2026**, Pete Lien & Sons withdrew its plan for exploratory graphite drilling at Pe' Sla following legal and community opposition, according to its opponents' statement and AP reporting. A withdrawn plan is neither a permanent ban on all mining nor a decision restoring land title. It is evidence of particular action within an ongoing contested governance regime.

These present events should be dated and described in their own terms. They demonstrate that federal land management, extraction and land-return demands remain live political matters; they do not by themselves specify the causal motives of participants in 1874–1877.

## 9. An analytic sequence, not a deterministic historical law

For purposes of comparison, the documentary sequence can be represented as:

\[
T_{1868}\ \to\ E_{1874-75}\ \to\ A_{1876}\ \to\ L_{1877}\ \to\ C_{1980},
\]

where \(T\) is the treaty constraint, \(E\) the entry/enforcement conflict, \(A\) the subsistence-linked agreement process, \(L\) congressional enactment, and \(C\) constitutional compensation judgment.

The arrows denote **historical order and documented institutional relations**, *not* a fitted Markov transition, an inevitable process, or proof that all later acts flowed from only the preceding one. The most defensible mechanism in this corpus is an institutional change of **what counted as an actionable question**: preventing treaty-prohibited settlement → handling prospectors as a policy problem → conditioning subsistence on cession → treating purported consent as a statute → treating unconstitutional taking as a payable obligation. The resulting legal form did not restore the treaty's consent threshold or transfer land back.

This causal account must still be tested against countervailing evidence: decisions favoring treaty enforcement, intra-agency disagreement, Indigenous resistance and negotiations, fiscal alternatives, and sources that show the contingency of events. The sequential form is an instrument of analysis, not a substitute for that evidence.

## 10. Limits and next source tests

**Federal-source bias.** National Archives and court documents are strong records of federal institutions' asserted powers, but cannot alone provide a community-authoritative account of land, treaty or refusal. Nation-specific oral, written and archival materials require careful consent, citation and contextual interpretation; do not dissolve Lakota, Dakota, Nakota and allied histories into one account.

**Fiscal hypothesis untested.** The author's historical essay connects Black Hills gold to the Panic of 1873, railroad bonds, hard money and the Resumption Act of 1875. The broader context is plausible; **this paper has not identified a primary causal record showing that the Treasury's specie-resumption or public-debt objectives determined the policy to seize the Black Hills**. The Secretary's 1875 subsistence-cost argument provides an observable fiscal calculus, but not that larger causal chain. A separate archival economic-history study is required before advancing it as a finding.

**Scale and legal plurality.** The specific Fort Laramie provisions do not define the universal form of treaty sovereignty. Congressional authority, treaty rights, constitutional takings doctrine and nation-specific law are distinguishable legal frameworks and must not be flattened into a single legal theory.

**Current claims.** The amount and title status of any compensation fund and the procedural status of proposed 2026 return legislation require current documentary receiving; contemporary announcements alone do not prove enactment or extinguishment of claims.

**Next evidence acquisitions:** actual 1875 Interior report scan and Congressional Record pages for the 1876 rider; 1876 commission testimony and Tribal correspondences; comparison of 1877 Act text against the 1868 treaty; original 1980 judgment opinion; contemporary resolutions and any introduced bill text. Each source should be pinned and received by the commissioned Research Operations provider, not recopied into multiple thematic repositories.

## 11. Conclusion

Within the bounded federal corpus, the Black Hills sequence is not best described as a simple disputed purchase. A consent rule was specified in 1868; policy turned to withdrawal of exclusion, purchase efforts, subsistence leverage and statutory action; the judiciary later recognized a compensable taking. Those are separately documentable acts. Neither later law nor monetary remedy establishes that the treaty threshold was met or that restitution occurred. Stronger claims about Indigenous sovereign continuity or the fiscal causes of federal expansion require the additional sources and authority stated above.

**Scientific status:** substantive working documentary article; **not externally peer-reviewed or released**. The separate emsenn essay *The Black Hills Were Not for Sale* retains its authored voice, historical identity and own unpublished editorial state. Its rhetoric can motivate inquiry but is not a replacement for source criticism; this paper's analytical conclusions do not impose a literary style upon that essay.
