Library and information science does not offer one settled taxonomy of
all possible domain subdivisions. It offers several competing ways to
slice a field, each grounded in a different problem: retrieval,
indexing, disciplinary mapping, or sociological explanation. The
literature is clearer about families of distinctions than about a
single master schema [@hjorland2017classification; @hjorland2017domainanalysis].

The classical knowledge-organization question is how to divide a domain
so that items can be found and related. In that literature,
classification is broader than simple tree-building. Birger Hjorland
treats classification as the general activity of arranging knowledge by
similarity and difference, while also distinguishing it from
taxonomies, typologies, and subject schemes that serve different
purposes [@hjorland2017classification]. That matters for directory
design: a field can be divided by logical genus-species relations, by
pragmatic retrieval categories, or by analytically constructed types,
and these are not the same act.

Faceted classification was developed partly because single-hierarchy
systems break down when a subject can be described along several
independent dimensions. Ranganathan's analytico-synthetic method, as
encoded in Colon Classification, decomposes compound subjects into
facets and then recombines them. The best-known PMEST sequence -
personality, matter, energy, space, time - is not a taxonomy of
disciplines but a grammar for assembling subjects from multiple aspects
[@satija2019colonclassification]. Claudio Gnoli's account of facet
analysis makes the same point in more general form: facets express
orthogonal dimensions such as thing, process, property, place, and
time, allowing a domain to be navigated by aspect rather than by one
privileged tree [@gnoli2014facet].

Faceted theory therefore says something specific about subdivision:
domains are often better decomposed into dimensions than into branches.
A subdivision can track what is being studied, what is happening to it,
where it occurs, when it occurs, or in what form it appears. In a
repository context, that literature does not imply one correct folder
set, but it does show that "subject" and "facet" are different kinds of
organizing principle [@gnoli2014facet; @satija2019colonclassification].

Domain analysis adds a social layer. Hjorland argues that knowledge
organization should not treat subjects as purely neutral conceptual
objects; domains are formed by communities, institutions, discourse
traditions, and divisions of labor [@hjorland2017domainanalysis]. On
that view, a domain can be subdivided not only by what it studies but
also by who studies it, for what purposes, with what accepted
literature, and under what professional or disciplinary constraints.
This is closer to the distinction between disciplines and schools than
classical shelf classification is.

The contrast between disciplinary and phenomenon-centered systems is
especially clear in Integrative Levels Classification. Gnoli presents
ILC as an attempt to classify phenomena directly rather than mirror the
departmental structure of academia. The system treats perspective,
agent, transformation, place, time, and form as combinable dimensions,
which lets one represent the same phenomenon under different analytic
views [@gnoli2020ilc]. That is a different answer to the question of
how knowledge divides: not by institutional field, but by ontological
level and relational aspect.

Another question that emerges from the literature is not only how to
divide a domain, but what justifies the division. Warrant theory asks
what evidence licenses a class, term, or relation in a KOS. Mario
Barite's survey of literary warrant shows that modern KOS design has
expanded beyond simple document-counting into a family of warrants:
literary, user, cultural, academic, organizational, and related forms
[@barite2018literarywarrant]. This means that subdivisions can be
grounded not only in ontology or disciplinary logic, but in use,
community, institutional mission, and cultural accommodation.

This matters because knowledge-organization research also treats
classification as consequential rather than neutral. Bowker and Star
frame classification systems as part of the scaffolding of information
infrastructures and show that they shape social interaction as well as
retrieval [@bowker1999sorting]. In the same register, Barite's account
of cultural warrant stresses that schemes inherit the social and
historical conditions of their construction and may exclude other
perspectives unless they are deliberately revised
[@barite2018literarywarrant]. For repository design, this introduces a
fifth dimension of subdivision pressure alongside subject, facet,
community, and tradition: warrant. A field may divide according to what
its literature supports, what its users seek, or what an institution
chooses to recognize as legitimate.

The sociology of knowledge complicates things further by treating
classification itself as socially situated. Karl Mannheim's sociology
of knowledge ties styles of thought to social location and historical
situation, making intellectual differentiation partly a function of the
positions from which groups think [@mannheim1991ideology]. Berger and
Luckmann shift the emphasis from position to institutionalization:
shared activity becomes habitual, habits become institutions, and
institutions sediment typifications that make social worlds appear
objective [@berger1966socialconstruction]. From that perspective, a
knowledge domain is not only subdivided conceptually. It is also
subdivided by stabilized communities, routines, and legitimation
structures.

Taken together, these literatures describe at least four recurrent
dimensions of subdivision. One is subject or phenomenon: what the work
is about. Another is facet or aspect: process, material, place, time,
agent, or form. A third is social organization: discipline, specialty,
profession, or epistemic community. A fourth is tradition or school:
the historically transmitted style or framework through which the
domain is approached [@hjorland2017classification;
@hjorland2017domainanalysis; @berger1966socialconstruction].

What remains unsettled is whether these dimensions form a standard
taxonomy. The field does not converge on one. Faceted classification
tries to replace fixed trees with combinable aspects. Domain analysis
foregrounds communities and purposes. Sociology of knowledge explains
why classifications stabilize without treating them as neutral mirrors
of reality. For a repository that needs directory names, the literature
therefore supplies a landscape of subdivision types rather than a
single canonical list.
