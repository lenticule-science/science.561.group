Process philosophy does not reject classification. It rejects the idea
that classification must begin from self-subsistent substances.
Process-oriented thinkers instead treat entities as stabilized outcomes
of activities, events, or relations. In that setting, categories are
ways of tracking patterns of becoming, not merely bins for finished
things [@processPhilosophyIep; @rescherProcess].

The Whiteheadian line is the clearest expression of this shift. As
Johanna Seibt summarizes, process philosophy in the Whitehead tradition
replaces substance-first metaphysics with an ontology of occurrence,
activity, and event [@processPhilosophyIep]. What appears as a stable
object is a relatively enduring pattern in a processual world. Read in
classificatory terms, this pushes categories away from static
essences and toward recurrent modes of relation, transformation, and
organization.

Nicholas Rescher develops the same point in a more explicitly
systematic register. His process metaphysics treats processes as
ontologically basic and understands things as complexes or nexuses of
processual activity [@rescherProcess]. Classification in that frame is
not abolished, but reoriented. One classifies not only by what a thing
is, but by what it does, how it persists, what changes it undergoes,
and what role it plays in larger developmental patterns.

Isabelle Stengers radicalizes Whitehead in a methodological direction.
Her account of "thinking with Whitehead" presents concepts as created
under pressure from experience rather than imposed as final containers
[@stengersWhitehead]. That is relevant to directory design because it
frames categorization as an ongoing experimental practice. Categories
should be judged by what they let a system notice, connect, and
transform, not only by whether they mirror a pre-given ontology.

Peircean semiotics offers a compatible but distinct processual schema.
Peirce's categories of Firstness, Secondness, and Thirdness distinguish
quality or possibility, brute encounter or reaction, and mediation,
habit, or law [@peirceIep]. In semiotic terms, meaning arises through
triadic semiosis rather than dyadic matching between word and object.
This matters for repository organization because a triad can describe
classification as movement among possible qualities, actual relations,
and stabilized mediations. A trinity of folder functions can therefore
be interpreted processually without pretending that Peirce dictates one
specific directory layout.

Process-oriented classification has analogues in information science.
Integrative Levels Classification was designed to classify phenomena
directly and to combine them with facets such as transformation, agent,
time, place, and perspective [@gnoli2020ilc]. That is process-friendly
in two ways. First, it does not privilege academic departments as the
basic ontology. Second, it treats processual relations and changes as
classifiable aspects rather than as secondary annotations.

Basic Concepts Classification pushes further toward relational syntax.
Its designers present it as phenomenon-centered and hospitable to
sentence-like combinations among things, processes, properties, and
relations [@szostak2019bcc]. In practical terms, BCC does not force a
repository to choose between nouns and verbs at the top level. It shows
that a classification language can represent both and can often become
more expressive when relations and activities are first-class.

This literature does not imply that every classification should be
verbalized as a gerund. Process thinkers still use nouns, kinds, and
types. What changes is the metaphysical interpretation of those nouns.
They denote relatively stable achievements within ongoing processes, not
independent substances outside relation [@processPhilosophyIep;
@rescherProcess]. Information-science analogues likewise remain
classificatory systems, not anti-taxonomies.

The field is also unsettled. Whiteheadian, Rescherian, and Peircean
traditions are compatible at a high level, but they do not offer the
same primitives. Whitehead emphasizes actual occasions and creativity.
Rescher emphasizes systematic process primacy. Peirce emphasizes
mediation, sign action, and habit. Knowledge-organization systems such
as ILC and BCC translate some of these intuitions into practice, but
they do so for retrieval and synthesis rather than for metaphysical
purity [@peirceIep; @gnoli2020ilc; @szostak2019bcc].

For the repository question, the main result is therefore negative and
positive at once. Negative: process philosophy does not hand down a
finished set of directory names. Positive: it does supply a disciplined
way to understand categorization itself as an activity of relating,
stabilizing, and transforming. That makes processive folder names
intellectually legible within an established philosophical landscape,
while leaving the actual classification design open.
