Second-pass reading of the directory-organization question points to a
distinct research direction: a knowledge repository may need to model
not only how a field is divided conceptually, but how knowledge is
transmitted socially. The relevant literatures do not begin from
taxonomic subdivision. They begin from participation, apprenticeship,
pedagogy, and mediation [@lave1991situatedlearning;
@wenger1998communities; @shulman2005signaturepedagogies].

Lave and Wenger's situated-learning framework is foundational here.
They define learning through legitimate peripheral participation: new
members move toward fuller participation in the sociocultural practices
of a community [@lave1991situatedlearning]. Knowledge transmission on
this account is not secondary to a finished body of content. It is part
of what the content is, because knowing is inseparable from becoming a
participant in a practice.

Wenger expands that model into a broader social theory of learning. In
his formulation, engagement in social practice is the fundamental
process by which people come to know what they know and become who they
are [@wenger1998communities]. The central unit is the community of
practice rather than either the isolated learner or the formal
institution. This gives a different way to think about subdivision.
Some divisions within a field may be best described as communities held
together by joint enterprise, mutual engagement, and shared repertoire.

Shulman's notion of signature pedagogy shows how transmission
structures also encode disciplinary norms. Signature pedagogies are the
forms of teaching that organize how future practitioners are educated
for a profession; they define what counts as knowledge, how it becomes
known, the functions of expertise, and the moral dispositions a field
expects [@shulman2005signaturepedagogies]. This suggests that methods,
practices, and standards are often reproduced together through recurring
pedagogical forms. The way a field teaches is part of how it maintains
its identity.

Knorr Cetina's epistemic cultures deepen the point by showing that
communities of knowing are assembled through specific arrangements,
mechanisms, and object-relations [@knorrcetina1999epistemiccultures].
Different scientific fields do not merely hold different beliefs. They
organize inquiry differently, distribute agency differently, and relate
to their epistemic objects differently. This turns attention from
doctrinal differences to knowledge machineries: the infrastructures,
instruments, routines, and subject positions through which a field
knows.

Star and Griesemer's boundary objects then show how differentiated
communities remain connected. Their study of scientific cooperation
argues that heterogeneous groups can work together through objects that
are adaptable to local needs yet stable enough to maintain a common
identity across sites [@star1989boundaryobjects]. Repositories,
standardized forms, ideal types, and coincident boundaries are not only
classification devices. They are coordination devices across partially
incommensurable worlds.

Taken together, these literatures open a direction not fully captured
by either "defining" or "differentiating." They focus on transmission
structures: apprenticeship paths, pedagogical forms, communities of
practice, epistemic cultures, and mediating objects. The common
question is not simply how knowledge is divided, but how a field
reproduces itself, admits newcomers, and coordinates across internal
difference.

The field is not unified on terminology. Wenger privileges communities
of practice. Shulman privileges pedagogical forms. Knorr Cetina
privileges epistemic machineries. Star and Griesemer privilege
mediating objects and translation. But they converge on one point:
knowledge organization is never only the arrangement of concepts. It is
also the arrangement of participation.
