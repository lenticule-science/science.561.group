The literatures relevant to knowledge work do not treat terms,
concepts, topics, methods, practices, and standards as a single tidy
ladder. They treat them as different kinds of epistemic object. The
most stable distinction is between linguistic labels, units of
knowledge, and broader fields of aboutness; the most contested
distinction is between formal method and situated practice
[@iskoTerminology; @sepConcepts; @sepScientificMethod].

In terminology and knowledge-organization theory, a term is not the
same thing as a concept. Terminology work usually treats concepts as
units of knowledge and terms as the linguistic designations that name
them [@iskoTerminology]. Ingetraut Dahlberg's concept theory gives that
distinction a realist form: concepts arise from statements about a
referent's characteristics, while terms provide the denominative handle
through which a concept becomes communicable [@dahlberg2006concepttheory].
This is the clearest support in the literature for separating
`terms/` from `concepts/`.

Epistemology adds a second distinction that bears on directory design:
the difference between knowing-that and knowing-how. The SEP survey on
knowledge-how presents this as a major fault line in contemporary
epistemology, with some theories reducing practical mastery to
propositional knowledge and others treating it as irreducibly practical
competence [@sepKnowledgeHow]. That distinction cuts across the
term-concept-topic triad. A repository may need separate places not
because one item is more general than another, but because one encodes
declarative knowledge while another encodes practical capability.

The distinction between concepts and topics is looser. Birger Hjorland
notes that in library and information science "subject" names the
aboutness of a document or discourse, not necessarily a single concept
[@hjorland2024subject]. A subject or topic can therefore gather many
concepts, terms, and problems under one heading. Topics are broader and
more document-oriented than concepts. They name regions of inquiry or
discussion rather than discrete units of meaning.

That does not yield a universal three-level ontology. Outside
terminology science, philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists
disagree sharply about what a concept is. The Stanford Encyclopedia
survey shows enduring competition among classical definitions,
prototype theory, exemplar theory, and theory-theory accounts
[@sepConcepts]. Some models treat concepts as decomposable structures,
others as use-guiding prototypes, and still others as nodes embedded in
folk or scientific theories. So the difference between an "atomic term"
and an "emergent concept" is not settled across disciplines. What is
settled is that labels, concepts, and discourse topics are analytically
distinct.

The same pluralism appears in the relation between concrete referents
and abstract patterns. Dahlberg's framework begins from referents and
their characteristics, which supports a progression from object to
concept to term [@dahlberg2006concepttheory]. Cognitive theories of
concepts, by contrast, often treat abstraction as inseparable from
categorization, inference, and background theory. A concept is not just
a compressed referent. It is a role in a wider inferential or
representational system [@sepConcepts]. For repository design, that
means a concept directory should not be assumed to house only
generalizations over concrete objects. It may also house patterns,
schemas, and theoretical constructs.

Philosophy of science draws a similar distinction among methods,
practices, and standards. The scientific-method literature is now
skeptical of any single invariant method shared by all sciences
[@sepScientificMethod]. Methods are better understood as families of
procedures, inferential strategies, and experimental designs. They
specify how inquiry is supposed to proceed in a given context.

Practice is broader and more situated than method. James Bogen's
account of theory and observation emphasizes that data production,
measurement, instrumentation, and interpretation are historically
specific practices, not mechanical applications of a timeless recipe
[@sepTheoryObservation]. Practices include trained perception, material
arrangements, tacit know-how, and local routines. They are how
knowledge work is enacted.

Learning theory strengthens that distinction. Lave and Wenger's theory
of situated learning treats learning as movement into fuller
participation in a community of practice rather than as mere internal
acquisition of propositions [@lave1991situatedlearning]. Wenger later
generalizes this claim by making engagement in social practice the
fundamental process through which people come to know what they know
[@wenger1998communities]. On this account, practices are not just where
knowledge gets applied after production. They are among the primary
sites where knowing is formed, transmitted, and stabilized.

Standards sit at a different level again. Debates about scientific
objectivity frame standards as normative criteria or epistemic values:
publicity, reproducibility, intersubjective criticism, procedural
fairness, or discipline-specific warrants [@sepScientificObjectivity].
A standard tells practitioners what counts as adequate evidence or
responsible conduct. A method tells them how to proceed. A practice is
the actual organized activity in which both are realized, negotiated,
and sometimes revised.

Professional education offers a parallel structure. Shulman argues that
signature pedagogies define how future practitioners in a field are
taught to think, perform, and act with integrity; they also define what
counts as knowledge, how it becomes known, and where authority sits in
the field [@shulman2005signaturepedagogies]. This introduces a further
connection among methods, practices, and standards: pedagogical forms
often carry all three at once. They teach inquiry procedures, enact the
field's ordinary practices, and transmit its normative dispositions.

This produces a useful but non-final triad. Methods are generalized
inquiry forms. Practices are situated enactments. Standards are
normative constraints or appraisal criteria. The three interact but are
not reducible to each other [@sepScientificMethod;
@sepTheoryObservation; @sepScientificObjectivity]. Philosophy of
science does not give a single canonical folder model, but it does
support distinguishing "how knowledge is produced," "how it is done in
situ," and "what governs acceptable doing."

The literature is least settled where these two clusters meet. Some
fields embed standards inside methods. Others treat practices as the
primary unit and see methods as idealized reconstructions. Terminology
science is more comfortable with stable concept systems than philosophy
of mind is. Library science is more pragmatic about topics as
aboutness-classes than metaphysics is about what a concept really is.
That disagreement is part of the landscape. It shows that any
repository-level distinction among terms, concepts, topics, methods,
practices, and standards will be a theoretically loaded choice, even
when it is well grounded in disciplinary precedent.
