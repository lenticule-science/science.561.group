The problem of how a field subdivides is treated very differently across
philosophy of science, sociology of knowledge, and the sociology of
professions. These literatures do not describe one generic process of
"differentiation." They name different units: paradigms, research
programmes, thought collectives, fields, and jurisdictions. Each unit
captures a different kind of intellectual division [@kuhnSep;
@abbott1988system].

Thomas Kuhn's framework is the most influential account of internal
scientific organization. In Kuhn's mature formulation, a paradigm is
better understood as a disciplinary matrix anchored by exemplars,
symbolic generalizations, and shared standards of puzzle-solving
[@kuhnSep]. Kuhnian differentiation is therefore exemplar-based and
normative. A scientific community divides when it no longer shares the
same model problems, standards, and normal-science routines. This is
not primarily a subject division. Two groups can study similar objects
while belonging to different paradigmatic orders.

Imre Lakatos shifts the unit from community consensus to long-duration
problem trajectories. Research programmes are organized around a "hard
core" protected by auxiliary hypotheses and guided by positive and
negative heuristics [@lakatosSep]. Lakatosian differentiation is
programmatic rather than disciplinary. Programmes can coexist within a
single field and compete over explanatory success. The subdivision here
is by strategic line of development: what is being defended, what kinds
of modifications are permitted, and whether a programme is progressive
or degenerating.

Ludwik Fleck's thought collective and thought style move the analysis
into social epistemology. Fleck treats facts as formed within
collectives that train perception, stabilize evidence, and define what
can even count as a sensible problem [@fleck1981scientificfact]. This
kind of differentiation is neither just paradigmatic nor just
disciplinary. It is style-based and transmissive. What divides
communities is a shared way of seeing, talking, and validating, often
carried through apprenticeship and circulation between esoteric and
exoteric circles.

Pierre Bourdieu's field theory introduces a different axis again. A
field is a structured space of positions and struggles, held together
by its own stakes and forms of capital [@bourdieu2004scienceofscience;
@bourdieu2018fieldtheory]. Intellectual differentiation on this model
is positional. Groups divide because they occupy different locations in
an arena of competition over legitimacy, authority, and autonomy. A
school is one possible formation inside a field, but the field is the
larger structure that makes such formations meaningful.

Andrew Abbott adds the question of jurisdiction. In Abbott's system of
professions, expert communities differentiate through claims over work,
problems, and client domains [@abbott1988system]. A profession is not
defined only by doctrine. It is defined by the tasks and territories it
can successfully claim. Abbott's later account of disciplines extends
this insight to academic life, showing how disciplines reproduce
themselves through recurrent oppositions and boundary work rather than
through stable essences [@abbott2001chaos]. This is a distinct kind of
division from Kuhn's or Lakatos's: it is organizational and
jurisdictional.

Second-pass reading points to another family of units that does not fit
cleanly into paradigm, school, or discipline. Wenger's communities of
practice are defined by mutual engagement, joint enterprise, and shared
repertoire, which makes them learning formations rather than merely
doctrinal camps [@wenger1998communities]. They differentiate through
participation, membership, and repertoire-building. This introduces a
kind of subdivision keyed to social learning rather than to subject,
method, or institution.

Karin Knorr Cetina's epistemic cultures push the point further by
treating knowledge communities as arrangements and mechanisms that make
up how a field knows what it knows [@knorrcetina1999epistemiccultures].
This framework suggests that two groups may share a nominal discipline
yet remain distinct because their objects, instruments, temporalities,
and epistemic subjects are organized differently. The unit here is not
only a school or profession but a culture of knowing.

Star and Griesemer's boundary objects add a relational category that is
easy to miss when focusing only on communities themselves. Their
analysis shows that heterogeneous groups can cooperate without full
consensus when they share objects flexible enough for local use yet
stable enough to travel across sites [@star1989boundaryobjects]. This
is not a subdivision type in the same sense as discipline or school,
but it is a mechanism by which differentiated groups hold together.
Once boundary objects are considered, the map of a field includes not
only its parts but also the mediating forms that connect them.

These frameworks therefore map different subdivision types. Kuhn gives
shared exemplars and standards. Lakatos gives strategic research
lines. Fleck gives transmissible thought styles and collectives.
Bourdieu gives relational fields of position and capital. Abbott gives
jurisdictions in systems of expert labor [@kuhnSep; @lakatosSep;
@fleck1981scientificfact; @abbott1988system].

The important consequence is that "discipline" and "school" do not
exhaust the landscape. A discipline is usually an institutional or
jurisdictional unit. A school is usually a style, lineage, or
framework. But other recurrent kinds appear in the literature:
programme, paradigm, collective, field, specialty, profession, and
problem area. Some of these cut across disciplines. Some sit inside
them. Some compete within the same institutional shell.

The sources also disagree about what is primary. Kuhn and Lakatos give
priority to the organization of inquiry. Fleck and Bourdieu give more
weight to social formation and reproduction. Abbott gives priority to
systems of work and claims over expertise. None of these collapses
cleanly into the others. The disagreement is useful because it shows
that intellectual subdivision can be keyed to at least three different
logics: epistemic organization, social style, and institutional
jurisdiction.

For a repository trying to understand the kinds of subdivision rather
than merely record that subdivision happens, this literature offers a
comparative map rather than a final schema. It shows that the naming
problem is not only lexical. It is also theoretical: each candidate
folder name presupposes a model of what a knowledge community is and
how it differentiates.
