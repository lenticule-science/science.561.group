Mariame Kaba (born 1961) is an organizer, educator, and curator based in New York. She founded Project NIA, a grassroots organization focused on ending youth incarceration, and has been involved in [abolitionist](../../sociology/terms/abolition.md) organizing for over three decades. Kaba's work insists that [abolition](../../sociology/terms/abolition.md) is not a distant ideal but a discipline of daily practice -- building the institutions, relationships, and responses that make carceral systems unnecessary.

## Core ideas

- **Abolition as practical horizon**: Kaba argues that [abolition](../../sociology/terms/abolition.md) is not a utopian endpoint but an orientation that shapes present action. It means building alternatives to policing and imprisonment now -- [mutual aid](../../sociology/terms/mutual-aid.md) networks, community accountability processes, conflict resolution practices -- rather than waiting for a transformed society to arrive. Every act of mutual support is [abolitionist](../../sociology/terms/abolition.md) work.
- **Transformative justice**: rather than punishment or even restorative justice (which Kaba argues can be co-opted by carceral institutions), transformative justice asks how the conditions that produced harm can be changed. It addresses violence without reproducing [the state](../../sociology/terms/the-state.md)'s monopoly on legitimate force.
- **"Hope is a discipline"**: Kaba rejects the framing of hope as a feeling or a temperament. Hope is a practice -- a decision to act as though change is possible, sustained through collective work rather than individual optimism. This formulation has become widely cited in [abolitionist](../../sociology/terms/abolition.md) and organizing communities.
- **Against saviorism**: Kaba's organizing model emphasizes collective capacity over individual leadership. She resists the centering of charismatic figures and insists that movements are built by ordinary people doing sustained, unglamorous work.
