The h-index is a bibliometric indicator of an individual researcher's combined productivity and citation impact: a researcher has index *h* if *h* of their papers have each been cited at least *h* times.
Hirsch, J.E. (2005), "An index to quantify an individual's scientific research output," *PNAS* 102(46):16569-16572, introduced it as a single number intended to balance sheer output against citation impact, correcting distortions from using either raw paper counts or raw citation counts alone -- a researcher with one hugely cited paper and a researcher with many modestly cited papers can score very differently on a metric that only counts one dimension.

## Context: metrics misuse and its critics

The h-index emerged against a documented misuse literature for the older journal-level [impact factor](./scientometrics.md#metrics-their-misuse-and-reform), whose Science Citation Index infrastructure traces to Eugene Garfield's 1955 citation-indexing proposal.
Seglen (1997) argued the impact factor should not evaluate individual researchers; Rossner et al. (2007) documented its statistical inappropriateness given skewed citation distributions; Wilhite and Fong (2012) documented editorial coercion of self-citation.
The h-index does not escape all such critique -- it is sensitive to field-specific citation norms and career length, and can itself be gamed by self-citation -- but it was proposed specifically as an individual-level alternative to journal-level metrics being misapplied to individuals.

## Relation to assessment reform

The h-index sits inside the broader research-assessment-reform literature this section documents: DORA (2012) recommends against journal-based metrics as a surrogate for individual quality, and the Leiden Manifesto (Hicks et al. 2015, *Nature* 520:429-431) proposes that any quantitative indicator, the h-index included, should support rather than replace qualitative expert judgment.
See [scientometrics](./scientometrics.md) for the fuller indicator literature and the [study of research synthesis and scientometrics](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-the-study-of-research-synthesis-and-scientometrics.md) for the full literature review, including Edwards & Roy's (2017) explicit Goodhart's-law argument that any single metric, once used as an evaluation target, invites gaming.
