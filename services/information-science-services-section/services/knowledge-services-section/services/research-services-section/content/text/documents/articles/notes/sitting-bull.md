Sitting Bull — Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake in Standard Lakota Orthography, rendered in older sources as Tatanka Iyotake and translated as "Buffalo Bull Who Sits Down" — was a Húŋkpapȟa Lakȟóta leader and wičháša wakȟáŋ (holy man) born around 1831 near the Grand River in present-day South Dakota, and killed December 15, 1890, at his home on the Standing Rock Reservation. He is the most widely recognized Lakota leader of the nineteenth century, both for his role in the 1876 defeat of Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer's 7th Cavalry at the Little Bighorn and for his subsequent decades resisting the reservation system, and his life is documented both in the standard non-Native scholarship — principally Robert M. Utley's *The Lance and the Shield: The Life and Times of Sitting Bull* (1993) — and, since 2009, in a family-authorized account by his own descendant.

## Biography

### Early life and rise to leadership

Sources give his birth name as either Húŋkešni ("Slow") or Ȟoká Psíče ("Jumping Badger"); his father was then named Returns Again. At around age 14, after the boy counted coup on a Crow warrior in battle, Returns Again held a feast conferring his own name, Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake, upon his son — the conferral marking the boy's passage into manhood, alongside an eagle feather, a war horse, and a buffalo-hide shield — and took for himself the new name Jumping Bull. His mother was Her-Holy-Door. He grew into both a respected warrior and, distinctively, a wičháša wakȟáŋ — a man understood among the Lakota to hold ceremonial knowledge, to interpret visions, and to communicate with the spirits — a status the standard biographical literature (Utley 1993) and the family account (Ernie LaPointe, *Sitting Bull: His Life and Legacy*, 2009) agree was central to his authority; his political leadership among the Húŋkpapȟa was inseparable from this spiritual role rather than a separate, secular office layered on top of it.

Through the 1860s he led and took part in resistance to U.S. military posts and railroad surveys pushing into Lakota territory — engagements including the 1864 Battle of Killdeer Mountain against General Alfred Sully's forces and continued raids on Forts Berthold, Stevenson, and Buford in support of the wider Lakota resistance associated with Red Cloud's War. When the United States and a number of Lakota leaders signed the Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1868 (see [Fort Laramie Treaty (1868)](../../../../../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/fort-laramie-treaty-1868.md)), Sitting Bull refused to sign, reportedly stating that he had no wish to sell any part of his country — a position consistent with his refusal, across his life, to treat Lakota land as an object of negotiation.

### Little Bighorn, 1876

By the mid-1870s, following the U.S. government's 1875–76 ultimatum ordering all Lakota onto reservations and the Army's incursions into the unceded hunting territory the 1868 treaty had guaranteed, Sitting Bull organized and drew a large combined encampment of Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Arapaho — estimated at several thousand people — along the Little Bighorn River in Montana Territory. In the days before the battle he performed a Sun Dance in which he made an offering of pieces of flesh cut from his arms and experienced a vision of soldiers "falling like grasshoppers" into the Lakota camp. On June 25, 1876, warriors under the field leadership of Crazy Horse and other war leaders annihilated Custer's battalion in the Battle of the Little Bighorn. Sitting Bull's role was principally that of the camp's senior spiritual authority rather than a battlefield tactical commander — a distinction both Utley and LaPointe emphasize, against a popular image of him as the battle's field general.

### Exile in Canada and surrender, 1877–1881

In the military campaign that followed the Army's defeat, Sitting Bull led his band north across the border the Lakota came to call the Medicine Line into the North-West Territories (present-day Saskatchewan), reaching Wood Mountain by May 1877 and remaining in Canada for roughly four years rather than accept the terms of surrender offered in the United States. Diminishing buffalo herds on the Canadian side made feeding his people increasingly difficult, and on July 19, 1881, he surrendered at Fort Buford, Dakota Territory, with 186 remaining followers; his young son Crow Foot handed over his rifle, and Sitting Bull is reported to have said he was the last man of his people to give up his gun.

### Imprisonment, Standing Rock, and Buffalo Bill's Wild West, 1881–1890

Rather than being released to the Standing Rock Agency, Sitting Bull and his band were held as prisoners of war at Fort Randall for roughly twenty months before being permitted to return to Standing Rock in 1883, where Indian agent James McLaughlin treated him as a rival to his own authority over the reservation. In 1885 Sitting Bull toured for about four months with Buffalo Bill Cody's Wild West show, earning a weekly salary and drawing paying crowds, and during this period met and befriended sharpshooter Annie Oakley, whom he is said to have symbolically adopted and nicknamed "Little Sure Shot." He returned to his cabin on the Grand River, where in his final years Brooklyn-born activist Caroline Weldon assisted him as an interpreter and advocate against further land cessions demanded of the Great Sioux Reservation.

### Ghost Dance and death, December 15, 1890

In 1890 the Ghost Dance religious movement, promising the return of the buffalo and Lakota ancestors and the disappearance of settlers, spread rapidly among reservation Lakota. Sitting Bull permitted Ghost Dancers to gather at his Grand River camp without leading the movement himself; McLaughlin and federal authorities nonetheless viewed him as its dangerous figurehead and ordered his arrest to forestall his possible flight from the reservation. Before dawn on December 15, 1890, a detachment of Indian police — Lakota men in federal employ — surrounded his cabin; accounts converge that Lieutenant Bull Head (Tȟatȟáŋka Pȟá) led the arrest, that Sitting Bull's followers resisted once he was forced outside, that a supporter named Catch-the-Bear fired the first shot, and that in the ensuing fight Bull Head and fellow officer Red Tomahawk (Čhaŋȟpí Dúta) both shot Sitting Bull, who was struck in the chest and head and died within hours. Fourteen people were killed in the confrontation, including six of the Indian police. He was buried at Fort Yates; in 1953, family members exhumed and reinterred his remains near Mobridge, South Dakota, close to his birthplace, where a monument now stands. In October 2021, DNA extracted from a lock of his hair, held by the Smithsonian Institution and repatriated in 2007, was matched by researchers at the University of Cambridge to South Dakota Sundancer Ernie LaPointe, confirming him as Sitting Bull's great-grandson and the source of the 2009 family-authorized biography.

**Citation status:** the chronology, treaty-related events, and death account above are attested-secondary — corroborated across Utley's scholarly biography, standard encyclopedic sources (Britannica, History.com), and secondary description of LaPointe's family account, though this entry has not read LaPointe's book directly and so does not attribute any specific claim to it beyond the widely reported fact of its existence and general emphasis (his identity as wičháša wakȟáŋ, his role at the Little Bighorn as spiritual rather than tactical). Exact birth year (sources range 1831–1837) and some details of the December 1890 gunfight (the precise sequence of shots) remain genuinely contested across sources and are stated here with that qualification.

## Significance for this research

Sitting Bull's life supplies this corpus's Medicine Line research with its central figure for asymmetric permeability: the same 49th parallel that let his band exit U.S. jurisdiction in 1877 offered no such passage back into full Lakota sovereignty, and his four years at Wood Mountain were bounded not by the line's fixed geography but by the political will on either side of it to feed, arm, or expel him — a border functioning, in this corpus's terms, as a [dispositional object](../../../../../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/property-as-disposition.md) rather than a fixed line, its exclusion-powers switched on and off by Canadian and American administrators according to their own convenience rather than by any change in what the parallel itself marked. His death is a case study in [settlerism-as-filing-system](../../../../../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/settlerism-as-filing-system.md): the Ghost Dance was filed by federal authorities as insurrection rather than ceremony, a filing that converted a Lakota spiritual movement into grounds for lethal police action, and that did so by routing the violence through Indian police — Lakota men employed to enforce the filing against their own relations. His refusal to sign the 1868 treaty, on the stated ground that his country was not his to sell, anticipates this corpus's broader argument that treaty land was never a transactable category from the Lakota side, only rendered transactable by the filing system that recorded it as such.

## Notable sources

- Robert M. Utley, *The Lance and the Shield: The Life and Times of Sitting Bull* (Henry Holt, 1993) — the standard scholarly biography, drawing on Stanley Vestal's 1920s–30s interviews with Lakota who knew Sitting Bull directly.
- Ernie LaPointe, *Sitting Bull: His Life and Legacy* (Gibbs Smith, 2009) — the first biography by a lineal descendant (great-grandson, DNA-confirmed 2021), presenting Lakota family oral history and correcting points of the standard record.
- Joseph M. Marshall III and other Lakota-authored histories of the period supply further corroborating context for the family account, though this entry has not verified specific claims against them.
