The FAIR data principles are a set of machine-actionable guidelines for scientific data management and stewardship, defined by Wilkinson, M.D., Dumontier, M., Aalbersberg, I.J., Appleton, G., Axton, M., Baak, A., et al. (2016), "The FAIR Guiding Principles for Scientific Data Management and Stewardship," *Scientific Data* 3:160018.
FAIR stands for:

- **Findable** -- data are assigned persistent identifiers and described with rich, indexed, searchable metadata.
- **Accessible** -- data are retrievable via standardized protocols, with metadata remaining available even when the underlying data itself is access-restricted.
- **Interoperable** -- data and metadata use shared, formal vocabularies enabling integration across systems.
- **Reusable** -- data carry clear licensing and provenance information sufficient for appropriate downstream reuse.

The principles are explicitly aimed at both human and computational ("machine-actionable") use, reflecting data-intensive science's need for automated discovery and integration rather than only human-mediated citation.

## Operationalizing infrastructure

FAIR-style identification for people and works is operationalized by: ORCID, which launched its registry on 16 October 2012 under founding executive director Laurel Haak, giving researchers a persistent identifier robust to name changes and transliteration; and Crossref, established 1999 and launched in early 2000 as a publisher cooperative functioning as a DOI Registration Agency, assigning persistent identifiers to scholarly works and resolving roughly 1.1 billion DOIs monthly.
Neither paper defines these as FAIR infrastructure retroactively; the connection is this study's own synthesis, not a claim made by Wilkinson et al., ORCID, or Crossref themselves.

## Distinct from metrics reform

FAIR is a data-infrastructure standard, not a research-evaluation standard; it should not be conflated with the [scientometrics](./scientometrics.md) reform literature (DORA, the Leiden Manifesto) that addresses how researchers and institutions are *measured*, even though both respond to a shared underlying concern about making research claims independently checkable.
