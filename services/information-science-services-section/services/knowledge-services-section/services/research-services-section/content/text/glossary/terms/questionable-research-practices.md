**Questionable research practices** (QRPs) are choices made during data collection, analysis, or reporting - not outright fabrication of data, but undisclosed flexibility exercised in a way that makes a significant result more likely to be found and reported.
Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson, and Uri Simonsohn formalized the underlying mechanism as "researcher degrees of freedom" in ["False-Positive Psychology: Undisclosed Flexibility in Data Collection and Analysis Allows Presenting Anything as Significant"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797611417632) (Psychological Science, 2011), showing by simulation and by experiment that choices such as when to stop collecting data, which measures or conditions to report, and which covariates to include can push the true false-positive rate well above the nominal .05 threshold even without any single dishonest step - this everyday flexibility is also called **p-hacking**.

Leslie John, George Loewenstein, and Drazen Prelec then measured how common such practices actually are, in ["Measuring the Prevalence of Questionable Research Practices with Incentives for Truth Telling"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797611430953) (Psychological Science, 2012), surveying over 2,000 academic psychologists with an incentive-compatible elicitation method and finding self-admission rates high enough to argue that some QRPs function as prevailing norms rather than rare misconduct.
Their paper is a self-report prevalence estimate, distinct from Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn's simulation-based demonstration of the mechanism itself.

QRPs are treated as a principal upstream cause of the [replication crisis](./replication-crisis.md), alongside [publication bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publication_bias).
[Preregistration](./preregistration.md) and registered reports are the reform proposals most directly aimed at foreclosing them, by fixing hypotheses and analysis plans before a study's outcome is known.
Paul Smaldino and Richard McElreath's ["The Natural Selection of Bad Science"](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsos/article/3/9/160384/56494/The-natural-selection-of-bad-scienceThe-Natural) (Royal Society Open Science, 2016) argues that such practices can persist and spread across a research community through incentives alone, without requiring that any individual scientist act in bad faith.
The category sits within the broader field of [metascience](./metascience.md).
