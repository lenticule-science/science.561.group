The **replication crisis** (also **reproducibility crisis**) is the empirical finding that a large share of published experimental results do not reproduce, at their original effect size or at all, when an independent team repeats the original study under comparable conditions.
It is the most publicized result of [metascience](./metascience.md), the broader field that studies research methods and practice.

The central evidence comes from three large, coordinated replication projects.
The Open Science Collaboration's ["Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aac4716) (Science, 2015) replicated 100 psychology studies and found a significant effect in only 36 percent of replications, versus 97 percent of the originals, at roughly half the original effect size.
Camerer and colleagues extended the method to economics in ["Evaluating Replicability of Laboratory Experiments in Economics"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aaf0918) (Science, 2016), replicating at 61 percent success, and to a cross-disciplinary sample of high-profile journals in ["Evaluating the Replicability of Social Science Experiments in Nature and Science between 2010 and 2015"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0399-z) (Nature Human Behaviour, 2018), replicating at 62 percent success - both at roughly half the original effect size.

The crisis is widely attributed to two upstream causes: [publication bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publication_bias), the tendency for significant results to be written up and accepted more readily than null results, documented empirically by Franco, Malhotra, and Simonovits's ["Publication Bias in the Social Sciences"](https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.1255484) (Science, 2014); and [questionable research practices](./questionable-research-practices.md), the undisclosed analytic flexibility described by Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn (2011) and measured by John, Loewenstein, and Prelec (2012).
The proposed remedies - [preregistration](./preregistration.md) and registered reports - aim at those upstream causes rather than at replication rates directly.

Whether "crisis" is the right frame is itself disputed: Daniele Fanelli's ["Is Science Really Facing a Reproducibility Crisis, and Do We Need It To?"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29531051/) (PNAS, 2018) argues the finding is concentrated in specific fields and journals and cautions against generalizing it to science as a whole.

This term draws on the library's [Study of Metascience and Replication](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-the-study-of-metascience-and-replication.md), which carries forward as a gap that these replication rates have not been verified for fields outside psychology, experimental economics, and the Nature/Science sample.
