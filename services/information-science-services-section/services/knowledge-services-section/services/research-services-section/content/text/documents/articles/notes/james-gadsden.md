James Gadsden (1788–1858) was a Charleston-born Army officer, railroad president, and diplomat — grandson of Christopher Gadsden — whose career connects nearly every register of nineteenth-century U.S. settler expansion: Indian removal, slavery's westward extension, railroad speculation, and territorial acquisition. The Arizona-Sonora border at Lochiel exists where it does because of him.

## Career

- **Jackson's officer**: aide-de-camp in the First Seminole War (1818); his detachment's captured documents fed the Arbuthnot–Ambrister executions; his artillery placements forced Pensacola's surrender. In 1818 he rebuilt the destroyed "Negro Fort" — the maroon community fort on the Apalachicola that U.S. forces had blown up in 1816 — and Jackson renamed it **Fort Gadsden**: his name written over a destroyed free Black settlement.
- **Removal negotiator**: negotiated both principal Seminole removal treaties — **Moultrie Creek (1823)**, with the explicit warning that non-attending bands would be "forced to comply," and **Payne's Landing (1832)**, which most Seminole leaders disavowed as coerced and which precipitated the Second Seminole War (1835–42). Both treaties obligated Seminoles to surrender people who had escaped slavery.
- **Railroad executive and slavery expansionist**: president of the South Carolina Railroad (1840–1850); called slavery "a social blessing"; after California entered free, schemed with allies to split the state and plant a slaveholder colony there, tied to a slave-built railroad to the goldfields (1852 petition).
- **The Purchase**: as Pierce's minister to Mexico (appointed May 1853, with Secretary of War Jefferson Davis the southern-railroad advocate behind the mission), instructed to offer up to $50 million for Baja California and much of northern Mexico. Santa Anna, bankrupt and facing filibusters, sold the minimal strip: signed December 30, 1853 at $15M; the Senate cut it to **$10 million for 29,670 square miles** — northern senators deliberately shrinking prospective slave territory, so the line's very shape is a sectional-conflict artifact. Article VIII secured U.S. transit — persons, goods, mails, "troops and munitions" — across the Isthmus of Tehuantepec. The sale was named among the indictments in the Plan de Ayutla; Santa Anna fell for the last time in 1855.

## Significance for this research

Gadsden anchors the social-history finding that the U.S.-Mexico border in the Gadsden strip is not a geographic or cultural boundary but a **relayed instrument of racial-property expansion**: a line drawn for a slaver's railroad, priced by a fight over slavery's extent, cut through O'odham lands without consultation, and now materialized as a $46.5B wall program. His biography makes the relay legible — maroon fort to removal treaty to railroad to boundary — one career, one continuous operation.
