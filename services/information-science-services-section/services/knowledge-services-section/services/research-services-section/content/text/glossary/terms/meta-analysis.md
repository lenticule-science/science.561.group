Meta-analysis is the statistical method of combining the quantitative results of multiple independent studies into a single pooled estimate of effect, with a corresponding estimate of uncertainty (typically a confidence interval) and, often, an assessment of heterogeneity across the pooled studies.

## Coining and founding definition

Gene V. Glass coined the term in Glass, G.V. (1976), "Primary, Secondary, and Meta-Analysis of Research," *Educational Researcher* 5(10):3-8, defining it as "the analysis of analyses" -- statistical analysis of a large collection of individual studies' results, for the purpose of integrating their findings.
Glass's tripartite distinction is the founding vocabulary for the field: a *primary* analysis works on raw data from a single study; a *secondary* analysis reworks an existing dataset; a *meta*-analysis analyzes the analyses (typically, the effect-size estimates) of many prior studies.

## Institutional context

Meta-analysis is rarely practiced as a standalone technique today; it is typically the quantitative-synthesis stage embedded within a [systematic review](./systematic-review.md), governed by the same Cochrane Handbook methodology and often reported according to the PRISMA or PRISMA 2020 checklists that apply to the review as a whole.
The distinction matters for citation practice: a claim about *pooling method* (fixed-effect vs. random-effects models, heterogeneity statistics) should cite meta-analysis methodology specifically, while a claim about *search and reporting rigor* belongs to the systematic-review literature.

See the [study of research synthesis and scientometrics](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-the-study-of-research-synthesis-and-scientometrics.md) for the fuller literature review this term draws on.
