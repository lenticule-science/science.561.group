## 1. Purpose and scope

This note examines United States federal cannabis scheduling as a case study in a specific institutional pattern: a classification that both asserts an epistemic state ("no currently accepted medical use") and controls the machinery by which that state could be revised. The claim under investigation is structural, not toxicological. It is not that cannabis is harmless, nor that scheduling was irrational given what was known in 1970. It is that the classification, once made, altered what could subsequently become known, in a direction that happened to keep confirming the classification — and that this circularity was produced by ordinary administrative mechanics (registration rules, a sole-source supply contract, a denial letter's evidentiary standard) rather than by any single decision that a critic could point to and say "there, that was the collapse." Where the record is contested or the causal story is not fully established, this note says so. Where harm findings are solid and replicated, it says that too, at full strength.

## 2. The criterion and its circularity

The Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970 (the Controlled Substances Act, or CSA) created five schedules of controlled substances. Under 21 U.S.C. § 812(b)(1), a substance qualifies for Schedule I if all three of the following are true: (A) it has a high potential for abuse; (B) it has no currently accepted medical use in treatment in the United States; and (C) there is a lack of accepted safety for use of the substance under medical supervision. Cannabis was placed in Schedule I at the CSA's enactment, alongside heroin and LSD, and above Schedule II substances such as cocaine and oxycodone, which are deemed to have high abuse potential but accepted medical use.

Criterion (B) is the pivotal one for this note, and it is worth being precise about what kind of claim it is. It is not a claim about the pharmacology of the substance; it is a claim about the state of the medical and scientific literature — about what has been demonstrated, published, and accepted by relevant institutions (historically, the FDA's New Drug Application process was treated by DEA and HHS as the operative definition of "accepted medical use"). That literature is not a fixed fact of nature; it is produced by researchers who require legal authorization, supply, and funding to generate it.

Schedule I placement imposed exactly the regime that made generating that literature difficult. A researcher wishing to study cannabis in humans needed (a) DEA registration as a Schedule I handler, a more burdensome process than for any other schedule; (b) approval from NIDA-administered review specific to cannabis (a requirement not imposed on other Schedule I compounds through most of this period, and formally ended only in 2015); (c) an FDA Investigational New Drug application; and, for decades, (d) plant material sourced exclusively from a single federally contracted grower (Section 3, below). Each requirement was individually defensible as a control on a substance presumed dangerous and unapproved. Together, they meant that the number of investigators able to run controlled trials was small, the material available to them was constrained, and the resulting evidence base grew slowly and unevenly relative to a substance already in widespread nonmedical and, later, state-legal medical use.

The mechanism, stated without polemic: a classification premised on absence of evidence installed the access controls that suppressed the production of exactly the evidence that could revise it. This is not a claim that anyone at DEA in 1970 intended this effect, or that every rescheduling denial since has cited the resulting evidentiary thinness in bad faith. It is a claim about structure — about how a formally neutral, three-part legal test, applied to a claim about the state of knowledge, can become self-reinforcing once it also governs the conditions of inquiry into that claim.

## 3. The supply monopoly

From 1968, the University of Mississippi's School of Pharmacy held a federal contract — later administered through NIDA — making it the sole legal grower of cannabis for research in the United States. For over five decades, essentially every federally authorized study of cannabis in humans used material from this single source. The exclusivity was renewed repeatedly (e.g., in 2015) before NIDA moved, gradually, toward a multi-source model; NIDA ended the Mississippi contract itself only in 2025, with the university's marijuana-growing program winding down by 2028 and not seeking renewal.

The chemical divergence between this supply and what people actually consumed is documented, not speculative. Vergara, Bidwell, Gaudino, and colleagues (2017, *Scientific Reports* 7:46528, "Compromised External Validity: Federally Produced Cannabis Does Not Reflect Legal Markets") compared NIDA's reported cannabinoid content against commercial drug-type cannabis samples from Denver, Oakland, Sacramento, and Seattle. They found NIDA material's mean THC and CBD concentrations were only about 27% and 48%, respectively, of the mean values in the commercial samples, with markedly lower diversity of cannabinoid ratios and elevated levels of cannabinol (CBN), a THC degradation product associated with aged or improperly stored plant material.

Genetic evidence reinforces the chemical picture, though the record here needs care about dates and study identity, since more than one paper is often run together in popular accounts. Sawler et al. (2015, *PLOS ONE* 10(8):e0133292) established, using genome-wide SNP data on 81 marijuana and 43 hemp accessions, that marijuana and hemp are genetically distinct populations independent of strain naming (their sample did not include NIDA material). Schwabe and colleagues then examined NIDA material specifically: a preprint posted in 2019 ("Research grade marijuana supplied by the National Institute on Drug Abuse is genetically divergent from commercially available Cannabis," bioRxiv 592725) used microsatellite markers across wild hemp, cultivated hemp, NIDA, high-CBD, and Sativa/Hybrid/Indica drug-type samples and found the two NIDA accessions fell within the 95% confidence ellipse for hemp rather than for any drug-type category. Follow-up whole-genome work published in 2021 (Schwabe, Hansen, Hyslop & McGlaughlin, *Frontiers in Plant Science* 12:675770, and a companion paper, *Frontiers in Plant Science* 12:668315) confirmed that NIDA's samples clustered together, separately from the 71–73 commercial varieties examined, with low diversity in cannabinoid-synthesis gene copy number and in maternally inherited chloroplast/mitochondrial haplotypes. In short: the federally supplied research substrate was not merely lower-potency than commercial cannabis, it was arguably a materially different population — closer, on several genetic measures, to hemp than to the high-THC drug-type material patients and consumers actually used. Every study of cannabis's effects, benefits, or harms conducted on federally sourced material through this period was, to a first approximation, a study of a different plant.

DEA announced on August 11, 2016 that it would begin accepting applications from additional growers, alongside — on the very same day — its denial of two long-pending rescheduling petitions (Section 5). Thirty-three entities eventually applied. DEA did not process a single application for nearly five years, missing the 90-day statutory response window by an order of magnitude; the delay ended only after litigation (led by the Scottsdale Research Institute) and a 2020 GAO-adjacent congressional pressure campaign. DEA published a final registration rule in December 2020/January 2021 and, in May 2021, announced it was nearing approval of the first cohort. By 2023 a handful of entities held active registrations (including Biopharmaceutical Research Company, Scottsdale Research Institute, Groff North America, Royal Emerald Pharmaceuticals, and others added subsequently); at least one of these, Groff North America, subsequently reported ceasing cultivation operations. As of this writing the supply base is modestly diversified but still small relative to the state-legal commercial market, and most of the pre-2020 federally sponsored literature remains built on the earlier, narrower, hemp-adjacent material.

## 4. The asymmetry

This is the note's central analytical claim, and it follows from an ordinary methodological fact: harm can be studied in populations that already exist, using data that already exists, without controlled administration by the investigator. Emergency department surveillance, poison-control call logs, driving-under-the-influence toxicology, birth-cohort and adolescent longitudinal studies, and psychiatric-registry linkage all let researchers study the consequences of a substance's use by people who obtained and used it entirely outside the research system. None of this requires DEA registration to administer cannabis, because the researcher is not administering it — they are observing outcomes in people who used a substance they sourced themselves.

Therapeutic benefit is structurally different. Establishing efficacy for a plausible medical indication — pain, nausea, appetite, spasticity — to a standard that regulators or clinicians treat as "accepted" generally requires controlled administration: a defined dose, a defined product, a comparison arm, ideally blinding. That is precisely the activity Schedule I status made hardest, because it required a registered investigator, an IND, and (for most of the period under study) genetically narrow, low-diversity, degraded plant material as the only legal input (Section 3).

The consequence is a structural skew in the accumulated literature, and it shows up in bibliometric work, though the available bibliometric studies are not as fine-grained as one would like, and this note should not overstate their precision. A 2022 bibliometric analysis of the cannabis and cannabinoid literature (Zhang et al., *Journal of Cannabis Research* 4:23, examining 29,802 documents from 1829–2021) found that nearly half (14 of 30) of the top-producing journals in the field carry harm-associated terms in their titles — "dependence," "addiction," "abuse," "forensic," "drug" — while the authors note, citing external analyses of funding patterns rather than providing their own topic-coded breakdown, that "more cannabis research has focused on the harms associated with the substance, as opposed to its medical uses, especially in the USA." A separate 2024 bibliometric study focused specifically on the beneficial/therapeutic cannabis literature (7,841 documents, 2012–2022) found strong recent growth (16.83% annually) but was itself framed as filling a documented gap — the authors state plainly that comprehensive bibliometric coverage of the *beneficial* research stream had not previously existed, whereas harm- and abuse-oriented bibliometric surveillance (e.g., NIDA's own long-running epidemiological monitoring) has existed for decades. The absence of an equivalent monitoring apparatus for benefit research is itself evidence of the asymmetry, independent of any specific ratio.

None of this establishes that cannabis's benefits are being systematically hidden by design, or that the harm literature is inflated or wrong. The claim is narrower and, this note argues, better supported: the *proportions* in which harm-evidence and benefit-evidence have accumulated are not a neutral reading-off of the underlying pharmacology. They are, in significant part, an artifact of which kind of study a Schedule I classification permits to happen easily and which kind it does not.

## 5. The isolate/plant discrepancy

The sharpest evidence that the Schedule I category has tracked legal and industrial form rather than the pharmacology of the molecules involved is the treatment of synthetic and purified cannabinoids relative to the plant.

Dronabinol — synthetic (–)-Δ9-THC in sesame oil, marketed as Marinol — was approved by the FDA on May 31, 1985 for chemotherapy-induced nausea and, later, AIDS-related anorexia. DEA responded with a product-specific rule placing this formulation in Schedule II in 1986: the identical molecule that, in plant form, was deemed to have "no accepted medical use," was found by the same agency to have a medical use sufficient for Schedule II once manufactured as a standardized pharmaceutical. In 1999, DEA moved dronabinol again, from Schedule II to Schedule III (Federal Register, July 2, 1999), citing the difficulty of extracting the drug from its oil-capsule formulation and its slow, oral-route onset as grounds for a lower abuse-potential finding — reasoning about formulation and delivery mechanics, not about the THC molecule itself.

The same pattern recurred with cannabidiol. GW Pharmaceuticals' Epidiolex, a purified plant-derived CBD oral solution, received FDA approval in June 2018 for two severe pediatric epilepsy syndromes (Lennox-Gastaut and Dravet). DEA placed this specific formulation in Schedule V in September 2018 — the least restrictive schedule — while CBD in any other plant-derived form remained, at least nominally, tied to marijuana's Schedule I status except where later covered by the 2018 Farm Bill's hemp exemption. On April 6, 2020, DEA went further and removed Epidiolex from the Controlled Substances Act entirely: the first time DEA had ever descheduled a cannabis-derived drug outright.

The molecule did not change between these actions. What changed was legal form: a standardized, patented, single-molecule pharmaceutical product, manufactured under FDA's approval process and marketed by a company with the resources to run the required trials, could satisfy "accepted medical use" in a matter of years. The whole, variable, non-patentable plant containing the identical active constituent could not, under the same statutory test, for over fifty years. This is the cleanest evidence in the historical record that "accepted medical use" as applied to cannabis tracked the industrial and legal pathway available to a given formulation — a pathway effectively closed to the raw plant by the same classification being justified by its absence — rather than tracking the pharmacological facts about THC or CBD as chemical entities.

## 6. The political history

The classification has been challenged repeatedly, by bodies with no obvious motive to be lenient, and rejected or left unimplemented each time, for reasons this note tries to state precisely rather than polemically.

**1972 — the Shafer Commission.** The National Commission on Marihuana and Drug Abuse, appointed by President Nixon and chaired by former Pennsylvania Governor Raymond Shafer, issued *Marihuana: A Signal of Misunderstanding* on March 22, 1972. It concluded that "neither the marihuana user nor the drug itself can be said to constitute a danger to public safety" and recommended that possession for personal use and not-for-profit distribution of small amounts no longer be criminal offenses — a decriminalization, not a legalization, recommendation, and one concerned primarily with criminal-justice proportionality rather than with rescheduling as such. Nixon rejected it before it was released, reportedly telling Shafer that a lenient report would "make your commission just look bad as hell," and the administration's marijuana policy did not change.

**1988 — the Francis Young ruling.** In 1972, NORML filed the first formal petition to reschedule marijuana; DEA did not accept it for review until ordered to by the D.C. Circuit in 1974, and did not hold hearings until 1986, fourteen years later. DEA's own Chief Administrative Law Judge, Francis L. Young, ruled on September 6, 1988 that "marijuana, in its natural form, is one of the safest therapeutically active substances known to man" and recommended it be moved to Schedule II. DEA Administrator John Lawn rejected this recommendation in a 1989 final order; NORML and co-petitioners appealed, and the D.C. Circuit in 1991 remanded the matter to DEA for a fuller explanation of the five-factor test it was applying to "currently accepted medical use." DEA (by then under Administrator Robert Bonner) reissued its denial in 1992 using that more explicit test, and the D.C. Circuit upheld the denial in *Alliance for Cannabis Therapeutics v. DEA*, 15 F.3d 1131 (D.C. Cir. 1994) — twenty-two years after the petition was filed.

**1999 — the Institute of Medicine report.** Commissioned by the White House Office of National Drug Control Policy after California's 1996 medical-cannabis ballot initiative, the IOM's *Marijuana and Medicine: Assessing the Science Base* (March 17, 1999) found "a potential therapeutic value for cannabinoid drugs, particularly for symptoms such as pain relief, control of nausea and vomiting, and appetite stimulation," and stated that "except for the harms associated with smoking, the adverse effects of marijuana use are within the range of effects tolerated for other medications." It stopped short of recommending rescheduling and instead called for a controlled-trial research pathway that, given the constraints described in Sections 2–3, was itself difficult to execute at scale.

**Repeated denied petitions.** DEA denied a rescheduling petition filed by Jon Gettman (1995) in 2001; denied a coalition petition (filed 2002, litigated into a response after DEA's initial non-response) in 2011; and denied a petition brought on behalf of the governors of Rhode Island and Washington (filed 2011) on August 11–12, 2016 — the same Federal Register notice in which DEA also announced it would begin licensing additional research growers (Section 3). Each denial rested on the same statutory finding: no currently accepted medical use, insufficient controlled studies, inadequate safety data — the evidentiary condition that the classification's own access controls had helped produce.

**2023–2026 — the current rescheduling process.** In October 2022, President Biden directed HHS to review cannabis's scheduling. In August 2023, HHS transmitted to DEA an FDA-led scientific review recommending marijuana be moved to Schedule III, with NIDA concurring; the FDA's analysis concluded cannabis had a currently accepted medical use, an abuse potential lower than Schedule I or II substances, and, notably, that it did not meet Schedule II criteria either, effectively recommending a two-schedule jump. DEA published a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking on May 21, 2024 proposing the move to Schedule III and opened a formal administrative hearing process in August 2024; procedural disputes over which parties could participate led that hearing to be stayed in January 2025, and DEA's sole administrative law judge subsequently retired without a ruling. A December 2025 executive order directed the Attorney General to expedite the matter. Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche then issued an order — citing U.S. treaty obligations under the 1961 Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs to bypass standard notice-and-comment rulemaking — immediately reclassifying FDA-approved marijuana drug products and marijuana obtained under a state medical-marijuana license from Schedule I to Schedule III, effective April 28, 2026. All other marijuana (unlicensed cultivation, bulk product, non-FDA-approved extracts — in practice, essentially the entire state-legal recreational and most of the state-legal medical market) remained Schedule I. A new expedited hearing on whether to reschedule marijuana in full began June 29, 2026 before newly appointed Chief Administrative Law Judge Derek Julius, concluded after eleven days of testimony on July 15, 2026, with post-hearing briefs due August 17, 2026; DEA itself has argued in its brief that the evidence no longer supports Schedule I status. As of this writing (August 26, 2026), Judge Julius's recommendation to the DEA Administrator is pending, with no statutory deadline for either his recommendation or the Administrator's final decision. Fifty-four years after the CSA and fifty-four years after the Shafer Commission, the classification remains legally live and formally unresolved.

## 7. The iron law of prohibition

Cannabis activist and writer Richard Cowan coined the phrase "iron law of prohibition" in a 1986 essay ("How the Narcs Created Crack"), arguing that intensified enforcement systematically selects for higher potency-per-unit-volume, since more concentrated product is cheaper to conceal, transport, and smuggle per unit of effect — an application of the economic Alchian-Allen effect to contraband markets.

The University of Mississippi's DEA-funded Potency Monitoring Program, which has analyzed seized and (later) purchased cannabis samples since the early 1970s, documents a pattern consistent with this prediction over the long run: average THC content in analyzed samples rose from roughly 1–2% in the early 1970s to 3–4% by 1990, about 6% by 2000, roughly 10% by 2010, and above 17% in some more recent reporting years. More granular published figures show mean THC rising from 9.75% in 2009 to 14.88% in 2018, with the mean THC:CBD ratio climbing from 24.81 in 2009 to 103.48 in 2017 before falling back to roughly 24–54 in 2018–2019 as CBD-forward products entered the legal market in several states — a partial reversal that is itself informative, since it appeared specifically where legal, tested retail markets (rather than continued interdiction pressure) began to reward products other than maximum THC-per-gram.

The consequence for the epistemic argument of this note is important and easy to miss: "cannabis" as an exposure category in epidemiological and clinical research spanning the 1970s to the present does not refer to a pharmacologically stable substance. A study population's cannabis exposure in 1975 and a study population's exposure in 2015 differ not only in dose or frequency but in the chemical composition of what was consumed — different THC concentration, different THC:CBD ratio, different presence of other cannabinoids and terpenes that may modulate effects. Prohibition-era enforcement did not only determine what could be studied about cannabis; by shaping the market's incentives, it also shaped what cannabis, as a chemical exposure, actually was during the period being studied. Cross-era comparisons in the literature — a real methodological hazard independent of any registration constraint — compound the access-driven skew described in Sections 3–4.

## 8. What changed with legalization, and what has not

Beginning with California's 1996 medical-cannabis initiative and accelerating after Colorado and Washington legalized recreational sales (effective 2014), state-level legalization created a large, uncoordinated natural experiment. It has partially addressed the asymmetry described in Section 4: researchers can now study actual commercial-market products (with state-mandated lab testing providing chemical characterization impossible with pre-2016 federal material), can run large linkage studies using state health records against known legal-market exposure, and can compare outcomes across states with different legal regimes and different market maturity. It has not resolved the underlying registration and interstate-commerce constraints on federally funded controlled trials of whole-plant cannabis, which as of 2026 remain largely in place outside the narrow FDA-approved-product and state-licensed-medical carve-outs created by the April 2026 order (Section 6).

It would be a distortion of this note's argument to conclude that cannabis is therefore harmless and only appears otherwise because of bad science. Some harm findings are robust and have been replicated across independent designs and populations. The association between adolescent-onset cannabis use and later psychotic and bipolar disorders is among the best-supported: a 2026 study in *JAMA Health Forum* following 463,396 adolescents (ages 13–17) treated at Kaiser Permanente Northern California through age 26 found past-year cannabis use associated with roughly double the risk of incident psychotic and bipolar disorders, with elevated risk for depressive and anxiety disorders as well, cannabis use typically preceding diagnosis by 1.7–2.3 years, and the association strongest for the youngest users — a pattern consistent with, though not identical in every detail to, earlier prospective cohort findings dating to the 2000s and 2010s. High-potency products specifically have been repeatedly linked to greater psychotic-outcome risk than lower-potency products, which is itself a finding shaped by the potency shift documented in Section 7. This note's argument is not that the harm literature is wrong; it is that the harm literature and the benefit literature were not permitted to grow at the same rate, by the same mechanism, for the same fifty years, and that this asymmetry is a fact about regulatory structure that should be weighed when reading the accumulated evidence base as a whole — including when reading the harm findings themselves, whose case-mix, dosing, and product characteristics were also shaped by the market conditions Section 7 describes.

## Hypotheses

1. **Publication-ratio shift after the 2016 grower-licensing announcement.**
   (i) *Prediction:* The ratio of cannabis publications indexed under therapeutic/clinical-trial MeSH terms to those indexed under harm/dependence/toxicology terms should show a measurable increase beginning roughly 3–6 years after the first non-Ole-Miss DEA registrations became operational (i.e., research using newly diversified material would need time to reach publication), rather than tracking the 2016 announcement itself.
   (ii) *Test:* Pull annual PubMed/Scopus counts 2010–2026 for cannabis-indexed records, coded by MeSH branch (e.g., "Cannabis/therapeutic use" vs. "Marijuana Abuse," "Substance-Related Disorders"), and fit an interrupted time-series model with change points at 2016 (announcement), 2021 (first registrations), and 2023 (broader registration base), controlling for total cannabis-publication growth.
   (iii) *Kill condition:* If no inflection in the therapeutic-to-harm ratio appears within 6 years of 2021, or if the ratio's growth is statistically indistinguishable from its pre-2016 trend, the hypothesis that supply diversification (rather than state legalization, funding shifts, or general research-field growth) drove any observed change is falsified.

2. **Federally-supplied-material studies diverge from state-legal-market studies in dose-response findings.**
   (i) *Prediction:* Human studies using NIDA/Ole-Miss-sourced cannabis (pre-2021) should show systematically lower reported efficacy and different (likely attenuated or noisier) dose-response curves for THC-mediated outcomes than studies using state-licensed-dispensary product or self-report of legal-market use, because of the potency and chemovar divergence documented in Section 3.
   (ii) *Test:* Systematically identify controlled human cannabis studies 2000–2026, code each by material source (federal monopoly, federal diversified post-2021, state-legal market, self-sourced/observational), and meta-regress reported effect sizes (e.g., for pain, nausea, or intoxication outcomes) on source category, controlling for dose and route.
   (iii) *Kill condition:* If effect sizes and dose-response slopes do not differ by material-source category once dose is controlled for, or if federally-sourced studies show equivalent or higher effect sizes, the external-validity mechanism proposed in Section 3 is not supported as a driver of the literature's characteristics.

3. **Rescheduling events produce a lagged surge in registered clinical trials, not an immediate one.**
   (i) *Prediction:* New cannabis/cannabinoid trial registrations on ClinicalTrials.gov should rise measurably 12–24 months after each major access-loosening event (2018 Farm Bill/hemp descheduling; 2021 grower registrations; April 2026 partial Schedule III order), reflecting IRB, funding, and protocol-development lag rather than an instantaneous response.
   (ii) *Test:* Extract monthly new-trial-registration counts for cannabis/cannabinoid interventions from ClinicalTrials.gov 2014–2027 and test for level/slope breaks at each event date plus a 12–24 month lag window, against a null of steady background growth.
   (iii) *Kill condition:* If no registration increase above background trend is detectable within 30 months of the 2021 registration expansion or the April 2026 order, the claim that supply/scheduling access — as opposed to funding or clinical demand alone — is a binding constraint on trial initiation is falsified.

4. **The self-sealing signature generalizes to psilocybin and MDMA, on a predictable timeline.**
   (i) *Prediction:* Both substances, currently Schedule I, will show the same sequence observed for cannabis but compressed: FDA Breakthrough Therapy designation and National Priority Voucher review (already granted/underway in 2026 for several psilocybin and MDMA-adjacent compounds) will produce approved, patentable, single-molecule or defined-protocol products (following the dronabinol/Epidiolex pattern) years before any generalized rescheduling of the raw substance or naturalistic-use category, because the approval pathway again runs through standardized pharmaceutical form rather than through the whole/naturalistic substance.
   (ii) *Test:* Track FDA approval and DEA scheduling actions for Compass Pathways' COMP360 psilocybin, Usona's psilocybin program, and any resubmitted MDMA-assisted therapy application through 2028; record whether product-specific rescheduling (as with dronabinol 1986, Epidiolex 2018/2020) precedes any change to psilocybin's or MDMA's general Schedule I status by 2+ years.
   (iii) *Kill condition:* If a generalized rescheduling of psilocybin or MDMA (not tied to a specific approved product) occurs before or concurrently with the first product-specific approval, the isolate/plant (or isolate/protocol) discrepancy documented for cannabis in Section 5 does not generalize as predicted.

5. **The "no accepted medical use" finding is measurably more sensitive to registration/access variables than to new toxicological findings.**
   (i) *Prediction:* Across the six major DEA rescheduling denials/actions (1989, 1992, 2001, 2011, 2016, and the 2023 HHS reversal), the stated evidentiary basis for the "accepted medical use" determination should correlate more strongly, over time, with the cumulative count of DEA-registered Schedule I cannabis investigators and completed controlled trials than with any new adverse-event or toxicological finding published in the intervening years.
   (ii) *Test:* Content-code each DEA/HHS scheduling document's cited evidentiary basis (trial count, trial quality, specific toxicological citations) and regress the "accepted medical use" determination (binary, per action) against contemporaneous counts of DEA-registered investigators and completed Phase 2/3 trials, controlling for the toxicological literature's volume and direction.
   (iii) *Kill condition:* If the 2023 HHS reversal cites primarily new toxicological/safety reassurance rather than an increased volume of controlled clinical evidence relative to 1999–2016, or if trial-count growth shows no relationship to the sequence of denials, the access-constraint mechanism proposed in Sections 2–4 is not the operative driver of the "accepted medical use" finding over time.
