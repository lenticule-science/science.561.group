A knowledge repository needs a directory structure that can hold the
full range of what knowledge work involves. The literature surveyed in
the supporting research texts [@asr-research-knowledge-organization-systems;
@asr-research-intellectual-differentiation;
@asr-research-epistemological-structure;
@asr-research-process-ontology; @asr-research-social-learning] does
not converge on a single canonical schema. It does converge on a
set of functional distinctions that any adequate schema must represent.
This text synthesizes those distinctions into four organizing groups
and their constituent directory types.

## 1. Grounding principle: categories as activities

Process philosophy establishes that categories are better understood as
ways of tracking patterns of becoming than as bins for finished things
[@asr-research-process-ontology]. This permits — and the relational
commitment of this project requires — that directory names take gerund
form: they name activities, not substances. A `methods/` directory
holds how-knowledge-is-produced, not an inert category called
"methods." The form is functional.

Peircean semiotics reinforces this. Peirce's triadic structure of
Firstness (quality/possibility), Secondness (reaction/encounter), and
Thirdness (mediation/habit) describes how meaning moves rather than
where it sits [@asr-research-process-ontology]. Trinities of directory
types within each group can be read as covering these three moments
without being mechanically derived from them.

## 2. Four organizing groups

### 2.1 Governing: methods, practices, standards

Philosophy of science establishes that methods, practices, and
standards are analytically distinct though intertwined
[@asr-research-epistemological-structure]:

- **Methods** are generalized inquiry forms — how knowledge is
  supposed to be produced in a given domain. They are prescriptive
  and transportable across situations.
- **Practices** are situated enactments — how knowledge work is
  actually done in context. They include tacit know-how, material
  arrangements, and local routines. Lave and Wenger establish that
  practices are not downstream from knowledge but among the primary
  sites where knowing is formed and transmitted
  [@asr-research-social-learning].
- **Standards** are normative constraints — what counts as adequate
  evidence, responsible conduct, or correct form. They tell
  practitioners what to meet, not only how to proceed. The lived
  account adds that standards often first materialize as interface
  problems: what must I produce for this to be legible to others?
  [@asr-research-lived-experience]

These three are not reducible to each other. A field can share
standards while disagreeing on method. Methods can be formally
specified while practices diverge. The directory types mark these
real distinctions.

### 2.2 Engaging: learning, researching, applying

These are the three modes of knowledge engagement — the ways a
practitioner or community transacts with a body of knowledge:

- **Researching** is knowledge intake from external sources:
  literature engagement, source analysis, citation, evidence
  gathering. The emsemioverse research cadence makes this the first
  stage of any new domain entry.
- **Learning** is knowledge output in accessible form: exposition,
  practical guidance, curricula, and materials that transfer
  knowledge to others. Wenger's situated learning establishes that
  this is not merely distribution of content but a form of
  participation [@asr-research-social-learning]. Shulman's signature
  pedagogies show that how a field teaches encodes its methods,
  practices, and standards simultaneously
  [@asr-research-social-learning].
- **Applying** is knowledge use in concrete situations: case
  analyses, implementations, worked examples, treatments of
  specific problems. This is where theory meets the world.

The cycle among these is iterative: researching fills gaps that
applying reveals; learning structures what researching produces;
applying tests what learning transmits.

### 2.3 Situating: disciplines, schools, lineages

A field subdivides along at least three independent axes
[@asr-research-intellectual-differentiation;
@asr-research-knowledge-organization-systems]:

- **Disciplines** are institutional and jurisdictional subdivisions
  — defined by what subject domain is studied and claimed (Abbott's
  system of professions). A discipline is not primarily a style but
  a scope: what falls within versus outside this field's work.
- **Schools** are epistemic and methodological subdivisions —
  defined by how inquiry is conducted, what counts as evidence, what
  exemplars structure normal work (Kuhn's disciplinary matrix, Fleck's
  thought styles, Lakatos's research programmes). Two researchers
  can share a discipline while belonging to different schools.
- **Lineages** are transmissive subdivisions — defined by
  intellectual descent and derivational chains. What was learned
  from whom, what frameworks are inherited, what derivations carry
  forward the commitments of a prior tradition. Fleck's analysis of
  knowledge carried through apprenticeship and Wenger's account of
  communities of practice as transmission structures both point here.
  In the emsemioverse, lineages are especially salient because
  original contributions (neuroreflexivity, semiotic-markdown) are
  explicitly located by derivational origin rather than by
  conventional disciplinary address.

The distinction among these three matters for directory placement: the
same content may belong to different locations depending on whether its
primary relationship is subject (discipline), approach (school), or
derivation (lineage).

### 2.4 Defining: topics, concepts, terms

Knowledge-organization theory and terminology science establish three
levels of conceptual specificity
[@asr-research-epistemological-structure;
@asr-research-knowledge-organization-systems]:

- **Terms** are specific referents: the linguistic handles that
  stabilize a single encountered thing. Dahlberg's concept theory
  grounds terms as the denominative face of concepts — they make
  concepts communicable. In practice, terms arise first, from
  concrete encounter or observation.
- **Concepts** are units of knowledge: patterns recognized across
  terms, emergent from inferential and theoretical relations. The
  philosophical literature treats concepts as roles in wider
  inferential systems, not merely compressed referents
  [@asr-research-epistemological-structure]. They gather and sharpen
  patterns that terms point at individually.
- **Topics** are aboutness-classes: broad regions of inquiry or
  discussion that gather many concepts and terms under one heading
  (Hjorland on subjects). They name areas of concern rather than
  discrete units of meaning.

The lived-experience account confirms the experiential sequence:
encounter something concrete → assign a term → recognize the pattern
across instances → name the concept → locate it within a region of
concern (topic). The progression is bottom-up, not deductive.

## 3. The structure as a whole

```
governing:   methods/    practices/   standards/
engaging:    learning/   researching/ applying/
situating:   disciplines/ schools/    lineages/
defining:    topics/     concepts/    terms/
```

Each of these twelve directory types is a cross-cutting subdirectory
that may appear within any Thing in a knowledge repository. Their
presence or absence in a given directory signals which aspects of that
Thing's knowledge work have been developed.

The four groups are not parallel in the same way: governing and
engaging are activity-oriented (they name what is DONE), while
situating and defining are structure-oriented (they name what IS
CREATED by those activities). But all twelve names are gerunds or
process-nouns, consistent with the process-ontological commitment that
structural categories name recurrent patterns of activity, not inert
bins.

## 4. What this synthesis does not settle

- Whether the twelve types exhaust the necessary cross-cutting
  directories, or whether domain-specific types (e.g., `proofs/` in
  mathematics, `formalization/` in formal systems) require additional
  cases.
- How these directory types interact with the `texts/` directory
  that already exists widely in the repository. Research argues that
  `researching/` and `learning/` absorb most of what `texts/`
  currently holds, but migration is a separate question from naming.
- Whether `lineages/` is already present implicitly in any existing
  directories, or is a genuinely new type.
- The relationship between `practices/` (a directory type for
  situated doing) and `practices/specification.md` (the normative
  face of a Thing). These share a name for good reason — both are on
  the interface/practice side of the structuration-interface dual —
  but their scope differs.
