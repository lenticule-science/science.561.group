Christopher Gadsden (1724–1805) was a Charleston merchant, radical revolutionary, and enslaver whose name attaches to two structures that still organize American political symbolism: the rattlesnake flag and the wharf.

## The two constructions

- **The flag**: Gadsden sat on the Continental Congress's Marine Committee that gave Commodore Esek Hopkins the yellow "DON'T TREAD ON ME" rattlesnake standard (flown aboard USS *Alfred*, December 1775), and presented a copy to the South Carolina Provincial Congress. Whether he personally *designed* it is disputed — vexillologist Whitney Smith argued the attribution is "often, falsely" made — but the name stuck. Its modern afterlife (Tea Party adoption from ~2009; the 2014–16 EEOC harassment-complaint episode) keeps the flag politically live.
- **The wharf**: Gadsden's Wharf on the Cooper River — 840 feet, the largest wharf in the colonies, built with enslaved labor, completed 1774 — became the principal landing of the transatlantic slave trade's final legal years in the U.S. An 1806 Charleston ordinance *required* slave ships to land there; public-history estimates (used by the International African American Museum, opened on the site June 2023) put roughly 100,000 enslaved Africans — on the order of 40% of all brought to what became the U.S. — through it across 1767–87 and 1803–08, though no published primary count underwrites the figure. Gadsden himself enslaved people at his Charleston properties and Beneventum plantation; his will divides "my negroes" into nineteen shares.

He was also genuinely radical within his politics: Stamp Act Congress delegate arguing Parliament had no jurisdiction at all, Sons of Liberty founder, and — captured at Charleston's fall — a man who refused parole and spent some 42 weeks in solitary at the Castillo de San Marcos rather than give his word to the British.

## Significance for this research

The Gadsden flag's slogan and the Gadsden family's record form a single object lesson in settler political symbolism: the man remembered for "don't tread on me" built the floor the slave trade walked across, and his grandson [James Gadsden](./james-gadsden.md) drew the treading line — through Seminole homelands, then through the O'odham. Useful wherever the research addresses how liberty-symbolism and exclusion-power cohabit in the same institutions (cf. the glossary's property-as-disposition line of argument, and the 2026-08-22 Lochiel history note).
