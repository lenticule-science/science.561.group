# Idle Machines: Time, Attention, Accumulation, and Monetized Return in Idle and Incremental Games

*Prepared from the uploaded research brief: “Pasted text.txt.”*

## Abstract

Idle and incremental games are not merely “games that play themselves.” They are **temporal interfaces** that convert elapsed time, return rituals, optimization decisions, and automated production into play. Their central aesthetic is not action but **supervised accumulation**: the player builds a machine, leaves it running, returns to inspect what it made, and periodically destroys or resets it to make the next machine stronger. The genre’s economic importance follows from the same structure. Waiting can be meaningful, comforting, boring, or coercive; monetization systems then sell acceleration, ad removal, certainty, event participation, prestige efficiency, and relief from the very delays the design has made valuable.

The best idle games make absence feel like participation without making the player feel trapped. The worst ones convert low-pressure companionship into engineered return pressure, opaque pricing, intrusive advertising, and manipulative scarcity. The genre is therefore a useful object for game studies, HCI, behavioral economics, platform studies, and media theory: it reveals how software can turn **time itself** into a resource, fantasy, habit, business model, and ethical problem.

## 1. Genre as play form

Idle and incremental games overlap but are not identical. **Incremental play** emphasizes accumulation, escalating costs, layered upgrades, and “unfolding” systems. **Idle play** emphasizes progress that continues with minimal or no input, including offline or background production. A clicker such as *Cookie Clicker* begins with direct action, but the genre’s decisive shift occurs when the player buys generators and becomes a manager of production rather than a worker of clicks. *Cookie Clicker* has been formally analyzed as a problem of optimal purchasing strategy, with researchers showing that its simple premise can generate nontrivial optimization questions involving approximation and dynamic programming.

The core loop is usually:

1. Produce a resource.
2. Spend it on generators, upgrades, or automation.
3. Wait while production compounds.
4. Return to harvest, optimize, unlock, or reset.
5. Repeat with new multipliers, currencies, or systems.

This loop makes **waiting as play** possible. The player is not simply absent; absence is staged as a productive interval. A return screen that says “you earned X while away” reframes not-playing as participation. HCI research on incremental games describes this pleasure as “playing less”: players plan, delegate, and periodically intervene rather than continuously perform.

Important genre terms:

| Term | Meaning in idle/incremental design |
|---|---|
| **Idle play** | Progress continues while the player is inactive, offline, or attending to something else. |
| **Incremental play** | A system of escalating numbers, upgrades, currencies, and unlocks. |
| **Return loop** | The pleasure of coming back to accumulated value and new decisions. |
| **Automation as reward** | The player earns the right not to repeat earlier actions. |
| **Accumulation fantasy** | A fantasy of compounding growth, abundance, scale, or control. |
| **Optimization play** | The pleasure of calculating best purchases, reset timing, build orders, and multipliers. |
| **Unfolding system** | A design that begins almost empty and gradually reveals new mechanics. |
| **Prestige** | A ritualized loss/reset that converts past progress into permanent future advantage. |
| **Background play** | The game coexists with work, study, commuting, or other media. |
| **Companion game** | A low-pressure persistent system that accompanies daily life. |
| **Spreadsheet play** | Play that shifts into calculators, guides, efficiency ratios, and community tools. |
| **Anti-play/minimal play** | A game form that reduces conventional action and foregrounds systems, waiting, and delegation. |
| **Live-service idle play** | Idle structure plus events, seasons, ads, subscriptions, telemetry, and retention design. |

Calling the genre “passive” is too crude. The player may be passive at the level of moment-to-moment execution but active at the level of **planning, timing, allocation, interpretation, and ritual**. The player is often a supervisor, investor, caretaker, optimizer, machine tender, or godlike allocator.

## 2. Time, waiting, and return

Idle games make time legible. Hours become coins, cookies, entropy, gold, experience, antimatter, eggs, leaves, or paperclips. This is why the return screen matters: it gives elapsed time a receipt.

The pleasure of return has several parts. First, it offers **deferred reward**: something happened without effort. Second, it offers **competence evidence**: the machine worked because the player designed it. Third, it creates **decision compression**: instead of constant input, the player receives a batch of meaningful choices. Fourth, it gives a small feeling of care: the game persisted in one’s absence.

This makes idle games different from ordinary waiting. In a normal timer, waiting blocks action. In an idle game, waiting produces value. The design converts delay into accumulation, then converts accumulation into a new decision.

Prestige is the genre’s most distinctive temporal ritual. It turns loss into renewal. When a player ascends in *Cookie Clicker*, reincarnates in *Realm Grinder*, gains Infinity Points in *Antimatter Dimensions*, or resets for Paragon in *Kittens Game*, the past run is not erased; it is metabolized into a multiplier. *Kittens Game*’s Paragon, for example, is gained through resets with more than a threshold number of kittens and then modifies future production and storage, making reset timing a central strategic question.

Waiting also creates ethical ambiguity. A well-paced idle game says, “come back when you want; your machine will be here.” A manipulative idle game says, “come back now or lose efficiency, event rewards, streak value, or paid boosts.”

## 3. Accumulation, automation, and labor

Idle games are fantasies about work becoming capital. Early actions are often manual: click, tap, hatch, mine, bake, blow leaves. Then the player buys workers, managers, buildings, bots, heroes, algorithms, dimensions, or machines that perform the work automatically. Automation is not an enemy of play; it is often the reward for play.

This creates several labor positions:

| Player role | Example fantasy |
|---|---|
| Worker | Clicking cookies, tapping eggs, manually starting businesses. |
| Manager | Assigning kittens, hiring managers, choosing builds. |
| Owner/investor | Buying businesses, collecting passive income, resetting for investors. |
| Machine tender | Monitoring an automated production chain. |
| Optimizer | Calculating best upgrades and reset timing. |
| Caretaker | Keeping a vault, farm, village, or party alive. |
| God/AI/cosmic force | Scaling from local production to planetary or universal transformation. |

*AdVenture Capitalist* is the clearest capitalist fantasy: the player starts with a lemonade stand, hires employees, buys upgrades, and attracts Angel Investors. Its official description openly frames play as becoming a business owner and building a monopolistic economy. *Universal Paperclips*, by contrast, turns accumulation into a philosophical horror-comedy: the player becomes an AI maximizing paperclip production until value alignment collapses into universal conversion. Wired’s account of Frank Lantz’s design notes the game’s use of exponential growth curves and the paperclip-maximizer premise.

Automation is therefore not one ideological thing. It can be comfort, satire, management fantasy, mathematical toy, care infrastructure, or critique. *Kittens Game* makes automation feel ecological and communal. *Adventure Capitalist* makes it feel like ownership. *Antimatter Dimensions* makes it feel like mathematical recursion. *Universal Paperclips* makes it feel like catastrophic instrumental reason.

## 4. Player affect and habit

Idle games produce a recognizable affective sequence:

**anticipation → return → satisfaction → optimization → plateau → reset/unlock → renewed anticipation.**

Their pleasure is not only “numbers go up,” though that phrase names something real. The deeper pleasure is that the number’s increase confirms a functioning relation between player, system, and time.

Self-determination theory helps explain why idle games can motivate without constant challenge. Ryan, Rigby, and Przybylski found that autonomy and competence are associated with game enjoyment; idle games can satisfy these through choice of build, timing, and long-term planning even when the action demands are low. But SDT can distort the genre if it assumes motivation must come from conventional challenge. Idle games often replace challenge with **calibration**: the player asks not “can I execute this?” but “when is this worth doing?”

Behaviorism and reinforcement schedules explain some checking behavior, but they should not be treated as the whole story. Players also narrate these games as comfort routines, background companions, optimization puzzles, joke systems, and long-term projects. HCI work on idle-game design emphasizes that these games were often jokes, experiments, and ambient systems before they became monetized mobile products.

Boredom is not a failure state; it is a material the genre sculpts. A plateau can be frustrating, but it also gives prestige emotional force. Stagnation says: “this run is exhausted.” Reset says: “begin again, but with memory.”

## 5. Monetization systems

Idle games monetize the same thing they aestheticize: **time under accumulation**.

Common monetized goods include:

| Monetization form | What is sold |
|---|---|
| Rewarded ads | Attention in exchange for boosts, currency, multipliers, revives, or offline gains. |
| Interstitial ads | Forced attention at transitions, returns, level breaks, or failed actions. |
| Banner ads | Persistent screen-space rent. |
| Paid ad removal | Relief from interruption, sometimes while preserving ad rewards. |
| Premium currency | A flexible abstraction that buys skips, boosts, unlocks, cosmetics, or loot. |
| Time skips | Direct conversion of money into elapsed time. |
| Speed multipliers | Increased productivity per unit time. |
| Offline caps | Scarcity placed on absence; payment may extend profitable absence. |
| VIP/subscriptions | A paid temporal class with recurring bonuses, ad-free status, extra rewards, or faster progression. |
| Starter packs | Early acceleration and price anchoring. |
| Events/battle passes | Limited-time goals that transform idle return into scheduled obligation. |
| Gacha/loot boxes | Randomized acquisition, relevant in hybrid RPG/collection idle games. |
| Convenience purchases | Less friction, more automation, more storage, more queueing, better batching. |

Industry-facing sources are explicit about the business logic. Google AdMob presents in-app advertising as a way to let users play for free while developers earn revenue, and describes rewarded ads as part of a hybrid model alongside IAP. Unity describes rewarded ads as opt-in exchanges for in-game rewards and stresses timing, value communication, A/B testing, retention, ARPDAU, and LTV. AppsFlyer’s monetization overview identifies IAP, in-app advertising, subscriptions, paid games, and hybrid models as the main mobile monetization strategies.

In idle games, rewarded ads can feel unusually “native” because the genre already converts time into value. An ad that doubles offline earnings is not external to the loop; it becomes a second prestige-like ritual: return, collect, optionally multiply by watching. This can preserve agency when genuinely optional and transparent. It becomes coercive when the unpaid pace is made tedious so the ad feels mandatory.

A useful economic model is “pay to not play.” Recent research on mobile games argues that paid skips monetize impatience and that high skip prices can coexist with low purchase rates, high revenue, and player utility under certain conditions, especially with a distribution containing a few high spenders. For idle games, this means the product is often not “content” but **temporal relation**: speed, compression, certainty, or relief.

## 6. Ethics and regulation

Ethical idle monetization depends on whether waiting remains meaningful play or becomes manufactured pain.

Fair monetization tends to be:

- transparent about odds, prices, and exchange rates;
- respectful of non-spenders;
- non-punitive about absence;
- careful with children and vulnerable users;
- honest about ad rewards and data collection;
- moderate with notifications;
- clear about subscriptions and renewals;
- designed so purchases accelerate or decorate rather than repair deliberately damaged pacing.

Exploitative monetization tends to involve opaque currencies, artificial friction, manipulative scarcity, FOMO events, misleading odds, child-directed pressure, forced ad density, loss-aversion timers, and telemetry-driven offers tuned to moments of frustration.

Regulators and platforms increasingly treat these systems as consumer-protection issues. Apple requires apps offering loot boxes or other randomized virtual items for purchase to disclose odds before purchase. Google Play similarly requires games offering randomized virtual items from purchase mechanisms to disclose odds in close proximity to purchase, and its policies also cover billing, child safety, ad data practices, and deceptive functionality. The FTC’s 2025 *Genshin Impact* settlement is especially relevant to gacha-adjacent idle games: the agency alleged deception around costs and odds, confusing virtual currencies, child-directed marketing, and COPPA violations; the settlement included a $20 million penalty and restrictions on selling loot boxes to children under 16 without parental consent.

Dark-pattern research also matters. The FTC describes dark patterns as design practices that trick or manipulate consumers into purchases or data sharing. A 2024 study of mobile games reports dark patterns exploiting players through temporal, monetary, social, and psychological means across a large dataset of games. King and Delfabbro argue that loot boxes and microtransactions raise issues of transparency, spending limits, consumer information, and accountability, while warning against reducing every question simply to “is it gambling?”

## 8. Ideology and cultural meaning

Idle games can be capitalist fantasies, critiques of accumulation, mathematical toys, management comfort objects, or jokes about software itself.

The difference between *Cookie Clicker* and *AdVenture Capitalist* is instructive. *Cookie Clicker* begins as absurd domestic production and mutates into cosmic horror, parodying accumulation through excess. *AdVenture Capitalist* aestheticizes investment and ownership more directly: buy, hire, upgrade, dominate. *Universal Paperclips* is more pointed still: it gives the player a single maximization objective and lets instrumental rationality consume everything.

Procedural rhetoric is useful here because idle games argue through rules. Bogost defines procedural rhetoric as persuasion through rule-based processes rather than only words or images. But the framework can overreach if it treats every idle game as having a single authorial thesis. Many idle games are not essays; they are toys, services, habits, jokes, and markets. Their ideology may emerge less from declared meaning than from the way they make growth feel inevitable.

The genre’s relation to capitalism is therefore not “idle games are capitalism simulators.” Better: idle games are **miniature theories of conversion**. They convert labor into automation, time into value, attention into revenue, loss into multiplier, and boredom into anticipation. In F2P mobile contexts, they also convert player impatience and return behavior into monetizable inventory.

## 9. Community and metagame

Idle games often become community math projects. Wikis, calculators, spreadsheets, Discords, Reddit threads, guides, save editors, and scripts turn private waiting into collective optimization.

*Kittens Game* explicitly has community calculators and third-party tools for unicorns, steel, blueprints, metaphysics, energy, science, and save editing. *Antimatter Dimensions* has guides focused on reaching goals quickly, and PC Gamer notes its custom scripting language for automation. Galaxy.click presents itself as an open-source site for finding and socializing around incremental games.

Community knowledge changes the game in four ways:

1. It turns opaque systems into solvable economies.
2. It shifts play from intuition to efficiency.
3. It creates norms around active vs idle legitimacy.
4. It can break intended pacing through scripts, save editing, or calculators.

This is not merely “cheating.” In many incrementals, spreadsheet play is the late-game form. The fantasy of automation extends beyond the game client into community infrastructure.

## 10. Business and traffic economics

Idle games fit modern platforms because they support retention, reactivation, and lightweight sessions. They can be played in short checks, produce many return opportunities, and pair naturally with rewarded ads. Mobile analytics emphasizes retention, ARPDAU, LTV, and A/B testing; academic work on retention and monetization also treats expected sessions, purchases, playtime, and lifetime value as central measurable quantities.

Platform differences matter:

| Platform | Typical economics |
|---|---|
| **Browser/web** | Virality, portals, low friction, community sharing, ads, donations, prestige among enthusiasts. |
| **Steam/PC** | Premium purchase, DLC, achievements, long-session background play, community guides. |
| **Mobile** | Ads, IAP, rewarded video, notifications, offline caps, subscriptions, events, user acquisition campaigns. |
| **Console** | More likely premium or ported experience; less native to ad-watching loops. |

Idle games acquire players through app-store search, platform featuring, ads in other mobile games, influencer/YouTube content, Reddit and Discord recommendations, web portals, Steam tags, and cross-promotion. They retain players through daily rewards, offline accumulation, prestige goals, events, unlock previews, achievement lists, and the emotional promise that progress continues.

The key economic design question is not simply “how often can we show ads?” It is: **when does an ad preserve the meaning of waiting, and when does it replace the game with an attention tollbooth?**

## 11. Worked example: an idle game about autonomous agents producing web content

Imagine a hypothetical idle game called **Agent Farm**, not as a product pitch but as a theory test.

**Core loop.** The player starts with one agent that drafts simple web posts. Posts generate “traffic.” Traffic generates “reputation.” Reputation buys better agents, templates, distribution channels, analytics, and moderation tools.

**What the player does.** The player allocates agents to writing, editing, fact-checking, SEO, image generation, moderation, syndication, and community management. The player decides whether to optimize for traffic, trust, revenue, originality, or safety.

**What happens while idle.** Agents produce drafts, publish scheduled posts, collect feedback, flag problems, and earn traffic. Bad automation may also accumulate misinformation, spam complaints, duplicate content, or moderation debt.

**What accumulates.** Traffic, subscribers, ad revenue, model quality, credibility, technical debt, regulatory risk, and community trust.

**Automation meaning.** Automation is both empowerment and alienation. The player is freed from writing but becomes responsible for systems that speak at scale.

**Prestige meaning.** A reset could be “platform collapse”: the player archives the old content farm, carries forward reputation lessons, safety policies, and better agents, but loses traffic. This makes prestige ethically meaningful: growth without governance collapses.

**What is sold.** Time skips, better agent queues, extra moderation capacity, premium analytics, ad-free UI, cosmetics for dashboards, event passes for trending topics, and maybe “trust insurance.” The ethically risky version sells virality boosts and opaque engagement manipulation.

**What ads change.** Rewarded ads could double traffic for a period, but that would make the game a satire of attention arbitrage. Ads are not neutral here; they become the thematic engine.

**Ethical risks.** Dark patterns, misinformation, privacy leakage, synthetic spam, child-directed content, deceptive analytics, and the conversion of public discourse into an idle revenue surface.

**Ideological story.** The game would ask whether autonomous content production is a care infrastructure, a spam machine, a media business, or an optimizer with no concept of truth.

**Optimization.** Players would build spreadsheets for agent allocation, topic timing, moderation thresholds, virality decay, and reset timing.

**Boredom/coercion.** If the game becomes only “traffic goes up,” it becomes boring. If it threatens loss of streaks, algorithmic rank, or paid event windows, it becomes coercive. Its best design would make trust and care as legible as growth.

## 12. Evaluation framework

A good idle/incremental game should be judged on more than retention.

| Criterion | Good design | Exploitative or weak design |
|---|---|---|
| Decision depth | Meaningful timing, allocation, and build choices. | Only wait or pay. |
| Pacing | Plateaus signal new strategy or reset. | Plateaus exist mainly to sell skips. |
| Automation | Removes repetition and opens new decisions. | Automates only after intolerable grind. |
| Prestige | Loss feels ritual, strategic, and renewing. | Reset is mandatory padding. |
| Clarity | Players understand rates, caps, odds, and currencies. | Obfuscation hides real costs. |
| Low-pressure play | Absence is respected. | Streaks, caps, and notifications punish absence. |
| Monetization | Optional, transparent, fairly priced. | Paywalls, misleading bundles, manipulative scarcity. |
| Ads | Opt-in, well-signposted, nonessential. | Intrusive, excessive, or required for normal pace. |
| Ethics | Data, children, odds, and notifications handled carefully. | Telemetry-driven pressure on vulnerable moments. |
| Thematic coherence | Mechanics express the fantasy. | Theme is a skin over generic monetization. |
| Accessibility | Clear UI, scalable text, offline respect, low motor burden. | Tiny numbers, clutter, inaccessible timers. |
| Community support | Guides and calculators deepen play. | External tools become required to survive opacity. |
| Financial viability | Revenue aligns with player trust. | Revenue depends on frustration or confusion. |

## 13. Open questions

1. How do players distinguish comforting routine from compulsion in their own accounts?
2. When does rewarded advertising become part of the game’s core loop rather than an optional layer?
3. How should offline caps be designed ethically?
4. Can prestige systems model ecological renewal rather than infinite extraction?
5. What does “fair” monetization mean in games where the central commodity is time?
6. How do children understand premium currency, randomized rewards, and time skips?
7. How much telemetry is needed for tuning, and when does tuning become manipulation?
8. Can idle games make care, maintenance, rest, and refusal as satisfying as accumulation?
9. How do calculators and automation scripts change authorship of play?
10. What would a non-extractive idle game economy look like?
