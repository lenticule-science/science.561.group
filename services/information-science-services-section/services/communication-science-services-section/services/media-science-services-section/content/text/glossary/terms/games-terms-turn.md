title: "turn"

A turn is a unit of play in which one [player](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/player.md) (or one team) has the opportunity to act before play passes to the next. Turns impose sequencing on a game — they set who decides when, and they create the rhythm of play.

Not all games use turns. Real-time games (many video games, some party games) allow simultaneous action. Conversational games like many [role-playing games](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/arts/ludics/games/topics/role-playing-games/index.md) use a looser model where play flows like dialogue rather than strict alternation. But turn structure is the most common way to organize player action, and the design of the turn — what you can do during it, how many actions you get, whether you can react on other players&rsquo; turns — is a fundamental [mechanic](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/mechanic.md).

Turn order interacts with information. In sequential turn games, later players have more information (they have seen earlier players&rsquo; choices) but may have fewer options (resources may be claimed). Managing this asymmetry is a core design problem, addressed through mechanisms like rotating first-player, draft order reversal, or compensation resources.
