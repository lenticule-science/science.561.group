Gatekeeping describes how information moving toward an audience passes through, or is blocked at, a series of "gates" controlled by individuals or institutional rules —
a concept originating outside communication research entirely before becoming central to the sociology of news production.

## Origin: Kurt Lewin

Kurt Lewin, "Frontiers in Group Dynamics II: Channels of Group Life; Social Planning and Action Research," *Human Relations* 1(2) (1947), pp. 143–153 ([SAGE record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/001872674700100201)), coined "gatekeeper" in a study of household food-purchasing decisions —
housewives deciding what food reaches the family table —
and generalized the idea that social action flows through channels divided into sections, with a "gate" at each section governed either by impartial rules or by a gatekeeper's decision.
Lewin's exact original wording is now verified directly against the primary text ([Internet Archive full text](https://archive.org/stream/lewin-1947-frontiers-in-group-dynamics/lewin1947_2_djvu.txt), checked 2026-08-22):
"Gate sections are governed either by impartial rules or by 'gate keepers.' In the latter case an individual or group is 'in power' for making the decision between 'in' or 'out.'"
Lewin adds that "the first diagnostic task in such cases is that of finding the actual gate keepers," and reports of the family food-buying channel specifically that the gatekeeper "was found to be most frequently the housewife."

## Application to news: White's "Mr. Gates"

David Manning White, "The Gate Keeper: A Case Study in the Selection of News," *Journalism Quarterly* 27(4) (Fall 1950), pp. 383–390 (page range corrected from "p. 383" on 2026-08-22 verification against Crossref/SAGE; [ProQuest record](https://www.proquest.com/openview/53d57a3356282ea22fdc52dca4e96db4/1?pq-origsite=gscholar&cbl=1818414)), applies Lewin's channel/gate model to a single wire-service news editor,
documenting the subjective criteria by which he selected which wire-service stories to run.
It is the foundational empirical gatekeeping study in journalism research, though it is a case study of one editor rather than a large-N study —
a scope limitation worth naming whenever it is cited as evidentiary support for a general gatekeeping claim.

## Multi-level synthesis

Pamela Shoemaker & Timothy Vos, *Gatekeeping Theory* (Routledge, 2009) ([Routledge record](https://www.routledge.com/Gatekeeping-Theory/Shoemaker-Vos/p/book/9780415981392)), is the standard contemporary synthesis, extending gatekeeping beyond individual editorial choice to a five-level model —
individual, communication routines, organizational, social-institutional, and social-system levels —
describing how information is filtered at each stage of the news-production process.
It is a scholarly monograph rather than an original empirical study.
