This specification defines the structural features that distinguish role-playing games from other game types. It provides constitutive criteria — the features whose presence or absence decides whether a game *is* an RPG — and descriptive criteria — features that are common but not definitional.

## Constitutive features

A game is a role-playing game if and only if:

- **Character portrayal**: participants adopt fictional personas ([characters](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/arts/ludics/games/terms/character.md)) and make decisions as those personas within a shared fiction. The player-character relationship is the primary interface between participant and game.

- **Shared fiction**: the [game state](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/game-state.md) includes a narrative world that all participants contribute to and reason about. The fiction is not merely decorative — it constrains and enables player action.

- **Open-ended action**: players can try any action their character might plausibly perform within the fiction, not only actions enumerated by the [rules](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/rules.md). The [system](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/logic-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/undated/system.md) provides resolution for uncertain outcomes, but the action space is defined by the fiction, not by a closed set of mechanical options.

## Common but non-constitutive features

These features appear in most RPGs but aren&rsquo;t definitional:

- **[Game master](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/services/role-playing-games-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/game-master.md) role**: most RPGs assign one participant the role of guide, narrator, and referee. But GM-less RPGs distribute this authority and are still RPGs.

- **Dice or randomization**: most RPGs use dice or other randomizers for resolution. But some diceless RPGs resolve uncertainty through narrative authority or resource spending.

- **[Campaign](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/arts/ludics/games/terms/campaign.md) structure**: most RPGs support extended play across multiple [sessions](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/arts/ludics/games/terms/session.md). But one-shot games are still RPGs.

- **Character advancement**: many RPGs include mechanical progression (gaining skills, levels, abilities over time). But games without progression are still RPGs.

## Boundary cases

- **Board games with character roles** (e.g., Betrayal at House on the Hill): these assign character identities but typically constrain action to mechanical options. The fiction is decorative rather than constitutive. Usually not RPGs by this specification.

- **Collaborative storytelling games without systems** (e.g., pure improv): these have character portrayal and shared fiction but lack a resolution system. Whether they count as RPGs depends on whether the group treats any constraints as [rules](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/rules.md). The boundary is fuzzy here.

- **Live-action role-playing (LARP)**: meets all three constitutive criteria. LARPs are RPGs; the medium of play (physical embodiment vs. table conversation) is a difference of format, not kind.

## Relationship to parent discipline

The constitutive features map to the general game features defined in [What is a Game?](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/arts/curricula/what-is-a-game.md): RPGs have rules (the system), objectives (character goals, narrative stakes), uncertainty (resolution mechanics, other players&rsquo; choices, GM decisions), and voluntariness. The RPG-specific addition is that the participant&rsquo;s relationship to the game is mediated through a fictional persona.
