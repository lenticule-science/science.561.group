This is a study of media science as a subject of formal research, read for one purpose:
to establish, for each of thirteen sub-literatures, which claims trace to peer-reviewed foundations versus which trace to textbook/industry canon,
so that later terms and notes in this section cite the right kind of source for the right kind of claim.
Method: a literature search with citations verified by web search where possible;
every load-bearing claim below carries its source as a markdown link (DOI, publisher, or stable URL),
and any claim this study could not verify to the primary-source level is marked **[UNVERIFIED]** inline rather than laundered into fact.

## 1. Foundational transmission models

Harold Lasswell's "The Structure and Function of Communication in Society," in L. Bryson (ed.), *The Communication of Ideas* (Harper and Row, 1948), is the field's most quoted opening formula —
"who says what in which channel to whom with what effect" —
and functions less as a testable theory than as a checklist for organizing communication research into sub-fields (control studies, content analysis, media analysis, audience analysis, effects analysis) ([Wikipedia summary, cross-checked against Semantic Scholar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasswell's_model_of_communication); [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Harold-D.-Lasswell-Reading:-Lasswell,-H.D.-(1948)-Mills-Barlow/a3393e652b083f87f7e6363f64fe60e4e5825eba)).

Claude Shannon's "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," *Bell System Technical Journal* 27 (1948), pp. 379–423 and 623–656 (DOI: [10.1002/j.1538-7305.1948.tb01338.x](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1948.tb01338.x)),
was reissued with an interpretive essay as Shannon & Weaver, *The Mathematical Theory of Communication* (University of Illinois Press, 1949) ([publisher record via Scientific Research Publishing](https://www.scirp.org/reference/ReferencesPapers?ReferenceID=1311827)).
Shannon's own concern was engineering —
how much signal a channel can carry before noise degrades it —
not meaning or human relationship;
Weaver's essay in the 1949 volume itself names three distinct levels of communication problem (A: technical accuracy, B: semantic meaning, C: effectiveness), stating plainly that Shannon's mathematical theory solves only level A, and that "the concept of information developed in this theory at first seems disappointing and bizarre —
disappointing because it has nothing to do with meaning" ([PMC7822471, "Information Theory for Human and Social Processes"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7822471/); primary text via [Max Planck Institute PDF](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2383164_3/component/file_2383163/Shannon_Weaver_1949_Mathematical.pdf)).
This is the field's own internal caution against over-applying the mathematical model to human communication, not a later corrective added by critics —
Weaver's discomfort is in the founding document itself.

## 2. Limited-effects tradition

Paul Lazarsfeld, Bernard Berelson & Hazel Gaudet, *The People's Choice: How the Voter Makes Up His Mind in a Presidential Campaign* (Duell, Sloan & Pearce, 1944; kept in print by Columbia University Press since 1948),
reports a panel study of Erie County, Ohio, voters across the 1940 campaign and finds that face-to-face contact with other people shaped vote decisions more than direct media exposure did —
the empirical basis of the "limited effects" turn away from earlier direct-effects ("hypodermic needle") assumptions ([Columbia University Press](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-peoples-choice/9780231197953/); [ResearchGate summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/404414700_Two-Step_Flow_of_Communication_Model_The_People's_Choice_How_the_Voter_Makes_Up_His_Mind_in_a_Presidential_Campaign_-_by_Paul_Felix_Lazarsfeld_Bernard_Berelson_Hazel_Gaudet_1944)).

Elihu Katz & Paul Lazarsfeld, *Personal Influence: The Part Played by People in the Flow of Mass Communications* (Free Press, 1955),
reports the Decatur, Illinois study (marketing, fashion, public-affairs, and movie-selection decisions) and formalizes the **two-step flow** hypothesis:
media influence reaches opinion leaders first, who then relay and reinterpret it to less-attentive members of their own social networks, so interpersonal ties mediate media effects rather than media reaching a mass audience directly ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Personal-Influence); [full-text PDF via ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Andreas-Hepp-3/publication/328078453_KatzLazarsfeld_1955_Personal_Influence/links/5e4c8e4b92851c7f7f456bd2/Katz-Lazarsfeld-1955-Personal-Influence.pdf)).
Both works are peer-adjacent Columbia Bureau of Applied Social Research studies rather than journal articles, but both are uncontroversially treated as the field's peer-reviewed-grade foundational empirical base for limited effects and two-step flow;
no textbook-canon substitute was needed for this sub-literature.

## 3. Agenda-setting

Maxwell McCombs & Donald Shaw, "The Agenda-Setting Function of Mass Media," *Public Opinion Quarterly* 36(2) (1972), pp. 176–187 (DOI: [10.1086/267990](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article-abstract/36/2/176/1853310)), is the Chapel Hill study:
undecided voters in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, were surveyed on their top five issue priorities in the 1968 US presidential election, and the ranking correlated with the volume of coverage those same issues received across nine local and national news outlets —
establishing agenda-setting's core claim that media do not tell people *what to think* but strongly influence *what to think about*.
The article has been cited in more than 6,000 subsequent studies, per the same source.

Second-level (attribute) agenda-setting —
the claim that media also transfer the salience of specific *attributes* of an issue or candidate, not just the salience of the issue itself —
traces to Maxwell McCombs, Juan Pablo Llamas, Esteban Lopez-Escobar & Federico Rey, "Candidate Images in Spanish Elections: Second-Level Agenda-Setting Effects," *Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly* 74(4) (1997), pp. 703–717 (DOI: [10.1177/107769909707400404](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/107769909707400404), now independently verified against Crossref),
a content-analysis-and-survey study of the 1995 Spanish regional and municipal elections.
The earlier draft of this note characterized the citation only informally, as "commonly cited as McCombs, Llamas, Lopez-Escobar & Rey on the 1996 Spanish elections";
that was imprecise on both the publication year (1997, not 1996) and the election studied (1995 regional/municipal elections, not a 1996 election), now corrected here on direct verification.

Dietram Scheufele & David Tewksbury, "Framing, Agenda Setting, and Priming: The Evolution of Three Media Effects Models," *Journal of Communication* 57(1) (2007), pp. 9–20 (DOI: [10.1111/j.0021-9916.2007.00326.x](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.0021-9916.2007.00326.x)), is the field's own reference for distinguishing the three models:
agenda-setting concerns issue *salience* transfer, framing concerns how an issue's *presentation* shapes interpretation, and priming concerns how accessible considerations (made salient by media exposure) are weighted when people form judgments later —
three related but analytically distinct effects, per this article's own framing of its purpose as an introductory essay for a themed *Journal of Communication* issue.

## 4. Framing

Erving Goffman, *Frame Analysis: An Essay on the Organization of Experience* (Harper and Row, 1974), supplies framing's sociological root:
"frames" as the interpretive structures ("what is going on here?") people use to organize experience and determine what counts as real in a given situation ([WorldCat record](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Frame-analysis-:-an-essay-on-the-organization-of-experience/oclc/587258315); [Internet Archive copy](https://archive.org/details/frameanalysisess0000goff_u8p5)).
This is general sociology, not communication research specifically, and is the textbook-canon anchor for the *concept* of framing prior to its media-effects operationalization.

Robert Entman, "Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm," *Journal of Communication* 43(4) (1993), pp. 51–58 (DOI: [10.1111/j.1460-2466.1993.tb01304.x](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1993.tb01304.x); full text via [UNC](https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/J-Communication-1993-Entman.pdf)), is the peer-reviewed anchor that translated Goffman's concept into a communication-research definition:
"to frame is to select some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more salient in a communicating text, in such a way as to promote a particular problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, and/or treatment recommendation."
Dennis Chong & James Druckman, "Framing Theory," *Annual Review of Political Science* 10 (2007), pp. 103–126 (DOI: [10.1146/annurev.polisci.10.072805.103054](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.polisci.10.072805.103054)),
reviews framing-effects research since Entman and supplies a psychological model (competitive framing, frame strength, and individual-level moderators of framing effects) for how frames influence public-opinion formation.

## 5. Gatekeeping

Kurt Lewin, "Frontiers in Group Dynamics II: Channels of Group Life; Social Planning and Action Research," *Human Relations* 1(2) (1947), pp. 143–153 ([SAGE record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/001872674700100201)), coins "gatekeeper" in a study of household food-purchasing decisions —
housewives as gatekeepers deciding what food reaches the family table —
and generalizes the idea that social action flows through channels divided into sections, with a "gate" at each section governed either by impartial rules or by a gatekeeper's decision.
Lewin's exact wording is now verified directly against the primary text ([Internet Archive full text](https://archive.org/stream/lewin-1947-frontiers-in-group-dynamics/lewin1947_2_djvu.txt)):
"Gate sections are governed either by impartial rules or by 'gate keepers.' In the latter case an individual or group is 'in power' for making the decision between 'in' or 'out.'"
Lewin adds that "the first diagnostic task in such cases is that of finding the actual gate keepers," and reports of the family food-buying channel specifically that the gatekeeper "was found to be most frequently the housewife."
This confirms the secondary-source paraphrase this note previously relied on.

David Manning White, "The Gate Keeper: A Case Study in the Selection of News," *Journalism Quarterly* 27(4) (Fall 1950), pp. 383–390 (page range corrected from "p. 383" on verification against [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/107769905002700403) and [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/107769905002700403); [ProQuest record](https://www.proquest.com/openview/53d57a3356282ea22fdc52dca4e96db4/1?pq-origsite=gscholar&cbl=1818414)),
applies Lewin's channel/gate model to a wire-service news editor ("Mr. Gates") and is the field's foundational empirical gatekeeping study —
a case study of one editor's subjective selection criteria rather than a large-N study, a scope limitation worth naming when citing it as evidentiary support.

Pamela Shoemaker & Timothy Vos, *Gatekeeping Theory* (Routledge, 2009) ([Routledge record](https://www.routledge.com/Gatekeeping-Theory/Shoemaker-Vos/p/book/9780415981392)), is the standard contemporary synthesis,
extending gatekeeping from individual editorial decisions to a multi-level model (individual, routine, organizational, social-institutional, and social-system levels) of how information passes through or is blocked at each level of the news-production system.
This is a scholarly monograph rather than an original empirical study, functioning as this sub-literature's textbook-canon anchor alongside White's peer-reviewed case study.

## 6. Cultivation

George Gerbner & Larry Gross, "Living with Television: The Violence Profile," *Journal of Communication* 26(2) (1976), pp. 172–199 (DOI: [10.1111/j.1460-2466.1976.tb01397.x](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1976.tb01397.x)), is cultivation theory's founding paper:
heavy television viewers were found to overestimate real-world violence and danger relative to light viewers,
supporting the claim that long-term, cumulative television exposure "cultivates" viewers' perceptions of social reality toward the patterns depicted on screen, rather than producing short-term attitude or behavior change in the manner classic effects research looked for.

Michael Morgan & James Shanahan, "Two Decades of Cultivation Research: An Appraisal and Meta-Analysis," *Communication Yearbook* 20 (1997), pp. 1–45 (DOI: [10.1080/23808985.1997.11678937](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/23808985.1997.11678937)), meta-analyzed the accumulated cultivation literature and reported a small average cultivation effect size (r ≈ .09) —
a real but modest correlation, an important qualifier against overstating cultivation's effect magnitude in a term entry.
Morgan & Shanahan later revisited the theory's status in "The State of Cultivation," *Journal of Broadcasting & Electronic Media* 54(2) (2010), pp. 337–355 (DOI: [10.1080/08838151003735018](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/08838151003735018), now independently verified against Crossref),
reviewing over 125 new cultivation studies published since 2000;
the 1997 meta-analysis above remains the load-bearing effect-size figure, since this 2010 follow-up is a narrative appraisal rather than a re-run of the meta-analysis itself.
Whether the small average effect generalizes past broadcast-era "television" viewing (a single, culturally shared medium at the time of Gerbner's original studies) to today's fragmented, on-demand/streaming/social-video environment is not established by this literature and is carried forward as a gap below.

## 7. Uses and gratifications

Elihu Katz, Jay Blumler & Michael Gurevitch, "Uses and Gratifications Research," *Public Opinion Quarterly* 37(4) (Winter 1973), pp. 509–523 (DOI: [10.1086/268109](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article-abstract/37/4/509/1816598)), is the field's foundational statement of the uses-and-gratifications approach:
rather than asking "what do media do to people," it asks "what do people do with media,"
treating audiences as active, goal-directed users who select media content to satisfy social and psychological needs (information seeking, personal identity, integration/social interaction, entertainment).
This inverts the implicit passivity assumption running through the limited-effects and cultivation traditions above,
making it a useful pivot point when a later term needs to contrast active-audience against effects-centered models.

## 8. Encoding/decoding and reception

Stuart Hall, "Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse" (Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies stencilled paper no. 7, 1973), later revised as "Encoding/decoding" in Stuart Hall, Dorothy Hobson, Andrew Lowe & Paul Willis (eds.), *Culture, Media, Language: Working Papers in Cultural Studies, 1972–79* (CCCS/Routledge, 1980), pp. 128–138 ([ResearchGate citation record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/290735500_19801973_'Encoding_decoding'_In_Culture_Media_Language_Working_Papers_in_Cultural_Studies_1972-1979_ed_by_Centre_for_Contemporary_Cultural_Studies_128-138_London_Routledge)),
argues that a televisual (or any mediated) message is "encoded" by its producers with a preferred meaning, but audiences actively "decode" it from one of (at least) three structural positions:
dominant-hegemonic (accepting the preferred reading), negotiated (partially accepting, partially contesting it), or oppositional (rejecting the preferred reading and substituting a counter-reading).
This is the founding text of British cultural studies' reception tradition and stands apart from the US behavioral-effects tradition (agenda-setting, cultivation, uses-and-gratifications) in method —
textual/structural analysis rather than survey or experimental measurement —
a distinction worth preserving when this section's terms cite it.

## 9. Medium theory

Harold Innis's communications-theory work —
most centrally *The Bias of Communication* (University of Toronto Press, 1951; reissued 1964 with an introductory essay by Marshall McLuhan) —
argues that a medium's physical properties bias the *kind* of society and knowledge it supports:
"time-biased" media (stone, parchment, oral tradition) favor durability, continuity, and religious/traditional authority,
while "space-biased" media (paper, print, electronic transmission) favor rapid movement across distance, administrative/commercial control, and secular/present-oriented power ([Wikipedia summary of Innis's communications theories, cross-checked against Canadian Journal of Communication scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Innis's_communications_theories); [Canadian Journal of Communication, "The McLuhan-Innis Field"](https://cjc.utppublishing.com/doi/full/10.22230/cjc.2019v44n4a3719)).

Marshall McLuhan, *Understanding Media: The Extensions of Man* (McGraw-Hill, 1964), builds directly on Innis and crystallizes the argument into "the medium is the message" —
that a medium's form, not its content, is the primary shaper of social and psychological effects.
*Understanding Media* is canonical across media studies and is cited constantly in this field, but it is essayistic and aphoristic argument rather than an empirical study with testable hypotheses or data;
it should be cited in this section's terms as canonical media theory, not as peer-reviewed social science, a distinction the source material itself supports by describing McLuhan's style as "provocative and aphoristic" against Innis's "dense, historically exhaustive, and cautionary" scholarship.

## 10. Sociology of news production

Gaye Tuchman, *Making News: A Study in the Construction of Reality* (Free Press, 1978) ([Internet Archive copy](https://archive.org/details/makingnewsstudyi0000tuch)), is an ethnographic study of newsroom routines arguing that "news" is not a neutral mirror of events but an organizationally constructed product,
shaped by routinized practices (beats, deadlines, source relationships, "typification" of stories into recurring categories) that make some events reportable and others invisible.

Herbert Gans, *Deciding What's News: A Study of CBS Evening News, NBC Nightly News, Newsweek, and Time* (Pantheon Books, 1979),
reports ten years of participant observation across four major national news organizations (spanning the War on Poverty, Vietnam, MLK's and RFK's assassinations, and Watergate) and identifies the professional values and organizational/economic pressures shaping story selection —
a direct empirical complement to Tuchman's more theoretical framing.

Michael Schudson, *Discovering the News: A Social History of American Newspapers* (Basic Books, 1978), argues historically that "objective" news reporting was not a timeless journalistic ideal but a socially-produced norm that emerged alongside 19th-century democratization of American political, economic, and social life —
situating journalistic objectivity itself as a historical artifact rather than a natural professional baseline.
Together these three works form the peer-reviewed-grade sociological/historical core this section should cite for any claim about *why* news looks the way it does,
as distinct from *what effect* it then has on audiences (covered in §§1–8 above).

## 11. Political economy

Edward Herman & Noam Chomsky, *Manufacturing Consent: The Political Economy of the Mass Media* (Pantheon Books, 1988), proposes the "propaganda model":
five structural filters (ownership/profit orientation, advertising dependence, reliance on official/elite sources, "flak" as a disciplining mechanism, and anti-communist/ideological framing, later broadened)
that the authors argue systematically shape US mass media output toward elite-congenial content, independent of any individual conspiracy ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing_Consent)).

The model's empirical status is explicitly contested rather than settled:
critics (for example Schlesinger, discussed in a 2010 *Sociology Compass* review of the model — [Mullen, "The Herman–Chomsky Propaganda Model," DOI 10.1111/j.1751-9020.2010.00275.x](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1751-9020.2010.00275.x)) argue it is overly deterministic,
leaves too little room for genuine adversarial journalism,
presents an overly "totalising" view of media systems,
and was developed in and may not generalize cleanly beyond a US media context.
Herman and Chomsky's own response, per the same review, is that the filters make critical journalism less likely and structurally harder, not impossible —
a qualification worth preserving in any term that cites the model, since the strong reading ("media simply manufacture elite consent, full stop") over-states the authors' own stated claim.

## 12. Spiral of silence

Elisabeth Noelle-Neumann, "The Spiral of Silence: A Theory of Public Opinion," *Journal of Communication* 24(2) (1974), pp. 43–51 (DOI: [10.1111/j.1460-2466.1974.tb00367.x](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1974.tb00367.x)),
proposes that individuals who perceive their opinion to be in the minority (via a quasi-statistical "sense" of the climate of opinion, partly informed by media coverage) become progressively less willing to voice that opinion publicly for fear of social isolation, which in turn makes the perceived-minority view appear even less represented in public discourse —
a self-reinforcing spiral.
This is a survey/social-psychological theory of public-opinion dynamics distinct from the media-content-effects theories in §§3–6,
since the mechanism runs through individuals' fear of social isolation rather than through direct persuasion by message content.

## 13. The contemporary computational turn (contested)

Eytan Bakshy, Solomon Messing & Lada Adamic, "Exposure to Ideologically Diverse News and Opinion on Facebook," *Science* 348(6239) (2015), pp. 1130–1132 (DOI: [10.1126/science.aaa1160](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aaa1160)),
analyzed 10.1 million US Facebook users and found that friends shared substantially less cross-cutting (opposing-ideology) news than same-ideology news.
**Correction (2026-08-22 verification pass):** an earlier draft of this note reported that "Facebook's News Feed algorithm reduced cross-cutting content by roughly 15%" and that "individual click behavior reduced cross-cutting exposure further, by roughly 70%";
those figures were wrong and are corrected here on direct verification against the primary text.
The paper itself reports considerably smaller, and asymmetric, risk-ratio reductions:
algorithmic ranking reduced the probability of encountering cross-cutting content by about 5% for self-identified conservatives and 8% for liberals, and individual click choices reduced cross-cutting exposure further by about 17% for conservatives and 6% for liberals.
This is a study frequently cited as partial evidence for algorithmic echo-chamber effects, though the authors themselves state that individual choice played a stronger overall role than the algorithm in limiting exposure to cross-cutting content.

Andrew Guess, "(Almost) Everything in Moderation: New Evidence on Americans' Online Media Diets," *American Journal of Political Science* 65(4) (2021), pp. 1007–1022 (DOI: [10.1111/ajps.12589](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ajps.12589), now independently verified against Crossref and the published abstract),
finds that "echo chamber" and "filter bubble" narratives are considerably overstated for most users:
most people across the political spectrum have relatively moderate online media diets, with Democrats' and Republicans' media-diet distributions overlapping by roughly 65% in 2015 and roughly 50% in 2016,
and it is a small minority of highly engaged partisans, rather than the typical user, who drive a disproportionate share of traffic to ideologically slanted sites.
**Note on one figure:** the earlier draft of this note also attributed to Guess (2021) a specific claim that "without social media, average news consumers were only about 3.5 percentage points more selectively exposed to like-minded information than someone reading a representative bundle of outlets."
That figure could not be re-located in the Guess (2021) abstract or in the portions of the paper checked during this verification pass;
it appears in secondary discussions grouped with citations to Flaxman, Goel & Rao (2016) and Gentzkow & Shapiro (2011) as well as Guess (2021), so it may belong to one of those other studies rather than to Guess (2021) specifically.
The "3.5 percentage points" figure itself is therefore marked **[UNVERIFIED]** and should not be attributed to Guess (2021) without a direct check of that paper's full text.
The rest of the Guess (2021) citation and its central findings, above, are independently verified.
This directly complicates rather than confirms the strong popular reading of Bakshy/Messing/Adamic.
This sub-literature is flagged as actively contested:
study design, platform/data access, and operationalization of "polarization" or "selective exposure" vary widely across this body of work,
and this study did not attempt to adjudicate between competing findings, only to note that both exist and disagree in emphasis.
