Cultivation theory holds that heavy, cumulative exposure to television gradually shapes viewers' beliefs about the real world toward the patterns of danger, violence, and social order that television content depicts,
distinct from the short-term attitude or behavior change that earlier effects research looked for and generally failed to find at scale.

## Founding study

George Gerbner & Larry Gross, "Living with Television: The Violence Profile," *Journal of Communication* 26(2) (1976), pp. 172–199 (DOI: [10.1111/j.1460-2466.1976.tb01397.x](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1976.tb01397.x)), found that heavy television viewers overestimated real-world violence and danger relative to light viewers —
the empirical basis for the theory's associated "mean world syndrome" claim, that heavy viewing cultivates an exaggerated sense of a dangerous, threatening world.

## Meta-analytic effect size

Michael Morgan & James Shanahan, "Two Decades of Cultivation Research: An Appraisal and Meta-Analysis," *Communication Yearbook* 20 (1997), pp. 1–45 (DOI: [10.1080/23808985.1997.11678937](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/23808985.1997.11678937)), meta-analyzed the accumulated cultivation literature and reported a small average cultivation effect size (r ≈ .09) —
real but modest, an important qualifier against overstating cultivation's effect magnitude when this term is cited elsewhere.
A later Morgan & Shanahan follow-up, "The State of Cultivation," *Journal of Broadcasting & Electronic Media* 54(2) (2010), pp. 337–355 (DOI: [10.1080/08838151003735018](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/08838151003735018), independently verified 2026-08-22),
reviews over 125 new cultivation studies published since 2000;
the 1997 meta-analysis above remains the load-bearing effect-size citation, since the 2010 piece is a narrative appraisal rather than a new meta-analysis.

## Open question: generalizability past broadcast television

Gerbner's original studies assumed a single, culturally shared medium —
broadcast television with a small number of channels and a common schedule across viewers.
Whether the theory's small average effect size generalizes to today's fragmented, on-demand, streaming, and social-video environment, where "heavy viewing" no longer implies exposure to a shared set of patterns,
is not established in the literature surveyed for this section and is carried forward as an open gap.
