A hand is the set of cards currently held by a [player](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/player.md) as private information in a [card game](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/arts/ludics/games/index.md). The hand is the player&rsquo;s primary resource: it defines what actions are available, what combinations are possible, and what information the player holds that opponents don&rsquo;t. In poker, the hand is what you are betting on. In a trick-taking game, the hand is the sequence of plays you have available across an entire round.

Hand management — deciding what to play now versus what to preserve for later — is the core strategic problem in most card games. Every card committed to a [trick](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/trick.md), [meld](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/services/card-games-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/meld.md), or discard is a card no longer available for future use. This creates a planning horizon: players must balance immediate tactical needs (winning this trick, completing this meld) against future strategic needs (holding a strong card for a critical moment, keeping flexibility for unknown situations). The tension between spending and saving is what makes card games feel like resource management even when the only resource is information.

Hand size and replenishment rules shape this tension. A large hand offers more options but is harder to optimize. A small hand forces faster commitments and higher-stakes individual decisions. Games that replenish hands from a [draw pile](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/draw-pile.md) extend the planning horizon by introducing new cards; games that deal all cards at the start (as in many trick-taking games) create a fixed horizon where every card in your hand is all you will ever have. Whether hands are hidden (standard in most games) or partially revealed (open-hand variants, games where some cards are played face up) changes the information structure of the entire game. See also [hand](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/hand.md) in the poker context, where the term refers specifically to the cards a player holds for evaluation against a ranking system.
