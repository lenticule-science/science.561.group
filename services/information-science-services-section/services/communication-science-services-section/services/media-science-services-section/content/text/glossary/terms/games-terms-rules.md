Rules are the constraints that define what actions are permitted, prohibited, or required within a game. They create the space of meaningful choice by limiting what [players](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/player.md) can do, which is what makes a game a game rather than free [play](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/play.md).

Rules need not be written. Children&rsquo;s games often operate on negotiated consensus; folk games transmit rules orally across generations; and many social games rely on implicit norms that only become visible when someone violates them. What matters is not the format but the function: rules set up shared expectations about how the game works.

A useful distinction is between *constitutive rules* (which define what the game is — chess without the movement rules is not chess) and *regulatory rules* (which govern conduct — tournament etiquette, time limits, sportsmanship norms). Constitutive rules determine the [mechanics](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/mechanic.md); regulatory rules determine the social context. [House rules](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/arts/ludics/games/archive/house-rule.md) sit in between, modifying constitutive rules by local agreement.
