Framing is the process by which a message's presentation —
which aspects of a situation are selected and made salient —
shapes how an audience interprets it, independent of any change in the underlying facts reported.
Its sociological root is Erving Goffman, *Frame Analysis: An Essay on the Organization of Experience* (Harper and Row, 1974) ([WorldCat record](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Frame-analysis-:-an-essay-on-the-organization-of-experience/oclc/587258315)),
which treats "frames" as the interpretive structures people use to answer the question "what is going on here?" and to determine what counts as real in a given situation.
Goffman's work is general sociology rather than communication research, and predates the concept's media-effects operationalization by two decades.

## The communication-research definition

Robert Entman, "Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm," *Journal of Communication* 43(4) (1993), pp. 51–58 (DOI: [10.1111/j.1460-2466.1993.tb01304.x](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1993.tb01304.x)), is the peer-reviewed anchor that translated Goffman's sociological concept into the field's working definition:
"to frame is to select some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more salient in a communicating text, in such a way as to promote a particular problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, and/or treatment recommendation for the item described."
Entman's own title names the paradigm as "fractured" —
the concept was, at the time, used inconsistently enough across communication research to need this clarifying synthesis.

## Framing effects on opinion

Dennis Chong & James Druckman, "Framing Theory," *Annual Review of Political Science* 10 (2007), pp. 103–126 (DOI: [10.1146/annurev.polisci.10.072805.103054](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.polisci.10.072805.103054)), reviews the accumulated framing-effects literature and supplies a psychological model —
accounting for competitive framing (multiple frames competing for influence), frame strength, and individual-level moderators —
for how exposure to a frame shapes an audience's subsequent opinion formation.

## Distinguishing framing from agenda-setting and priming

Framing is frequently confused with the related but distinct agenda-setting and priming models;
see [`agenda-setting.md`](./agenda-setting.md) for the standard three-way distinction (Scheufele & Tewksbury, 2007).
