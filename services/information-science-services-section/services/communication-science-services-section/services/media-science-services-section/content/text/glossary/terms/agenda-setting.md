Agenda-setting is the theory that mass media coverage shapes public priorities not by dictating opinions but by determining which issues audiences regard as important enough to think about at all.
Its founding empirical study is Maxwell McCombs & Donald Shaw, "The Agenda-Setting Function of Mass Media," *Public Opinion Quarterly* 36(2) (1972), pp. 176–187 (DOI: [10.1086/267990](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article-abstract/36/2/176/1853310)):
undecided voters in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, were surveyed on their top five issue priorities during the 1968 US presidential campaign, and the resulting ranking correlated with the volume of coverage those same issues received across nine local and national news outlets.
The article has since been cited in more than 6,000 subsequent studies.

## Second-level (attribute) agenda-setting

A later elaboration argues media also transfer the salience of specific *attributes* of an issue or candidate — not merely which issues matter, but which characteristics of those issues are foregrounded.
This extension traces to Maxwell McCombs, Juan Pablo Llamas, Esteban Lopez-Escobar & Federico Rey, "Candidate Images in Spanish Elections: Second-Level Agenda-Setting Effects," *Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly* 74(4) (1997), pp. 703–717 (DOI: [10.1177/107769909707400404](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/107769909707400404)), a study of the 1995 Spanish regional and municipal elections.
This citation was independently pulled and DOI-verified in a 2026-08-22 verification pass; an earlier draft of this term described it only informally, as "a 1996 study of Spanish elections," which was imprecise on both the publication year and the election studied.

## Distinguishing agenda-setting from framing and priming

Dietram Scheufele & David Tewksbury, "Framing, Agenda Setting, and Priming: The Evolution of Three Media Effects Models," *Journal of Communication* 57(1) (2007), pp. 9–20 (DOI: [10.1111/j.0021-9916.2007.00326.x](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.0021-9916.2007.00326.x)), is the field's standard reference for keeping these three models separate: agenda-setting concerns issue *salience* transfer; framing (see [`framing.md`](./framing.md)) concerns how a message's presentation shapes interpretation of an issue already on the agenda; priming concerns how media exposure makes certain considerations more accessible when audiences later form judgments (for example, evaluating a president).
Treating these as interchangeable synonyms is a common error this section's terms should avoid.
