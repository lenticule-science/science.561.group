Encoding/decoding is a model of mediated communication in which a message (originally: televisual discourse) is "encoded" by its producers with a preferred meaning reflecting dominant cultural and institutional assumptions, but is then actively "decoded" by audiences —
who are not passive receivers but interpreters working from their own social position.

## Origin and formulation

Stuart Hall, "Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse" (Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies stencilled paper no. 7, 1973), later revised as "Encoding/decoding" in Stuart Hall, Dorothy Hobson, Andrew Lowe & Paul Willis (eds.), *Culture, Media, Language: Working Papers in Cultural Studies, 1972–79* (CCCS/Routledge, 1980), pp. 128–138 ([citation record via ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/290735500_19801973_'Encoding_decoding'_In_Culture_Media_Language_Working_Papers_in_Cultural_Studies_1972-1979_ed_by_Centre_for_Contemporary_Cultural_Studies_128-138_London_Routledge)), proposes three structural decoding positions available to an audience member:
**dominant-hegemonic**, accepting the message's preferred reading essentially as encoded;
**negotiated**, partially accepting the preferred reading while contesting or adapting parts of it against local or situated knowledge;
and **oppositional**, understanding the preferred reading but rejecting it outright and substituting a counter-reading, typically from an alternative ideological framework.

## Distinctness from the behavioral-effects tradition

Hall's model is the founding text of British cultural studies' reception tradition, and it stands apart in method from the US-centered behavioral-effects tradition surveyed elsewhere in this section —
agenda-setting (see [`agenda-setting.md`](./agenda-setting.md)), cultivation (see [`cultivation-theory.md`](./cultivation-theory.md)), and uses-and-gratifications research measure effects via survey or experimental methods;
Hall's approach is textual and structural analysis of how meaning is produced and contested, not a quantitatively measured effect.
This methodological distinction should be preserved rather than flattened when this term is cited alongside the effects-tradition terms in this glossary.
