Prerequisites: you should be able to define a role-playing game and name the core roles ([RPG Overview](../../../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/services/role-playing-games-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/rpg-overview.md)).

## Starting from a concrete example

A [game master](../../../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/services/role-playing-games-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/game-master.md) says: &ldquo;The bridge ahead is old — the planks are rotten and the ropes are fraying. The river below is fast and rocky. Your guide says there is another crossing two hours downstream, but you can hear the search party behind you.&rdquo; The players now face a choice. Cross the dangerous bridge? Take the detour and risk being caught? Try to hide? Something else entirely?

This moment is a **scene**: a discrete unit of play where characters are in a specific situation that demands a response. The scene has a location (the bridge), stakes (pursuit, danger), and an implicit question (what do you do?). Play advances when the players answer that question.

## The scene-choice-consequence loop

Role-playing games flow through a recurring cycle:

- **Situation**: The GM describes where the characters are and what is happening — the environment, the threats, the opportunities, the pressures. This sets up the scene.

- **Choice**: A player declares what their [character](../../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/arts/ludics/games/terms/character.md) does. This is the moment of agency. The choice might be cautious, reckless, creative, or social — the fiction accommodates whatever the player imagines, constrained by what is plausible in the world.

- **Resolution**: If the outcome is uncertain, the game&rsquo;s [mechanics](../../../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/mechanic.md) intervene. In [Brave Old World](../../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/texts/brave-old-world.md), this means rolling two dice: 10+ succeeds, 7–9 succeeds with a cost, 6 or less fails and something happens. Other [systems](../../../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/logic-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/undated/system.md) use different resolution mechanics, but the function is the same — translating uncertain intention into a concrete outcome.

- **Consequence**: The result of the resolution changes the fiction. The bridge holds and you cross safely. Or the bridge holds but collapses behind you, cutting off retreat. Or you fall into the river. The new situation becomes the setup for the next scene.

This loop is the heartbeat of play. A [session](../../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/arts/ludics/games/terms/session.md) is a series of these loops — sometimes rapid (combat, chases, arguments), sometimes slow (exploration, investigation, social negotiation). The GM&rsquo;s art is framing situations that make the choice difficult, and the players&rsquo; art is making choices that feel true to their characters even when the optimal strategy is obvious.

## Worked example: tracing the loop in Brave Old World

From [Brave Old World](../../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/texts/brave-old-world.md)&rsquo;s rules:

> - The narrator describes where the party is and what is happening around them.
>
> - An adventurer describes what they do.
>
> - If what they do is risky, they **roll**.

This maps directly to the loop. Step 1 is the situation. Step 2 is the choice. Step 3 is the resolution. The consequence flows from the roll result and feeds into the next iteration.

Suppose the narrator says: &ldquo;The merchant&rsquo;s wagon has overturned on the road. Crates are scattered, and the merchant is pinned under a wheel. Bandits are approaching from the tree line.&rdquo; An adventurer says: &ldquo;I want to lift the wheel off the merchant before the bandits reach us.&rdquo; This is risky — it takes time and strength, and the bandits are close. The adventurer rolls: an 8. Success, but with a cost. The narrator might say: &ldquo;You heave the wheel aside and pull the merchant free, but in the effort you drop your pack. The bandits are on you before you can retrieve it.&rdquo; The fiction has changed — the merchant is free, but the adventurer&rsquo;s supplies are now in bandit-controlled territory. A new situation has emerged.

## Not every choice triggers a roll

A nuance worth noting: the [mechanics](../../../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/mechanic.md) only intervene when the outcome is uncertain. If a character wants to walk across a room, no roll is needed — it just happens. If a character wants to persuade a friendly ally to share information, the GM might just narrate the response. Rules resolve *uncertainty*, not every action. Knowing when to roll and when to simply narrate is a skill that develops with play, and different systems draw this line differently.

## Exercises

- Write a scene setup (2-3 sentences) that presents a situation with at least two plausible choices and meaningful stakes. Identify what makes the choice uncertain.

- Take the bridge example from the opening of this lesson. Imagine a player says &ldquo;I try to repair the bridge quickly using rope from my pack.&rdquo; Using [Brave Old World](../../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/texts/brave-old-world.md)&rsquo;s resolution mechanic, describe what happens on a 10+, a 7–9, and a 6 or less. Each outcome should change the fiction in a way that sets up a new scene.
