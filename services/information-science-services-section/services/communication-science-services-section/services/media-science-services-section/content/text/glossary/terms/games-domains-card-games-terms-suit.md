A suit is a categorical grouping of cards within a [deck](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/deck.md). In the standard French-suited deck, the four suits are hearts, diamonds, clubs, and spades — each with thirteen [ranks](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/services/card-games-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/rank.md) from ace through king. Other deck traditions use different suit systems: German-suited decks use hearts, bells, acorns, and leaves; Italian-suited decks use cups, coins, swords, and batons; [tarot](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/humanities/domains/spirituality/divination/tarot/index.md) decks add a fifth suit of trumps.

Suits serve multiple structural functions depending on the game. In [trick](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/trick.md)-taking games, suits govern legality: the [follow-suit](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/follow-suit.md) rule requires players to match the [led](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/services/card-games-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/lead.md) suit, creating the constraint structure that makes trick-taking strategic. In [trump](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/services/card-games-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/trump.md) games, one suit is elevated above the others, creating a power hierarchy among the four categories. In rummy-family games, suits define which cards can form runs (consecutive ranks must be in the same suit). In poker, suits decide whether a [hand](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/hand.md) qualifies as a flush.

In many games, suits are nominally equal — no suit is inherently stronger than another. But some games break this symmetry. Bridge assigns a bidding hierarchy (clubs lowest, then diamonds, hearts, spades, and no-trump highest). Pinochle values certain suits differently in scoring. Some games use suit as a tiebreaker. Even when suits are mechanically equal, players often develop preferences or superstitions about &ldquo;lucky&rdquo; suits — a social phenomenon that has no basis in the game&rsquo;s [mechanics](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/mechanic.md) but is consistent enough across cultures to be worth noting.
