---
title: "mimicry"
type: "term"
date-created: 2026-03-06
---

Mimicry is one of Roger Caillois&rsquo;s four fundamental categories of play: play structured around becoming someone or something else — taking on a role, inhabiting a fiction, simulating a situation. Theater, [role-playing games](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/arts/topics/role-playing-games/index.md), children&rsquo;s pretend play, cosplay, and simulation games all involve mimicry. The [player](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/player.md) temporarily sets aside their ordinary identity and operates as a fictional persona, and the interest comes not from competition or chance but from the experience of transformation itself — seeing through different eyes, making choices you wouldn&rsquo;t otherwise make, inhabiting a world that doesn&rsquo;t exist.

Mimicry depends on the [magic circle](../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/magic-circle.md) in a specific way. The player must simultaneously be themselves and not-themselves: aware enough to maintain the fiction, committed enough to invest it with meaning. A child playing house knows they aren&rsquo;t really a parent, but the play only works if they act as though they are. An RPG player knows their character isn&rsquo;t real, but the game&rsquo;s emotional and strategic depth requires genuine engagement with the character&rsquo;s situation. This double consciousness — knowing it&rsquo;s pretend while taking the pretense seriously — is what distinguishes mimicry from delusion on one side and mere description on the other.

What makes mimicry distinctive among Caillois&rsquo;s categories is that it doesn&rsquo;t require a determinate outcome. [Agon](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/arts/ludics/games/archive/agon.md) produces winners and losers, [alea](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/arts/ludics/games/archive/alea.md) produces results, but mimicry can sustain itself indefinitely as long as the fiction holds. This is why mimetic [play](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/play.md) often resists the [win-conditions](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/win-condition.md) and scoring systems that structure other game types — and why role-playing games, which are built on mimicry, have historically struggled with the question of whether &ldquo;winning&rdquo; even applies to them.
