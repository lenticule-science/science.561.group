# Study of News-Media Science

This is a study of news media as a subject of formal research, read for one purpose: to establish which claims in this section's five standing triage memos —
`news-topology.md`, `editorial-production.md`, `claims-to-prose.md`, `claim-infrastructure.md`, and `accountable-agentic-newsrooms.md` —
trace to peer-reviewed journalism-studies literature, and which trace instead to industry or practitioner canon (professional codes of ethics, trade books, trade press),
so that later production work in this section can cite the right kind of source for the right kind of claim.
Method: web-verified citations gathered directly from publisher, repository, and indexer records rather than restated from memory;
every load-bearing claim below carries a markdown-linked source, and any claim this study could not verify to that standard is marked **[UNVERIFIED]** inline rather than presented as settled.

## 1. News values

Johan Galtung and Mari Holmboe Ruge's ["The Structure of Foreign News"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002234336500200104) (*Journal of Peace Research* 2(1), 1965, pp. 64–90) is the founding peer-reviewed statement of news-values theory:
a set of twelve factors (frequency, threshold, unambiguity, meaningfulness, consonance, unexpectedness, continuity, composition, reference to elite nations, reference to elite people, reference to persons, and negativity) proposed, via a study of Norwegian coverage of the Congo, Cuba, and Cyprus crises,
as jointly predicting whether an event becomes news.
The theory's own additivity, complementarity, and exclusion hypotheses are testable claims, not just a checklist.

Tony Harcup and Deirdre O'Neill's ["What Is News? Galtung and Ruge Revisited"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14616700118449) (*Journalism Studies* 2(2), 2001, pp. 261–280) re-tested the 1965 taxonomy against a content analysis of three UK national daily newspapers and found it wanting empirically,
proposing a revised list that adds categories such as celebrity, entertainment, and relevance that Galtung and Ruge's Cold War-era categories did not anticipate.
Harcup and O'Neill returned to the question again in ["What is news? News values revisited (again)"](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/95423/) (*Journalism Studies* 18(12), 2017, pp. 1470–1488), re-running the content analysis fifteen years on and revising the list further for a social-media environment,
while stating explicitly that no taxonomy can capture everything that makes an event newsworthy.
This two-revisitation pattern —
a founding taxonomy repeatedly falsified and rebuilt by empirical content analysis rather than treated as fixed theory —
is itself a load-bearing fact for any of this section's memos that invoke "newsworthiness" as if it were a stable input.

## 2. Objectivity and its rituals

Gaye Tuchman's ["Objectivity as Strategic Ritual: An Examination of Newsmen's Notions of Objectivity"](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/225193) (*American Journal of Sociology* 77(4), 1972, pp. 660–679) is the founding sociological account:
newspapermen invoke procedural markers —
presenting conflicting possibilities, supplying supporting evidence, using quotation marks, and structuring information in an appropriate sequence —
as a ritual that protects them from libel suits, deadline pressure, and editorial reprimand, not as a guarantee that the resulting account is true.
Objectivity, on this account, is a defensible *procedure*, not an epistemic *result*,
a distinction this section's memos need whenever they treat "editorial gates" as truth-certifying rather than procedure-certifying.

Michael Schudson's ["The objectivity norm in American journalism"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/146488490100200201) (*Journalism* 2(2), 2001, pp. 149–170) historicizes the norm itself, treating its emergence in early-twentieth-century American journalism as an instance of a general pattern in how occupational norms arise,
rather than as a timeless professional value.
Read together, Tuchman and Schudson establish that "objectivity" is a historically situated, procedurally defined occupational ritual —
a claim any formal model of "editorial gates" or "verification status" in this section should cite to Tuchman/Schudson rather than treat as a philosophically primitive truth-predicate.

## 3. Epistemology of journalism

Mats Ekström's ["Epistemologies of TV journalism"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/146488490200300301) (*Journalism* 3(3), 2002, pp. 259–282) proposes a three-part theoretical framework distinguishing the *form* of journalistic knowledge, the *production* rules and routines that generate it, and the conditions of *public acceptance* of its knowledge claims —
a framework more granular than a single "verification gate" and directly relevant to any formal gate algebra this section proposes.

Yigal Godler and Zvi Reich have produced the closest peer-reviewed analogue to a formal claim-verification model already in this section's memos.
Their earlier ["How journalists 'realize' facts: Epistemology in practice at press conferences"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17512786.2013.791067) (*Journalism Practice* 7(6), 2013, pp. 674–689) examines journalists' epistemic practice at press conferences specifically, a narrower setting than the claim below;
more centrally, ["Journalistic evidence: Cross-verification as a constituent of mediated knowledge"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1464884915620268) (*Journalism* 18(5), 2017, pp. 558–574) analyzes cross-verification practices across Israeli print, radio, online, and television news (N = 847) and theorizes that reporters routinely rely on "evidence of sources' evidence" —
a second-order evidentiary category that a provenance-graph model needs to represent explicitly, not collapse into a single "verified/unverified" flag.
Godler, Reich, and Boaz Miller extend this into ["Social epistemology as a new paradigm for journalism and media studies"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1461444819856922) (*New Media & Society* 22(2), 2020, pp. 213–229), arguing that journalism studies lacks robust concepts for algorithmic and big-data-mediated knowledge generation specifically —
directly relevant to this section's agentic-newsroom memos.
**[UNVERIFIED]** whether the 2017 cross-verification finding, drawn from a single national media system, has a confirmed cross-national peer-reviewed replication;
this study located none.

Barbie Zelizer's ["Journalists as Interpretive Communities"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15295039309366865) (*Critical Studies in Mass Communication* 10(3), 1993) proposes that journalists cohere not as a licensed profession but as an interpretive community bound by shared discourse and collective retellings of formative events
(her cases are Watergate and McCarthyism).
This matters for any memo treating "editorial judgment" as an individual decision function:
Zelizer's account locates warrant partly in a community's shared narrative practice, which is a sociological claim, not a formal one,
and resists the kind of gate-typing this section's formal memos otherwise attempt.

## 4. Fact-checking as a field

Lucas Graves's *[Deciding What's True: The Rise of Political Fact-Checking in American Journalism](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/deciding-whats-true/9780231542227/)* (Columbia University Press, 2016) is the anchor peer-reviewed monograph on fact-checking as an institutional practice,
based on immersive fieldwork inside PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, and the Washington Post Fact Checker.
Its central finding is that fact-checking is a genre with its own professional norms and internal disputes about method, not a neutral truth-oracle function —
directly relevant to any memo treating `ClaimReview`/fact-check output as a settled truth-value rather than a genre-specific artifact.

Michelle Amazeen's empirical program on fact-check *effectiveness* is the field's leading experimental complement to Graves's institutional account.
Amazeen, Thorson, Muddiman & Graves, ["Correcting Political and Consumer Misperceptions: The Effectiveness and Effects of Rating Scale Versus Contextual Correction Formats"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1077699016678186) (*Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly*, 2018) found that visual truth-scale ratings improve correction effectiveness for consumer-fact misperceptions more reliably than for identity-linked political misperceptions,
and — contrary to some journalists' fears — did not find that rating scales increase partisan backlash.
Amazeen's ["Journalistic interventions: The structural factors affecting the global emergence of fact-checking"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1464884917730217) (*Journalism*, 2020) extends the institutional account cross-nationally.
**[UNVERIFIED]**: whether the rating-scale effectiveness finding generalizes beyond the US survey-experiment studies reviewed here to other media systems was not established by sources this study located.

The single most important correction this study can make to the memos' own citation practice concerns the "backfire effect."
Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler's ["When Corrections Fail: The Persistence of Political Misperceptions"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11109-010-9112-2) (*Political Behavior* 32, 2010, pp. 303–330) is the study that introduced the backfire effect into the literature:
in two of four mock-news experiments, correction exposure *increased* misperception among the ideologically predisposed group.
This finding has been widely cited since —
including, informally, in general discourse about corrections this section's memos touch on —
but it does **not** represent current scholarly consensus.
Thomas Wood and Ethan Porter's ["The Elusive Backfire Effect: Mass Attitudes' Steadfast Factual Adherence"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11109-018-9443-y) (*Political Behavior*, 2019) is the field's largest replication attempt:
five experiments, more than 10,000 participants, and 52 corrected issues chosen specifically because they were expected to be backfire-prone, found *zero* backfire cases;
corrections moved beliefs toward the facts in every issue tested.
Any memo in this section that invokes correction-resistance or a "backfire effect" as an established mechanism should cite Wood & Porter (2019) as the governing finding,
with Nyhan & Reifler (2010) cited historically as the originating — and since substantially revised — claim.

## 5. Corrections and accountability

The scholarly literature on correction *effectiveness* is the fact-checking literature reviewed in §4 above;
there is no separate peer-reviewed sub-field studying newsroom correction *practice* (how corrections are logged, propagated, and made public) at the same depth.
Craig Silverman's *Regret the Error: How Media Mistakes Pollute the Press and Imperil Free Speech* ([Amazon listing](https://www.amazon.com/Regret-Error-Mistakes-Pollute-Imperil/dp/1402751532); reviewed at [Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/regret_the_error)) is the field's best-known treatment of correction practice and argues that thorough, timely, public, and searchable corrections should be a newsroom's pride rather than its embarrassment —
but it is trade/practitioner journalism criticism, not a peer-reviewed study, and should be cited in this section as practitioner canon alongside the SPJ Code of Ethics and the AP/Reuters standards this section's memos already cite,
not as equivalent in evidentiary weight to Graves, Amazeen, Nyhan & Reifler, or Wood & Porter.

## 6. Computational and automated journalism

Mark Coddington's ["Clarifying Journalism's Quantitative Turn"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21670811.2014.976400) (*Digital Journalism* 3(3), 2015, pp. 331–348) supplies the field's governing typology, distinguishing computer-assisted reporting, data journalism, and computational journalism by their journalistic values and practices rather than treating "computational journalism" as one undifferentiated category —
directly useful for this section's memos, which currently use "computational journalism" loosely.

Nicholas Diakopoulos's *[Automating the News: How Algorithms Are Rewriting the Media](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674976986)* (Harvard University Press, 2019) is the anchor monograph on algorithmic and automated journalism as a socio-technical practice, documenting automation as supplementing rather than replacing newsroom labor and proposing accountability mechanisms for algorithmically produced content —
directly relevant to this section's `claim-infrastructure` and `accountable-agentic-newsrooms` memos.

Matt Carlson's ["The Robotic Reporter"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21670811.2014.976412) (*Digital Journalism* 3(3), 2015, pp. 416–431) analyzes automated-journalism provider Narrative Science and journalists' published reactions to it as a "technological drama" over journalistic authority and labor,
not merely a technical capability question.
Christer Clerwall's ["Enter the Robot Journalist"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17512786.2014.883116) (*Journalism Practice* 8(5), 2014, pp. 519–531) is the founding reader-perception experiment:
respondents rated software-generated news as more "descriptive and boring" but comparably objective, and could not reliably distinguish it from human-written content —
the peer-reviewed evidentiary basis for any claim in this section that readers "can't tell" automated from human-written news.

The Associated Press's 2014 deployment of Automated Insights' Wordsmith platform to automate corporate-earnings stories —
expanding coverage from roughly 300 to roughly 4,400 companies per quarter without displacing reporters —
is documented in trade press ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2015/robot-writing-increased-aps-earnings-stories-by-tenfold/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2014/07/01/the-ap-is-using-robots-to-write-earnings-reports)), not in a peer-reviewed case study.
This section's `claim-infrastructure` memo cites AP/Reuters licensing practices generally;
the earnings-automation deployment specifically should be cited to this trade coverage, not treated as an academically studied case.
**[UNVERIFIED]**: no peer-reviewed academic case study of the AP/Automated Insights deployment itself was located by this study,
though it is widely cited as background in the automated-journalism literature (e.g., Carlson 2015, Diakopoulos 2019) discussed above.

## 7. Wire services and news agencies

Oliver Boyd-Barrett's *The International News Agencies* ([Constable/Sage, 1980](https://www.amazon.com/International-News-Agencies-Communication-Society/dp/0094634807)) is the founding sociological study of the major international news agencies (AP, Reuters, UPI, AFP), establishing wire-service dependence and syndication as a structural feature of the news system rather than an incidental distribution mechanism —
directly relevant to this section's treatment of "syndication morphisms" and "feed capital."

Menahem Blondheim's *[News over the Wires: The Telegraph and the Flow of Public Information in America, 1844–1897](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.4159/9780674732957/html?lang=en)* (Harvard University Press, 1994) documents the co-emergence of the telegraph and the Associated Press as, respectively, the first US industrial monopoly and the first private-sector national news monopoly,
tracing how "timely news" became a commodity through this specific technological-commercial pairing.
Jonathan Silberstein-Loeb's *[The International Distribution of News](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/international-distribution-of-news/CDCC690D34C82CCF38F8653709FF7E8D)* (Cambridge University Press, 2014) extends this history through 1947 across AP, the Press Association, and Reuters, arguing that publishers formed news associations specifically to cut news-collection costs and exclude competitors —
a market-structure account any memo framing "claim infrastructure" or "catalog capital" as a novel agentic-web phenomenon should set against this century-plus precedent.

Justin Lewis, Andrew Williams, and Bob Franklin's ["A Compromised Fourth Estate? UK news journalism, public relations and news sources"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14616700701767974) (*Journalism Studies* 9(1), 2008, pp. 1–20) is the empirical study that gave "churnalism" its academic grounding:
content analysis of UK national newspapers and broadcast news found large proportions of published copy traceable directly to wire-service and public-relations material with little independent verification.
This is the peer-reviewed finding behind any claim in this section that "agency-copy dependence" is a documented risk of syndication-heavy news production,
not a merely hypothetical one.

## 8. Sourcing and indexing

W. Lance Bennett's ["Toward a Theory of Press-State Relations in the United States"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1990.tb02265.x) (*Journal of Communication* 40(2), 1990, pp. 103–127) is the founding statement of the indexing hypothesis:
news coverage of public affairs "indexes" itself to the range of viewpoints already visible in mainstream government debate, tested against four years of *New York Times* coverage of US funding for the Nicaraguan contras,
where quoted opinion came overwhelmingly from government officials.
Leon V. Sigal's *Reporters and Officials: The Organization and Politics of Newsmaking* ([D.C. Heath, 1973](https://archive.org/details/reportersofficia0000siga)) is the earlier foundational study of beat structure and official-source dependence that indexing theory builds on.
Together, Bennett and Sigal supply the peer-reviewed grounding for any claim in this section that "source power" or "official-source dependency" is a structural feature of news production rather than an individual reporter's failing —
directly relevant to `accountable-agentic-newsrooms`'s discussion of source power, which currently cites this literature only descriptively.

## 9. Trust in news

The Reuters Institute's [*Digital News Report 2026*](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2026/dnr-executive-summary) is the field's largest recurring cross-national trust survey (48 countries).
Its 2026 edition finds overall trust in news at 37% globally, the lowest level measured since 2015, with 29 of 48 countries surveyed showing year-on-year trust declines;
for the first time, social media and video platforms have overtaken news websites and apps as the primary access route to news (54% vs. 51%);
and trust in AI-chatbot answers to news questions sits at 20%, well below trust in news itself.
This is survey-based industry research, not peer-reviewed academic literature, but it is the standard citable source for any claim in this section about audience trust, distribution shift, or AI-chatbot substitution,
and should be cited by year rather than treated as a static fact.

## Gaps carried forward

This study did not resolve, and carries forward rather than launders into settled fact:

- No peer-reviewed cross-national replication of Godler & Reich's (2017) cross-verification finding was located; it rests on a single national media system (Israel, N = 847).
- No peer-reviewed academic case study of the AP/Automated Insights earnings-automation deployment (2014–) was located; it is documented only in trade press (Poynter, TechCrunch) and cited as background by Carlson (2015) and Diakopoulos (2019) rather than studied directly as a case.
- Amazeen et al.'s (2018) rating-scale correction-effectiveness finding is a US survey-experiment result; this study did not locate a peer-reviewed test of its generalization to other media/political systems.
- This study did not locate a peer-reviewed sub-field studying newsroom correction *practice* (logging, propagation, public disclosure) at the depth of the fact-checking-effectiveness literature reviewed in §4; Silverman's *Regret the Error* fills that space as practitioner canon, not as peer-reviewed research.
- Gatekeeping theory, agenda-setting theory, and Entman's framing account are already cited directly, by name, in this section's own memos (`news-topology.md`, `accountable-agentic-newsrooms.md`) with their own sources; this study did not re-verify those citations since they were not flagged as needing a peer-reviewed/practitioner distinction the way news values, objectivity, fact-checking, and wire-service claims were.
