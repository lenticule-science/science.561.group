The strategic ritual of objectivity names Gaye Tuchman's finding, in ["Objectivity as Strategic Ritual: An Examination of Newsmen's Notions of Objectivity"](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/225193) (*American Journal of Sociology* 77(4), 1972, pp. 660–679), that newspaper reporters invoke procedural markers of objectivity —
presenting the conflicting possibilities in a situation, supplying supporting evidence for assertions, using quotation marks to attribute claims to sources rather than the writer, and structuring information in a conventional sequence —
as a *ritual* that protects them from deadline pressure, anticipated libel suits, and editorial reprimand.
On this account, objectivity is a defensible **procedure**, not a guarantee of an epistemically true result:
following the ritual insulates the reporter regardless of whether the resulting story is accurate.

Michael Schudson's ["The objectivity norm in American journalism"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/146488490100200201) (*Journalism* 2(2), 2001, pp. 149–170) historicizes the norm itself, treating its emergence in early-twentieth-century American journalism as an instance of a more general pattern in how occupational norms arise,
rather than as a professional value journalism has always held.
Read together, Tuchman and Schudson establish that "objectivity" is a historically situated, procedurally defined occupational ritual, not a philosophically primitive truth-predicate —
a distinction any system that treats an "editorial gate" or "verification status" as truth-certifying, rather than merely procedure-certifying, needs to account for.
