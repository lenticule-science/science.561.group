A wire service (or news agency) collects news centrally and distributes it by subscription to member or client publishers and broadcasters,
rather than each outlet gathering its own report of the same event.
Oliver Boyd-Barrett's *The International News Agencies* ([Constable/Sage, 1980](https://www.amazon.com/International-News-Agencies-Communication-Society/dp/0094634807)) is the founding sociological study of the major international agencies —
AP, Reuters, UPI, AFP —
establishing wire-service dependence and syndication as a structural feature of the modern news system rather than an incidental distribution mechanism.

Menahem Blondheim's *[News over the Wires: The Telegraph and the Flow of Public Information in America, 1844–1897](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.4159/9780674732957/html?lang=en)* (Harvard University Press, 1994) documents the co-emergence of the telegraph and the Associated Press as, respectively, the first US industrial monopoly and the first private-sector national news monopoly,
tracing how "timely news" became a commodity through this specific technological-commercial pairing.
Jonathan Silberstein-Loeb's *[The International Distribution of News](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/international-distribution-of-news/CDCC690D34C82CCF38F8653709FF7E8D)* (Cambridge University Press, 2014) extends this history through 1947 across AP, the Press Association, and Reuters, arguing that publishers formed news associations specifically to cut news-collection costs and exclude competitors from the news market —
a market-structure account that predates by roughly a century any framing of "agent-readable claim infrastructure" as a novel phenomenon of the present agentic web.

**Churnalism** is the empirically documented risk that comes with wire-service dependence:
published news copy traceable directly to wire-service or public-relations material with little independent verification.
Justin Lewis, Andrew Williams, and Bob Franklin's ["A Compromised Fourth Estate? UK news journalism, public relations and news sources"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14616700701767974) (*Journalism Studies* 9(1), 2008, pp. 1–20) is the peer-reviewed study that gave the term its academic grounding,
via content analysis of UK national newspapers and broadcast news finding large proportions of published copy traceable to agency and PR material.
