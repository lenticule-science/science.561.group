News values are the factors that make an event more or less likely to be selected, treated, and prominently placed as news.
The founding peer-reviewed statement is Johan Galtung and Mari Holmboe Ruge, ["The Structure of Foreign News"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002234336500200104) (*Journal of Peace Research* 2(1), 1965, pp. 64–90), which proposes twelve factors —
frequency, threshold, unambiguity, meaningfulness, consonance, unexpectedness, continuity, composition, reference to elite nations, reference to elite people, reference to persons, and negativity —
derived from a study of Norwegian newspaper coverage of the Congo, Cuba, and Cyprus crises.
The theory makes testable claims about how these factors combine:
the *additivity hypothesis* (more factors raise the probability of coverage), the *complementarity hypothesis* (factors tend to substitute for one another), and the *exclusion hypothesis* (events satisfying very few factors go unreported).

The taxonomy is not treated in the literature as a stable input.
Tony Harcup and Deirdre O'Neill's ["What Is News? Galtung and Ruge Revisited"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14616700118449) (*Journalism Studies* 2(2), 2001, pp. 261–280) re-tested it against a content analysis of three UK national daily newspapers and revised it,
adding categories such as celebrity, entertainment, and relevance that the original Cold War-era list did not anticipate.
Harcup and O'Neill returned to the question again fifteen years later in ["What is news? News values revisited (again)"](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/95423/) (*Journalism Studies* 18(12), 2017, pp. 1470–1488), revising the list further for a social-media environment
while explicitly noting that no taxonomy can capture everything that makes an event newsworthy.

This two-revisitation pattern is the load-bearing fact for any formal or computational model that treats "newsworthiness" as a fixed scoring function:
the peer-reviewed literature treats it instead as an empirically contingent, historically shifting construct, re-derived by content analysis roughly once per generation rather than fixed by Galtung and Ruge's original 1965 list.
