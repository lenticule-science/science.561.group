Fact-checking, as a distinct institutionalized practice within American and international journalism, is anchored in the peer-reviewed literature by Lucas Graves's *[Deciding What's True: The Rise of Political Fact-Checking in American Journalism](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/deciding-whats-true/9780231542227/)* (Columbia University Press, 2016),
based on immersive fieldwork inside PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, and the Washington Post Fact Checker.
Graves's central finding is that fact-checking is a genre with its own professional norms, methodological disputes, and internal contestation —
not a neutral function that outputs a settled truth-value.
A structured-data artifact such as schema.org's `ClaimReview` should therefore be read as the serialization of a genre-specific editorial judgment, not as a truth-oracle result.

**Correction effectiveness** is a separate, experimentally studied question.
Michelle Amazeen and colleagues, in ["Correcting Political and Consumer Misperceptions: The Effectiveness and Effects of Rating Scale Versus Contextual Correction Formats"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1077699016678186) (*Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly*, 2018), found that visual truth-scale ratings improve correction effectiveness more reliably for consumer-fact misperceptions than for identity-linked political ones,
and did not find that rating scales increase partisan backlash against the correction.

The **backfire effect** —
the claim that correcting a misperception can increase belief in it among predisposed audiences —
originates with Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler's ["When Corrections Fail: The Persistence of Political Misperceptions"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11109-010-9112-2) (*Political Behavior* 32, 2010, pp. 303–330), which found backfire in two of four mock-news experiments.
This finding does **not** represent current scholarly consensus.
Thomas Wood and Ethan Porter's ["The Elusive Backfire Effect: Mass Attitudes' Steadfast Factual Adherence"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11109-018-9443-y) (*Political Behavior*, 2019) —
five experiments, more than 10,000 participants, 52 corrected issues chosen specifically for backfire susceptibility —
found zero backfire cases;
corrections moved beliefs toward the facts in every issue tested.
Any claim invoking a "backfire effect" as an established mechanism should cite Wood & Porter (2019) as the governing finding,
with Nyhan & Reifler (2010) cited historically as the originating, and since substantially revised, claim.
