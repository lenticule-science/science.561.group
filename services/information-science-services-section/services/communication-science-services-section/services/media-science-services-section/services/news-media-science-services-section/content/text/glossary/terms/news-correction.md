A news correction is a public, after-the-fact repair of an error in a previously published news artifact —
a factual error, a misattribution, a misleading headline, or a claim later found unsupported.
Two distinct bodies of evidence bear on it:
how *effective* a correction is at repairing a reader's misperception, and how correction is *practiced* institutionally.

**Effectiveness.** Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler's ["When Corrections Fail: The Persistence of Political Misperceptions"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11109-010-9112-2) (*Political Behavior* 32, 2010, pp. 303–330) introduced the "backfire effect" into the literature:
in two of four mock-news experiments, correction exposure *increased* misperception among the ideologically predisposed group.
This finding does **not** represent current scholarly consensus.
Thomas Wood and Ethan Porter's ["The Elusive Backfire Effect: Mass Attitudes' Steadfast Factual Adherence"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11109-018-9443-y) (*Political Behavior*, 2019) —
five experiments, more than 10,000 participants, 52 corrected issues chosen specifically for backfire susceptibility —
found zero backfire cases;
corrections moved beliefs toward the facts in every issue tested.
Michelle Amazeen and colleagues' ["Correcting Political and Consumer Misperceptions"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1077699016678186) (*Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly*, 2018) further found that visual truth-scale correction formats improve effectiveness for consumer-fact misperceptions more reliably than for identity-linked political ones,
without increasing partisan backlash.

**Practice.** There is no peer-reviewed sub-field studying newsroom correction *practice* —
how corrections are logged, propagated, and made public —
at comparable depth to the effectiveness literature above.
Craig Silverman's *Regret the Error: How Media Mistakes Pollute the Press and Imperil Free Speech* ([reviewed at Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/regret_the_error)) is the field's best-known treatment of correction practice and argues that thorough, timely, public, and searchable corrections should be a newsroom's pride rather than its embarrassment —
but it is trade/practitioner journalism criticism, not a peer-reviewed study, and should be cited as practitioner canon rather than with the evidentiary weight of the effectiveness literature above.

See the [study of news-media science](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-the-study-of-news-media-science.md) for the fuller literature review, including this gap around correction-practice research.
