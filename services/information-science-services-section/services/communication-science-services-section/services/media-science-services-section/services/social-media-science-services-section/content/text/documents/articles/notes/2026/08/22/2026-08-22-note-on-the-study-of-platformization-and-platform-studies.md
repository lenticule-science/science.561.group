The method is web-verified citation: every load-bearing claim below carries a markdown-linked source (DOI, publisher page, or other stable URL), and anything that could not be verified to the primary-source level is marked **[UNVERIFIED]** inline rather than laundered into settled fact.

## 1. The politics of platforms: Gillespie's founding move

Tarleton Gillespie's "The Politics of 'Platforms'" opened the sub-literature by arguing that online content intermediaries such as YouTube deliberately exploit the ambiguity of the word "platform" --- technical, architectural, figurative, and political all at once --- to position themselves favorably to users, advertisers, and regulators while disclaiming responsibility for what they carry.
It appeared in *New Media & Society* 12(3), pp. 347--364, in 2010, published by SAGE ([DOI 10.1177/1461444809342738](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1461444809342738)).
This is peer-reviewed, and it is the term-of-art's most-cited origin point in communication studies; later literature in this study (Helmond, Nieborg & Poell, Plantin et al.) treats it as the canonical starting reference rather than merely one entry among several.

Gillespie extended this analysis into a monograph, *Custodians of the Internet: Platforms, Content Moderation, and the Hidden Decisions That Shape Social Media*, published by Yale University Press in 2018 ([publisher page](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300261431/custodians-of-the-internet/); [DOI 10.12987/9780300235029](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.12987/9780300235029/html?lang=en)).
The book argues that content moderation is not incidental housekeeping but a constitutive, always-political activity through which platforms produce the categories of acceptable speech, and that algorithmic moderation systems inherit and encode those politics rather than escaping them.
It is an academic monograph from a university press, not trade nonfiction, and its claims are supported by case-based analysis of platform policy documents and enforcement patterns rather than by original large-N empirical data --- a methodological point worth carrying forward when citing it for empirical claims about moderation *outcomes* versus claims about moderation *politics*, which is what the book actually establishes.

## 2. Van Dijck: platforms as (culturally produced) infrastructure

José van Dijck's *The Culture of Connectivity: A Critical History of Social Media*, Oxford University Press, 2013 ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/9914); [publisher page](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-culture-of-connectivity-9780199970773)), traces the history of platforms including Flickr, YouTube, Wikipedia, and Facebook through the first decade of the 2000s, arguing that "connectivity" is not a neutral technical affordance but a culturally and economically engineered condition that platforms manufacture and monetize.
It is a peer-reviewed university-press monograph and sits alongside Gillespie as one of the sub-literature's two founding single-author works.

Van Dijck extended this line of argument with Thomas Poell and Martijn de Waal in *The Platform Society: Public Values in a Connective World*, also Oxford University Press, 2018 ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/12378); [publisher page](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-platform-society-9780190889777)).
The book's central claim is that platforms have moved from a peripheral, market-facing role into infrastructural positions across health, education, news, urban transport, and civic life, and that this migration puts long-standing public values (accountability, privacy, fairness, democratic control) into tension with the private, proprietary, and often opaque governance regimes platforms impose.
Van Dijck is a distinguished professor at Utrecht University and a former president of the Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences; Poell is a senior lecturer at the University of Amsterdam; de Waal is affiliated with the Amsterdam University of Applied Sciences.
This is again a peer-reviewed university-press work, and it is treated in this literature (see Nieborg & Poell, below) as the canonical mid-decade synthesis of the platformization argument, not as a standalone or contested position.

## 3. Helmond: platformization as a named process

Anne Helmond's "The Platformization of the Web: Making Web Data Platform Ready" appeared in *Social Media + Society* volume 1, issue 2 (July 2015) ([DOI 10.1177/2056305115603080](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2056305115603080)), an open-access, peer-reviewed SAGE journal, in the journal's first publication year but not its inaugural issue --- Crossref metadata for the journal's 2015 volume shows issue 1 (the true launch issue, a themed "What Is Social Media?" special issue) was published in April 2015, with Helmond's article following two months later in issue 2; an earlier draft of this note incorrectly called it the "inaugural issue," corrected here after verification.
The article is the sub-literature's most-cited source for the specific noun "platformization," which it defines as the extension of a social media platform's economic and infrastructural logic into the rest of the web, chiefly through the drive to make external web data "platform ready" (via APIs, social plugins, and tracking pixels) so that it can be captured back into the platform's own data architecture.
Helmond develops the argument through a historical case study of Facebook's Open Graph and Like-button infrastructure.
This is a peer-reviewed journal article grounded in primary technical documentation (Facebook developer materials, API changelogs), which makes it a stronger empirical anchor for platformization claims than either Gillespie's or van Dijck's more discursive/historical methods.

## 4. Nieborg & Poell: platformization of cultural production

David B. Nieborg and Thomas Poell's "The Platformization of Cultural Production: Theorizing the Contingent Cultural Commodity" appeared in *New Media & Society* 20(11), pp. 4275--4292, in 2018 ([DOI 10.1177/1461444818769694](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1461444818769694)).
The article argues that platformization restructures the political economy of cultural industries (the authors' case studies are news and games) by replacing simple two-sided market structures with "contingent" multi-sided platform configurations dominated by a small number of large platform corporations, such that cultural commodities themselves become contingent on platform-imposed rules, formats, and algorithmic distribution logics that can change unilaterally.
This is peer-reviewed and explicitly builds on both Helmond's and van Dijck/Poell/de Waal's definitions of platformization, making it the sub-literature's clearest bridge between platform studies and cultural/media-industry studies proper --- which is the specific gap this section's scope names ("the platformization of media and cultural production").

## 5. Plantin, Lagoze, Edwards & Sandvig: infrastructure vs. platform

Jean-Christophe Plantin, Carl Lagoze, Paul N. Edwards, and Christian Sandvig's "Infrastructure Studies Meet Platform Studies in the Age of Google and Facebook" appeared in *New Media & Society* 20(1), pp. 293--310, in 2018 ([DOI 10.1177/1461444816661553](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1461444816661553); [LSE Research Online copy](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/67571/)).
The article puts two previously separate scholarly traditions in conversation: infrastructure studies (which treats systems as shared, often state-adjacent, public-interest utilities) and platform studies (which treats systems as privately governed spaces that both enable and constrain communication).
Using Google and Facebook as cases, the authors argue neither frame alone captures how these firms operate --- they behave *as* infrastructure (widely relied upon, difficult to substitute) while retaining the private, discretionary governance of a platform, a combination the authors call "infrastructuralization."
This is peer-reviewed and is the sub-literature's key methodological corrective against treating "platform" and "infrastructure" as synonyms, a distinction this section's future terms should preserve.

## 6. boyd: networked publics and the affordance lineage

danah boyd's chapter "Social Network Sites as Networked Publics: Affordances, Dynamics, and Implications" appeared in Zizi Papacharissi's edited volume *A Networked Self: Identity, Community, and Culture on Social Network Sites*, Routledge, 2010, pp. 47--66 ([publisher page](https://www.routledge.com/A-Networked-Self-Identity-Community-and-Culture-on-Social-Network-Sites/Papacharissi/p/book/9780415801812)).
The chapter argues that social network sites produce "networked publics" --- publics restructured by networked technologies that combine a space (the technical architecture) and an imagined collective (the people who make sense of themselves through that architecture) --- and that these publics are shaped by four affordances boyd identifies as persistence, replicability, scalability, and searchability, plus the resulting dynamics of invisible audiences, context collapse, and the blurring of public and private.
This is a peer-reviewed book chapter (not boyd's more widely circulated but non-peer-reviewed blog and conference writing on the same topic), and it is the direct conceptual ancestor this section's README names for "networked publics" as a term.
One citation-metadata inconsistency surfaced in verification and is flagged rather than silently resolved: some library and course records date the volume 2010, others 2011 (reflecting differing print/online release dates); this note follows the publisher's own 2010 listing.

## 7. Srnicek: platform capitalism as a political-economy frame

Nick Srnicek's *Platform Capitalism*, Polity Press, 2017 ([publisher/Wiley listing](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Platform+Capitalism-p-9781509504862); [LSE Review of Books](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/lsereviewofbooks/2017/06/05/book-review-platform-capitalism-by-nick-srnicek/)), is a short (~120-page) political-economy monograph, part of Polity's "Theory Redux" series, tracing platform business models to the long economic downturn beginning in the 1970s and the 2008 financial crisis, and taxonomizing platforms into five types: advertising platforms (Google, Facebook), cloud platforms (Salesforce, AWS), industrial platforms (GE, Siemens), product platforms (Rolls-Royce, Spotify), and lean platforms (Uber, Airbnb).
Srnicek is an academic (King's College London) and the book is treated as a serious scholarly intervention in reviews (e.g., the LSE Review of Books piece linked above), but it is explicitly a work of Marxist political economy and theory-building rather than an empirical or peer-reviewed journal-article contribution in the same sense as items 1--5 above; its five-part typology is widely cited but is a theoretical classification, not a result derived from a reviewed dataset, and should be cited as such.

## 8. Zuboff: surveillance capitalism, and its contested reception

Shoshana Zuboff's *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism: The Fight for a Human Future at the New Frontier of Power*, PublicAffairs, 2019 (ISBN 978-1-61039-569-4), argues that platform firms pioneered a new economic logic --- "surveillance capitalism" --- in which human experience itself is claimed as free raw material, translated into behavioral data, and rendered into predictive products sold in markets for future behavior, and that this logic is a rupture from industrial capitalism rather than a continuation of it.
The book was widely reviewed as a major trade and academic crossover work, but its reception among specialists is genuinely split rather than uniformly positive, and this note treats that split as itself part of the record rather than smoothing it away.
A peer-reviewed academic review essay in *Media Theory* (an open-access journal) by William Morgan surveys and engages this contested reception directly ([Media Theory review](https://mediatheoryjournal.org/2019/06/11/review-shoshana-zuboffs-the-age-of-surveillance-capitalism-by-william-morgan/)).
Among the specific critiques that review and adjacent commentary raise: Evgeny Morozov has argued the book under-theorizes capitalism itself, and Kirstie Ball, a surveillance-studies scholar, is quoted arguing the book does not substantially extend the preceding two decades of surveillance-studies scholarship on datafication and social sorting.
A follow-up verification pass (2026-08-22) confirmed the Media Theory review itself is genuine and does directly quote both scholars with citations: Morozov, "If you don't want to talk about capitalism then you'd better keep quiet about surveillance capitalism" (Morozov, 2019), and Ball, "For me, it contained very few surprises. ...
I would even go so far as to say it is not a work of surveillance scholarship at all" (Ball, 2019: 253).
That pass also located and read Morozov's actual 2019 *Baffler* essay, "Capitalism's New Clothes" (Evgeny Morozov, February 4, 2019, [thebaffler.com](https://thebaffler.com/latest/capitalisms-new-clothes-morozov)), confirming the essay itself is real and matches the byline and title the Media Theory review implies --- but the specific quoted line above ("if you don't want to talk about capitalism...") was **not** found verbatim anywhere in that essay's full text on a direct search.
This is flagged **[UNVERIFIED]** at the level that matters: either the Media Theory review's citation "(Morozov, 2019)" points to a different, shorter Morozov text (a tweet or remark elsewhere, not this essay) that has not been located, or the line is the reviewer's paraphrase rendered as a direct quotation.
Ball's citation ("Ball, 2019: 253") likewise has not been traced to its original publication this session.
This study's method requires flagging both attributions rather than repeating them as settled: the secondary source (the Media Theory review) is confirmed genuine and does contain both quotes as given, but neither quote has been confirmed against a primary Morozov or Ball text, and in Morozov's case the one primary text located does not contain the quoted words.
A follow-up session should either locate the specific Morozov source the quote actually comes from, or the original Ball 2019 review at page 253, before either is cited as fact.
The takeaway for later terms in this section: cite Zuboff's book for the *coinage and popularization* of "surveillance capitalism" and for its behavioral-futures-market argument, but do not cite it, uncontested, as settled surveillance-studies consensus; attribute contested claims to Zuboff by name.

## 9. Governance and moderation labor: Gillespie and Roberts

Beyond *Custodians of the Internet* (Section 1), the app-store and content-moderation governance literature in this study is anchored by Sarah T. Roberts' *Behind the Screen: Content Moderation in the Shadows of Social Media*, Yale University Press, 2019 ([publisher page](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300261479/behind-the-screen/); [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvhrcz0v)).
The book is an ethnographic study --- reviewed favorably in, e.g., the *LSE Review of Books* ([LSE review](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/lsereviewofbooks/2019/11/25/book-review-behind-the-screen-content-moderation-in-the-shadows-of-social-media-by-sarah-t-roberts/)) and *Digital Journalism* ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/21670811.2020.1724517)) --- based on field research with commercial content moderators (a term Roberts coins as "commercial content moderation," CCM) across sites including the Philippines, rural Iowa, Silicon Valley, India, Canada, and Mexico.
It is peer-reviewed-adjacent (a university-press monograph reviewed in peer-reviewed venues) and is the sub-literature's primary empirical source for moderation *labor conditions*, as distinct from Gillespie's book, which is the primary source for moderation *policy and politics*.
Together they form this section's two-pillar citation base for governance work: cite Gillespie for the politics and categorization logic of moderation, cite Roberts for the labor and workforce empirics; neither substitutes for the other.

## Gaps carried forward

- **Zuboff's contested reception** (Section 8) needs a direct read of Morozov's and Ball's own review texts, not a secondhand summary; the two specific attributions above are marked **[UNVERIFIED]** and should be resolved before any glossary term cites them as fact.
- **Empirical verification of platform power claims.** Much of this literature (Gillespie, van Dijck et al., Srnicek, Zuboff) argues from case study, discourse analysis, or theoretical synthesis rather than from large-N empirical measurement of platform effects on markets, labor, or public discourse; where later notes need quantitative claims (e.g., market concentration, labor-market effects of gig platforms), a separate empirical/economics literature should be sought rather than over-extending these sources.
- **Geographic concentration.** All anchor authors here work from European or North American institutions and case studies are disproportionately Google/Facebook/US-platform-centric; Roberts' fieldwork is the partial exception (Philippines, India, Mexico sites), but a fuller Global South content-moderation and platform-labor literature (e.g., work on data-annotation and moderation labor markets in Kenya, the Philippines more broadly, and South/Southeast Asia) is not covered here and should be sought separately.
- **Infrastructure/platform boundary in practice.** Plantin et al.'s (Section 5) conceptual distinction between infrastructure and platform is not yet cross-checked in this study against the engineering-side literature on platform architecture; see the relation noted below.
- **App-store governance specifically** (as distinct from social-feed content moderation) is covered here only at the level named in the task brief (Gillespie; Roberts) and was not independently deepened with app-store-specific sources (e.g., work specifically on Apple App Store or Google Play review policy) within this session's search budget; flagged as a gap rather than filled speculatively.
