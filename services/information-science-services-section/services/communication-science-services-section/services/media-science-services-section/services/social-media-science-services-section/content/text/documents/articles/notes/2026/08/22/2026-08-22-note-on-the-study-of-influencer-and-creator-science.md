# Study of Influencer and Creator Science

This is a study of the influencer/creator stratum of platform-native media production as a subject of formal research, read for one purpose: to establish, per sub-literature, which claims trace to peer-reviewed sources versus practitioner, industry, or trade canon, so that later terms and notes in this section cite the right kind of source for the right kind of claim.
Method: web-verified citations — every load-bearing claim below carries a markdown-linked source (a DOI, a publisher page, or another stable URL), checked directly against Crossref or the publisher where possible; anything not verifiable to the primary-source level is marked **[UNVERIFIED]** inline rather than laundered into fact.
This section's README already seats "Influencer, creator, and microcelebrity science: Senft, Marwick, Abidin, Duffy, Cunningham & Craig, parasocial-interaction lineage" as this section's fourth scope item; this note supplies the literature standing behind that line and behind the four glossary terms it accompanies (`microcelebrity`, `influencer`, `creator-economy`, `parasocial-interaction`).

## 1. The parasocial-interaction lineage (foundational, pre-platform)

Donald Horton and R. Richard Wohl, "Mass Communication and Para-Social Interaction: Observations on Intimacy at a Distance," *Psychiatry* 19(3), pp. 215–229 (1956) ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00332747.1956.11023049); [PubMed 13359569](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13359569/)), is the paper that coins "para-social interaction" — the one-sided, performer-to-audience intimacy simulated by broadcast media (radio and television personae in the original study) and consumed by audiences as though it were a face-to-face social bond.
Horton & Wohl's argument is specifically that mass media personae are constructed to invite this illusion of reciprocity, and that audiences develop an ongoing sense of relationship with the persona despite the interaction's actual one-sidedness.
This is the concept every later influencer/parasocial-marketing paper in this note inherits and re-applies to platform contexts the original 1956 paper could not have anticipated; it predates social media, microcelebrity, and the creator economy by roughly half a century and is treated here as the field's shared conceptual root rather than as influencer-specific literature in its own right.

## 2. Microcelebrity: Senft's coinage and its uptake

Theresa M. Senft, *Camgirls: Celebrity and Community in the Age of Social Networks* (Peter Lang, 2008) ([publisher page](https://www.peterlang.com/document/1105668); [Internet Archive copy](https://archive.org/details/camgirlscelebrit0000senf)) is the source of the term "microcelebrity," coined in Senft's study of early webcam performers ("camgirls") who built audiences and community through sustained, intimate self-broadcast on personal homepages and early social networks (LiveJournal, early YouTube, MySpace).
Senft's contribution is definitional and ethnographic rather than quantitative: microcelebrity names a *practice* — a self-presentation strategy of treating one's audience as a community to be built and maintained through ongoing, seemingly personal disclosure — rather than a fame threshold.
This practice-level definition is what later authors (Marwick, Abidin) extend to Instagram- and YouTube-native influencers rather than camgirls specifically.

Alice E. Marwick's subsequent work operationalizes microcelebrity for the platform-native influencer economy.
*Status Update: Celebrity, Publicity, and Branding in the Social Media Age* (Yale University Press, 2013) ([Yale Books](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300209389/status-update/); reviewed at [Celebrity Studies via Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19392397.2016.1187866)) is an ethnography of San Francisco's tech and social-media scenes in the Web 2.0 era, arguing that these technologies, contrary to their democratizing rhetoric, produced a status economy that turns ordinary users into self-marketers and self-branders.
Marwick, "Instafame: Luxury Selfies in the Attention Economy," *Public Culture* 27(1 [75]), pp. 137–160 (2015) ([Duke University Press](https://read.dukeupress.edu/public-culture/article-abstract/27/1%20(75)/137/31071/Instafame-Luxury-Selfies-in-the-Attention-Economy)) extends microcelebrity specifically to Instagram, defining it as "a self-presentation strategy endemic to social media in which users strategically formulate a profile, reach out to followers, and reveal personal information to increase attention and improve their online status," applied to the study's "Instafamous" subjects — from Singaporean socialites to high-school-age accounts with five-figure followings.

## 3. Abidin: influencer as a distinct object and its ethnography

Crystal Abidin's work is the most directly load-bearing for an `influencer` term specifically (as distinct from microcelebrity as a practice).
Abidin, "Communicative ❤ Intimacies: Influencers and Perceived Interconnectedness," *Ada: A Journal of Gender, New Media, and Technology* 8 (2015) ([UWA Research Repository](https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/communicative-intimacies-influencers-and-perceived-interconnected/); full text via [University of Oregon ScholarsBank](https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/a18cd997-772b-42f9-acb3-096dadfa2eae/content)), draws on original fieldwork in Singapore's influencer industry (underway since 2010) to conceptualize "influencer" as a platform-agnostic category distinct from the press's platform-specific labels ("bloggers," "YouTubers," "Instagrammers"), and coins "communicative intimacies" for the perceived interconnectedness — a felt closeness that is not incompatible with the relationship's often-commercial nature — that influencers cultivate with followers.
Abidin, *Internet Celebrity: Understanding Fame Online* (Emerald Publishing, 2018) ([Emerald Books Gateway](https://www.emerald.com/books/monograph/13023/Internet-Celebrity-Understanding-Fame-Online)) extends this into a cross-cultural framework of internet-celebrity types and the pathway from ordinary user to professional influencer, reviewed as offering "an introduction to the affection economy" ([Cleveland Review of Books](https://clereviewofbooks.com/2018-09-21-2018-9-21-internetcelebrity/)).

## 4. Labour: aspirational labour, relational labor, algorithmic precarity

Three distinct labour concepts, from three distinct authors, are load-bearing for how this literature analyzes creator work as work.

**Aspirational labour.** Brooke Erin Duffy, *(Not) Getting Paid to Do What You Love: Gender, Social Media, and Aspirational Work* (Yale University Press, 2017) ([Yale Books](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300264753/not-getting-paid-to-do-what-you-love/); reviewed at [International Journal of Communication](https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/8322/2200)) is based on interviews with 50+ young women in fashion blogging and adjacent creator work, plus fieldwork at blogging "boot camps" and industry events.
Duffy's "aspirational labour" names the unpaid or underpaid work of self-branding, content production, and networking performed on the promise (rarely realized) of eventual paid, lucrative creator careers, situating this within longer histories of gendered, unpaid creative and domestic labour.

**Relational labor.** Nancy K. Baym, *Playing to the Crowd: Musicians, Audiences, and the Intimate Work of Connection* (NYU Press, 2018) ([NYU Press](https://nyupress.org/9781479821587/playing-to-the-crowd/); [NYU Press Scholarship Online via Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/nyu-press-scholarship-online/book/21317)) draws on in-depth interviews with professional musicians (including Billy Bragg, Richie Hawtin, and members of the Cure, UB40, and Throwing Muses) to define "relational labor" as "the ongoing, interactive, affective, material, and cognitive work of communicating with people over time to create structures that can support continued work" — visibility- and relationship-maintenance work that is effortful but rarely directly compensated, and that Baym argues has become a demand placed on creative workers generally, not only musicians.

**Algorithmic precarity and algorithmic gossip.** Sophie Bishop, "Managing visibility on YouTube through algorithmic gossip," *New Media & Society* 21(11–12), pp. 2589–2606 (2019), DOI: [10.1177/1461444819854731](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1461444819854731) (verified via Crossref), is an ethnography of beauty vloggers and industry stakeholders defining "algorithmic gossip" as the communally shared, informal theories and strategies about recommender-algorithm behavior that creators circulate and act on (e.g., upload frequently, produce "feminised" beauty content) to manage visibility on an opaque, algorithmically structured platform — gossip treated as a genuinely productive form of algorithmic knowledge, not mere rumor.
Brooke Erin Duffy & Colten Meisner, "Platform governance at the margins: Social media creators' experiences with algorithmic (in)visibility," *Media, Culture & Society* 45(2), pp. 285–304 (2023; published online 2022), DOI: [10.1177/01634437221111923](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01634437221111923) (verified via Crossref), draws on in-depth interviews with 30 social media creators sampled specifically from historically marginalized identities and/or stigmatized content genres, and develops "algorithmic precarity" — the condition of building a platform-dependent business around algorithmic configurations that can shift without notice or explanation, with the added finding that this precarity is not evenly distributed but concentrated at the margins of platform governance.
This 2023 article builds on Duffy's own earlier introduction of "algorithmic precarity" and on Duffy, Pinch, Sannon & Sawey, "The Nested Precarities of Creative Labor on Social Media," *Social Media + Society* (2021) ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20563051211021368)), which this note flags as directly relevant background but did not independently re-verify to the same DOI-confirmed standard as the 2023 article.

## 5. The industry account: Cunningham & Craig's "social media entertainment"

Stuart Cunningham & David Craig, *Social Media Entertainment: The New Intersection of Hollywood and Silicon Valley* (NYU Press, 2019) ([NYU Press](https://nyupress.org/9781479846894/social-media-entertainment/)) names and analyzes "social media entertainment" (SME) as a new creative-industry formation distinct from legacy Hollywood models — built on YouTube, Instagram, and other platforms, and characterized by direct creator-audience interactivity as a structural feature of the content itself rather than an add-on to it.
The book won the International Communication Association's 2020 Outstanding Book Award and received an Honorable Mention for the Association of Internet Researchers' 2020 Nancy Baym Book Award, per the NYU Press and Wiley review listings surfaced in verification searches — cited here as evidence of the book's standing in the field, not independently re-confirmed against the awarding bodies' own records, so that award detail carries a light **[UNVERIFIED]** qualifier.

## 6. Parasocial relationships in the marketing literature

Distinct from the media-science parasocial lineage in §1, a quantitative marketing-and-consumer-behavior literature applies parasocial-relationship theory specifically to influencer marketing outcomes.
Kumju Hwang & Qi Zhang, "Influence of parasocial relationship between digital celebrities and their followers on followers' purchase and electronic word-of-mouth intentions, and persuasion knowledge," *Computers in Human Behavior* 87, pp. 155–173 (2018), DOI: [10.1016/j.chb.2018.05.029](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2018.05.029) (verified via Crossref), finds that followers' parasocial relationships with digital celebrities have a measurable positive effect on purchase intention and electronic word-of-mouth intention, moderated by followers' persuasion knowledge.
This paper is flagged here specifically because it sits on this section's boundary with market science (§8 below): the *mechanism* (parasocial relationship formation) is media science's to study; the *dependent variable* here (purchase intention) is squarely influencer-marketing effectiveness, owned by `market-science-services-section`.
It is included in this note as the clearest example of that boundary in practice, not annexed as this section's own finding.

## 7. Creator-economy industry sizing (trade tier)

Creator-economy market-size figures circulate widely in trade and industry-analyst reporting but are, per this note's verification pass, harder to pin to a stable primary source than the academic literature above.
A secondary trade aggregator ([Influencer Marketing Hub](https://influencermarketinghub.com/creator-economy-stats/)) reports, citing Statista, a creator-economy market size of "$21 billion in 2023," up from "$16.4 billion in 2022," and separately reports a Goldman Sachs Research projection that the creator economy "can almost double in size from $250 billion to a potential $480 billion" by 2027.
This note could not directly load Goldman Sachs's own report page during verification (repeated fetch attempts returned HTTP 403), so the $250bn/$480bn figures are confirmed only at the secondary-aggregator level, not against Goldman Sachs's primary publication; they are marked **[UNVERIFIED]** at the primary-source level accordingly, though multiple independent trade outlets report the same figures consistently.
SignalFire's creator-economy market map, frequently cited alongside Goldman Sachs's figures in trade coverage, could not be located at a stable URL during this verification pass (repeated fetch attempts against plausible SignalFire URLs returned HTTP 404); any SignalFire-specific creator-count or market-size figures this section's terms may want to cite in future should be independently re-sourced and are marked **[UNVERIFIED]** here rather than asserted.

## 8. Boundary with market science

This section (`social-media-science-services-section`) owns influencer, creator, and microcelebrity *media* science: the practice, the labour, the platform mechanics, and the parasocial mechanism of audience-creator relationships.
It explicitly does not own influencer-*marketing* effectiveness or disclosure research — brand lift, purchase-intention modeling as a marketing-science question, sponsored-content disclosure compliance and effects.
That literature is owned by `market-science-services-section`, seated at `/home/emsenn/info/lib/emsenn/services/561-group/services/science-services-section/services/philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agent-science-services-section/services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/business-science-services-section/services/marketing-science-services-section`, whose own README explicitly names "personal branding and self-branding scholarship, corporate-brand scholarship, and influencer marketing" as within its scope and lists this section as the cross-link partner at that boundary.
The Hwang & Zhang (2018) paper in §6 above is the clearest example of a paper that straddles the boundary: this note cites it for its parasocial mechanism, not its marketing outcome.
