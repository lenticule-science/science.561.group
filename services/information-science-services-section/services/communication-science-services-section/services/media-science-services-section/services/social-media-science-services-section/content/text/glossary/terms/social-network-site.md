A **social network site** (SNS) is a web-based service that lets an individual (1) construct a public or semi-public profile within a bounded system, (2) articulate a list of other users with whom they share a connection, and (3) view and traverse their own list of connections and those made by others within the system.
The definition is danah boyd and Nicole Ellison's, from ["Social Network Sites: Definition, History, and Scholarship"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1083-6101.2007.00393.x) (*Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication* 13(1), 2007, pp. 210-230), and it is deliberately worded "network" rather than "networking": the sites' primary social function, on this account, is to make relationships that already exist (offline acquaintance, shared institutional membership, mutual friends) visible and navigable, not to introduce strangers who share only an interest.

## The 2013 revision

Ellison and boyd revisited the definition six years later in ["Sociality through Social Network Sites"](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34364/chapter/328422615), a chapter in William H. Dutton's *The Oxford Handbook of Internet Studies* (Oxford University Press, 2013, pp. 151-172).
By 2013 the behaviors the 2007 definition had treated as SNS-specific — maintaining a profile, "friending," browsing a network of connections — had diffused across the wider web (into commerce sites, news sites, and general web services), so a definition built only around bounded profiles no longer distinguished SNSs from a broader field of "social" web services.
The 2013 revision recenters the definition on the maintained, viewable articulation of a connection list as the persistent core, while relaxing the emphasis on a strictly bounded profile as the defining container.

## Relation to affordance theory

The boyd-and-Ellison lineage defines *what a social network site is* structurally; it does not, on its own, explain what a given SNS makes easy or hard for a given user to do.
That latter question is the domain of [social-media-affordance](social-media-affordance.md), the affordance-theoretic framework (Treem & Leonardi 2013; Evans, Pearce, Vitak & Treem 2017) this section treats as complementary rather than competing: a platform can satisfy the SNS definition structurally while affording quite different visibility, persistence, editability, and association profiles to its users, and much of the platform-specific media-effects literature (see [echo-chamber](echo-chamber.md) and the study note below) turns on those affordance differences rather than on the SNS definition itself.
