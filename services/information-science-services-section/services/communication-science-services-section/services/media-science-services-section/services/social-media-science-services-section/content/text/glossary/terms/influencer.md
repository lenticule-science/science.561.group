An influencer is a platform-agnostic category of person who builds and monetizes an audience through ongoing, apparently intimate self-disclosure — distinguished from platform-specific press labels ("bloggers," "YouTubers," "Instagrammers") by treating the underlying practice as continuous across platforms.
Crystal Abidin, "Communicative ❤ Intimacies: Influencers and Perceived Interconnectedness," *Ada: A Journal of Gender, New Media, and Technology* 8 (2015) ([UWA Research Repository](https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/communicative-intimacies-influencers-and-perceived-interconnected/); full text via [University of Oregon ScholarsBank](https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/a18cd997-772b-42f9-acb3-096dadfa2eae/content)), draws on fieldwork in Singapore's influencer industry (underway since 2010) to establish this definition and coins "communicative intimacies" for the felt closeness — not incompatible with the relationship's often-commercial nature — that influencers cultivate with followers.
Abidin, *Internet Celebrity: Understanding Fame Online* (Emerald Publishing, 2018) ([Emerald Books Gateway](https://www.emerald.com/books/monograph/13023/Internet-Celebrity-Understanding-Fame-Online)) extends this into a cross-cultural typology of internet celebrity and the pathway from ordinary user to professional influencer.
See [microcelebrity](./microcelebrity.md) for the related but broader self-presentation practice influencer draws on, and [parasocial interaction](./parasocial-interaction.md) for the audience-side mechanism of the intimacy Abidin describes.

## Influencer labour

Three distinct labour concepts analyze influencer/creator work as work.
Brooke Erin Duffy's "aspirational labour," from *(Not) Getting Paid to Do What You Love: Gender, Social Media, and Aspirational Work* (Yale University Press, 2017) ([Yale Books](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300264753/not-getting-paid-to-do-what-you-love/)), names the unpaid or underpaid self-branding and content work performed on the promise, rarely realized, of eventual lucrative creator careers.
Nancy K. Baym's "relational labor," from *Playing to the Crowd: Musicians, Audiences, and the Intimate Work of Connection* (NYU Press, 2018) ([NYU Press](https://nyupress.org/9781479821587/playing-to-the-crowd/)), names "the ongoing, interactive, affective, material, and cognitive work of communicating with people over time to create structures that can support continued work."
Brooke Erin Duffy & Colten Meisner, "Platform governance at the margins: Social media creators' experiences with algorithmic (in)visibility," *Media, Culture & Society* 45(2), pp. 285–304 (2023), DOI: [10.1177/01634437221111923](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01634437221111923), develop "algorithmic precarity" from interviews with 30 creators sampled from historically marginalized identities/stigmatized genres, finding this precarity unevenly distributed at the margins of platform governance.
Sophie Bishop, "Managing visibility on YouTube through algorithmic gossip," *New Media & Society* 21(11–12), pp. 2589–2606 (2019), DOI: [10.1177/1461444819854731](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1461444819854731), documents the communally shared, informal algorithm theories creators circulate to manage that same visibility precarity.
The industry these creators work within is analyzed at scale by Stuart Cunningham & David Craig, *Social Media Entertainment: The New Intersection of Hollywood and Silicon Valley* (NYU Press, 2019) ([NYU Press](https://nyupress.org/9781479846894/social-media-entertainment/)), and its economic footprint is tracked, with less certainty, by trade sizing figures — see [creator economy](./creator-economy.md).

## Boundary with market science

This term and its literature describe influencers as a media-science object: the practice, the labour, the platform mechanics.
