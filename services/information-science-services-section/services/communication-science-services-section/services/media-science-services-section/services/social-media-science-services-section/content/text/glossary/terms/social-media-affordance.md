A **social media affordance** is not a property of a platform alone, and not a property of a user alone, but a property of the relation between a specified technology and a specified actor: what that technology makes easier, harder, possible, or impossible for that actor to do, in a given practice.
The term enters social-media scholarship principally through Jeffrey Treem and Paul Leonardi's ["Social Media Use in Organizations: Exploring the Affordances of Visibility, Editability, Persistence, and Association"](https://academic.oup.com/anncom/article-abstract/36/1/143/7885569) (*Annals of the International Communication Association* 36(1), 2013, pp. 143-189 — the same chapter is also published, with different pagination (pp. 157-203), as *Communication Yearbook 36*), which names four affordances recurring across social media platforms:

- **Visibility** — the affordance of making behaviors, knowledge, preferences, and network connections that would otherwise be invisible or effortful-to-observe visible to others.
- **Persistence** — the affordance by which communicative content remains accessible beyond the moment of its transmission, including after the author has left the interaction.
- **Editability** — the affordance of investing effort in composing, revising, or curating a communicative act before (or after) it reaches its audience.
- **Association** — the affordance of establishing connections between individuals, between individuals and content, or between an actor and a piece of content-as-presentation.

## Explicating affordance as a term

Sandra Evans, Katy Pearce, Jessica Vitak, and Jeffrey Treem's ["Explicating Affordances: A Conceptual Framework for Understanding Affordances in Communication Research"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jcc4.12180) (*Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication* 22(1), 2017, pp. 35-52) reviewed 82 communication-oriented empirical works using affordance terminology and found the concept was inconsistently applied in three recurring ways: treating an affordance as an inherent property of the technology rather than of the technology-actor relation, conflating an affordance with the outcome it enables (as though the affordance and its effect were the same claim), and failing to specify which actor the affordance is being claimed for.
Their proposed framework — an affordance exists between a specified technology, a specified actor, and a specified goal or practice — is the discipline this section applies whenever a term or note invokes "affordance" rather than a bare capability claim.

## Relation to social network sites

Affordance is a claim about what a platform lets a user do; it presupposes but does not supply the structural definition of what counts as the platform in question.
See [social-network-site](social-network-site.md) for the structural definition (boyd & Ellison 2007; Ellison & boyd 2013) that affordance claims are typically made about.
Affordance differences between platforms — not the SNS definition itself — are what much of the platform-effects literature (see [echo-chamber](echo-chamber.md)) actually turns on: two services can both satisfy the SNS definition while affording very different visibility or persistence profiles to a given user, and it is that difference, not shared SNS-hood, that drives divergent effects findings across platforms.
