This study is read for one purpose: to establish, per sub-literature, which claims in social media science trace to peer-reviewed sources and which belong instead to practitioner/industry/trade canon, so that later glossary terms and notes in this section cite the right kind of source rather than quietly upgrading a trade claim to a scholarly one.
The method is web-verified citation: every load-bearing claim below carries a markdown-linked source (DOI, publisher page, or a stable URL to the venue of record), and anything that could not be confirmed to the primary-source level in this pass is marked **[UNVERIFIED]** inline rather than laundered into settled fact.
Effects claims — the adolescent-wellbeing question and the echo-chamber question above all — are reported at the contested status the literature itself has, not resolved toward whichever side is louder.

## 1. The social-network-site definitional lineage

danah boyd and Nicole Ellison's ["Social Network Sites: Definition, History, and Scholarship"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1083-6101.2007.00393.x) (*Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication* 13(1), 2007, pp. 210-230) is the field's founding definitional paper.
It defines a social network site as a web-based service that lets an individual (1) construct a public or semi-public profile within a bounded system, (2) articulate a list of other users with whom they share a connection, and (3) view and traverse their list of connections and those made by others within the system — deliberately choosing "network" over "networking" to mark that these sites make latent, already-existing ties visible rather than being primarily oriented toward introducing strangers.
The paper also gives the pre-2007 history of SNS platforms (SixDegrees.com forward) and surveys the scholarship to that date across self-presentation, networks and network structure, and privacy.

Ellison and boyd revisited the definition six years later in ["Sociality through Social Network Sites"](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34364/chapter/328422615), a chapter in William H. Dutton's *The Oxford Handbook of Internet Studies* (Oxford University Press, 2013, pp. 151-172; preprint available via [boyd's own site](https://www.danah.org/papers/2013/SocialityThruSNS-preprint.pdf)).
The 2013 revision responds to the platform landscape having changed under the original definition — most notably that by 2013 "friending" and profile-browsing were no longer SNS-specific behaviors but had diffused across the wider web — and updates the definition to center the maintenance of an articulated list of connections presented for viewing, while relaxing the emphasis on the bounded-profile requirement.
Both papers are peer-reviewed (a journal article and a handbook chapter from an academic press), and both are treated in this section as the definitional anchor for `social-network-site` as a term.

## 2. Affordance theory for social media

Jeffrey Treem and Paul Leonardi's ["Social Media Use in Organizations: Exploring the Affordances of Visibility, Editability, Persistence, and Association"](https://academic.oup.com/anncom/article-abstract/36/1/143/7885569) (*Annals of the International Communication Association* 36(1), 2013, pp. 143-189 — this is the journal-indexed republication Crossref lists at this DOI and URL; the same chapter also appears, differently paginated (pp. 157-203), as a chapter in the *Communication Yearbook 36* book volume, so a citation giving "Communication Yearbook 36, pp. 143-189" — as an earlier draft of this note did — mismatches venue and page range) is the paper that introduced the four-affordance framework — visibility, persistence, editability, and association — that most subsequent social-media affordance work in communication studies departs from or extends.
Affordances, on this framing, are not properties of the technology alone nor of the user alone but of the technology-user relation: what actions a given technology makes easier or harder for a given actor to accomplish.
Treem and Leonardi's original focus was organizational communication, but the four affordances have been taken up well beyond that context.

Sandra Evans, Katy Pearce, Jessica Vitak, and Jeffrey Treem's ["Explicating Affordances: A Conceptual Framework for Understanding Affordances in Communication Research"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jcc4.12180) (*Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication* 22(1), 2017, pp. 35-52) is the methodological follow-up: reviewing 82 communication-oriented empirical works using affordance terminology, the authors find three recurring inconsistencies in how "affordance" gets used (treating it as a property of the technology alone, conflating affordances with outcomes, and failing to specify the actor for whom something is afforded) and propose a framework that keeps affordances relational and scoped to a specified actor, artifact, and practice.
This paper is the citation used in this section whenever a term needs to be precise about what "affordance" does and does not mean, as distinct from Treem and Leonardi's original substantive four-affordance framework.

## 3. Computational social science manifestos

David Lazer and fourteen coauthors published ["Computational Social Science"](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1167742) (*Science* 323(5915), 2009, pp. 721-723; DOI 10.1126/science.1167742 — title, author list, volume, issue, and pages independently confirmed this pass via the Crossref and PubMed records for that DOI, and via the formal citation template on Wikipedia's David Lazer page).
**Correction:** an earlier draft of this note gave the title as "Life in the Network: The Coming Age of Computational Social Science"; that phrase is used informally in some secondary discourse (including, loosely, in Wikipedia's own prose) but is not the article's published title — Crossref, PubMed, and the formal citation record all agree the title is simply "Computational Social Science."
The paper is widely credited as the field-founding manifesto for computational social science, arguing that the new availability of large-scale behavioral data from digital systems (mobile communication, social media, online transactions) opened a research program that could not be pursued with prior survey- and observation-based methods alone, while also flagging early the privacy and access constraints that would recur through the following decade.

Eleven years later, Lazer, Pentland, and thirteen other coauthors (a substantially different and expanded author list) published ["Computational Social Science: Obstacles and Opportunities"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aaz8170) (*Science* 369(6507), 2020, pp. 1060-1062), taking stock of a field that had "exploded in prominence" in the intervening decade and naming the obstacle that most concerns this section directly: that platform-held data essential to the field's questions is increasingly gated behind proprietary access, inconsistent researcher-data-sharing arrangements, and — as covered in Section 6 below — outright retraction of previously available tools.

## 4. The adolescent-wellbeing controversy

This is presented as genuinely contested because the peer-reviewed literature itself is genuinely split on effect size and interpretation, not because of adjacent political noise around it.

Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski's ["The Association between Adolescent Well-Being and Digital Technology Use"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0506-1) (*Nature Human Behaviour* 3, 2019, pp. 173-182) applies specification curve analysis — running the association across many defensible combinations of variables, controls, and analytic choices rather than a single model — to three large-scale datasets (combined n = 355,358) and finds a negative but very small association between digital technology use and adolescent wellbeing, explaining at most about 0.4% of variance, which the authors argue is too small to justify the scale of policy alarm the topic attracts.

Jean Twenge and Jonathan Haidt, among others, have contested both the size and the interpretation of that estimate.
A specification-curve study from the Twenge side of the debate, Jean M. Twenge, Jonathan Haidt, Jimmy Lozano, and Kevin M. Cummins's ["Specification Curve Analysis Shows That Social Media Use Is Linked to Poor Mental Health, Especially among Girls"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001691822000270) (*Acta Psychologica* 224, 2022, article 103512; DOI 10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103512 — author list, journal, volume, and article number confirmed this pass via the Crossref record for that DOI, which also confirms the ScienceDirect PII given in the link above), applies the same general method to argue for a larger, gender-differentiated association than Orben and Przybylski's headline figure.
The broader shape of the counter-argument — that pooling heterogeneous "screen time" categories together, not disaggregating by gender or age band, and treating a population-average association as if it applied uniformly each obscure a real and larger effect for particular subgroups — is attested across multiple secondary sources found in this research pass but is reported here as the contested position it is, not adopted as settled.

Haidt's trade book *The Anxious Generation: How the Great Rewiring of Childhood Is Causing an Epidemic of Mental Illness* (Penguin Press, 2024; publisher and subtitle confirmed this pass via Open Library edition records, in addition to the existence, 2024 date, and Haidt's authorship already confirmed via his own [social-media argument page](https://jonathanhaidt.com/social-media/) and via the Nature review below) argues for a causal, large-effect account in which smartphone- and social-media-mediated childhood substantially drives a documented rise in adolescent anxiety and depression.
Candice Odgers' review in *Nature*, ["Social-Media Influence on Teen Mental Health Goes Beyond Just Cause and Effect"](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-024-01488-5) (2024), is the prominent scholarly critique named in this study's scope: Odgers argues the book's causal and rewiring claims outrun what the correlational and quasi-experimental evidence supports, stating that the large effects Haidt describes have not been found by the "hundreds of researchers" (herself included) who have looked, whose work instead "produced a mix of no, small, and mixed associations."
The controversy is carried forward here exactly as contested: peer-reviewed specification-curve evidence gives small effects; a prominent trade book argues for large causal effects; a peer-reviewed review essay directly disputes the book's causal reading.
None of the three resolves the other two.

## 5. Echo chambers and selective exposure

Eytan Bakshy, Solomon Messing, and Lada Adamic's ["Exposure to Ideologically Diverse News and Opinion on Facebook"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25953820/) (*Science* 348(6239), 2015, pp. 1130-1132), conducted with Facebook's own data on 10.1 million U.S. users, found that algorithmic feed ranking reduced exposure to ideologically cross-cutting content by roughly 5% for conservatives and 8% for liberals relative to a random baseline, while users' own choices about what to click reduced cross-cutting exposure considerably more than the algorithm did — a finding frequently cited on both sides of the echo-chamber debate, since it locates a real but comparatively modest platform-side effect alongside a larger individual-choice effect.

Andrew Guess's "(Almost) Everything in Moderation: New Evidence on Americans' Online Media Diets" (*American Journal of Political Science*, 2021) is the complicating evidence named in this section's scope: using behavioral browsing data rather than self-report, Guess finds that politically one-sided news consumption ("echo chambers" in the strict sense) characterizes a small, highly engaged minority of the public, while most people's online news diets are dominated by centrist, non-political, or no news consumption at all — directly complicating any simple story in which most social-media users inhabit sealed ideological chambers.
This builds on earlier work by Guess with Brendan Nyhan, Benjamin Lyons, and Jason Reifler, ["Avoiding the Echo Chamber about Echo Chambers"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330144926_Avoiding_the_echo_chamber_about_echo_chambers_Why_selective_exposure_to_like-minded_political_news_is_less_prevalent_than_you_think) (2018), which argues the same point from earlier data.

Axel Bruns' book-length critique, *Are Filter Bubbles Real?* (Polity, 2019; reviewed at [LSE](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/lsereviewofbooks/2020/01/17/book-review-are-filter-bubbles-real-by-axel-bruns/) and [LSE USAPP](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/usappblog/2020/02/02/book-review-are-filter-bubbles-real-by-axel-bruns/)), is the named critique of the echo-chamber thesis in this section's scope.
Bruns argues the filter-bubble and echo-chamber concepts have been substantially overstated relative to the evidence, that they function in public discourse as much as a moral panic about platforms as an empirically supported mechanism, and that the focus on algorithmic sorting obscures more consequential drivers of polarization such as the rise of populism itself.
This is a trade-facing academic book (Polity's Digital Futures series) rather than a peer-reviewed journal article, and is treated in this section as a scholarly critique carrying somewhat different evidentiary weight than the peer-reviewed Bakshy/Guess findings it argues against, even though its author is an academic (QUT) writing in that capacity.

Taken together: the peer-reviewed empirical record (Bakshy et al., Guess et al.) finds algorithmic and behavioral echo-chamber effects that are real but smaller and less universal than the popular "filter bubble" narrative claims, and a named scholarly critique (Bruns) argues the popular narrative substantially overshoots even that qualified empirical record.
This section's `echo-chamber` glossary term reflects that qualified, contested status rather than presenting echo chambers as an established mass phenomenon.

## 6. Misinformation circulation

Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy, and Sinan Aral's ["The Spread of True and False News Online"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aap9559) (*Science* 359(6380), 2018, pp. 1146-1151) is the best-verified, least-contested claim in this study.
Analyzing roughly 126,000 rumor cascades spread by about 3 million people on Twitter between 2006 and 2017 (verified against independent fact-checking organizations to establish ground truth), the authors find that false news reached far more people than true news — the top 1% of false-news cascades reached between 1,000 and 100,000 people, while true news rarely exceeded 1,000 — and that falsehood spread faster, deeper, and more broadly than truth across every category of news studied, with political news showing the largest such effect.
The authors' analysis further finds that this pattern held after controlling for bot activity (bots accelerated both true and false news at roughly equal rates), attributing the differential instead to the novelty of false claims and the stronger emotional reactions (surprise, disgust) they provoke in human sharers.
This paper's finding is treated in this section as settled peer-reviewed fact about the dataset studied (Twitter, 2006-2017), not as an unqualified claim about all platforms or all periods.

## 7. Platform data access after the API era

This section's scope calls for citing what is verifiable about the shrinking of platform data access and marking the rest **[UNVERIFIED]**.

What is verifiable: Meta's CrowdTangle — the free content-tracking tool journalists, researchers, and civil-society groups relied on for public-content monitoring across Facebook and Instagram — was shut down on August 14, 2024, as confirmed by multiple contemporaneous reports including [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/meta-announces-shut-down-crowdtangle-monitoring-app/710358/) and [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/metas-crowdtangle-to-shut-down-in-august-2024/).
Meta stated the shutdown was to redirect resources toward its replacement, the Meta Content Library and Content Library API, access to which (per [Meta's own announcement](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/11/new-tools-to-support-independent-research/)) is restricted to academic and nonprofit researchers applying through the Inter-university Consortium for Political and Social Research (ICPSR), and which a joint investigation by Proof News, the Tow Center for Digital Journalism, and the Algorithmic Transparency Institute — reported by the [Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/tow_center/meta-is-getting-rid-of-crowdtangle.php) — found to be less transparent and less accessible than CrowdTangle had been, and unavailable to journalists at all.

Not independently re-verified in this pass: the parallel and widely reported claim that Twitter/X sharply restricted or priced out academic API access following its 2022-2023 change of ownership, ending the free academic-research API tier that had underpinned a large share of computational social science research on that platform.
This is consistent with what is described in this study's own Section 3 sources as a recognized obstacle facing the field, and is very likely true, but the specific pricing and policy details were not re-confirmed against a primary source in this research pass, so the X/Twitter claim specifically is marked **[UNVERIFIED]** pending direct verification.

## Gaps carried forward

- The Twitter/X post-2023 API pricing and academic-access restriction (Section 7) is widely reported but was not re-verified against a primary Twitter/X source or a peer-reviewed methods paper in this pass; it should be confirmed (or the platform's current developer-terms page checked directly) before any term states it as settled.
- Bruns' *Are Filter Bubbles Real?* (Section 5) is a trade-facing academic book rather than a peer-reviewed journal article, and could not be independently re-confirmed at the publisher level in this pass (Polity does not register trade/academic books with Crossref); its existence and argument are corroborated by Bruns' related, Crossref-indexed works on the same thesis ("Filter bubble," *Internet Policy Review*, 2019; "Echo chambers? Filter bubbles? The misleading metaphors that obscure the real problem," Routledge, 2021) and by the cited LSE and LSE USAPP reviews, but the book's own front-matter (exact page count, ISBN) was not checked — future terms citing it should keep the trade/peer-reviewed distinction visible rather than treating it as equivalent in evidentiary weight to the peer-reviewed Bakshy and Guess papers it argues against.
- This study does not yet cover the platformization, networked-publics, or influencer/creator literatures named in the section README's scope (Gillespie, van Dijck/Poell/de Waal, Helmond, Nieborg & Poell, Senft, Marwick, Abidin, Duffy, Cunningham & Craig) — those remain open for a follow-up study.
- The Guess, Nyhan, Lyons & Reifler (2018) "Avoiding the Echo Chamber about Echo Chambers" report (Section 5) is treated as a genuine, non-peer-reviewed report (no Crossref DOI was found for it, consistent with its being a foundation-commissioned report rather than a journal article) on the strength of its title, authors, and 2018 date matching training-era knowledge and the linked ResearchGate record's URL slug; the ResearchGate page itself returned HTTP 403 to both direct fetch and WebFetch in this pass and so could not be loaded to double-check its content directly.
