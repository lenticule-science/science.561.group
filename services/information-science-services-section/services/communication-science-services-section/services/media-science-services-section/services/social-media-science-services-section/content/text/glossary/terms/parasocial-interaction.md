Parasocial interaction is the sense of intimacy, familiarity, and relationship that an audience member develops toward a mass-media performer or persona, despite the interaction being structurally one-sided — the performer does not know the audience member exists, yet the audience member experiences something like a genuine social bond.
The term was coined by Donald Horton and R. Richard Wohl in "Mass Communication and Para-Social Interaction: Observations on Intimacy at a Distance," *Psychiatry* 19(3), pp. 215–229 (1956) ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00332747.1956.11023049); [PubMed 13359569](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13359569/)), in a study of radio and television personae.
Horton & Wohl's argument was that broadcast personae are constructed — through direct address, conversational tone, and simulated responsiveness — specifically to invite this illusion of reciprocity.

## From broadcast to platform

The concept predates social media, [microcelebrity](./microcelebrity.md), and the [creator economy](./creator-economy.md) by roughly half a century, and this section treats it as the shared conceptual root that the later influencer literature re-applies rather than reinvents.
Platform-native performers — [influencers](./influencer.md) in particular — intensify the conditions Horton & Wohl described: direct comment-reply, livestreaming, and algorithmically frequent exposure to the same persona all deepen the audience's felt closeness relative to one-way broadcast.
Crystal Abidin's "communicative intimacies" (see [influencer](./influencer.md)) is one attempt within this section's own literature to name that platform-specific intensification.

## The marketing-effectiveness application

A distinct, quantitative literature applies parasocial-relationship theory to influencer-marketing outcomes specifically.
Kumju Hwang & Qi Zhang, "Influence of parasocial relationship between digital celebrities and their followers on followers' purchase and electronic word-of-mouth intentions, and persuasion knowledge," *Computers in Human Behavior* 87, pp. 155–173 (2018), DOI: [10.1016/j.chb.2018.05.029](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2018.05.029), finds parasocial relationship strength has a measurable positive effect on purchase intention and electronic word-of-mouth intention, moderated by followers' persuasion knowledge.
This paper sits on this section's boundary with market science: the underlying mechanism (parasocial-relationship formation) is media science's object; the dependent variable studied (purchase intention) is influencer-*marketing* effectiveness, owned by [market-science-services-section](/home/emsenn/info/lib/emsenn/services/561-group/services/science-services-section/services/philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agent-science-services-section/services/social-science-services-section/services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/business-science-services-section/services/marketing-science-services-section).
See the companion study note, [Study of Influencer and Creator Science](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-the-study-of-influencer-and-creator-science.md), §1 and §6, for the fuller discussion.
