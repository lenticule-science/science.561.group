An **echo chamber** (sometimes "filter bubble" when the emphasis is on algorithmic sorting rather than user choice) names the claim that social media exposes users mainly to ideologically-congenial content and people, whether through platform ranking algorithms, users' own selective following and clicking behavior, or both together — reinforcing existing beliefs and reducing exposure to disconfirming or cross-cutting views.
The term is used across this section's literature in a qualified, contested sense rather than as an established finding about most users' experience of social media.

## Empirical qualification

Eytan Bakshy, Solomon Messing, and Lada Adamic's ["Exposure to Ideologically Diverse News and Opinion on Facebook"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25953820/) (*Science* 348(6239), 2015, pp. 1130-1132), using Facebook's own data on 10.1 million U.S. users, found that algorithmic feed ranking reduced exposure to ideologically cross-cutting content by a real but modest amount relative to a random baseline, and that users' own choices about what to click reduced cross-cutting exposure considerably more than the ranking algorithm did.

Andrew Guess's "(Almost) Everything in Moderation: New Evidence on Americans' Online Media Diets" (*American Journal of Political Science*, 2021), using behavioral browsing data rather than self-report, found that strictly one-sided ("echo chamber") news consumption characterizes a small, highly politically engaged minority, while most users' online news diets are centrist, mixed, or largely absent of political news altogether — building on earlier work with Brendan Nyhan, Benjamin Lyons, and Jason Reifler, ["Avoiding the Echo Chamber about Echo Chambers"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330144926_Avoiding_the_echo_chamber_about_echo_chambers_Why_selective_exposure_to_like-minded_political_news_is_less_prevalent_than_you_think) (2018).

## Critique of the thesis itself

Axel Bruns' *Are Filter Bubbles Real?* (Polity, 2019; reviewed at [LSE Review of Books](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/lsereviewofbooks/2020/01/17/book-review-are-filter-bubbles-real-by-axel-bruns/)) goes further than qualifying the effect size and argues the thesis itself is substantially overstated — a moral panic about platforms that displaces attention from more consequential drivers of polarization, such as the rise of populism.
This is a trade-facing academic book rather than a peer-reviewed journal article, and this section treats it as a named scholarly critique carrying different evidentiary weight than the peer-reviewed Bakshy and Guess findings it argues against, not as an equally weighted empirical study.

## Standing of the claim in this section

Taken together, the peer-reviewed record finds a real but qualified and non-universal echo-chamber effect, and a named critique argues even that qualified record is overstated relative to the popular narrative.
No term or note in this section should state "echo chambers" as an established, general effect of social media use; the qualified, contested status above is what the term names.
