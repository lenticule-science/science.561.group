Platformization names the extension of a platform's economic and infrastructural logic beyond its own boundaries, into the rest of the web and into the production processes of the industries that come to depend on it.
The term was given its influential technical definition by [Anne Helmond](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2056305115603080) in *Social Media + Society* volume 1, issue 2 (July 2015) --- the journal's first publication year, though its second issue rather than its inaugural one --- where she traces Facebook's Open Graph and Like-button infrastructure as a case of making external web data "platform ready" --- that is, structured so it can be captured back into the platform's own data architecture via APIs, plugins, and tracking mechanisms.
On this reading, platformization is not a metaphor but a describable technical and economic mechanism: a platform grows not only by adding users but by making the rest of the web an extension of itself.

David Nieborg and Thomas Poell extended the concept from web architecture to cultural political economy in their 2018 *New Media & Society* article, ["The Platformization of Cultural Production"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1461444818769694).
Using news and games as case studies, they argue that platformization replaces simple two-sided market structures with "contingent" multi-sided configurations dominated by a small number of large platform corporations, and that this restructuring makes cultural commodities themselves *contingent*: their form, distribution, and monetization become dependent on platform rules and algorithmic distribution logics that the platform, not the producer, controls and can change unilaterally.
This is the sense of platformization most directly relevant to this section's scope on media and cultural production.

Both definitions build on the earlier, broader claim --- developed by [José van Dijck, Thomas Poell, and Martijn de Waal](./platform-society.md) in *The Platform Society* --- that platforms have migrated from a peripheral, market-facing role into infrastructural positions across society.
[Jean-Christophe Plantin, Carl Lagoze, Paul Edwards, and Christian Sandvig](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1461444816661553) complicate this migration further by arguing that firms like Google and Facebook behave *as* infrastructure while retaining the private, discretionary governance of a platform, a hybrid condition distinct from platformization proper but frequently entangled with it in the literature.

For fuller sourcing and the gaps this literature still carries, see the [study of platformization and platform studies](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-the-study-of-platformization-and-platform-studies.md).
