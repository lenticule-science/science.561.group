The platform society names a societal condition, not merely a technical or economic one: platforms have moved from occupying a narrow market niche (search, social networking, e-commerce) to occupying infrastructural positions across sectors --- health records, classroom software, news distribution, urban mobility, and civic organizing --- such that opting out of platform mediation becomes practically difficult across large areas of public life.
The term and its sustained argument come from [José van Dijck, Thomas Poell, and Martijn de Waal](https://academic.oup.com/book/12378), *The Platform Society: Public Values in a Connective World* (Oxford University Press, 2018).
Van Dijck is a distinguished professor at Utrecht University and a former president of the Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences; Poell is a senior lecturer at the University of Amsterdam; de Waal is affiliated with the Play & Civic Media research group at the Amsterdam University of Applied Sciences.

The book's central tension is normative as much as descriptive: as platforms take on infrastructural roles once held by public or heavily regulated institutions, the public values those institutions were built to protect --- accountability, fairness, privacy, democratic oversight --- come into conflict with platforms' private, proprietary, and frequently opaque governance.
This argument presupposes and extends van Dijck's earlier solo work, [*The Culture of Connectivity*](https://academic.oup.com/book/9914) (2013), which traced the cultural and economic history of platforms including Flickr, YouTube, and Wikipedia through the 2000s and argued that "connectivity" is an engineered condition rather than a neutral technical affordance.

The platform society thesis is the broader societal claim from which [platformization](./platformization.md) --- the more specific process by which a platform's logic extends into web data and cultural production --- is derived by Helmond and by Nieborg and Poell.
It also motivates [Tarleton Gillespie's](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300261431/custodians-of-the-internet/) governance-focused *Custodians of the Internet* (2018) and the infrastructure/platform distinction drawn by [Plantin, Lagoze, Edwards, and Sandvig](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1461444816661553) (2018), both of which examine specific mechanisms by which platforms hold the infrastructural power the platform-society thesis describes at the societal level.

For fuller sourcing and the gaps this literature still carries, see the [study of platformization and platform studies](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-the-study-of-platformization-and-platform-studies.md).
