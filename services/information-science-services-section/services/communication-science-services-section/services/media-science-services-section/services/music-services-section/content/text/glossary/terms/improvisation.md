Improvisation is the practice of making musical decisions in real time during performance — composing and performing simultaneously. It is not the absence of structure but the navigation of structure: the improviser works within constraints (a scale, a [rhythmic](../../../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/rhythm.md) framework, a [harmonic](./harmony.md) progression, a social convention, a tradition) and makes choices that are at once responsive to the moment and shaped by deep practice.

Western music education has tended to treat improvisation as either a primitive stage before "real" composition or an advanced skill reserved for specialists (jazz soloists, organ improvisers). But across the world's musical traditions, improvisation is the norm rather than the exception. The fixed, fully notated musical work — reproducible identically across performances — is a historical anomaly specific to a particular period of European art music. Most of the music humans have made has involved substantial real-time decision-making.

Improvisation is inherently relational. The solo improviser relates to the instrument, to the tradition, to the room, to their own body's state. The ensemble improviser relates to all of these and to the other musicians — listening, responding, anticipating, provoking. [Call-and-response](./call-and-response.md) is a formalized improvisatory relation. Jazz rhythm section interaction is an informal one. [Tishoumaren](tishoumaren.md) guitar traditions involve collective improvisation within melodic and [rhythmic](../../../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/rhythm.md) frameworks inherited from Tuareg musical practice.
