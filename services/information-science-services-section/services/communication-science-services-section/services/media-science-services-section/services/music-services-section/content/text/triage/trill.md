# Trill

The trill is a sustained rapid alternation between two adjacent scale-degrees on the [cedar-flute](./cedar-flute.md), produced by rapidly oscillating a finger between covered and uncovered positions on a single [finger-hole](./finger-hole.md), while the other fingers remain in a fixed position. The trill is the [ornament](./ornament.md) of *extended alternation*: where the [finger-pop](./finger-pop.md) is a single brief event (one lift-and-return), the trill is a sustained event (many lift-and-returns in quick succession), and the trill's duration may extend from a fraction of a second to a substantial fraction of an entire held tone.

Mechanically, the trill is straightforward but technically demanding. The player establishes a fingering for the lower of the two scale-degrees involved in the trill, then rapidly lifts and replaces the finger controlling the next-higher scale-degree's hole, sustaining this alternation for the desired duration. The trill's rate — how many alternations per second occur — is in the range of roughly 4 to 8 alternations per second for an idiomatic cedar-flute trill, slower than the [warble](./warble.md)'s acoustic rate but fast enough that the listener hears the trill as a single ornamented event rather than as a sequence of separate notes. The exact rate is at the player's discretion and admits some expressive variation.

The trill's pitch interval is determined by which two scale-degrees it alternates between, which in turn is determined by the player's choice of fingerings. The most idiomatic trills on the cedar flute are between adjacent scale-degrees — a trill between 1 and b3 (a minor third), a trill between b3 and 4 (a major second), a trill between 4 and 5 (a major second), a trill between 5 and b7 (a minor third), and a trill between b7 and 8 (a major second). Non-adjacent trills (across a wider interval) are physically possible but require more finger-coordination and are less common in idiomatic playing.

The trill's musical functions are principally three. First, *ornamental extension*: the trill provides a way to fill an extended duration with continuous articulative activity, where a plain held tone might feel static. A held tone of two or three seconds with a trill in its middle section has a more dynamic shape than the same tone held plain throughout. Second, *cadential preparation*: the trill on the upper neighbor to a cadential approach-tone (a trill between 4 and b3 just before the cadential descent to 1, or a trill between b3 and 1 itself approaching the final tonic) is an idiomatic cedar-flute cadential gesture, often combined with a [warble](./warble.md) on the terminal tonic. Third, *expressive variation across strophes*: a melodic position that is held plain in one strophe of a [courting-song](./courting-song.md) may receive a trill in another strophe, providing one of the principal axes of [ornament-substitution](../../code/transformers/ornament-substitution.md).

The trill is distinct from the [finger-pop](./finger-pop.md) in being a sustained alternation rather than a single brief event. It is distinct from the [warble](./warble.md) in being a *fingering*-controlled alternation between two distinct scale-degrees (each of which is a stable acoustic mode of the bore) rather than a *jet-instability*-controlled alternation between two harmonic modes of the same fingering. It is distinct from the [finger-slide](./finger-slide.md) in being a discrete alternation between two pitches rather than a continuous pitch glide. The trill is acoustically a *melodic* ornament — it sounds two distinct scale-degrees, alternating — whereas the warble is a *timbral* ornament — it sounds a single perceived event with a characteristic wobble timbre.

In the [instrumentalization](./instrumentalization.md) of [wiilowan](./wiilowan.md) vocal song onto the cedar flute, the trill renders vocal melisma — extended vocal ornamentation that crosses several pitches in the duration of a single syllable — into a finger-controlled flute equivalent. Vocal melisma is more pitch-flexible than the flute trill (it can cross more than two pitches, and at varying intervals), so the flute trill is in some cases a simplification of the vocal source rather than a one-to-one rendering.
