A drone is a sustained or continuously repeated pitch (or cluster of pitches) that persists beneath or alongside other musical activity. It provides a fixed harmonic and timbral ground against which [melody](../../../../services/music-theory-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/melody.md), [rhythm](../../../../services/music-theory-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/rhythm.md), and [timbral](../../../../services/music-theory-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/timbre.md) variation unfold.

Drone-based music operates on different organizational principles from chord-progression-based music. Where [tonal](../../../../services/music-theory-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/tonality.md) [harmony](../../../../services/music-theory-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/harmony.md) creates movement through a sequence of changing harmonic states, a drone creates movement through the changing *relationship* between the moving voices and the fixed ground. The drone does not progress; the listener's perception of it shifts as the melodic and rhythmic activity above it changes. This makes drone music fundamentally relational: the musical interest lies not in the drone or the melody alone but in the interaction between them.

Drone practices appear across many of the world's musical traditions: the tanpura in Hindustani and Carnatic music, the bordun of medieval European organum, the didgeridoo in Aboriginal Australian ceremonial music, the bagpipe drone across Celtic and Balkan traditions, the shruti box in devotional singing, and the amplifier feedback exploited in electric guitar music. These are not the same practice — each drone tradition carries its own aesthetic, spiritual, and social functions — but they share the structural principle of a fixed ground that produces relational meaning.
