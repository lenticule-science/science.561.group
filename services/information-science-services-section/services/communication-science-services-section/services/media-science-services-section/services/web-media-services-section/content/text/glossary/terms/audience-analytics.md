Audience analytics are the real-time and aggregate web-traffic measurement tools — page views, time-on-page, scroll depth, referral source, concurrent readers — that web newsrooms use to observe how their content is actually being read, as distinct from the peer-based quality judgments that structured pre-digital newsroom gatekeeping.
The peer-reviewed literature on their organizational effect resists a single simple story.
Angèle Christin's *Metrics at Work: Journalism and the Contested Meaning of Algorithms* (Princeton University Press, 2020), based on four years of fieldwork and 100+ interviews in US and French web newsrooms, found American journalists in her sample often disregarded traffic numbers and deferred to peer judgment of quality, while French journalists fixated on traffic as a marker of public resonance — the same technology producing divergent effects across newsroom cultures (see [Princeton University Press](https://press.princeton.edu/books/ebook/9780691200002/metrics-at-work-pdf-1)).
Caitlin Petre's *All the News That's Fit to Click* (Princeton University Press, 2021), studying the New York Times, Gawker, and the analytics vendor Chartbeat directly, documents dashboards deliberately built with game-like feedback — immersive displays, instant scores, live rankings — to win journalists' trust and attention, while also showing journalists turning metrics into leverage for professional autonomy rather than being purely subject to them (see [Princeton University Press](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691177649/all-the-news-thats-fit-to-click)).
Edson C. Tandoc Jr.'s "Journalism Is Twerking? How Web Analytics Is Changing the Process of Gatekeeping" (*New Media & Society*, 2014), based on 150 hours of observation and 30 interviews across three online newsrooms, names "de-selection" — analytics driving what gets pulled or downplayed after publication, not only what gets produced in the first place — as a distinct new gatekeeping mechanism (see [the article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1461444814530541)).

This section's 2026-07-29 note (a Gemini Notebook-generated speculative governance framework, not an empirical source) frames a "recommender game equilibrium" of fast manipulation signals crowding out slow quality signals; Christin's finding that two comparable Western newsrooms responded to the same technology in opposite ways is the kind of empirical complication that framework has not yet engaged, and any production work built on that note should reconcile it against Christin, Petre, and Tandoc rather than cite the note as if it were itself evidence.
