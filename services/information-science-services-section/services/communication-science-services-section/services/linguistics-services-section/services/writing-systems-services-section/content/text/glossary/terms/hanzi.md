Hanzi (漢字/汉字, *hànzì*, "Han characters") are the logographic characters
used to write Mandarin and other Sinitic languages, and — historically or
still — Japanese (as kanji), Korean (hanja, now marginal), and Vietnamese
(chữ Nôm, now obsolete). Linguists more precisely call the system
**morphosyllabic**: each character typically represents one morpheme and
one syllable, not a phoneme or a whole word (DeFrancis 1984) — a distinction
worth holding onto, since "ideographic" (each character a picture of an
idea, unconnected to sound) is the popular but inaccurate folk account. Most
characters encode sound information too, through a phonetic component; see
[liùshū](./liushu.md) below.

## As a meme-gauge script

This section reads hanzi through [script as gauge](./script-as-gauge.md):
where an alphabet's letters coordinate the phoneme layer, a hanzi character
coordinates the meme layer — the unit of meaning — directly, and does so
with its formation history still legible in the glyph. A character is not
merely a meaning-bearing symbol; it is a meaning-bearing symbol that shows
its work. See [radical as witness mark](./radical-as-witness-mark.md) for
the mechanism, and [liùshū](./liushu.md) for the traditional taxonomy of how
that work gets shown.

## Structural properties

- **Compositional**: the large majority of characters are compounds of
  smaller components (radicals and phonetic elements), not atomic shapes —
  of the roughly 3,500 characters in common modern use, most are
  [phono-semantic compounds](./liushu.md#形聲-xíngshēng--phono-semantic-compounds).
- **One character, roughly one syllable**: this holds with more regularity
  than English spelling's relation to phonemes, though tone is not marked
  in the character itself.
- **Radical-indexed**: dictionaries traditionally index characters by a
  semantic radical (214 in the standard Kangxi set), which is itself a
  claim about which component of a compound character is load-bearing for
  meaning.
- **Simplified vs. traditional**: mainland China's 1950s–60s simplification
  reduced stroke counts for many common characters; Taiwan, Hong Kong, and
  most overseas communities retain the traditional forms. This is a real
  gauge-change worth its own future entry — a re-fixing of the meme gauge
  that in some cases thins the visible witness structure a character
  carries (compare 愛 traditional, which contains 心 "heart", against 爱
  simplified, which does not).
