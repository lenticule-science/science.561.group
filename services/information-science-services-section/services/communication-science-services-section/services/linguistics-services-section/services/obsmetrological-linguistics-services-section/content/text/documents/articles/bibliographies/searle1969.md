# Searle 1969 — Speech Acts: An Essay in the Philosophy of Language

*Speech Acts: An Essay in the Philosophy of Language*. Cambridge:
Cambridge University Press, 1969.

## Why this is central

Searle systematizes Austin's (1962) felicity conditions into a general
apparatus — propositional-content conditions, preparatory conditions,
sincerity conditions, and an essential condition — that together specify
what must hold for an illocutionary act of a given type to be performed
successfully and non-defectively. Building from this apparatus in his
later work, Searle classifies illocutionary acts by **direction of
fit**: assertives fit words to an independent world (word-to-world);
directives and commissives fit the world to the words (world-to-word);
declarations achieve both directions of fit simultaneously, by the act's
own performance; expressives carry no direction of fit. This taxonomy is
the standard successor to Austin's own provisional force-verb classes,
and its direction-of-fit distinction is load-bearing for how the house's
[speech-act](../../../glossary/terms/speech-act.md) term separates
epistemic from deontic and constitutive illocutionary force.

## What it does not settle

Searle's felicity-condition apparatus does not by itself address uptake
as a distinct requirement the way Austin's Lecture IX does; Searle
treats successful communication largely through the sincerity and
essential conditions rather than through a separately named hearer-side
act. Searle's own taxonomy paper post-dates this 1969 book by several
years; the direction-of-fit distinction cited here is the mature form
of an apparatus this book lays the groundwork for rather than states in
final form.
