# Cavell 1969 — Knowing and Acknowledging

"Knowing and Acknowledging," in *Must We Mean What We Say? A Book of
Essays*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1969.

## Why this is central

Cavell argues, against the assumption that a failure to respond to
another person's condition (paradigmatically, their suffering) is an
epistemic failure correctable by more or better knowledge, that
acknowledging is a distinct kind of response — one *owed*, not one that
reduces to or is repaired by knowing. Knowledge of another's state does
not by itself discharge what the situation calls for; acknowledging is
the further, irreducible act the situation calls for. Read onto
[uptake](../../../glossary/terms/uptake.md), this sharpens Austin's
(1962) securing of uptake from a merely epistemic condition (the hearer
now possesses correct information about what was meant) into an
agentive one (the hearer has done something, owed by the speaker's act,
that mere correct understanding does not itself constitute).

## What it does not settle

Cavell's essay is not about speech-act theory, illocutionary force, or
Austin's uptake condition directly; its case is knowing and
acknowledging another person's suffering. The house's application of
Cavell's knowing/acknowledging distinction to Austin's uptake is this
corpus's own transfer, made explicit as such on the
[uptake](../../../glossary/terms/uptake.md) term, not a claim Cavell
himself makes about speech-act theory.
