# Austin 1962 — How to Do Things with Words

*How to Do Things with Words*. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962. Delivered as
the William James Lectures at Harvard University, 1955; edited from
Austin's notes and published posthumously.

## Why this is central

This is the founding text of speech-act theory and the direct source of
the house's own locution/illocution/perlocution factorization. Austin
overturns the assumption that an utterance's only job is to describe a
state of affairs truly or falsely (his target, the "descriptive
fallacy"), starting from the performative/constative distinction and
generalizing it into the claim that every utterance is, among other
things, a locutionary act (saying something), an illocutionary act
(doing something in saying it, with a force), and a perlocutionary act
(bringing something about by saying it). Lecture IX adds a further,
separable requirement on an illocutionary act's completion: the securing
of uptake, the hearer's understanding of the locution's meaning and
force — the source concept for the house's [uptake](../../../glossary/terms/uptake.md)
term.

## What it does not settle

Austin's own taxonomy of illocutionary-force verb classes (Lecture XII)
is famously provisional and is superseded in the literature by Searle's
(1969) systematization and by Bach and Harnish's (1979) taxonomy. Austin
also does not formalize uptake, force, or the felicity conditions
mathematically; the house's categorical and channel-theoretic readings
of locution, illocution, and perlocution are the corpus's own
formalization, not Austin's.
