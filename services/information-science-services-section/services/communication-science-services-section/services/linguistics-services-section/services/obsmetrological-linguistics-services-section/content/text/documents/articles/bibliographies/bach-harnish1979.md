# Bach & Harnish 1979 — Linguistic Communication and Speech Acts

*Linguistic Communication and Speech Acts*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press,
1979.

## Why this is central

Bach and Harnish give speech-act theory an inferential, Gricean
grounding — a hearer recognizes an illocutionary act by inferring the
speaker's communicative intention, not by decoding a fixed force marker —
and, on that basis, refine the Austin/Searle taxonomy into four families
of communicative illocutionary acts: **constatives** (asserting,
predicting, reporting), **directives** (requesting, questioning,
advising), **commissives** (promising, offering), and, distinctly,
**acknowledgments** (apologizing, thanking, greeting, congratulating,
accepting, rejecting) — courtesy acts that express an attitude toward
the hearer rather than asserting a proposition or committing to a future
act, and whose felicity typically presupposes a prior act by the hearer
that the acknowledgment responds to. This fourth class is the point of
contact with the house's [uptake](../../../glossary/terms/uptake.md)
term: acknowledgments are, on this taxonomy, the family of illocutionary
acts whose point is a response to the hearer's own prior act, not a
claim about or commitment toward the world.

## What it does not settle

Bach and Harnish's acknowledgments class is a taxonomy of illocutionary
*act types* a speaker performs (apologizing is something a speaker does),
not a formal account of the securing of uptake itself as Austin poses it
(a condition on whether *any* illocutionary act, of any type, has been
successfully done at all). The house's reading connecting the
acknowledgments class to uptake is this corpus's own inference, stated
as such on the [speech act](../../../glossary/terms/speech-act.md) term,
not a claim Bach and Harnish make.
