A **speech act** is what a speaker does in producing an utterance, analyzed
by Austin (1962) into three simultaneous acts performed by any one
utterance: the **locutionary** act (saying something, with a certain sense
and reference), the **illocutionary** act (the force with which it is said
— asserting, promising, warning, christening), and the **perlocutionary**
act (the effect brought about by saying it — persuading, alarming,
convincing). Searle (1969) systematizes Austin's felicity conditions
(propositional-content, preparatory, sincerity, and essential conditions
per act type) and, building from that account, classifies illocutionary
acts by **direction of fit**: assertives are word-to-world (the words are
meant to match an independent world), directives and commissives are
world-to-word (the world is meant to come to match the words), declarations
carry both directions of fit at once, and expressives carry none. Bach and
Harnish (1979) refine the taxonomy into four families of communicative
illocutionary acts: **constatives** (asserting, predicting, reporting),
**directives** (requesting, questioning, advising), **commissives**
(promising, offering), and a fourth class outside the constative/directive/
commissive core, **acknowledgments** (apologizing, thanking, greeting,
congratulating, accepting, rejecting) — courtesy acts that express an
attitude toward the hearer rather than asserting a proposition or
committing to a future act, and whose felicity typically presupposes a
prior act by the hearer that the acknowledgment responds to.
