## 0. What this is

American English /ɹ/ — the sound at the start of "red," the end of "car" — looks like one sound and is not one gesture. This article collects what the sourced literature says about that gap: the tongue can reach a correct-sounding /r/ by at least two genuinely different routes, the difference between those routes is invisible to the ear, the sound is the single most common holdout in children's speech development, and even *which* of the two routes a speaker habitually uses carries social information nobody consciously reports hearing. Every claim below is tagged with the evidence grade it was given in the sourced research pass — **well-established**, **emerging**, or **speculative** — and nothing has been upgraded from that pass. See [`mandarin-articulation-for-the-english-trained-vocal-tract.md`](mandarin-articulation-for-the-english-trained-vocal-tract.md) for how this same body of technique (motor learning, biofeedback, articulatory setting) applies to a tonal, non-rhotic target language, and [`nervous-system-differences-and-speech-motor-learning.md`](nervous-system-differences-and-speech-motor-learning.md) for the separate, more heavily hedged literature on individual differences in speech-motor learning.

## 1. Two tongues, one sound: the bunched/retroflex problem

**Well-established.** American /ɹ/ is produced with at least two distinct tongue-shape strategies — "bunched" (tongue tip down, dorsum raised into a hump) and "retroflex" (tongue tip raised and curled back) — plus intermediate variants, and these different vocal-tract shapes converge on nearly identical acoustic output, especially in the first three formants (F1–F3). The articulatory route to a correct-sounding /r/ is genuinely underdetermined by the sound itself. This is confirmed by two independent imaging traditions forty years apart: Delattre & Freeman's 1968 cineradiographic (X-ray movie) study of 46 speakers found roughly six to eight distinct tongue configurations for American /r/ across dialects; Zhou, Espy-Wilson, Boyce, Tiede, Holland & Choe's 2008 MRI study directly compared bunched and retroflex tokens and found the first three formants indistinguishable between shapes, with only the higher F4/F5 differing (a roughly 1400 Hz F4–F5 gap for retroflex tokens versus roughly 700 Hz for bunched).

**Well-established.** This bunched/retroflex variability is largely *covert* — invisible to ordinary auditory and acoustic transcription, and it went undetected through decades of prior sociolinguistic fieldwork, becoming visible only once real-time tongue imaging (ultrasound, MRI, X-ray) was applied. Lawson, Scobbie & Stuart-Smith (2011) state directly that tongue-shape variation for /r/ "had gone unidentified despite decades of auditory and acoustic analysis" before ultrasound tongue imaging revealed it. The broader phenomenon this instances — acoustically neutralized but articulatorily distinct "covert contrast," revealed only by tongue imaging — is separately documented in the clinical phonetics literature (see the review work associated with Cleland and colleagues on ultrasound tongue imaging and covert contrasts in children's speech).

## 2. Why /r/ is the sound that won't go away

**Well-established.** The American English rhotic is among the most commonly persisting error sounds in residual speech sound disorder (RSSD): children with RSSD past the expected age of acquisition are more likely to have a residual error on /ɹ/ than on any other single English phoneme, and the clinical literature widely attributes this persistence to the sound's unusual articulatory and motor complexity — multiple viable, precisely tuned tongue configurations, low proprioceptive visibility, and subtle acoustic targets. Boyce (2015) states plainly that /r/ is "the speech sound most likely to persist" among children with residual speech errors. Prevalence figures (RSSD affecting roughly 2–5% of school-age children, persisting into adolescence or adulthood in a smaller subset) are corroborated by the C-RESULTS randomized-controlled-trial protocol paper and related clinical literature.

**Emerging.** Whether it is specifically the *multiplicity of viable tongue shapes* — as opposed to acoustic subtlety, coarticulatory variation across vowel contexts, or clinician cueing practice — that makes /r/ uniquely therapy-resistant is argued and plausible, but not established as a proven mechanism. Researchers treat this as an open, actively studied question. McAllister Byun, Hitchcock & Swartz (2014) found individualized tongue-shape cueing — not a fixed, bunched-only target — produced markedly better treatment outcomes than a one-size-fits-all approach, which is consistent with a motor-complexity account but is not a direct test of it, and itself argues against there being one single motor-complexity bottleneck. A 2024 follow-up (Eads, Kabakoff, King, Preston & McAllister) found that children with treated RSSD show atypical patterns of contextual tongue-shape variation — no normal vowel-context conditioning — relative to typically developing peers, suggesting treatment-induced articulatory patterns can differ qualitatively from natural acquisition. This remains an actively researched, unresolved area.

## 3. Rhoticity as sociolinguistic marker — including beneath the audible signal

**Well-established.** Rhoticity — presence, absence, and realization of postvocalic /r/ — is a robust, repeatedly replicated sociolinguistic marker of social class and prestige across multiple English-speaking communities, with speech style and social context shifting the rate of the variant. Labov's 1966 department-store study of New York City (Saks > Macy's > S. Klein, tracking store prestige) is foundational; Mather (2012) replicated the same store-status stratification 54 years later, with rhoticity overall increased but the same ordering intact. Trudgill (1974) found comparable class and style stratification in Norwich English.

**Well-established, on a narrower evidence base.** The bunched/retroflex articulatory choice for /r/ is *itself* socially stratified — not just whether /r/ is present, but which tongue shape a speaker habitually uses. Lawson, Scobbie & Stuart-Smith (2011) found middle-class speakers of Scottish English favoring bunched articulations and working-class speakers favoring tongue-tip/front-raised, more retroflex-like variants — a genuinely embodied, neuromotor sociolinguistic marker operating beneath the audible signal. This is graded well-established for the core empirical finding, but it rests primarily on this one Scottish English dataset; generalization to American English tongue-shape sociophonetics has not been independently confirmed (see Open Questions).

**Well-established.** Listener perception of speech sounds, including sociolinguistic variables, is measurably shaped by social and identity information about the speaker, not by the acoustic signal alone — accommodation and social meaning operate on the perceptual side of the loop, not only the production side. Niedzielski (1999) found Detroit listeners told a speaker was Canadian versus Michigander perceived her vowels differently despite identical audio. Purnell, Idsardi & Baugh (1999) found listeners reliably identify speaker ethnicity or dialect from very brief samples ("hello"), underlying documented linguistic-profiling effects in housing audits.

**Emerging.** There is an active neuroscience literature on how the brain processes accented and dialectal speech — engaging prosody-related regions, talker-normalization circuitry in superior temporal cortex, and social-salience responses (including amygdala activity) that differ for in-group versus out-group accents — giving a genuine nervous-system dimension to sociophonetic perception in general. However, no study located isolates rhoticity or postvocalic-/r/ perception specifically as the triggering variable in a neuroimaging paradigm. The rhotic-specific version of this claim is an extrapolation from the broader accent-perception literature, not a direct finding, and is graded emerging rather than well-established for that reason.

## 4. Why the tongue can't just watch and copy

**Well-established.** Somatosensory and proprioceptive feedback — distinct from auditory feedback — plays a fundamental, independently necessary role in achieving precise speech-articulator movements. The tongue's position is monitored by an internal felt sense, not by vision, unlike most other articulators (lips, jaw), which have some visible or tactile-visible component. Tremblay, Shiller & Ostry (2003) used a robotic perturbation to dissociate somatosensory from auditory feedback and showed somatosensory targets are independently learned and defended. Nasir & Ostry (2006) extended this to precision requirements specifically. This body of work underlies — and is cited by — the rationale given in the ultrasound-biofeedback clinical literature for why /r/, an internally postured, non-visible articulation, is hard to teach through modeling or mimicry alone.

**Well-established.** Ultrasound and related visual-acoustic biofeedback, which gives learners real-time visual access to tongue shape that is otherwise proprioceptive-only, produces faster and/or more robust correction of persistent /r/ misarticulation than traditional auditory/verbal-cue therapy alone, particularly for children who did not respond to conventional therapy. McAllister Byun, Hitchcock & Swartz (2014) and the C-RESULTS multi-site randomized-controlled-trial protocol both document this. A 2025 RCT ("Rhotic Acquisition Is More Rapid in Biofeedback Than Motor-Based Treatment for Residual Speech Sound Disorder," JSLHR) reports biofeedback produced faster rhotic acquisition than a motor-based, non-visual comparison treatment in a randomized design, strengthening the causal claim that visual access to the otherwise-hidden tongue target speeds learning.

## 5. Articulatory setting: the whole-vocal-tract baseline

**Well-established, as a documented foundational text.** The concept of "articulatory setting" — that each language has a habitual, whole-vocal-tract postural baseline (tongue, jaw, lips, larynx height) that speakers default to between and across utterances, shaping the accent quality of everything spoken in that language — originates in Honikman's 1964 descriptive, pedagogical phonetic teaching tradition. Honikman defines articulatory setting as "the disposition of the parts of the speech mechanism and their composite action." This original formulation was itself impressionistic and pedagogical rather than instrumentally measured, which is why later work sought quantitative confirmation.

**Well-established.** Modern instrumental studies provide genuine quantitative evidence that speakers of different languages — and bilinguals switching languages — hold measurably different vocal-tract rest and inter-utterance postures, supporting the core empirical claim behind articulatory-setting theory. Gick, Wilson, Koch & Cook (2004) found English versus French speakers' inter-speech rest tongue postures differed significantly at multiple vocal-tract locations (a lower, more retracted tongue in English), consistent with the classic descriptive claims. Wilson & Gick (2014) found the same bilingual speakers shift rest posture — mainly lip configuration — depending on which language they are about to speak or have just spoken, directly supporting the idea that a learner's first-language articulatory setting is a real, measurable baseline that differs from a second language's.

## 6. A flagged non-finding

**Speculative — explicitly not a claim about any individual.** Proprioceptive and sensorimotor integration differences (for example, altered reliance on visual versus proprioceptive feedback in fine motor control, and altered interoception) are documented in the autism research literature at the level of general motor control and bodily-signal perception, but that literature is not about speech articulation specifically, and no study was located directly linking autism-associated proprioceptive differences to rhotic or other speech-sound articulation difficulty. General ASD sensorimotor literature (documenting, for example, over-reliance on visual over proprioceptive feedback in some studies) and separate interoception reviews describe real, verifiable findings about non-speech motor and interoceptive processing. Extending this to speech articulation, and specifically to rhotic production, would be an inference beyond what any located study tested. This is flagged here only as possibly-relevant background literature, never as a diagnostic statement, and it is taken up again — with the same caveat — in [`nervous-system-differences-and-speech-motor-learning.md`](nervous-system-differences-and-speech-motor-learning.md).

## Open questions

- Is it the multiplicity of viable tongue-shape solutions for /r/ — rather than, for example, its acoustic subtlety, its unusually large coarticulatory footprint across vowel contexts, or purely historical/orthographic factors in how it's taught — that actually causes its therapy-resistance? The literature argues this plausibly but has not isolated it as the mechanism.
- Does the bunched/retroflex social stratification found for Scottish English postvocalic /r/ (Lawson et al. 2011) replicate in American English dialects, where rhoticity itself, rather than a non-rhotic-versus-rhotic split, is closer to categorical? No American study directly testing this was located.
- Is there direct neuroimaging evidence of a rhotic-specific, as opposed to general-accent, perceptual/social-accommodation signature in the brain? The current neuroscience-of-accent literature is real but not rhotic-specific.
- Does proprioceptive acuity or sensorimotor integration style, as studied in the general autism motor-control and interoception literatures, predict speech-sound acquisition difficulty in adult L2 learners specifically? This connection has not been directly studied as far as this search found, and would need dedicated research, or individualized clinical assessment, rather than extrapolation.
- How well does the American-English-specific bunched/retroflex and articulatory-setting literature translate to explaining difficulty with Mandarin's retroflex series (zh/ch/sh/r) and apical vowels, which involve a partially overlapping but not identical set of tongue postures? This is a genuinely open cross-linguistic question not directly addressed by the English-focused sources reviewed here — see [`mandarin-articulation-for-the-english-trained-vocal-tract.md`](mandarin-articulation-for-the-english-trained-vocal-tract.md) for what the Mandarin-specific literature does say.
