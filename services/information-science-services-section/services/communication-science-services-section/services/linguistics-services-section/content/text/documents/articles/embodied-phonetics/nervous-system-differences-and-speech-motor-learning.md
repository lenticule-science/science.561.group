## 0. Scope note — read this first

This article documents two adjacent literatures — general autism-and-motor-control research, and general trauma-and-orofacial-tension research — because they keep coming up as candidate explanations when speech-motor learning feels unusually hard. **Neither literature was tested, in the sources located, against adult second-language articulation learning specifically, and neither was tested at the intersection of both.** Every claim below is a claim about a research literature and what it found in the population it studied, not a diagnostic claim about any individual, including Morgan. Where a claim below could be misread as predictive of one person's experience, that misreading is flagged explicitly. See [`the-american-rhotic-r-as-embodied-phenomenon.md`](the-american-rhotic-r-as-embodied-phenomenon.md) and [`mandarin-articulation-for-the-english-trained-vocal-tract.md`](mandarin-articulation-for-the-english-trained-vocal-tract.md) for the phonetics and second-language-acquisition literatures this one supplements.

## 1. Autism and motor control, in general

**Well-established.** Praxis — motor planning and sequencing of skilled, purposeful movement — is measurably impaired in many autistic children relative to non-autistic peers, extending beyond imitation to gesture-to-command and tool-use tasks; praxis deficits correlate with core social-communicative features even after controlling for basic motor skill. Dziuk et al. (2007) and related work from the Mostofsky lab, together with Fournier et al.'s 2010 meta-analysis (83 studies, pooled motor-coordination-deficit standardized mean difference ≈1.20), describe motor differences as pervasive across autism spectrum disorder.

**Emerging.** In motor-learning tasks, autistic individuals in some paradigms show an atypically strong reliance on proprioceptive (joint-position) feedback relative to visual feedback when forming internal models of movement, correlated with imitation and social impairment — but the direction of sensory-weighting bias is not consistent across all studies. Haswell, Izawa, Dowell, Mostofsky & Shadmehr (2009) is the foundational, specific, well-cited study; Izawa, Pai & Shadmehr (2012) extends and partially replicates it, finding proprioceptive over-selectivity specific to autism versus ADHD. However, other studies report intact proprioceptive acuity or even greater visual reliance in some fine-motor tasks. This should be treated as a documented finding in one influential research line, not a settled general law of autistic motor learning.

**Well-established.** Autistic speakers show measurable expressive-prosody differences — including greater utterance-final pitch (F0) excursions, slower speech rate, and altered rhythm — more consistent than differences in receptive prosody, and classifiable via machine learning across languages. Patel et al. (2020, 2023) and a cross-linguistic machine-learning classification study (PLOS ONE, 2022) converge from multiple independent peer-reviewed groups on rhythm, rate, and F0-excursion differences and an expressive-greater-than-receptive asymmetry.

**Emerging.** Interoceptive processing differs in autism, but the literature is heterogeneous and not reducible to a simple "poor interoception" claim; frameworks linking interoceptive and exteroceptive differences (the Garfinkel/Quadt/Williams line) are actively being refined. Proff, Williams, Quadt & Garfinkel (2022) and a 2025 *Frontiers in Psychiatry* systematic review and meta-analysis are both real peer-reviewed reviews that themselves describe the field as fragmented, with mixed effect directions — appropriately graded emerging, not well-established.

## 2. Attention, feedback, and motor-speech control, in general adults

**Well-established.** An external focus of attention — attending to the effect of a movement, such as the resulting sound — produces more accurate, less variable oral-motor and tongue-control performance than an internal focus, attending to the movement or muscle itself, in general adult speech-motor-control research. Freedman, Maas, Caligiuri, Wulf & Robin (2007) is a controlled study directly measuring tongue and hand control under both attentional conditions, consistent with the broader external-focus motor-learning literature.

**Emerging.** Reduced feedback frequency and self-controlled feedback — the learner decides when to receive correction — tend to support better long-term motor-skill retention than constant external correction, a principle applied in pediatric speech-sound-disorder therapy. General motor-learning feedback-frequency and self-controlled-practice literature, and SLP practice materials, apply this to speech therapy, but no study was found testing this specifically in adult L2 phonology acquisition — extrapolation from child speech-sound-disorder therapy and general motor learning is reasonable but unverified in that specific population.

**Emerging.** PROMPT (tactile-kinesthetic-proprioceptive therapy) shows treatment gains for childhood apraxia of speech and has been used with autistic children with motor-speech involvement, but the evidence base is mostly small pediatric studies and case series, not adults learning a second language. Dale & Hayden (2013, a four-child study) and a 2023 case study applying PROMPT in autism spectrum disorder are real, peer-reviewed, but small-N, pediatric, and only analogically relevant to adult L2 phonology.

**Emerging.** Childhood apraxia of speech co-occurs with autism at elevated but highly variable reported rates — roughly 3% to 65% depending on sample and verbal ability — particularly in minimally verbal autistic children; comorbid autism does not appear to increase apraxia severity, and shared genetics are suspected but not well characterized. Multiple sources converge on co-occurrence but with wide, inconsistent prevalence estimates, and this is of uncertain relevance to autistic adults specifically, since childhood apraxia of speech is a pediatric diagnostic category.

## 3. Trauma, stress, and orofacial/laryngeal tension, in general

**Emerging.** Bruxism, jaw clenching, and other orofacial muscle-tension patterns are associated specifically with PTSD, not just general stress, with trauma exposure predicting awake bruxism and TMD pain mainly when PTSD symptoms are present. The primary source located was a preprint (not confirmed journal-published), together with a related published paper on PTSD in orofacial-pain-clinic patients; the preprint status means this specific PTSD–bruxism claim is documented but should not be treated as strongly as a published peer-reviewed cohort study.

**Well-established.** Muscle tension dysphonia is a well-characterized, multifactorial clinical entity in which excess laryngeal and paralaryngeal muscle tension disrupts vocal-fold behavior; psychological stress is cited among several contributing factors alongside vocal overuse, reflux, and impaired respiratory-phonatory coordination, with interdisciplinary treatment showing the best prognosis. This is supported by multiple peer-reviewed reviews in the voice literature, plus a review of EMG assessment methods.

**Well-established.** Adults and children with induced laryngeal obstruction show markedly elevated rates of anxiety (roughly 60–63%), depression (roughly 30–32%), and PTSD (roughly 34% of adults, 16% of children) versus general-population rates, with elevated somatic symptom burden. Fujiki, Fujiki & Thibeault (2024), a rigorous cross-sectional study of 83 adults and 81 children using validated instruments, is a clear, well-powered association finding. A separate, weaker claim — that trauma-informed clinical care approaches improve management of paradoxical vocal fold movement — is suggestive and descriptive rather than a rigorously tested efficacy claim, and is graded no higher than emerging on its own.

**Emerging.** Globus pharyngeus — a persistent throat-lump sensation without structural cause — is associated with anxiety and psychological distress in a substantial share of studied clinical samples, though the field explicitly treats causation as unresolved and multifactorial. The located sources are smaller clinical studies that explicitly state causation "is hard to establish or refute."

**Well-established, with a named exception.** Fundamental frequency (pitch) increase under stress, and vocal jitter, are the most consistently reported acoustic correlates of anxiety and stress across recent studies, per three independent 2025 systematic reviews and meta-analyses. By contrast, shimmer's relationship to stress is explicitly described as mixed, inconsistent, or non-significant in some of the same reviews, so any claim about shimmer specifically as a stress marker is graded speculative, not well-established.

## 4. Polyvagal theory and adjacent interventions — a contested framework

**Speculative.** Polyvagal Theory's core claims — a distinct "ventral vagal" pathway specifically supporting calm social engagement, including laryngeal and pharyngeal muscles and middle-ear tuning; an evolutionary phylogenetic hierarchy; and respiratory sinus arrhythmia as a straightforward index of this system — have been substantively challenged on neuroanatomical, evolutionary, and measurement grounds, and the debate is ongoing and unresolved in either direction. Grossman (2023, *Biological Psychology*) is a substantive peer-reviewed critique arguing RSA-as-vagal-tone-index is a category error and that the ventral/dorsal vagal anatomical distinction does not hold as claimed; Porges has published rebuttals (*Frontiers in Integrative Neuroscience*, 2022). This is a live scientific dispute. Any polyvagal-derived claim about voice or prosody signaling safety via specific vagal or middle-ear mechanisms should be treated as a contested theoretical framework, not established physiology. The general, uncontested physiology underneath it — vagal innervation of laryngeal and pharyngeal muscles, sympathetic/parasympathetic balance, vocal tension changing under sympathetic arousal — is well-established; the Porges-specific architecture and causal story is not.

**Speculative.** The Safe and Sound Protocol, a Porges-inspired filtered-music listening intervention marketed for autism, trauma, and voice/throat complaints, has only a small number of published outcome studies, mostly small pilot or exploratory work, lacks large independent randomized controlled trials, and is promoted mainly by commercially affiliated sources. A 2023 exploratory pilot study on autistic adults and various practitioner and commercial pages describe the evidence as "growing" but not yet rigorous or independent at scale.

## 5. Regulation practices with real evidence, in adjacent domains

**Well-established, not tested in speech.** Heart-rate-variability biofeedback — slow-paced breathing with real-time feedback — shows small-to-moderate effect sizes for reducing performance anxiety and improving performance under pressure in randomized controlled trials, in domains such as music and sport. A meta-analysis ("Heart Rate Variability Biofeedback Improves Emotional and Physical Health and Performance") and an RCT in musicians ("Matter over mind...") support this general effect. This evidence is not from L2 speech practice specifically; extending it to speech or pronunciation practice is a reasonable analogy but an unverified extrapolation, graded emerging if stated as a claim about speech practice specifically.

**Emerging.** Foreign/second-language speaking anxiety produces measurable physiological arousal — elevated heart rate, heart-rate-variability changes, skin conductance — in adult L2 learners, and higher anxiety is associated with more critical self-perception of pronunciation and muscular tension that can alter articulation. A 2025 study on Chinese ESL learners' speech and physiological responses, and the *Annual Review of Applied Linguistics* piece on second-language speaking anxiety, make the arousal-during-L2-speech finding fairly direct; the pronunciation-self-criticism/muscular-tension link is more inferential.

**Well-established, in other populations.** Tai chi and related somatic/bodywork practices — Alexander Technique, Feldenkrais, yoga — are associated in controlled trials with improved motor control, balance, and postural regulation in populations such as older adults, Parkinson's disease patients, and stroke patients. Systematic reviews on tai chi for Parkinson's motor function, tai chi for stroke, and tai chi across older adults generally are legitimate and well-powered, though none studied autistic adults or L2 learners specifically.

**Speculative.** Tai chi specifically improving vocal or singing function, or speech-motor control, has no controlled trial located. Sources discussing this link were pedagogical or commercial writing rather than empirical outcome studies. By contrast, diaphragmatic breathing exercises — a component of tai chi, but studied independently of it — do have controlled-study support for vocal outcomes in singers, so the breathing-mechanism plausibility is stronger than the tai-chi-specific evidence.

## 6. Autism, multilingualism, and the actual gap

**Emerging.** Autistic adults report multilingualism at rates that appear comparable to or higher than commonly assumed, and some report second-language use as subjectively easier for self-expression than the first language — but rigorous, controlled research on autistic adults' second-language phonological or articulatory learning process specifically is very limited. Digard, Sorace, Stanfield & Fletcher-Watson (2020) is a real peer-reviewed survey of roughly 297 autistic adults finding substantial self-reported multilingualism, but it is focused on language-learning profiles and social experience, not phonological or motor-speech acquisition specifically. The leap from this to "what predicts autistic adults' L2 pronunciation-learning process" is not directly evidenced.

## Open questions

- Is there any published research on autistic adults' L2 phonological/articulatory acquisition specifically, tone languages in particular? Search found bilingualism/multilingualism-in-autism research (Digard et al.) but nothing on the adult motor-speech-learning process itself.
- Does the Haswell/Izawa proprioceptive-over-visual-feedback finding, established in arm and tool-use motor-adaptation paradigms in autistic children, generalize to oral/articulatory motor learning, or to autistic adults? Not tested as far as could be verified.
- Is there any direct empirical study combining autism and trauma-history and speech-motor practice design? Each pairing — autism-and-motor, trauma-and-voice — was separately verified, but no literature was found at the specific three-way intersection this question asks about; any synthesis across the two literatures is inference, not a documented finding.
- What does rigorous, independent, non-industry-affiliated research say about the Safe and Sound Protocol's actual efficacy? Evidence located was thin, mostly pilot-stage, and heavily represented by commercially affiliated sources.
- Is the medRxiv PTSD-bruxism preprint since published in a peer-reviewed journal, and does it hold up under peer review? Status was unclear from search alone.
- Are there controlled studies of tai chi's effects on voice/speech production specifically, versus general motor function or informal singing-pedagogy writing? None were found; this is an open gap, not a settled negative.
