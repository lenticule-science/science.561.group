The modern French language emerged not as a neutral outcome of linguistic evolution, but through deliberate processes of standardization and exclusion. Among the most significant effects of this history was the subordination and erasure of France’s internal vernaculars—collectively referred to as _patois_. These nonstandard varieties, including Occitan, Breton, Alsatian, Corsican, and many others, were not merely sidelined in favor of a standard language, but were structurally and semiotically recoded as illegitimate, irrational, or non-modern. This essay presents a technical account of how French was elevated above its regional counterparts, how patois were framed and governed, and how this internal linguistic stratification formed a primary instance of **semiolinguistic ontoeidic kyriarchism**.

### 1. The Formalization of French: From Regional Dialect to National Standard

What we now call “French” developed from the _langue d’oïl_, one of several Romance dialect continua spoken in northern France. During the medieval period, it coexisted with other Romance languages, most notably _langue d’oc_ (Occitan) in the south. These were not dialects of a single language, but separate, internally complex linguistic systems.

The consolidation of the French monarchy in the late Middle Ages set the conditions for linguistic standardization. The Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539), issued under François I, required all administrative and legal documents to be written in French rather than Latin. While this has often been interpreted as a democratizing move, it also elevated one regional language—Parisian French—into the role of state language. This move was not descriptive but prescriptive: it did not reflect the language of all French speakers, but sought to impose a centralized form of speech for the purposes of administration and surveillance.

The standardization of French continued over the next two centuries through grammarians, dictionaries, and institutions such as the **Académie Française** (founded 1635). By the 18th century, French had become a codified and explicitly policed language, prized for its clarity, logic, and universality. In parallel, other vernaculars were increasingly referred to as _patois_—a term that marked them not only as regional, but as corrupted, backward, and incoherent. This transformation occurred through **semiolinguistic differentiation**: French was framed as a language; patois were not.

### 2. The Construction of Patois as Non-Languages

The term _patois_ itself functions as a **semiotic operator**. It marks a speech form as non-standard, and in doing so removes it from the ontological domain of “language proper.” Under Enlightenment rationalism, language came to be associated with clarity, abstraction, and universality—features that were attributed to standard French. Patois, by contrast, were framed as overly local, phonologically unstable, orally transmitted, and incapable of conveying philosophical or scientific ideas. This is not an empirical judgment but a structural move: the **intelligibility of a linguistic form was made conditional upon its distance from the standard**.

This transformation was institutionalized by Abbé Grégoire’s 1794 report, _“Sur la nécessité et les moyens d’anéantir les patois et d’universaliser l’usage de la langue française.”_ The report explicitly argued that the French Revolution’s goals of universal citizenship and rational governance could only be achieved through the elimination of regional languages. Patois speakers were seen as _not fully modern_, _not fully rational_, and therefore _not fully governable_. Grégoire’s framing linked **linguistic standardization to ontological legibility within the modern state**.

Thus, the French state engaged in a project of **internal linguistic colonization**, not by banning patois outright, but by rendering them **epistemologically inert**—unspeakable in courts, unteachable in schools, and unintelligible in modern institutions. This move enacted what may be called **internal ontological filtration**: patois speakers were still part of the French polity, but only to the extent that they could **abandon their speech in favor of a legible, modular, state-sanctioned form**.

### 3. Schooling, Punishment, and the Biopolitics of the Tongue

The 19th-century educational reforms under Jules Ferry (beginning in the 1880s) operationalized this semiolinguistic hierarchy at the level of the body. French-only schooling, compulsory and secular, became the primary tool for linguistic and epistemological standardization. Children who spoke patois were publicly punished—sometimes by being made to wear signs labeling them as backward, other times through corporal discipline or forced silence.

This form of **biopolitical language training** did not merely teach vocabulary or grammar. It taught children to **dissociate from their own relational forms of speech** and to internalize **shame for nonstandard enunciation**. This process, often referred to by scholars as _la honte_ (shame), is central to understanding the embodied effects of semiolinguistic ontoeidism: the tongue itself is recoded not as a site of relational knowledge but as a **disordered mechanism in need of correction**.

Standard French thus became a **disciplinary instrument**, and its acquisition was framed not just as upward mobility but as **ontological ascent**: one became a citizen-subject through phonological legibility. Meanwhile, patois were allowed to persist only as folklore, performance, or nostalgia—never as a legitimate vehicle of knowledge, governance, or instruction.

### 4. Ontoeidic Dynamics and Internal Coloniality

This internal stratification of language functioned in tandem with French imperialism abroad. Just as Berber, Arabic, and Wolof were subordinated to French in the colonies, Occitan and Breton were subordinated within the metropole. This reveals the logic of **ontoeidic kyriarchism**: a system that enforces not just geopolitical dominance, but a semiotic and acoustic template for being.

In this system, French does not merely dominate because it is widespread; it dominates because it is structurally positioned as the **language of what counts as real**. Patois, meanwhile, are not just linguistically marginal—they are ontologically misaligned. Their disappearance is framed not as a loss, but as a **progression toward proper being**.

This model operates through a sequence of transformations:

- from speech to noise,
    
- from dialect to defect,
    
- from presence to absence.
    

Through these transformations, French functions as a **semiolinguistic ontoeidizer**: not only replacing other forms of language, but **installing a normative model of thought, personhood, and community coherence** that erases noncompliant formations.

---

### Conclusion

The history of French and its patois is not a matter of linguistic variation, but of **semiolinguistic governance**. The standardization of French involved more than the promotion of a language; it was a political project that used language to define the contours of legibility, rationality, and citizenship. Patois were not simply left behind—they were rendered inaudible as forms of worlding. In this sense, the French language, in both structure and institution, exemplifies a model of **internal colonial ontoeidization**, in which the semiotic structure of speech functions as an instrument of epistemic and ontological control.
