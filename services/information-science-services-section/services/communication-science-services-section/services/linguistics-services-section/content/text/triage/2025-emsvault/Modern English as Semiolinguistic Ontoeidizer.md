## **

Modern English operates today not only as a global lingua franca, but as a **semiolinguistic infrastructure**: a language whose very form and circulation patterns act upon the world by shaping what kinds of beings, problems, and relations are communicable. This power does not derive from English’s literary tradition or grammatical features in isolation, but from the specific historical conditions under which it evolved into a language of empire, computation, and global administration. As such, Modern English must be understood as an **ontoeidic agent**: it does not merely transmit meaning, but **formats reality**, producing a world intelligible to and governed by technocratic systems. In this way, it acts as a **semiolinguistic ontoeidizer**—a mechanism by which the eidic structure of the modern world is sustained, standardized, and globally enforced.

The emergence of English as the language of modernity was not preordained. In the early modern period, English was still regionally contained and largely excluded from diplomacy and scholarship, which were conducted in Latin and French. Its rise was closely tied to the **expansion of the British Empire** in the 18th and 19th centuries, during which English was imposed as a language of administration and education across colonized territories. As English spread, it underwent a process of **standardization and modularization**, especially through colonial grammar books, missionary grammars, and civil service examinations. The result was a form of English increasingly optimized not for poetic nuance or oral tradition, but for **bureaucratic uptake**: short, declarative sentences; rigid subject-verb-object structure; and clearly demarcated lexical categories.

This formal simplification did not weaken English’s power—it made it **interoperable**. Across continents, English could be taught, evaluated, and processed in uniform ways. By the early 20th century, English had become the preferred language of international science, air traffic control, and global finance. This shift intensified with the rise of **digital computing**, where English’s word order, minimal inflection, and character encoding made it particularly compatible with early programming languages and interface design. In this moment, English became not just the medium of global discourse but the **language layer of global systems**—spoken not only by people, but by machines parsing queries, classifying inputs, and executing commands.

This transformation gave Modern English a new ontological role. Unlike Enlightenment French, which prized clarity through elegance, Modern English prizes **clarity through modularity**: its forms are stripped to what can circulate between systems, whether those systems are governmental, commercial, or computational. In development, policy, and law, English functions as a **template engine**, allowing for the rapid construction and deployment of repeatable statements: “climate risk,” “public-private partnership,” “digital inclusion,” “gender-responsive budgeting.” These phrases do not merely describe the world—they **preformat it**, defining what counts as a problem and what kinds of solutions are imaginable.

In this context, the **eidolon** of Modern English is **procedural intelligibility**: the ability of an utterance to be received and acted upon by a human-machine bureaucracy. Language that exceeds the bounds of modular structure—whether poetic, relational, nonbinary, or highly context-dependent—is treated as inefficient, irrelevant, or unintelligible. In international development, Indigenous place-names, oral traditions, and affective knowledge practices are frequently overwritten by English templates that make projects legible to donors and dashboards. In this way, Modern English does not simply erase other languages; it **replaces the conditions of legibility themselves**, enforcing a global ontoeidic order.

This order is reinforced by institutions that treat English not as a cultural form but as a **technical infrastructure**. The dominance of English in AI training corpora, metadata schemas, academic journals, and international law ensures that what can be said—and by whom—is shaped by a specific grammatical and semiotic regime. In practice, this means that forms of knowledge that do not fit into English’s modular syntax, or whose referents resist atomization into discrete, translatable units, are rendered **epistemically inert**. They cannot be indexed, and so they cannot be known or acted upon within the dominant systems of decision-making.

Importantly, Modern English **naturalizes its own authority** through the aesthetics of neutrality. Where Enlightenment French made its power visible through refinement and decorum, English conceals its ontological work under the guise of functionalism. Its modular vocabulary, passive voice, and clipped style are treated not as ideological choices but as efficient defaults. This is especially apparent in academic, technical, and legal registers, where statements such as “X was implemented to address Y” obscure agency, erase contingency, and frame interventions as rational necessities. In this mode, English functions as a **neutral-seeming conduit** for **ontoeidic enforcement**.

To be clear, this is not an argument against English as a medium of communication. It is a recognition that the form of English that has become globally dominant is not culturally innocent or ideologically empty. It is a historically situated **semiolinguistic system** designed for legibility within a world organized by computational governance, extractive capitalism, and transnational policy networks. As such, it carries with it a **specific metaphysics**: one that privileges modularity over relation, speed over resonance, and procedural clarity over situated knowledge.

Modern English is therefore not merely a tool of communication. It is an active **formatting agent** within the global order. It shapes not only how we speak but what kinds of truths are speakable, and what kinds of realities are actionable. In this sense, it functions as a **semiolinguistic ontoeidizer**: a language whose structural affordances and infrastructural dominance impose a particular model of the real. Recognizing this is the first step toward disrupting the naturalization of its authority—and imagining new forms of relation that exceed the boundaries of legibility it has imposed.
