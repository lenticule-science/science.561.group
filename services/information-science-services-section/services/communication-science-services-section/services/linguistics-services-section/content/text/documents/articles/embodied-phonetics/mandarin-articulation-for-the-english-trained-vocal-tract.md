## 0. What this is

Learning to produce Mandarin as an adult English speaker is two separable motor problems wearing one name: a laryngeal-pitch problem (tone) and a tongue-posture problem (consonants and vowels the English vocal tract has never had to make). This article collects what the sourced literature says about both, plus what the two dominant theories of adult second-language sound learning predict about why some of it is hard, and what training methods actually have evidence behind them. See [`the-american-rhotic-r-as-embodied-phenomenon.md`](../the-american-rhotic-r-as-embodied-phenomenon.md) for the parallel case of English's own hardest sound, and [`nervous-system-differences-and-speech-motor-learning.md`](../nervous-system-differences-and-speech-motor-learning.md) for individual-difference literature that bears on, but was not tested against, adult Mandarin learning specifically.

## 1. Tone is a motor-learning problem, not only a perceptual one

**Well-established.** Perceptual training on Mandarin lexical tones — using multiple talkers and high variability — reliably improves tone identification in adult English-speaking learners, and this perceptual gain transfers, at least partially, to production. Pitch *height* is more resistant to training than pitch *contour* (shape). Wang, Spence, Jongman & Sereno (1999) and the companion production-transfer study (Wang, Jongman & Sereno, 2003) found roughly 18% production improvement post-training, with contours moving closer to native norms and height lagging contour in improvement. A 2003 fMRI study from the same group (Wang, Sereno, Jongman & Hirsch) found training-related cortical reorganization. This is a multi-decade, replicated line of work, corroborated by independent high-variability-training meta-analyses (below).

**Well-established.** High Variability Phonetic Training (HVPT) — training with many different talkers and tokens rather than one voice — is a robust, medium-to-large-effect method for L2 sound perception, with smaller but real gains transferring to production; effectiveness is moderated by individual perceptual aptitude. Two recent meta-analyses converge: Uchihara, Karas & Thomson (2025) synthesized 79 studies (g≈0.92 pre–post, g≈0.67 versus control); a companion 2024 meta-analysis by the same authors synthesized 31 studies on perception-to-production transfer (roughly 10.5% production gain on trained items, roughly 4.5% on untrained items). A further meta-analysis (Mahdi & Mohsen, 2023/2024, 18 studies, g=0.77) found larger effects for consonants and lexical tones specifically. For Mandarin tones specifically, Dong, Clayards, Brown & Wonnacott (2019) found high-variability training's benefit interacts with individual aptitude.

**Emerging.** Adult learners must build genuinely new sensorimotor (laryngeal-control) skill for rapid tone production, not just new perceptual categories; sensorimotor tone control in L2 adults, measured via pitch-perturbation/auditory-feedback paradigms, is intermediate between naive English speakers and native Mandarin speakers, and does not fully converge even with several years of experience. Ning, Shih & Loucks (2014) tested this directly with a pitch-shift/auditory-feedback paradigm across naive English speakers, L2 Mandarin learners, and native Mandarin speakers, finding L2 learners' compensatory pitch responses differed from both groups — partial but incomplete sensorimotor reorganization. This is a single, carefully designed study rather than a large replicated literature.

## 2. The consonant series English doesn't have

**Well-established.** Mandarin retroflex consonants (zh/ch/sh/r, tongue tip curled back) and alveolo-palatal consonants (j/q/x, tongue blade raised to the hard palate with spread lips) are both articulatorily absent from English and are among the hardest Mandarin consonants for English-L1 learners. English learners frequently confuse the two series *with each other* — perceiving or producing retroflex targets as palatal or vice versa — rather than substituting an English category for either. Wang & Chen (2020) found accuracy ranging from 25% to 88% across these consonants and documented that intended ch/zh sounds were often perceived by native judges as the palatal q sound: a cross-series confusion, not an English-sound substitution. Tsao, Liu & Kuhl (1999) established that even discrimination of the alveolo-palatal fricative/affricate series is difficult for American English listeners relative to Mandarin natives. Chang (2021) used ultrasound to show Mandarin [ɕ] and English [ʃ] differ in tongue-body posture and lip configuration, explaining why simple "closest English sound" substitution instruction fails.

**Well-established, on a young sub-literature.** The Mandarin rhotic (the 儿 er-suffix / "r" onset) is acoustically and articulatorily not simply the retroflex sound textbooks describe. It can be produced with either a tongue-tip-up (retroflex) or tongue-tip-down (bunched) gesture, differs from English postvocalic /r/ in formant structure (generally less rhotic than English), and Mandarin prevocalic /r/ specifically favors a bunched, not retroflexed, gesture — closer to some variants of English /ɹ/ than folk descriptions suggest, but not identical. Chen, Whalen & Mok (2024, *Phonetica*) used ultrasound with 18 speakers to document this tongue-shape variability and the cross-language acoustic differences (higher F3, less rhoticity in Mandarin than English). A companion paper by the same authors examined production of English /ɹ/ by Mandarin–English bilinguals, showing L1 Mandarin rhotic habits carry over into L2 English rhotic production.

## 3. What speech-learning theory predicts for an adult

**Well-established.** Flege's original Speech Learning Model (SLM) and its 2021 revision (SLM-r, Flege & Bohn) hold that first- and second-language sound categories exist in a common phonological space and interact; new L2 categories can be formed by adults across the lifespan, with no hard critical-period cutoff for the mechanism itself, but formation is gated by how phonetically *dissimilar* the L2 sound is perceived to be from the nearest L1 category. Sounds heard as "a bit different" from an L1 category are hardest to acquire — they partly assimilate and get pulled toward the L1 category — while sounds heard as very different, or genuinely new, are more learnable. SLM-r (Flege & Bohn 2021) and the companion "SLM-r Applied" chapter (Flege, Aoyama & Bohn 2021) represent four decades of Flege's cumulative empirical program and are among the most cited frameworks in L2 phonetics; core claims are well corroborated across many first–second-language pairs, though some sub-predictions remain actively debated.

**Well-established.** Best's Perceptual Assimilation Model, extended to second-language learning (PAM-L2, Best & Tyler 2007), predicts L2 contrast difficulty from how two sounds map onto the listener's first-language category system: contrasts assimilating to two distinct first-language categories are easiest to discriminate; those assimilating to a single first-language category, or differing only in "category-goodness," are harder; genuinely uncategorizable sounds are variable in difficulty. PAM-L2 is a standard framework alongside SLM-r and has been applied directly to Mandarin sibilants for non-native listeners in the literature. Like SLM-r, it is primarily a perception-side model with less direct coverage of articulatory/motor acquisition — an explicit gap in the field, taken up next.

**Emerging.** Articulatory/production-side L2 motor learning is comparatively under-theorized and under-evidenced relative to perception: there is far less research directly testing whether or how adults form new stable articulatory routines, and the few studies that exist show partial, effortful, feedback-dependent motor learning rather than automatic transfer from perceptual gains. Bu, Nagano, Harel & McAllister (2021) explicitly note this gap: "while the roles of practice variability and practice schedule have been clearly documented in non-speech-motor learning, there is limited evidence regarding the role of these practice conditions in speech-motor learning." Their single-subject study of Korean adults learning English rhotics via intraoral tactile feedback found high-variability practice produced significantly better within-session gains, but long-term retention and generalization were limited for both groups — only one of eight participants reached functionally meaningful lasting change.

**Emerging.** Whether perception training must precede production training is unsettled: Flege's classical SLM held perception must lead production, but the revised SLM-r frames perception and production as co-developing without strict precedence, and empirical training studies show both perception-only training improving production *and* production-focused training with visual feedback improving perception — the relationship is bidirectional rather than strictly sequential. This follows from combining the SLM-r revision with separate empirical strands rather than one direct head-to-head trial of training order for Mandarin specifically, hence graded emerging.

## 4. Training methods with evidence behind them

**Emerging.** Ultrasound tongue imaging used as real-time visual biofeedback measurably improves L2 segmental articulation, including sounds requiring novel tongue postures, faster than audio-only instruction, with gains reported to transfer to untrained words and persist after training ends, in small studies across several first–second-language pairs. Antolík, Pillot-Loiseau & Kamiyama (2019) trained six Japanese adult learners on French /y/–/u/ with real-time ultrasound feedback across three individual 45-minute sessions and reported improvement. Broader review literature on ultrasound biofeedback and computer-assisted visual articulation feedback describes converging small-sample evidence across studies, with an explicit analogy to ultrasound biofeedback's well-established success in clinical speech-sound-disorder therapy (see [`the-american-rhotic-r-as-embodied-phenomenon.md`](../the-american-rhotic-r-as-embodied-phenomenon.md), section 4). L2-specific studies remain small-n, often under ten participants, without large randomized controlled trials; the clinical, non-L2 evidence for ultrasound biofeedback is considerably stronger and lends indirect plausibility.

**Emerging.** Shadowing — real-time imitation of a model speaker's speech stream, including prosody — shows a generally positive but mixed evidence picture for L2 pronunciation: consistent benefits for fluency, comprehensibility, and global accentedness, and for listening/perception skills, but inconclusive evidence specifically for improving discrete segmental accuracy. A 2025 systematic review and a parallel systematic review both explicitly state segmental-level findings were inconclusive while global fluency and prosody findings were more consistently positive. This is a young, heterogeneous literature without strong meta-analytic consensus on segmental outcomes specifically.

**Emerging.** Practice structure matters for L2 speech-motor learning: high-variability, randomly interleaved practice — multiple talkers, multiple target words and contexts mixed rather than blocked by item — tends to produce better retention and generalization than low-variability or blocked practice, mirroring the well-established "contextual interference effect" from general, non-speech motor learning, though the speech-specific evidence base is thinner and effects on long-term retention are less robust than short-term performance gains. Maas, Robin, Austermann Hula, Freedman, Wulf, Ballard & Schmidt (2008) is the central tutorial review here, noting that conditions which enhance performance *during* practice — blocked practice, constant feedback — are often not the conditions that best support retention and generalization, where random practice and faded or summary feedback do better despite feeling harder during practice. This "practice-performance dissociation" is well-established in the general motor-learning literature the review draws on, but its direct application to L2 speech-sound acquisition specifically, as opposed to disordered-speech treatment, is thinner: supported mainly by Bu et al. (2021, above) and the HVPT literature, which is speech-specific but doesn't itself manipulate blocked-versus-random practice.

## 5. Articulatory setting, applied to Mandarin

**Well-established as a general finding; extrapolated for the Mandarin–English pair specifically.** Speakers maintain distinct, measurable habitual "articulatory settings" — baseline resting/interspeech tongue, jaw, and lip posture — for each of their languages when bilingual. Wilson & Gick (2014) used instrumental articulatory data to show native-like bilinguals maintain distinct interutterance rest postures per language, directly confirming Honikman's 1964 classic descriptive concept (see [`the-american-rhotic-r-as-embodied-phenomenon.md`](../the-american-rhotic-r-as-embodied-phenomenon.md), section 5). No dedicated instrumental study specifically comparing Mandarin-versus-English articulatory setting was located in this search. The Mandarin–English comparison specifically should be treated as a reasonable extrapolation from the general cross-linguistic finding plus segment-level ultrasound data (Chang 2021; the Chen/Whalen/Mok rhotic papers), not as a directly confirmed finding.

## 6. Two flagged non-findings

**Emerging, and not individually predictive.** Autistic individuals as a population show group-level differences in motor coordination and oral/speech praxis relative to non-autistic comparison groups, across gross, fine, and oral-motor domains — a documented association in the broader motor/autism literature, not a claim that predicts any individual autistic person's articulatory experience, and it has not been specifically studied in the context of adult L2 tonal-language articulation. Maffei et al. (2023) synthesize existing oral-motor findings in a scoping review; broader meta-analytic literature describes group differences in praxis, imitation, and coordination. No literature located connects this specifically to adult foreign-language articulation learning. This is developed further, and hedged the same way, in [`nervous-system-differences-and-speech-motor-learning.md`](../nervous-system-differences-and-speech-motor-learning.md).

**Speculative.** There is a documented clinical/dental literature connecting emotional stress, trauma history, and chronic orofacial/jaw muscle bracing (parafunctional clenching, bruxism, myofascial tension), but this literature is largely clinical/dental rather than experimental-psycholinguistic, and no study was located that connects trauma-related orofacial tension specifically to L2 articulation learning or tongue-posture control during foreign-language speech production. Sources found were clinical/dental practice literature of variable rigor rather than peer-reviewed research directly testing trauma-tension-articulation-learning links. This is plausible background context at best — an unverified bridge between two separate literatures that have not, as far as this search found, been studied together.

## Open questions

- Is there any direct, non-extrapolated instrumental study comparing Mandarin versus English habitual articulatory setting (resting tongue root position, jaw posture) specifically? None was located — the Wilson & Gick (2014) finding is about bilinguals generally, and Mandarin–English was not the tested pair.
- Has anyone studied L2 tonal-language articulation learning specifically in autistic adult learners, or in learners with trauma history? No such study was found; this remains a genuine gap rather than a documented finding.
- What is the optimal dose or schedule — session length, spacing, total hours — for high-variability phonetic training and ultrasound-biofeedback training specifically for adult Mandarin learners, as opposed to general L2 phonetic training? The meta-analyses aggregate across many first–second-language pairs and protocols rather than isolating Mandarin-specific dosing.
- Does blocked-versus-random practice (the contextual interference effect) hold up in larger, adequately powered studies specifically for L2 segmental or tonal speech-motor training? The Bu et al. (2021) study is an n=8 single-subject design; larger replication is needed before treating this as settled for speech specifically, as opposed to general motor learning, where it is well-established.
- Do gains from ultrasound biofeedback on Mandarin-specific segments — retroflex, alveolo-palatal, rhotic — persist long-term and generalize to conversational speech, or only to trained isolated words? Most located studies test only short-term, lab-based gains.
