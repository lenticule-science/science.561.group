## 3.1 What this chapter does, and does not, do

Part I of this book earned its instrument before making a single claim about
language. That was the discipline: obsmetrologia — measurement with
provenance, fusion laws, consistency conditions, error budgets — was put on
the table on its own terms, as mathematics, before anyone was asked to
believe it says something true about speech. This chapter cashes that
patience. It makes the first substantive claim of the book: that the correct
primitive unit of linguistic theory is not the sentence, not the
proposition, and not the token, but the *witnessed utterance-event* — an
observed occurrence comprising participants (each holding a kin or social
position), a place and time, a medium, a form-trace, an uptake, and a
consent status.

Three things this chapter is not yet doing, stated up front so later
chapters are not accused of having failed to do them here. It does not yet
give the internal composition of a form-trace — what makes a trace
well-formed, how smaller traces bind into larger ones — because that is the
job of Chapter 4, which introduces the meme as the field's quantum and
Lambek's calculus as the binding algebra. It does not yet show that an
item's meaning is recoverable from its bundle of witnessed uses, because
that is Chapter 5's Yoneda argument. And it does not yet show how a
community fixes what a form means, because that is Chapter 6's settlement
account. This chapter's job is narrower and, we will argue, prior to all
three: to establish what the unit *is* that Chapters 4 through 6 go on to
structure, bind, and settle. Get the primitive wrong and everything built on
it inherits the wrongness; this is the chapter where that risk is taken on
directly, in the open, against the strongest competition available.

The competition is real, and it is treated as such. Generative grammar,
model-theoretic semantics, and distributional linguistics are not straw
opponents erected to be knocked down for the pleasure of it — they are, on
their own instruments, the best theories linguistics has produced, each
answering a real class of questions this book cannot yet answer half as
precisely. The argument of this chapter is not that they are wrong. It is
that each is the best theory available to an instrument that measures
something other than the witnessed field, and that a foundation adequate to
consent, kinship, and provenance requires a different unit than any of the
three ever needed to define. Sections 3.3 through 3.5 give each rival its
strongest statement before Section 3.6 says precisely what none of the
three can hold. Readers who already grant the primitive can skip ahead to
3.7; readers who do not should not skip the sections that follow, because
the case is made there or nowhere.

## 3.2 The primitive stated

Axiom A1 of the working theory reads, in full:

> **A1 — Primitive: the witnessed utterance-event.** An event comprises
> participants (each with kin/social position), place-time, medium, a form
> trace, uptake, and consent status. There are no unobserved linguistic
> facts; there are only witnessed observations, fused with provenance.
> Consent is axiomatic: regions of the field may be legitimately kin-gated
> or season-gated, and the theory represents gating natively rather than
> treating all data as extractable.

Two moves are compressed into that one paragraph, and it matters that they
be pulled apart before either is defended. The first move is ontological:
where prior theories start from an idealized unit — the sentence, the
proposition, the token — and treat actually occurring speech as noisy
evidence *for* that idealization, the Witnessed Field theory inverts the
relation. There is no idealized object standing behind the data, waiting to
be recovered more or less accurately; there is only the data, and what a
grammar, a truth-conditional semantics, or a lexicon states is downstream
compression of it — a chart, in the vocabulary Chapter 8 develops in full,
not the territory. The second move is that consent is written into the
primitive's definition, not appended to it as a research-ethics protocol
that governs what a scientist is permitted to do with data that is, on the
theory's own terms, already fully hers to have. A witnessed utterance-event
that was never meant to leave its kin group is not incomplete or
under-sampled data. Its consent status is part of what the event *is*, in
exactly the sense that its place-time or its medium is part of what it is.
Remove the consent field and you have not simplified the object; you have
changed which object you are talking about.

It is worth dwelling, before the argument proper begins, on what kind of
claim a primitive is. To name something a primitive in a formal theory is
not to claim it is metaphysically simple, or that it resists further
analysis — a witnessed utterance-event plainly has structure, and this
chapter spends its second half describing that structure field by field. To
name something a primitive is to claim that it is the unit the theory's
other constructions are built *from*, rather than a unit derived from
something more basic within the theory itself. Generative grammar's
primitive is the sentence (or, more precisely, the well-formed structural
description a competent speaker's grammar generates) not because sentences
cannot be decomposed into morphemes and features — they obviously can — but
because the grammar itself is defined as a procedure over that unit and no
smaller one is where the theory's real work happens. The question this
chapter puts to each rival is exactly parallel: not "can your theory
represent kinship, consent, medium, and uptake somehow, with enough
appended apparatus," but "is the unit your theory is built *from* one that
carries this data natively, or must the data be attached from outside after
the primitive has already been fixed." That question turns out to have a
sharp and, we think, decisive answer for all three rivals, but the fairness
of the answer depends on first showing why each rival's primitive was
chosen the way it was — which is where the chapter turns next.

## 3.3 The sentence, in its strongest form

Generative grammar's founding move, made across the second half of the
twentieth century and still the working assumption of the great majority of
syntactic theory, was to isolate what a speaker of a language *knows* —
competence — from what a speaker actually *does* with that knowledge on any
given occasion — performance (Chomsky 1965). The distinction was not a
throat-clearing preliminary; it did constitutive work. A speaker's actual
utterances are shot through with false starts, memory limitations, lapses
of attention, changes of plan mid-sentence, and outright errors, and a
theory that took the utterance stream as its primitive would be a theory of
those accidents as much as of the underlying system that makes them
recognizable as accidents in the first place. Chomsky's competence,
famously stated relative to "an ideal speaker-listener, in a completely
homogeneous speech-community, who knows its language perfectly and is
unaffected by such grammatically irrelevant conditions as memory
limitations, distractions, shifts of attention and interest, and errors" (Chomsky
1965: 3), was an idealization chosen deliberately, in exactly the way a
physicist idealizes away friction to find the law that friction
subsequently perturbs. The idealization was not naivety about real speech;
it was a wager that the interesting invariants of language are visible only
once performance noise is factored out, and the sentence — or, in the later
vocabulary of *Knowledge of Language* (Chomsky 1986), the I-language, the
internal, individual, intensional system that generates structural
descriptions — is the unit at which that invariant structure lives.

The wager paid off, repeatedly, in ways this book is obliged to credit in
full rather than mention in passing. The poverty-of-stimulus argument is
the sharpest instance: children reliably acquire structure-dependent rules
— for instance, that English polar-question formation moves the auxiliary
associated with the matrix clause's subject, respecting hierarchical
constituent structure, rather than the linearly simpler rule of moving
whichever auxiliary occurs first in the string — from input that
underdetermines the choice between the two rules, and do so without
correction, without exposure to the relevant disambiguating sentences in
sufficient number to explain the uniformity of the result by domain-general
learning alone (Chomsky 1965, 1980; Crain and Nakayama 1987). No attested
natural language is reported to use the linear rule. That is a genuine
empirical universal, discovered by treating the sentence and the grammar
that generates it as the unit of analysis, and it is not an artifact that a
theory built around a different primitive gets to simply inherit for free —
it has to be re-earned, or explained away, on the new theory's own terms.
The same is true of island constraints on movement, of binding-theoretic
generalizations about pronoun and reflexive distribution that hold with
remarkable uniformity across typologically unrelated languages, and of the
Minimalist Program's later result that a great deal of this structure
follows from an austere generative engine — recursive binary Merge,
composing lexical items into hierarchical structure, interfacing with a
conceptual-intentional system on one side and a sensorimotor system on the
other (Chomsky 1995) — rather than from a proliferation of construction-specific
rules. Sixty years of results of this character are not nothing, and a
theory that proposes a different primitive owes an account of why these
results survive the change, which is a debt Chapter 5 undertakes to pay by
recovering the generative grammar's structural apparatus as a gauge-fixed
compression of the witnessed field's arrow-bundle — a real object, on this
theory's own account, just not the fundamental one.

What the sentence-as-primitive cannot hold, however, is not a matter of
generative grammar having overlooked something it could easily add. It is
built into the idealization's own definition. The idealized speaker-listener
belongs to a *homogeneous* speech-community and is *unaffected* by memory,
distraction, and error — which is to say, the primitive is defined
precisely by abstracting away from who is speaking to whom, under what
social relation, in what medium, with what response, and under what
standing to speak at all. Kin-position, consent, medium, and uptake are not
absent from generative theory because no one thought of them; they are
absent because the theory's founding idealization is the operation of
setting them to a fixed, uninteresting value so that the invariant
structure beneath them becomes visible. That is a legitimate and, as just
shown, extraordinarily productive research strategy. It is also, on its own
terms, a strategy that cannot be the foundation for a theory whose central
empirical claims are about exactly the variables it idealizes away — and
consent, this book will argue, cannot be added back to competence as an
afterthought, because to hold consent as a primitive-level datum, one needs
a primitive with a slot for it, and competence, defined the way Chomsky
defines it, has no such slot by design.

## 3.4 The proposition, in its strongest form

Where generative grammar isolates the system that generates well-formed
structure, model-theoretic semantics in the tradition Richard Montague
founded isolates the system by which those structures come to have
truth conditions. Montague's founding papers, most influentially "English
as a Formal Language" (1970) and "The Proper Treatment of Quantification in
Ordinary English" (1973, hereafter PTQ), made a claim that was radical for
its moment and has proven durable since: that natural language could be
given a compositional semantics with the same rigor as a formal logical
language, governed by a strict Principle of Compositionality — the meaning
of a complex expression is a function of the meanings of its immediate
constituents and the mode in which they are syntactically combined — and
cashed out in a type theory built from a small base (entities, *e*, and
truth values, *t*) together with intensions indexed to possible worlds and
times, so that a sentence's meaning could be identified with a function
from world-time pairs to truth values: a proposition, in the classical
philosophical sense, given a fully explicit formal life.

The payoff here, too, is not to be waved away. Compositional,
truth-conditional semantics predicts entailment relations with a precision
no prior semantic theory achieved: it explains why "every farmer who owns a
donkey beats it" entails something specific about the donkey's fate under
each farmer's ownership without stipulating the entailment case by case; it
gives scope ambiguity — the two readings of "every student read some book"
— a principled formal source, in the order in which quantifiers take scope
over the sentence's logical form, rather than treating the ambiguity as an
unexplained fact about how the sentence sounds. Barwise and Cooper's
generalized quantifier theory (1981) extended the framework to give
"every," "some," "most," "few," and "no" a uniform semantics as relations
between sets, unifying quantification phenomena that earlier treatments had
handled with a patchwork of special cases. To know the meaning of a
sentence, on this tradition's central thesis, is to know the conditions
under which it would be true — and that thesis, whatever its limits, gives
semantics an object precise enough to be right or wrong about, which is the
condition any science needs to make progress at all. This book's account of
settlement (Chapter 6, Axiom A7) explicitly credits this tradition as
capturing something real: classical truth-conditional semantics will be
recovered there as the special case of settlement in which a community's
closure operator happens to be classical logic's — not dismissed, situated.

What the proposition-as-primitive cannot hold is, again, not incidental to
the framework but constitutive of its choice of unit. A proposition, as a
function from possible worlds to truth values, is individuated by its truth
conditions and by nothing else the theory tracks as a matter of definition:
two utterances with the same truth conditions are, at the level the theory
is built to explain, the same semantic object, regardless of who uttered
either one, under what authority, to whom, in what medium, and with what
uptake. This is not a gap Montague semantics happened to leave; it is
required by the very move that gives the framework its power, because a
semantics indexed to speaker identity, social position, and consent status
in addition to possible worlds and times would no longer be a semantics of
propositions in the sense the tradition means the term — it would be a
different, richer object, which is more or less what this book is
proposing. A sentence uttered with standing authority and the same sentence
uttered by someone with no right to assert it have, on Montague's own
account, identical truth conditions and therefore identical meaning; the
theory has no primitive-level place to record that they nonetheless differ
in whether they *ought* to have been uttered, or whether the second
uttering constitutes an extraction. That difference is precisely what
consent-as-axiom is built to hold, and it cannot be recovered from a unit
defined to be indifferent to it.

## 3.5 The token-stream, in its strongest form

The third rival is older than either of the first two in its founding
intuition and newer than both in its most consequential modern form.
Bloomfieldian structuralism bracketed meaning as unscientific — a matter of
stimulus and response outside the linguist's proper object of study
(Bloomfield 1933) — and the distributionalist program that grew from it,
most rigorously stated in Zellig Harris's "Distributional Structure" (1954),
proposed instead that linguistic structure could be discovered directly
from the patterning of forms in a corpus, without appeal to meaning at all:
substitutable elements occupy the same distributional class, and the
grammar of a language is, in the limit, a statement of which substitutions
are and are not attested. J. R. Firth's often-quoted maxim — "You shall
know a word by the company it keeps" (Firth 1957: 11) — gives the same
intuition its most memorable slogan: meaning as a function of collocation,
recoverable from co-occurrence and nothing else.

For most of the twentieth century this was a minority tradition next to
generative grammar's ascendancy, not least because Chomsky's own 1959
review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* argued forcefully that stimulus-response
accounts could not explain the productivity and structure-dependence of
syntax — an irony this book is obliged to note rather than paper over,
since the sentence-as-primitive tradition partly defines itself against the
token-stream tradition, and a theory proposing yet a third primitive owes
honesty about standing on contested ground between the two. But the
distributional hypothesis has, in the twenty-first century, produced
results neither Bloomfield nor Harris could have anticipated and that no
serious theory of language can now decline to explain. Vector-space
semantic models — word2vec (Mikolov et al. 2013), GloVe, and their
successors — derive genuinely useful semantic representations purely from
co-occurrence statistics over raw token streams, with no annotation of
reference, truth, or speaker intent anywhere in the pipeline, and the
transformer-based large language models built on the same distributional
foundation now produce fluent, contextually appropriate linguistic behavior
at a level that has forced every existing theory of language to say
something about why that is possible. This is an empirical result of the
first order. A theory that cannot explain why modeling nothing but the
statistics of token sequences produces meaning-shaped output has a hole in
it exactly where the most consequential linguistic technology of the
present moment sits.

What the token-stream cannot hold, once again, is written into its own
definition rather than missing by oversight. A distributional model's unit
of analysis is the token in its window of co-occurring tokens — a purely
formal object, individuated by surface identity and context, with no
representation of who produced it, under what social relation to whom, in
what medium, with what consent, or with what uptake beyond the brute fact
that some further token followed. This is not a limitation distributional
linguists failed to notice; Harris's own program was explicitly a wager
that structure could be recovered *without* appeal to exactly this kind of
extralinguistic information, on the grounds that appeal to it was
unscientific or at least methodologically premature. The wager was
productive precisely because it was strict. But it is strict in a way that
makes the resulting primitive constitutively blind to provenance: nothing
in a token-stream model distinguishes a form witnessed under standing
consent from the same form extracted without it, because the token, as the
theory defines it, carries no witness at all — only surface identity and
context. Chapter 5's projection lemma states this precisely in the
vocabulary this book builds: a distributional embedding is the
provenance-forgetting projection of the presheaf of witnessed use, which is
offered there as a candidate explanation of why such models produce
meaning-shaped behavior while lacking any accountable authority over what
they produce. That argument is Chapter 5's to make in full; the point owed
here is narrower and sufficient for this chapter's purpose — that the
token, exactly like the sentence and the proposition, is a primitive
defined in a way that has no native place for kinship, consent, medium as
constitutive rather than incidental, or uptake as an observed event rather
than a further token in the window.

## 3.6 What none of the three can hold

Laid side by side, the diagnosis is uniform across all three rivals, and it
is worth stating that uniformity plainly rather than letting it remain
implicit across three separate sections. Competence is defined by
abstracting away the speaker's social position and the circumstances of
utterance in order to reveal an invariant grammatical system. The
proposition is defined by truth conditions alone, indifferent by
construction to who asserted it and under what standing. The token is
defined by surface form and co-occurrence, with no representational slot
for the witness who produced it or the consent under which it did. In each
case the omission is not a contingent gap the theory happened not to fill —
it is the specific thing the idealization does, the operation that gives
each theory its power over the phenomena it explains best. This is why the
case against the three primitives has to be made generously rather than by
complaint: the very thing that makes the sentence, the proposition, and the
token each the strongest available primitive for their respective research
programs is the thing that disqualifies each from being the primitive a
theory of consent, kinship, and provenance needs.

It is worth being precise about the shape of this argument, because a
weaker version of it is easy to state and would not survive scrutiny. The
claim is not that generative grammar, model-theoretic semantics, and
distributional linguistics *cannot possibly represent* facts about kinship,
consent, medium, or uptake — plainly they can, the way any sufficiently
rich formalism can represent almost anything given enough additional
apparatus bolted on from outside. Sociolinguistic variables can be added to
a corpus as metadata; a pragmatic theory can be layered on top of a
truth-conditional semantics to handle speaker meaning and felicity
conditions; consent can be enforced as an institutional review protocol
governing which corpora a distributional model is permitted to train on.
All of this happens, routinely, and none of it is a mistake. The claim this
chapter makes is sharper and metrological rather than merely
representational: in each rival, this data is *not part of what the
primitive unit is*, and so it enters the theory, when it enters at all, as
an external annotation on an object already fully individuated without it
— a covariate, not a constituent. A theory whose primitive already
individuates by kin-position, place-time, medium, form-trace, uptake, and
consent status treats the same information differently in kind, not merely
in degree of completeness: the information is part of what makes two
witnessed events the same event or different events, not a label attached
afterward to an event whose identity was already settled by something else.
This is exactly the distinction an instrument-minded reader of Part I
should expect this book to insist on, since it is the same distinction
between what an instrument measures natively and what must be inferred or
appended after the fact — and it is why the primitive move of this chapter
is properly called ontological, not merely a preference for richer
metadata.

One further clarification earns its place here, because the strongest
version of an objection deserves to be met rather than left to be raised
later as a surprise. It might be said that this argument proves too much:
any primitive, no matter how it is defined, omits *something*, and a
theory whose primitive includes kin-position and consent will itself omit
some other datum a future theory decides matters — genre, register,
co-speech gesture, the physiological state of the speaker, and so on
without evident end. This is true, and the theory does not claim otherwise.
What it claims is narrower: that consent, kinship, and provenance are not
one variable among an open-ended list of possible future refinements, but
the specific data this book's founding empirical commitments require to be
primitive rather than derived, because the falsification program stated in
the working theory note's obligations — measured community cohomology that
shrinks under standardization pressure, cone-edge advantage in acquisition,
confluence failures co-locating with real misunderstanding — cannot even be
stated, let alone tested, over a unit that does not record who witnessed
what, under what authority, with what response. A future theory may well
need to add still more to the primitive than this one does; that is not an
objection to this chapter's argument, it is the argument's own logic
continuing to apply one theory later.

## 3.7 Consent as axiom

### 3.7.1 Why a foundation, and not a policy

Every research tradition surveyed above treats consent, when it treats it
at all, as something external to the theory of language itself: an
institutional review board's requirement, a data-use agreement, a
best-practice recommendation issued by a professional society. This is not
a minor procedural difference from what this chapter proposes; it is the
same structural move already diagnosed twice above, applied a third time.
When consent lives outside the theory's primitive, the theory's own account
of what a linguistic unit *is* has nothing to say about whether that unit
was rightfully witnessed, and consent becomes something a researcher must
remember to check, rather than something the object itself carries. The
working theory note states this plainly: no predecessor theory of language
carries consent in its foundations. This chapter takes that absence
seriously as a theoretical, not merely an ethical, deficiency — a theory
built on a primitive with no slot for consent status cannot, even in
principle, represent the difference between an utterance-event that
happened and an utterance-event that happened *and was permitted to
circulate beyond the circumstances of its happening*, because those are, on
such a theory, the same object.

Axiom A1 closes that gap by construction rather than by policy. Regions of
the field may be legitimately kin-gated or season-gated — some knowledge is,
as a matter of the communities this book's later chapters study in detail,
rightfully restricted to particular kin relations or particular times of
year — and a witnessed utterance-event that was never meant to circulate
outside its kin group is, on this theory, not incomplete data awaiting
extraction. Its consent status is part of what it is, the same way its
place-time is part of what it is. This has an immediate and, we think,
clarifying consequence for how the theory represents such an event: nothing
in the theory's own definitions treats a kin-gated event as a defective or
partial instance of the primitive that a sufficiently resourced researcher
could complete by gaining access. It is a complete instance of the
primitive, complete exactly as it is, with a consent status that the theory
records rather than overrides.

### 3.7.2 OCAP and CARE: the constructive antecedent

This axiom is not proposed in a vacuum, and it would be a significant
failure of the engagement discipline this book commits to if it were
presented as though it were. Indigenous data governance has, over three
decades, developed exactly this argument independently and in far greater
institutional and legal detail than this chapter can offer, and this
chapter's job is to credit that literature honestly rather than to treat
consent-as-axiom as a novel philosophical move this book is making for the
first time.

The OCAP principles — Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession —
were developed beginning in the 1990s and formalized by Canada's First
Nations Information Governance Centre as a statement of First Nations'
rights over data collected about their communities: that a nation owns
information about itself collectively, in the same way an individual owns
their own personal information; that it controls all aspects of research
and information management that affect it, from data collection design
through to dissemination; that it must have access to information about
itself and its members, wherever that information is held; and that it
possesses the data physically, not merely nominally, wherever possible, as
the mechanism by which ownership and control are actually exercised rather
than merely asserted. OCAP is, in the vocabulary this chapter has been
building, a proposal that consent and custody are not add-on protocols
governing access to an already-individuated body of data, but constitutive
of what counts as legitimate data about a community in the first place —
which is the same structural claim Axiom A1 makes about the witnessed
utterance-event, arrived at from a different direction and with the
authority of the communities whose data was at stake.

The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance — Collective Benefit,
Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics — were articulated more
recently by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (Carroll et al. 2020) as an
explicit complement to the data-science community's FAIR principles
(Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). The complement is
pointed and, for this book's purposes, load-bearing: FAIR is a standard for
making data maximally usable by anyone with the technical means to use it,
and Carroll and colleagues argue directly that FAIR is necessary but not
sufficient where Indigenous data is concerned, because maximal usability by
anyone is precisely the property that makes extraction easy rather than the
property that protects a community's authority over its own knowledge.
CARE asks that data governance return a collective benefit to the people
the data is about, that those people retain the authority to control how it
is used, that those handling the data hold themselves responsible for how
that authority is exercised, and that the whole arrangement be answerable
to an explicit ethics rather than to technical usability alone. A
metrological reading of this book's own instrument recognizes the same
shape immediately: an obsmetrological instrument that fuses beliefs with
provenance and represents gating natively is, among other things, an
attempt to build FAIR-style findability and interoperability for a
witnessed field *without* purchasing that usability at CARE's expense — to
make the field measurable without making it, by the same stroke,
maximally extractable. Whether the instrument actually achieves that
balance is an engineering and community-trust question this chapter is not
positioned to settle unilaterally; it is named here as the standard the
instrument must be held to, not as a standard this chapter claims to have
already met.

### 3.7.3 The Lakota Language Consortium episode, named and bounded

The monograph plan assigns a full case study of the Lakota Language
Consortium episode to Chapter 9, as part of the extraction theorem's
treatment of colonial standardization, and states explicitly that Part
III through V Lakota content passes through the owner's consent, always.
This chapter honors that boundary rather than working around it. What
belongs here, and only here, is the narrower claim that the episode is why
consent-as-axiom is not an abstract or merely philosophical addition to
this theory's foundations, but a response to a documented failure this
book's own house has already recorded in its own words.

The house's prior note on the design program's grounding states the shape
of the episode plainly: "The Lakota Language Consortium episode —
outsiders asserting ownership over recordings of elders, then selling
materials back to the community — is the canonical case of extractive
language technology, and the community's anger was not about price but
about custody" (2026-08-21-the-game-the-flywheel-and-the-five-instruments.md,
§1). That characterization, and the public reporting it draws on, is the
extent of what this chapter states about the episode. It does not attempt
to adjudicate the dispute's specifics, name individuals, or characterize
motivations, because doing any of that responsibly requires the
sourced, fact-checked treatment Chapter 9 is charged with providing, subject
to the owner's and community's ongoing consent as the plan requires — and
because a foundations chapter is the wrong place to litigate a contested
episode in passing, even correctly, when a full chapter downstream exists
to do it properly. What this chapter needs from the episode, and what it
takes from it, is narrower and does not require adjudicating any of its
disputed particulars: it is the clearest available illustration that a
theory of language which cannot represent the difference between a
recording made with standing kin-authority and the same recording
extracted and resold without it is not merely ethically incomplete but
descriptively inadequate to a real, documented linguistic event. The
episode is evidence for the axiom's necessity; its full treatment as a case
study is Chapter 9's debt to pay, not this chapter's.

### 3.7.4 Representing gating natively — what is built and what is asserted

Evidence honesty requires a direct statement of how much of this section
is already engineering and how much is still commitment. The theory's
formal substrate — the Mark and Utterance primitives examined in Section
3.9 below, the FOUR-valued Chu-space evaluation used elsewhere in the
estate's design notes, the bilattice charge machinery Chapter 4 introduces
in full — gives the theory a mathematics capable of representing more than
simple true/false judgments: a form can be evidenced, contradicted,
unsupported, or conflicted, which is already a richer information order
than classical truth-conditional semantics has room for. None of this
existing formal machinery, however, yet includes a typed field for consent
status, kin-position, or season-gating as such. Axiom A1 asserts that the
primitive *must* carry these fields; it does not yet cite a Lean
development that has built the typed structure carrying them, because that
development does not yet exist. This is stated openly rather than
elided, and it is restated in this chapter's obligations section below: the
gap between the axiom and its formalization is real, is currently open, and
is exactly the kind of gap the monograph plan's writing discipline requires
each chapter to name rather than paper over with borrowed rigor from
elsewhere in the estate's formal corpus.

## 3.8 The event's structure: six fields, six measurements

Having argued for the primitive against its three rivals and having set
consent inside it as axiomatic rather than appended, this chapter turns to
the primitive's internal structure. Each of the six fields Axiom A1 names
is introduced below with the same discipline: what the field is, why none
of the three rivals could hold it, and — the test the plan sets for this
section specifically — what measurement having the field, rather than
lacking it, actually enables. A field that cannot be tied to some
measurement it makes possible does not earn its place in the primitive; it
is decoration. None of the six below is decoration, and the section states
plainly, for each, what would be lost if it were dropped.

### 3.8.1 Participants-with-kin-position

A witnessed utterance-event's participants are not recorded merely as
distinguishable individuals — speaker A, addressee B — but each with a kin
or social position relative to the others and, where relevant, relative to
the content of the utterance itself. This is a stronger requirement than
recording speaker identity, which distributional and even most
sociolinguistic corpora already do in some form; it requires recording the
*relation* between participants, not merely their separate identities.

None of the three rivals holds this natively. Generative competence is,
again, defined relative to a homogeneous speech-community precisely so that
who is speaking to whom becomes irrelevant to the object under study.
Truth-conditional semantics individuates by truth conditions alone,
indifferent to speaker-addressee relation by construction. Distributional
models record co-occurrence between tokens, not relation between the
persons who produced them. Where any of the three does track something in
this neighborhood — register variation in sociolinguistics, honorific
agreement in formal syntax and semantics — it is typically handled as an
additional layer describing how the primitive object varies across
contexts, rather than as part of what individuates the primitive object
itself.

What recording kin-position natively enables is, first, exactly the
measurement Chapter 6 needs for settlement: authority-weighted fusion of
belief about what a form means or whether it is acceptable, where a
grandmother's judgment and a stranger's judgment are neither simply
averaged nor one flatly overridden by the other, but combined under a
weighting the theory can state and audit because the weighting inputs —
who stands in what relation to whom — are already part of the witnessed
record. It enables, second, exactly the authority model the design
dialogue names directly: a friend-of-a-friend voice layer in which a
cousin's recording surfaces before a stranger's, not as an engagement
feature but as the culturally correct account of whose testimony counts and
how much, for a given form, in a given community. Neither measurement is
available to a theory whose primitive does not record who stands in what
relation to whom.

### 3.8.2 Place-time

Place-time is the least novel of the six fields taken alone — corpus
linguistics has recorded date and location metadata for as long as corpora
have existed, and generative grammar's own historical-linguistic wing has
never denied that language changes across place and time. What is novel is
only that place-time is recorded as constitutive of the primitive's
identity rather than as metadata layered onto a unit already individuated
by something else, and the consequence of that difference is not trivial:
a form witnessed in one place-time and the same form witnessed in another
are, on this theory, different events by definition, related to one
another only insofar as the theory's later machinery — the causal cones of
Axiom A6, worked out in Part III — establishes a propagation relation
between them.

This is what makes it possible, in a way none of the three rivals
straightforwardly supports, to treat synchrony and diachrony as one
phenomenon at different scales rather than as two separate objects of
study requiring two separate methodologies. A generative grammar of a
language at one historical stage and a generative grammar of the same
language two centuries later are, in the standard picture, two different
grammars related by a theory of language change that sits alongside, not
inside, the synchronic theory. On the account this book is building,
comparative reconstruction, ordinary synchronic variation, and an
individual learner's acquisition trajectory are the same operation — cone
propagation — applied at three different place-time scales over the same
kind of primitive object. Recording place-time as constitutive is what
makes that unification available to be stated at all; without it, there
would be no field over which a causal cone's edges could even be defined.

### 3.8.3 Medium

Medium is the channel and modality through which an utterance-event
occurs — spoken or written or signed, synchronous or asynchronous, face to
face or mediated by a particular platform with its own affordances and
constraints — and it is treated here as constitutive rather than as an
incidental fact about how a message happened to be delivered. This
distinction matters because a great deal of linguistic theory, generative
grammar prominently among it, has historically treated spoken language as
primary and other media as derivative encodings of an underlying
competence that is, in principle, medium-neutral. That assumption is not
available to this theory, and for a specific, measurable reason: medium
determines which form-traces are even possible to witness. Prosody,
voice quality, and the timing information carried in real-time spoken
interaction are simply absent from a written record, not merely harder to
transcribe; a signed utterance's simultaneous use of multiple articulators
has no linear text equivalent that does not lose information the signed
form-trace itself carries. A theory that treats medium as incidental risks
treating the absence of information a written corpus never had the
capacity to record as evidence about the language, rather than as an
artifact of which medium was witnessed.

What recording medium natively enables is a measurement this book's own
critical-linguistics material, formalized in Chapter 9's extraction
theorem, depends on directly: distinguishing meme grammar's affectively
saturated platform vernacular, which borrows syntactic form while
discarding the relational obligations that form originally carried in its
medium of origin, from the same syntactic forms as they occur in the
communities and media that still carry those obligations. That
distinction cannot be drawn by a theory whose primitive has already
collapsed medium into an incidental detail, because meme grammar's defining
character is precisely a mismatch between borrowed form and the medium-appropriate
relational content that form used to require.

### 3.8.4 Form-trace

The form-trace is the observed linguistic material itself — the acoustic
signal, the orthographic string, the sequence of manual and non-manual
articulators in a signed utterance — witnessed as part of the event. It is
deliberately named a trace rather than, say, an utterance or a sentence, to
mark that this chapter is not yet claiming anything about its internal
composition: whether it decomposes into smaller bound units, and by what
algebra those units compose into larger ones, is Chapter 4's question, not
this chapter's. What this chapter commits to is only that the field
exists, that it is what a form-trace actually was as witnessed rather than
a normalized or corrected version of it, and that its identity as a field
of the event is separate from its medium — the same phonological content
witnessed through different media (spoken, then transcribed) produces
related but distinct form-traces, connected by a transduction relation the
theory has to state rather than assume away.

What recording the raw form-trace, rather than a grammar-normalized
version of it, enables is the measurement this book's account of gauge
depends on entirely: the difference between the field itself and any one
chart drawn on it. A grammar or an orthography is, on this theory, a
compression of witnessed form-traces into a coordinate system chosen for
convenience — this is Axiom A5, developed in full in Chapter 8 — and that
claim can only be stated, let alone tested, if the theory has independent
access to what was actually witnessed, prior to any grammar's
normalization of it. Without the raw form-trace as a distinct field, "the
grammar is a chart, not the territory" would be an assertion with nothing
behind it to serve as the territory; with it, the claim becomes a
measurable comparison between what a chart says a form should look like and
what the witnessed record shows it actually looked like.

### 3.8.5 Uptake

Uptake is what happened next: how the utterance-event's addressee or
audience responded, whether the form was repeated, corrected, ratified,
ignored, or refused, and by what further witnessed events the exchange
continued or closed. This is, of the six fields, the one whose absence from
all three rival primitives is easiest to state precisely, because each
rival has a specific technical reason to exclude it that is worth stating
rather than merely asserting. A generative grammar's object is a structural
description generable by the grammar, full stop; whether any given
generable structure was ever actually produced, and if so how it was
received, is a fact about performance, external to competence by
definition. A truth-conditional semantics assigns a proposition its truth
conditions independent of whether anyone accepted, disputed, or acted on
the assertion — uptake might affect pragmatics, layered on top of
semantics, but it is not part of what a sentence *means* on the classical
picture. A distributional model's unit is the token in its window; a
reply is, at most, further tokens in a longer window, not a distinguished
kind of event standing in a special relation — ratification, repair,
refusal — to what preceded it.

What recording uptake as a distinguished, typed field enables is arguably
the single most consequential measurement this chapter's primitive makes
possible, because it is what lets the theory state, rather than merely
assert by fiat, that communication is a process with an outcome that can
be checked. Chapter 6's settlement account will build directly on this
field: a successful exchange is treated there as a proof that normalizes,
in the technical sense borrowed from the estate's Church-Rosser confluence
result for the Curry-Howard-Lambek system, and the empirical claim that
follows — that confluence failures should co-locate with actual
misunderstandings in recorded discourse — is stated as one of the working
theory's five falsification obligations. None of that is testable over a
primitive that does not record what happened after an utterance as part of
the event itself; uptake is the field that makes settlement an empirical
claim rather than a definitional stipulation.

### 3.8.6 Consent status

The sixth field has already been argued for at length in Section 3.7 and
is restated here only to complete the structural inventory and to name its
measurement explicitly, in the same register as the other five. Consent
status records whether, and under what scope, an utterance-event was
witnessed with standing to circulate beyond the immediate circumstances of
its occurrence — kin-gated, season-gated, openly witnessed, or any further
gradation a given community's own protocols require the theory to
represent rather than flatten.

What recording consent status as a field, rather than as an external
access-control layer, enables is the measurement this chapter has already
named as the axiom's whole point: the theory can state, and in principle
check, the difference between a witnessed utterance-event and the same
event stripped of its witnesses and moved forward as bare form — the
operation Chapter 9's extraction theorem names the witness-forgetting
functor. A theory without this field cannot even formulate that
distinction, because on such a theory an utterance-event with its consent
intact and the same event's content extracted without it are, at the level
the theory tracks, identical. Consent status is what keeps them
distinguishable, and distinguishability at the primitive level is the
precondition for everything the extraction theorem later proves formal
about the operation that erases it.

## 3.9 A note on the word "utterance": substrate object and primitive event

A terminological honesty check belongs here, because the estate's own Lean
development already uses the word "utterance" for something considerably
thinner than the primitive this chapter has been defending, and conflating
the two would misrepresent what is and is not yet formalized.
`Obsmetrologia/Articulation/Articulating/Utterance.lean` defines an
`Utterance` as nothing more than the ordered pair of a token-side `Mark`
and a claim-side `Mark`, where a `Mark` itself is defined as an atomic
primitive presented by its finite surface string and built from nothing
further (`Obsmetrologia/Primitive/Mark.lean`). That structure carries no
participant field, no place-time, no medium, no consent status, and no
uptake — it is a two-cell carrier, the input to the articulating operation
Chapter 4 develops in full as part of the mark-to-token-to-articulation-to-utterance
substrate stratification that gives the meme, the field's quantum, its
internal composition.

This is not an inconsistency to be quietly smoothed over; it is a
deliberate layering that this chapter's own argument requires making
explicit. The witnessed utterance-event of Axiom A1 is not identical to,
and is not yet formalized as, the Lean `Utterance` — the former is the
philosophical and empirical primitive this chapter has spent its length
defending, currently stated as an axiom without a Lean citation because no
such citation yet exists; the latter is a much thinner formal object,
already proven to have certain properties (deriving `DecidableEq`, a
positional read-off function `at_` with the expected simp lemmas), that
will serve, in Chapter 4's stratification, as part of the substrate a
witnessed utterance-event's form-trace is ultimately built from. The
shared name is a source of possible confusion this book should not
compound by pretending the two objects are already the same thing. They
are related — the substrate object is a candidate lower layer of the
primitive event's form-trace — but that relation is asserted here, not
proven, and its formalization is exactly the kind of open obligation
Section 3.10 below states plainly rather than leaves implicit.

## 3.10 What the rivals keep, provisionally

It would be a failure of the engagement discipline this book has committed
to if this chapter's argument against three primitives read as though
nothing built on any of the three survives the argument. Nothing said
above requires that conclusion, and later chapters exist specifically to
show that it is false. Generative grammar's structural apparatus —
constituency, movement, binding, the parametric variation Minimalism
organizes across languages — is not discarded; Chapter 5 recovers it as the
H⁰, gauge-fixed compression of the witnessed field's arrow-bundle, real in
exactly the sense a coordinate chart is real, useful, and non-arbitrary,
without being the field itself. Montague semantics is not discarded;
Chapter 6 recovers classical truth-conditional semantics as the special
case of settlement in which a community's closure operator happens to
coincide with classical logic's — a genuine special case, not a
approximation to be corrected, when that coincidence actually holds.
Distributional semantics and the large language models built on it are not
discarded, and are certainly not dismissed as failures; Chapter 5's
projection lemma treats them as exhibiting a real and important
phenomenon — meaning-shaped behavior recoverable from provenance-stripped
statistics — that this book's theory is specifically built to explain
rather than merely note.

What this chapter has argued is narrower and does not require any of that
recovery work to fail: that each of the three, taken as the *foundational*
primitive rather than as a derived or special-case construction, cannot
hold what a theory of consent, kinship, and provenance needs held from the
ground up. The chapters that follow are where the recovery is actually
carried out; this chapter's job was only to clear the ground for that
recovery by establishing, we hope with the generosity the plan's
engagement discipline demands, that the ground needed clearing at all.

## 3.11 Research obligations

Per the monograph's writing discipline, this chapter's claims are graded
against what has actually been shown rather than what the argument would
like to have shown, and the obligations below are specific to this chapter
rather than a restatement of the working theory note's full five-item
falsification program.

1. **The primitive move is asserted, not yet formally discharged.** This
   chapter argues that the witnessed utterance-event, not the sentence,
   proposition, or token, is the correct foundational unit, and that
   everything the three rivals explain is recoverable as compression or
   projection from it. The recovery itself — the H⁰ compression argument for
   generative structure, the classical-closure special case for Montague
   semantics, the projection lemma for distributional embeddings — is
   Chapters 5 and 6's burden, not yet discharged here. Until those
   recoveries are actually carried out in full, this chapter's central claim
   remains an argued position, not a demonstrated theorem, and should be
   read that way.

2. **No corpus currently records events at this granularity.** As the
   glossary entry for the witnessed utterance-event already states, no
   claim is made that any existing corpus records participants-with-kin-position,
   place-time, medium, form-trace, uptake, and consent status jointly, as
   one witnessed object. Building an apparatus that does is future work,
   named in the monograph plan as Chapter 14's first apparatus (the living
   Lakota corpus and the Mandarin learner). Every measurement named in
   Section 3.8 as "enabled" by a given field is enabled in principle, by
   the primitive's structure; none has yet been run.

3. **Consent status is asserted as constitutive; its formal representation
   does not yet exist.** Section 3.7.4 states this directly and it is
   restated here as a standing obligation rather than a settled matter: the
   estate's existing formal substrate (Mark, Utterance, the FOUR-valued Chu
   evaluation, bilattice charge) does not yet include a typed field for
   consent status, kin-position, or season-gating. The axiom commits the
   theory to building this; the building is not yet done.

4. **The sharpest objection to the whole chapter is a notational-variance
   objection, and it is not yet answered.** The strongest challenge to this
   chapter's argument is not that the rivals are secretly adequate after
   all, but that fusing witnessed events under provenance might, in the
   end, produce no empirical predictions different from a sentence-based or
   token-based corpus carrying the same information as external metadata —
   in which case the ontological claim this chapter has made would reduce
   to a notational preference dressed as a foundational move. This chapter
   does not resolve that objection; it names what would resolve it. The
   working theory note's Obligations #1 and #4 — measured community
   cohomology (H¹) that is nonzero for a healthy speech community and
   shrinks under documented standardization pressure, and confluence
   failures that co-locate with actual recorded misunderstanding — are
   both measurements that presuppose event-level fusion with provenance in
   a way no rival primitive is built to support; if those measurements,
   once run, turn out to be equally available from metadata bolted onto a
   sentence corpus, this chapter's central argument is falsified in the
   strongest sense a foundational claim can be. That is the theory's own
   stated stake, not a hedge added after the fact.

5. **This chapter's engagement with the three rival traditions is
   necessarily selective.** Sixty-plus years of generative syntax, fifty
   years of formal semantics, and seventy years of distributional
   linguistics are each represented here by their central, best-known
   results and founding statements, chosen for their argumentative
   relevance to the primitive-adequacy question this chapter asks. This is
   not, and does not claim to be, a comprehensive literature review of any
   of the three traditions; readers expert in any one of them will find
   real internal debates, later refinements, and dissenting voices within
   each tradition that this chapter's necessarily compressed treatment does
   not represent. The claim being defended is about what each primitive, in
   its founding and still-dominant form, structurally cannot hold — not a
   claim that no one within any of the three traditions has ever proposed
   extensions in this direction.
