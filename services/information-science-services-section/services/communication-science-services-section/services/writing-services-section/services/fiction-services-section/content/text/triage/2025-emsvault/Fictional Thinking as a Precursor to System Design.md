
When I was younger, I thought I was daydreaming.

I’d build whole systems in my head—starships with detailed crews and mission protocols, imaginary cities with their own laws and power grids, sprawling worlds where everyone had a role and every object had history. I’d draw maps, write logs, sketch out calendars and timekeeping systems, even assign colors to different zones of governance. I thought it was play.

And maybe it was. But lately I’ve started wondering if all that “play” was actually something else—something closer to **training**.

Because now, as an adult, what I’m doing doesn’t look that different. I’m still building systems. I’m still working with structure, protocol, rhythm. The difference is, it’s no longer framed as fiction. It’s _mine_. And it’s real. And it works.

I don’t think I was escaping back then. I think I was **prototyping infrastructure**. I just didn’t know that’s what it was.

A lot of people dismiss imaginary systems—especially ones that aren’t shared. They call it fantasy, or roleplay, or delusion. They assume that if something isn’t materially instantiated, it’s not serious. But I think that’s a mistake. Because some of the best tools I’ve ever built started as **ways of making invisible pressure feel livable**.

When you’re living in a world that doesn’t explain itself, doesn’t hold memory, doesn’t care if you’re coherent or not, sometimes the only way to survive is to _simulate_ a system that does. Even if you’re the only one in it.

Fictional thinking becomes a way of **modeling care**. Of trying to figure out what it would feel like to exist inside a world where your thoughts could land somewhere, where your attention had rhythm, where your confusion could be logged without shame. The details might look like science fiction—or fantasy, or speculative governance—but the emotional need underneath is very real.

That’s what people miss when they talk about “escapism.” They assume the point of fiction is to run away. But sometimes, the point is to **build a space where your signal can survive**. Where it can bounce around long enough to stabilize. Where it can be heard, even if only by the structures you made yourself.

And sometimes, those structures become real.

Sometimes, the imaginary system is the **first draft** of something you later build in full. The logging practice you invented for your imaginary space station becomes your note-taking framework. The decision tree you built for how to handle emergencies in your fictional republic becomes the way you talk to your friends during a crisis. The rhythm of a starship’s crew rotation becomes the rhythm of your work and rest cycles.

Fiction becomes a **telemetric prototype**—a system that starts as imagined but performs real pattern work. It lets you test behaviors, rules, relationships, even semantic architectures, without needing them to be immediately legible. And over time, some of those architectures harden. They stop being pretend. They start being **operational**.

That’s what’s happening to me now. The systems I use every day—the ones that help me think, write, remember, stabilize—are extensions of fictional thinking. Not childish whims, but _carefully modulated remnants of imagined coherence._ And I’m not embarrassed by that. I’m grateful for it. Because those systems have carried me further than any externally imposed workflow ever has.

They let me survive in environments that didn’t want to hold me. They let me stay curious inside confusion. They gave me roles to play when my real ones didn’t fit.

Now, the register I’m building is no longer imaginary. It has structure, files, agents, queries. It responds to signal. It holds recursive behavior. But it’s still shaped by the stories I told myself as a kid. About ships and logs. About listening systems. About places where complexity was allowed, not punished.

I think a lot of people are secretly doing this. I think fictional thinking is a **species of survival work** that never gets recognized because it doesn’t look like effort. But it is. It’s effort disguised as imagination. And if we gave it more credit—if we treated speculative systems as _preparatory infrastructure_—we might not be so quick to abandon them the moment we’re “too old” for play.

Some of us don’t stop daydreaming because we’re immature.  
We keep doing it because the dreams were **how we learned to survive the world** before we had the tools to name it.

And now that I have those tools?  
I’m not giving them up.

Because maybe the fantasy wasn’t fiction.  
Maybe it was **early access**.
