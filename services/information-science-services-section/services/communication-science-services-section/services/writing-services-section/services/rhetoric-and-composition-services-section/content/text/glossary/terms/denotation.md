***Denotation*** is the literal, dictionary meaning of a word — what it refers to, independent of associations, feelings, or context. It is the counterpart of [connotation](./connotation.md), which is what a word suggests or evokes. "Dog" denotes a domesticated canine; it connotes loyalty, companionship, or (in some cultures) uncleanliness. Denotation is shared; connotation varies.

Denotation matters most in writing that prioritizes precision: [technical writing](../disciplines/technical-writing/index.md), [specifications](../../../../../technical-writing-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/specification.md), legal language, and scientific prose. In these contexts, a word must mean exactly what it says — "shall" means a requirement, "should" means a recommendation, "may" means a permission. Ambiguity in denotation creates real-world failures: a misread specification can produce a bridge that doesn't hold weight.

In literary writing, the tension between denotation and connotation is productive. A poet who writes "house" instead of "home" chooses the word with less emotional connotation — and that choice is meaningful. [Irony](./irony.md) depends on the gap between denotation (what the words literally say) and implication (what the writer means). [Subtext](./subtext.md) depends on the reader understanding more than the words denote.

The skill across all disciplines is awareness: knowing what a word denotes, what it connotes, and choosing deliberately between alternatives based on which meaning — literal, associative, or both — the writing needs.
