# Graphs underneath categories and orders

A [partial order](../../../../glossary/terms/partial-order.md) is a transitive antisymmetric [DAG](../../../../glossary/terms/dag.md). A small [category](../../../../glossary/terms/category.md) is a [quiver](../../../../glossary/terms/quiver.md) closed under associative composition with identities. A [tree](../../../../glossary/terms/tree.md) is the connected acyclic kind of [graph](../../../../glossary/terms/graph.md), and any rooted tree carries an obvious DAG orientation away from the root. The pattern repeats: the order-theoretic and category-theoretic objects the corpus builds on are graph-theoretic objects with extra equations imposed. Graph theory sits *underneath* both directories — supplying the discrete combinatorial substrate they decorate.

The order-theory case is the cleanest. Take a strict partial order $(P, <)$. The Hasse-diagram presentation displays it as a [directed graph](../../../../glossary/terms/directed-graph.md): vertices are the elements of $P$, edges are the cover relations $a \lessdot b$ (no element strictly between). The full $<$ relation is recovered as the [reachability](../../../../glossary/terms/reachability.md) closure of the cover graph; the [acyclicity](../../../../glossary/terms/acyclicity.md) of the cover graph is what makes the closure a partial order rather than a preorder. The [partial-order-as-transitive-acyclic-digraph](../examples/partial-order-as-transitive-acyclic-digraph.md) example walks this through end-to-end. The downstream `depends-on: [directed-graph]` edge added to the [partial-order](../../../../glossary/terms/partial-order.md) note typographically records this dependency that has been silent in the corpus until now.

The category case is the same pattern with composition added. Take a small category $\mathcal{C}$. Forget composition: you get a quiver (the *underlying quiver* of the category). The objects are the vertices, the morphisms are the edges, the identity arrows are loops. The composition operation is *extra structure* on top — an associative partial operation on consecutive edges with identities as units. The free category on a quiver is what you get by adding all formal compositions and quotienting by the category axioms; this is the universal way of going *back* up from a quiver to a category. The Bourbaki / categorical-logic tradition systematically uses "quiver" for the underlying combinatorial datum because the question "what category is this?" depends on what composition you pick on a fixed quiver. Graph theory is the layer where the question of *what edges exist between what vertices* is settled before the question of *what compositions of edges are equal* gets asked.

The same layering shows up further up the corpus. The predication-categories directory sits four levels up the math hierarchy from here. Its [predicate category](../../../../glossary/terms/predicate-category.md) is generated by four classes of edges (specialization, converse, subject-projection, object-projection) — that is, by a quiver. Its [predication fibration](../../../../glossary/terms/predication-fibration.md) acts on the predicate category by reindexing along this quiver. The fibrational structure is rich, but the underlying combinatorics — what generators exist and which compositions of them carry which fiber action — is graph-theoretic. The predication-categories' own dependency graph of typed notes is also a DAG, in the same sense the rest of this narrative talks about.

This is *not* an argument for "everything is a graph". Graphs do not give you the equations a category satisfies, the join and meet operations a lattice satisfies, the limits and colimits a fibration carries, the cohomological information a topos has. What graphs give you is the *discrete-relational layer* every other layer is built on, and a small library of theorems — [tree-equivalences](../../research-articles/tree-equivalences.md), [dag-topological-sort](../../research-articles/dag-topological-sort.md), [dependency-soundness-iff-dag](../../research-articles/dependency-soundness-iff-dag.md) — that downstream theories can cite rather than reprove. A theorem-rich corpus uses its layers for what each layer is good at: graphs for combinatorial structure, orders for partial-order arithmetic, lattices for join-meet algebra, categories for compositional structure, fibrations for parameterized indexing. Graph theory in this directory is here so the lower layers do not have to bootstrap their own discrete structure each time.

The third instance worth naming explicitly is the corpus's own bookkeeping. The `depends-on:` relation on typed notes is a graph; it is required to be a DAG for the renderer to produce a sound reading order; the [dependency-soundness-iff-dag](../../research-articles/dependency-soundness-iff-dag.md) theorem proved in this directory is what makes that requirement formal rather than hopeful. The corpus practices the same layering it preaches: the `depends-on:` graph is a directed graph (vertex per note, edge per declared dependency) which is checked for acyclicity (DAG) before the topological sort produces the linear render. Graph theory shows up at the bottom of the corpus's own machinery, and naming that shows up here is what promotes this directory from a staging area to a load-bearing component.
