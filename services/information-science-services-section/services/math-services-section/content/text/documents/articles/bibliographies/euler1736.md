# Euler 1736 — Solutio problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis

The founding paper of graph theory. Euler analyzes the puzzle of whether one can walk through Königsberg crossing each of its seven bridges exactly once, abstracts the city's geography into what is now recognized as a multigraph, and proves the answer is no by what is now recognized as the necessity direction of the [Eulerian-circuit criterion](../research-articles/eulerian-circuit-criterion.md): each landmass would have to be incident to an even number of bridges, and every Königsberg landmass is incident to an odd number.

## Why this is cited

This is *the* historical origin of graph theory as a subject. Cited from the [seven-bridges-of-konigsberg example](../teaching-articles/examples/seven-bridges-of-konigsberg.md) (which reproduces the analysis), the [eulerian-circuit-criterion theorem](../research-articles/eulerian-circuit-criterion.md) (whose necessity direction Euler proved), and the [from-konigsberg-to-networks narrative](../teaching-articles/explanations/from-konigsberg-to-networks.md) (which traces the historical arc). The corpus cites Euler 1736 to anchor graph theory's date of birth; the modern presentation invokes it as the founding example.

## Notable claims

- The seven-bridges puzzle has no Eulerian walk (open or closed).
- The proof generalizes: a closed walk crossing each edge of an arbitrary edge-set exactly once exists only if every vertex is incident to an even number of edges.
- The geography of Königsberg is irrelevant to the answer; only the abstract "configuration of bridges" matters. *This abstraction is the move that founded the subject.*

## Status

Latin original published 1741 (in the 1736 volume of Petersburg Academy commentaries; the date discrepancy is editorial). English translations are widely available; the Newman 1953 translation in *The World of Mathematics* Vol. 1 is the standard reference. Diestel's textbook reproduces the relevant content of the proof.
