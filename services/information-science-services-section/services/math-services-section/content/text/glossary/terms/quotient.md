A quotient of a structure identifies elements that are equivalent under some relation, collapsing the distinctions the relation says do not matter.

Given a set $S$ and an [equivalence relation](./equivalence-relation.md) $\sim$ on $S$, the quotient $S / {\sim}$ is the set of equivalence classes: each class groups the elements that are equivalent to each other.

The integers modulo 3: $0 \sim 3 \sim 6 \sim \ldots$, $1 \sim 4 \sim 7 \sim \ldots$, $2 \sim 5 \sim 8 \sim \ldots$. The quotient $\mathbb{Z}/3$ has three elements: the classes $[0]$, $[1]$, $[2]$. Addition and multiplication pass through to the quotient, making it a [ring](./ring.md).

For algebraic structures (groups, rings, lattices), a quotient must respect the operations: the equivalence relation must be a **congruence**, meaning equivalent inputs give equivalent outputs. The quotient then inherits the operations of the original.

Quotients connect to [free algebras](./free-algebra.md): every algebra on a set of generators is a quotient of the free algebra on those generators. The free algebra imposes no extra relations; a quotient imposes some. The four-valued [bilattice](./bilattice.md) is the free bounded [distributive](./distributivity.md) bilattice on one generator (Fitting 2002). Any other bounded distributive bilattice generated by one element is a quotient of it — obtained by imposing additional equations.
