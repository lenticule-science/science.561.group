A **predicate converse** is a morphism in the [predicate category](predicate-category.md), denoted $f : p^c \to p$, induced by the swap of the entity-pair index:

$$(s, o) \mapsto (o, s).$$

The induced fiber-algebra map $f^* : A_p \to A_{p^c}$ acts cellwise:

$$f^*(v)(s, o) = v(o, s).$$

A predicate converse encodes the relational converse of $p$: the predicate $p^c$ holds at the pair $(s, o)$ exactly where $p$ holds at $(o, s)$. It is one of the four canonical generator classes of the [predicate category](predicate-category.md) under $\mathrm{Sym}(E)$-equivariance.

## What converse encodes

A relating like *parent of* has a converse — *child of* — that is structurally distinct but related by exactly this swap. The converse is not a separate predicate invented from scratch; it is the same content read from the opposite direction. The predicate category's converse generator gives this duality a precise structural form: the converse is a specific morphism between predicates, not an arbitrary association.

A predicate's relationship to its own converse is informative:

- A **symmetric predicate** is one with $p = p^c$: the swap fixes it. Examples: *equal to*, *adjacent to*, *married to* (in symmetric jurisdictions). For symmetric predicates, the converse generator collapses to the identity on $p$, and the directional asymmetry of the cell becomes vacuous on this predicate's content.
- An **asymmetric predicate** has $p \neq p^c$. Most relations are asymmetric in this sense: *parent of*, *taller than*, *manager of*. The converse generator produces a structurally distinct predicate, and the framework recognizes both as separate objects of $\mathcal{C}$.

## Composition with other generators

Predicate converse can compose with the other three generator classes — [specialization](predicate-specialization.md), [subject-projection](predicate-subject-projection.md), [object-projection](predicate-object-projection.md) — but the composition is not always another converse. The composition rules are part of the predicate category's structure; they encode how directional flips interact with sub-support inclusion and diagonal collapses.

For instance, applying converse twice yields the identity on $p$: $f_{\mathrm{conv}} \circ f_{\mathrm{conv}} = \mathrm{id}$. Applying converse to a subject-projection gives an object-projection (and vice versa): the swap of the entity-pair index converts collapse-onto-subject-diagonal into collapse-onto-object-diagonal.
