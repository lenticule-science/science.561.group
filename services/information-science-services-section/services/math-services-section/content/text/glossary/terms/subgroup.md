A subgroup is a group that lives inside a larger [group](./group.md) — a subset that is itself a group under the same operation. The even integers form a subgroup of the integers under addition: they are closed under addition (even plus even is even), they contain the identity element 0, and every even integer has an additive inverse that is also even. The odd integers do *not* form a subgroup: odd plus odd is even, so they are not closed under addition.

Formally, a subset $H$ of a group $G$ is a subgroup if it satisfies three conditions: $H$ is non-empty; if $a$ and $b$ are in $H$, then $ab$ is in $H$ (closure under the group operation); and if $a$ is in $H$, then $a^{-1}$ is in $H$ (closure under inverses). These three conditions together guarantee that $H$ inherits all the group axioms from $G$ — associativity comes for free because $H$ uses the same operation as $G$.

Every group has at least two subgroups: the trivial subgroup $\{e\}$ containing only the identity element, and the group $G$ itself. Any subgroup other than these two is called a proper subgroup. Finding and classifying the subgroups of a group is one of the central problems in group theory, because the subgroups reveal the internal structure of the group.

Lagrange's theorem connects the size of a subgroup to the size of the group: if $G$ is finite and $H$ is a subgroup, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$. Joseph-Louis Lagrange proved this in *Réflexions sur la résolution algébrique des équations* (1770–1771), in the context of studying permutation groups associated with polynomial equations. The proof works through [cosets](./coset.md): the distinct left cosets of $H$ partition $G$ into non-overlapping pieces of equal size $|H|$, so $|G| = |G:H| \cdot |H|$, where $|G:H|$ is the number of cosets (the index of $H$ in $G$). This immediately implies, for instance, that a group of order 12 can have subgroups of order 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, or 12, but not of order 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, or 11.

The subgroups of a group form a [lattice](./lattice.md) ordered by inclusion. The [meet](./meet.md) of two subgroups is their intersection (which is always a subgroup). The [join](./join.md) is the subgroup generated by their union — the smallest subgroup containing both. This lattice of subgroups is one of the primary tools for understanding a group's structure.
