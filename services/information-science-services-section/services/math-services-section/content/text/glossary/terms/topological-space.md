A topological space is a set equipped with a notion of "nearness" — a way of saying which points are close to which, without necessarily measuring exact distances. The idea is to keep the parts of geometry that matter for continuity (can you deform one shape into another without tearing?) while throwing away the parts that don't (how far apart are these two points, exactly?).

In a metric space — a set where you can measure distances — you already have a notion of nearness: a point is near a set if it can be reached with arbitrarily small steps. But many important spaces (function spaces, quotient spaces, spaces arising in algebraic geometry) don't come with a natural distance function, and different distance functions on the same set can give the same notion of nearness. The topological space axioms capture nearness directly, without going through distance.

The formal definition works by specifying which subsets count as [open sets](./open-set.md). A topology on a set $X$ is a collection $\tau \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ satisfying three axioms: (1) the empty set $\emptyset$ and the whole space $X$ belong to $\tau$; (2) any union of sets in $\tau$ belongs to $\tau$; and (3) any finite intersection of sets in $\tau$ belongs to $\tau$. The pair $(X, \tau)$ is a topological space. An open set is one that belongs to $\tau$ — a set is open because the topology says it is, not because of any intrinsic property.

This may sound circular, but it is precisely the point. The axioms do not tell you *which* subsets are open — that is a choice. What the axioms constrain is *how* open sets must relate to each other: you can always take unions and finite intersections of open sets and get open sets back. Different choices of $\tau$ on the same underlying set $X$ give genuinely different topological spaces with different notions of continuity, convergence, and connectedness.

The most familiar example is the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$, where the open sets are unions of open intervals $(a, b)$. This is the metric topology generated by the distance $d(x, y) = |x - y|$: a set $U$ is open if every point in $U$ has some room around it — formally, for every $x \in U$, some open ball $B(x, \varepsilon) = \{y : |x - y| < \varepsilon\}$ fits entirely inside $U$. Every metric space generates a topology this way.

At the extremes, the discrete topology declares every subset open (so every function out of the space is [continuous](./continuous-map.md)), while the indiscrete topology declares only $\emptyset$ and $X$ open (so every function into the space is continuous). Between these extremes lie topologies of real mathematical interest: the cofinite topology (open sets are those whose complements are finite), Zariski topologies in algebraic geometry, and the weak topologies of functional analysis.

Felix Hausdorff gave the first systematic axiomatization in *Grundzüge der Mengenlehre* (1914), using neighborhood axioms that included a separation condition (now called the Hausdorff or T2 condition). Kazimierz Kuratowski offered an alternative via closure-operator axioms in 1922. The modern open-set formulation — the one given above — was consolidated by the Bourbaki group in *Topologie Générale* (1940) and is now standard.

The collection $\tau$ of open sets, ordered by inclusion, forms a [frame](./lattice.md): a complete [lattice](./lattice.md) in which finite [meets](./meet.md) (intersections) distribute over arbitrary [joins](./join.md) (unions). Equivalently, $\tau$ is a complete Heyting algebra. This algebraic structure is the starting point of pointless topology, where one studies frames (called locales) directly, without reference to an underlying set of points.

Topological spaces and [continuous maps](./continuous-map.md) between them form the [category](./category.md) $\mathbf{Top}$, which admits products, coproducts, subspaces, and quotients. Viewing the open sets of a space as a category (with inclusions as [morphisms](./morphism.md)) is the starting point of sheaf theory: a [presheaf](./presheaf.md) assigns data to each open set, and a [sheaf](./sheaf.md) is a presheaf whose local data glue uniquely.

## Additional material from the Google Drive library

Let $X$ be a set.

**Definition.** A *topology* on $X$ is a collection $\tau \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ satisfying:

1. $\emptyset \in \tau$ and $X \in \tau$.
2. If $\{U_i\}_{i \in I} \subseteq \tau$, then $\bigcup_{i \in I} U_i \in \tau$ (closure under arbitrary unions).
3. If $U_1, \ldots, U_n \in \tau$, then $U_1 \cap \cdots \cap U_n \in \tau$ (closure under finite intersections).

The pair $(X, \tau)$ is a *topological space*. Members of $\tau$ are called [open sets](./open-set.md).

**Proposition.** *On any set $X$, there is a coarsest topology $\tau = \{\emptyset, X\}$ (the indiscrete topology) and a finest topology $\tau = \mathcal{P}(X)$ (the discrete topology). The collection of all topologies on $X$, ordered by inclusion, forms a complete lattice.*

*Proof sketch.* An arbitrary intersection of topologies on $X$ is a topology on $X$ (each axiom is preserved by intersection). The lattice join of a family of topologies is the coarsest topology containing all of them, which exists because the intersection of all topologies containing the family is itself a topology. $\square$

**Proposition.** *The open sets $\tau$ of $(X, \tau)$, ordered by inclusion, form a [frame](./lattice.md): a complete [lattice](./lattice.md) in which finite [meets](./meet.md) (intersections) distribute over arbitrary [joins](./join.md) (unions). Equivalently, $\tau$ is a complete Heyting algebra with [Heyting implication](./heyting-implication.md) $U \Rightarrow V = \bigcup\{W \in \tau : U \cap W \subseteq V\}$.*

**Proposition.** *Every metric space $(M, d)$ carries a canonical topology whose open sets are the unions of open balls $B(x, \varepsilon) = \{y \in M : d(x, y) < \varepsilon\}$.*

**Proposition.** *The [category](./category.md) $\mathbf{Top}$ of topological spaces and [continuous maps](./continuous-map.md) admits arbitrary products and coproducts, as well as subspace and quotient constructions.*

**Examples.**

1. **Standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$.** Generated by open intervals $(a, b)$. Equivalently, the metric topology for $d(x, y) = |x - y|$.
2. **Discrete topology.** Every subset of $X$ is open. Every function out of a discrete space is [continuous](./continuous-map.md).
3. **Indiscrete topology.** Only $\emptyset$ and $X$ are open. Every function into an indiscrete space is continuous.
4. **Cofinite topology.** The open sets are $\emptyset$ and all subsets whose complement is finite.

*Remark (categorical and algebraic perspectives).* Viewing $(X, \tau)$ as a [category](./category.md) whose objects are open sets and whose [morphisms](./morphism.md) are inclusions is the starting point of sheaf theory: a [presheaf](./presheaf.md) assigns data to each open set, and a [sheaf](./sheaf.md) is a presheaf whose local data glues uniquely. The frame description of $\tau$ generalizes to [locales](./site.md) (pointless topology) and to the [closure operators](./closure-operator.md) studied in order theory.
