The sample space is the [set](./set.md) of all possible outcomes of a probabilistic experiment — everything that could happen, listed exhaustively. For a coin flip, the sample space is $\Omega = \{\text{heads}, \text{tails}\}$. For rolling a standard die, $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$. For measuring the temperature outside right now, $\Omega$ might be the real numbers, or more realistically some interval like $[-50, 60]$ degrees Celsius. The sample space is the universe within which probability operates: everything that receives a probability must be expressible in terms of outcomes from $\Omega$.

Choosing the right sample space is the first and most consequential decision in modeling uncertainty. The sample space determines what questions can be asked. If you model a coin flip with $\Omega = \{\text{heads}, \text{tails}\}$, you cannot ask "did the coin land on its edge?" — that outcome is not in the sample space. If you model two dice rolls with $\Omega = \{2, 3, \ldots, 12\}$ (the possible totals), you lose the ability to distinguish whether a 7 came from 3+4 or from 2+5 — information that matters if the dice are not identical. A finer sample space $\Omega = \{(i, j) : 1 \leq i, j \leq 6\}$, listing all 36 ordered pairs, preserves this information.

An [event](./event.md) is a [subset](./subset.md) of the sample space: a collection of outcomes to which a [probability](./probability.md) can be assigned. "Rolling an even number" is the event $\{2, 4, 6\}$. "The temperature exceeds 30°C" is the event $(30, 60]$. Events combine through set operations: [union](./union.md) ("$A$ or $B$"), [intersection](./intersection.md) ("$A$ and $B$"), complement ("not $A$").

For finite or countable sample spaces, every subset is typically treated as an event. But for uncountable sample spaces like $\mathbb{R}$, not every subset can be consistently assigned a probability — there exist pathological subsets (non-measurable sets, whose existence depends on the axiom of choice) that break the probability axioms. The solution is to restrict events to a σ-algebra $\mathcal{F}$: a collection of subsets of $\Omega$ that is closed under complement and countable union, and that contains $\Omega$ and $\emptyset$. The standard σ-algebra on $\mathbb{R}$ is the Borel σ-algebra, generated by all open intervals — it contains every set you would encounter in practice while excluding the pathological ones.

The triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ — sample space, σ-algebra of events, probability measure — is a probability space, the formal foundation for all probabilistic reasoning. Andrey Nikolaevich Kolmogorov established this framework in *Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung* (1933), grounding probability theory in measure theory and giving it the same axiomatic rigor as the rest of mathematics.

The sample space Ω of a probabilistic experiment is the [set](./set.md) of all possible outcomes. For a coin flip, Ω = {heads, tails}. For rolling a die, Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. For measuring a temperature, Ω might be the real numbers or some interval.

An [event](./event.md) is a [subset](./subset.md) of the sample space — a collection of outcomes that may or may not occur. Not every subset need be an event; in general, events form a σ-algebra: a collection of subsets closed under complement, countable [union](./union.md), and countable [intersection](./intersection.md), and containing Ω and ∅. A [probability](./probability.md) measure assigns a number in [0, 1] to each event.

The triple (Ω, F, P) — sample space, σ-algebra of events, probability measure — is a probability space, the formal foundation for probabilistic reasoning. Different sample spaces model different experiments, and the choice of sample space determines what questions can be asked and answered.
