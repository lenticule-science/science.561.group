# The mathematical frontier of quasicrystals

**Date of survey:** 27 June 2026. **Scope:** mathematics of aperiodic order, with physical interfaces only where they sharpen mathematical questions. **Conventions:** citations are Pandoc/CSL keys referring to the YAML `references` block.
I use “quasicrystal” in the broad modern mathematical sense: an aperiodically ordered structure whose long-range order is most cleanly expressed by diffraction, dynamical spectrum, Meyer/model-set geometry, or quasiperiodic/topological spectral data.
That broader usage includes ideal tilings, weighted Dirac combs, model sets, substitution Delone sets, and quasiperiodic operators; it is wider than “thermodynamically stable intermetallic phase.”

## 0. Executive orientation

The mathematical frontier is no longer the question “can nonperiodic order exist?” It is the problem of organizing many inequivalent notions of order—pure point diffraction, pure point dynamical spectrum, model-set geometry, Meyer property, finite local complexity, substitution hierarchy, quasiperiodic spectral gaps, topological gap labels, random-tiling entropy, and physical realizability—into a theory robust under defects, disorder, finite samples, interactions, and computation.
The field has split into several interacting programs.
A useful 2024 synthesis by Lifshitz emphasizes that quasicrystals are best treated as a meeting point of harmonic analysis, crystallography, tilings, and symmetry rather than as exceptions to crystallography [@lifshitz2024].

1. **Diffraction and almost periodic measures.** The modern theorem-level formulation treats a quasicrystal as a translation-bounded measure \\(\omega\\), defines autocorrelation \\(\gamma\\), and studies \\(\widehat\gamma\\).
  Pure point diffraction is now characterized in terms of mean/Besicovitch/Weyl almost periodicity under broad hypotheses, while “Fourier quasicrystal” measure theory is rapidly changing through recent counterexamples and almost-periodicity classification results [@hof1995; @baake_grimm2012_diffraction; @lenz_spindeler_strungaru2020; @lenz2024_pure_point; @favorov2024; @mazac_richard_strungaru2026].
2. **Model sets, Meyer sets, and cut-and-project order.** Regular model sets remain the best-understood ideal quasicrystals: they have explicit Bragg amplitudes, low complexity, and algebraic internal-space structure.
  But many physically relevant systems are only approximately model-set-like, and many aperiodic tilings are not currently known to be regular model sets [@meyer1972; @lagarias1996; @moody2000; @baake_moody2004; @baake_grimm2013].
3. **Substitution, inflation, and monotiles.** The 2023 discovery of the hat and Spectre monotiles reopened classical local-rule and monohedral-aperiodicity questions.
  Follow-up work found surprisingly subtle diffraction: a tiling can be forced aperiodic while its unlabeled vertex diffraction is periodic, and a monotile tiling can require labeled/enriched structures to reveal pure point order [@smith_myers_kaplan_goodmanstrauss2024_hat; @smith_myers_kaplan_goodmanstrauss2024_spectre; @kaplan_okeeffe_treacy2024_hat; @kaplan_okeeffe_treacy2024_spectre; @baake_gahler_mazac_sadun2025; @baake_gahler_mazac_mitchell2025].
4. **Topology and operator algebras.** Tiling hulls, groupoids, crossed products, K-theory, and Čech cohomology provide invariants not reducible to metaphor.
  They classify deformations, compute gap-label groups, and organize topological phases in quasiperiodic media [@sadun2008; @bellissard1995; @bellissard_benedetti_gambaudo2006; @kaminker_putnam2003; @benameur_oyono2003; @liu2026_gap].
5. **Quasiperiodic quantum and wave systems.** Quasicrystal mathematics now intersects the sharp spectral theory of quasiperiodic Schrödinger operators, moiré quasicrystals, photonic lattices, cold-atom/superconducting simulators, and real-space topological phases [@jitomirskaya2022; @damanik_embree_gorodetski2012; @avila_you_zhou2017; @else_thorngren_pretko_vishwanath2021; @chen_lou_hu_lim2024; @li2023_aah; @uri2023; @lai2025].
6. **Computational and inverse-design frontier.** Computation is not merely generating pretty tilings.
  It is producing conjectures about diffraction renormalization, spectral gaps, entropy-stabilized phases, inverse-designed interactions, automated identification of aperiodic motifs, and physical realizability of mathematical order [@baake_gahler_klick_manibo_mazac2026; @hege2022; @fayen2024; @bedolla_montiel2024; @widom_mihalkovic2024; @automatic_detection2025].

The memo below is organized as a research map: definitions, canonical models, rigorous theorems, active programs, and open problems.

## 1. Core mathematical object

### 1.1 Minimal mathematical definitions

A **Delone set** \\(\Lambda\subset \mathbb R^d\\) is simultaneously uniformly discrete and relatively dense: there exist \\(r,R>0\\) such that every open ball of radius \\(r\\) contains at most one point of \\(\Lambda\\), while every closed ball of radius \\(R\\) meets \\(\Lambda\\).
Uniform discreteness is a hard-core condition; relative density prevents arbitrarily large holes.
A Delone set is **periodic** if there exists a full-rank lattice \\(L\subset \mathbb R^d\\) such that \\(\Lambda+L=\Lambda\\).
A Delone set is **nonperiodic** if no such full-rank translational symmetry lattice exists.

A Delone set has **finite local complexity** (FLC) when, for every radius \\(s\\), there are only finitely many \\(s\\)-patches up to translation.
Equivalently, for point sets under standard assumptions, \\((\Lambda-\Lambda)\cap B_s(0)\\) is finite for each \\(s\\).
FLC supplies compactness of the hull and lets local-pattern frequencies become dynamical invariants [@baake_grimm2013; @sadun2008].

A **tiling** \\(\mathcal T\\) of \\(\mathbb R^d\\) is a covering by compact tiles with disjoint interiors.
It becomes a geometric Delone object by marking tiles with control points, or a colored Delone set if tile types are retained.
A **nonperiodic tiling** has no full-rank translation period.
An **aperiodic prototile set** admits at least one tiling but admits no periodic tiling.
A **monotile** is a one-prototile set; an **einstein** is an aperiodic monotile.
The hat and Spectre papers resolved long-standing plane monotile questions, with the important caveat that the original hat aperiodicity allows reflected copies, whereas the Spectre is chiral and does not require reflection [@smith_myers_kaplan_goodmanstrauss2024_hat; @smith_myers_kaplan_goodmanstrauss2024_spectre].

A physically and mathematically useful object is not just \\(\Lambda\\), but its **Dirac comb** \\[ \omega=\sum_{x\in\Lambda} w(x)\,\delta_x, \\] where \\(w(x)\\) may encode atomic species, scattering lengths, tile colors, spins, or weights.
Along a van Hove sequence \\((A_n)\\), the **autocorrelation** is, when the vague limit exists, \\[ \gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\omega|_{A_n}*\widetilde{\omega|_{A_n}}}{\operatorname{vol}(A_n)}, \qquad \widetilde\mu(f)=\overline{\mu(\widetilde f)},\quad \widetilde f(x)=\overline{f(-x)}. \\] The **diffraction measure** is \\(\widehat\gamma\\), the Fourier transform of \\(\gamma\\).
Bragg peaks are atoms of \\(\widehat\gamma\\); diffuse scattering is its continuous part.
The modern diffraction program starts here [@hof1995; @baake_grimm2012_diffraction; @richard_strungaru2017].

The IUCr redefinition deliberately shifted crystallinity away from lattice periodicity: a crystal is any solid with an essentially discrete diffraction diagram, and an aperiodic crystal lacks three-dimensional lattice periodicity [@iucr1992].
This is why “quasicrystal” is naturally formulated by diffraction and dynamics rather than by translational unit cells.

### 1.2 Aperiodic long-range order and quasiperiodicity

**Long-range order** means that correlations do not decay as they do in liquids or gases.
In the strongest mathematical form, the diffraction is **pure point**: \\[ \widehat\gamma=\sum_{k\in \mathcal B} I(k)\,\delta_k, \qquad I(k)\ge 0, \\] where \\(\mathcal B\\) is a countable set of Bragg positions.
For a lattice crystal, \\(\mathcal B\\) is a reciprocal lattice.
For many quasicrystals, \\(\mathcal B\\) lies in a finitely generated \\(\mathbb Z\\)-module whose rank exceeds \\(d\\), so it is dense in reciprocal space even though the Bragg intensities are not uniformly large.
This dense module is one expression of quasiperiodicity [@shechtman1984; @lifshitz2011; @baake_grimm2017].

A function \\(f:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb C\\) is **quasiperiodic** when it is the pullback of a periodic function on a higher-dimensional torus, for example \\[ f(x)=F(Ax) ,\qquad F:\mathbb T^N\to\mathbb C,\quad A:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^N, \\] with \\(A\mathbb R^d\\) irrationally embedded in \\(\mathbb T^N\\).
Model-set quasicrystals are geometric versions of this idea; quasiperiodic Schrödinger operators are spectral-theoretic versions.

A quasicrystal is therefore **ordered but nonperiodic** when it has reproducible long-range correlations, Bragg diffraction or pure point dynamical components, and a compact hull with structured translation action, but has no full-rank translation lattice.
The source of order may be a higher-dimensional lattice, an inflation hierarchy, local matching rules, entropy in a constrained ensemble, or a quasiperiodic potential.

### 1.3 Meyer sets and model-set order

A **Meyer set** is a Delone set \\(\Lambda\\) such that \\(\Lambda-\Lambda\\) is uniformly discrete.
An equivalent Lagarias formulation is that there is a finite set \\(F\\) with \\[ \Lambda-\Lambda\subset \Lambda+F. \\] Meyer sets are robust mathematical abstractions of quasicrystalline order: they allow relatively dense Bragg-like modules and sit close to cut-and-project constructions [@meyer1972; @lagarias1996].
Not every Meyer set is a regular model set, and not every physically interesting quasicrystal is known to be Meyer under realistic disorder, but Meyer structure is one of the strongest bridges between geometry and diffraction.

A **cut-and-project scheme** consists of locally compact Abelian groups \\(G\\) and \\(H\\), a lattice \\(\mathcal L\subset G\times H\\), and projections \\[ G \xleftarrow{\ \pi_G\ } G\times H \xrightarrow{\ \pi_H\ } H \\] with \\(\pi_G|_{\mathcal L}\\) injective and \\(\pi_H(\mathcal L)\\) dense.
For physical space \\(G=\mathbb R^d\\), define the star map \\(x\mapsto x^\star\\) by lifting \\(x\in L=\pi_G(\mathcal L)\\) to \\(\ell\in\mathcal L\\) and setting \\(x^\star=\pi_H(\ell)\\).
Given an **acceptance window** \\(W\subset H\\), the model set is \\[ \Lambda(W)=\{x\in L: x^\star\in W\}. \\] If \\(W\\) is compact with nonempty interior and Haar-null boundary, \\(\Lambda(W)\\) is a **regular model set**.
Regular model sets are Delone, Meyer, repetitive under natural hypotheses, and have pure point diffraction with explicit amplitudes determined by the Fourier transform of the window [@moody2000; @baake_moody2004; @baake_grimm2013].

### 1.4 What distinguishes quasicrystals from other regimes?

The essential distinction is not “complexity” but the type of invariant carried by correlations.

- A **crystal** has a full-rank period lattice; all global structure is reducible to a compact unit cell and a reciprocal lattice.
- A **quasicrystal** has no full-rank period lattice, but may have pure point diffraction on a higher-rank Fourier module, a compact hull with pure point factors, a Meyer/model-set geometry, or a substitution hierarchy.
- A **glass** is solid-like but lacks long-range translational/quasiperiodic order; its diffraction has broad peaks and diffuse scattering rather than a pure Bragg module.
  Some amorphous materials have strong local order, but the mathematical invariant is usually a rapidly decaying or non-Bragg pair-correlation structure, not a Meyer/model-set hull.
- A **liquid** is statistically homogeneous and often isotropic, with short-range pair correlations and mobile local environments; its structure factor has broad rings/peaks rather than Bragg atoms.
- A **gas** is dilute and often close to a Poisson point process except for interaction corrections.
- A **plasma** is dominated by collective charged-field dynamics, screening, and kinetic equations; the mathematical object is typically a distribution function or random field, not a fixed Delone set.

## 2. Foundational models

### 2.1 Penrose tilings

The Penrose rhomb tilings use thick and thin rhombi with angles derived from \\(\pi/5\\), matching rules, or inflation by the golden ratio \\[ \varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}. \\] They exhibit fivefold orientational symmetry at the level of diffraction and local patches, but no translational period.
De Bruijn’s pentagrid construction realizes Penrose tilings through higher-dimensional projection, making them canonical examples of cut-and-project order [@penrose1974; @debruijn1981; @senechal1995].

**Captures:** nonperiodicity with finite local rules; noncrystallographic rotational order; pure point model-set diffraction; local indistinguishability classes; substitution hierarchy. **Fails to capture:** atomistic energetics, defects, thermal phason fluctuations, chemical decoration complexity, and finite growth kinetics.
Penrose tilings are exact infinite ideals, not thermodynamic models by themselves.

### 2.2 Ammann–Beenker tilings

The Ammann–Beenker tiling uses squares and \\(45^\circ\\) rhombi, inflation factor \\(1+\sqrt2\\), and eightfold symmetry.
It has a cut-and-project realization from \\(\mathbb R^4\\) with a two-dimensional internal space and octagonal acceptance windows.
Like Penrose tilings, it is a regular model-set prototype and appears in tiling databases as a standard example of mutual local derivability between substitution and projection descriptions [@baake_grimm2013; @tilings_encyclopedia].

**Captures:** octagonal diffraction modules, inflation/deflation, exact tile-frequency vectors from Perron–Frobenius theory, and explicit window geometry. **Fails to capture:** disorder and soft-matter ensembles where octagonal order may be entropically selected rather than exactly imposed [@fayen_smallenburg2023].

### 2.3 Substitution and inflation tilings

A **substitution tiling** begins with prototiles \\(T_1,\ldots,T_m\\), an expansive linear map \\(Q\\), and a rule replacing \\(Q T_i\\) by a patch of translates of prototiles.
The substitution matrix \\(M=(M_{ij})\\) records how many tiles of type \\(i\\) occur in \\(\sigma(T_j)\\).
If \\(M\\) is primitive, Perron–Frobenius theory gives tile frequencies, and \\(\det Q\\) is the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue in volume-normalized cases [@solomyak1997; @queffelec2010].

In one dimension the Fibonacci substitution \\[ a\mapsto ab,\qquad b\mapsto a \\] produces a Sturmian sequence with slope \\(1/\varphi\\).
In operator form it often yields the Fibonacci Hamiltonian \\[ (H_{\lambda,\omega}\psi)_n=\psi_{n+1}+\psi_{n-1}+\lambda v_\omega(n)\psi_n, \qquad v_\omega(n)\in\{0,1\}. \\] This model is mathematically central because its spectrum is a Cantor set of zero Lebesgue measure in many regimes and because its trace-map dynamics links spectral theory, hyperbolic dynamics, and quasicrystal transport [@damanik_embree_gorodetski2012].

**Captures:** recursive self-similarity, exact patch frequencies, symbolic dynamics, computable cohomology, renormalization of diffraction and spectra. **Fails to capture:** generic quasiperiodic order not generated by a finite substitution, atomic relaxation, and systems whose order comes from entropy rather than deterministic inflation.

### 2.4 Cut-and-project schemes and model sets

For a regular model set \\(\Lambda(W)\\), diffraction can be written in a Poisson-summation form.
In the Euclidean case, the Bragg positions lie in the Fourier module \\(L^\circledast\\), and intensities have the schematic form \\[ I(k)=\operatorname{dens}(\mathcal L)^2\,\left|\widehat{1_W}(-k^\star)\right|^2, \qquad k\in L^\circledast, \\] up to normalization and weights.
This is the cleanest rigorous bridge between higher-dimensional crystallography and observed Bragg spectra [@moody2000; @baake_moody2004; @richard_strungaru2017].

**Captures:** quasiperiodic Bragg order, phason coordinates, dense Fourier modules, explicit amplitudes, exact local indistinguishability classes. **Fails to capture:** non-model-set aperiodic order, random tiling entropy, diffuse scattering, and the kinetics by which atomic systems choose a window.

### 2.5 Meyer sets and relatively dense Bragg peaks

The Meyer property is weaker and more flexible than being a regular model set.
For primitive substitution Delone sets, a major theorem says that pure point diffraction implies the Meyer property; conversely, for such systems the Meyer property is equivalent to a relatively dense set of Bragg peaks under suitable assumptions [@lee_solomyak2008].
This is a key “geometry from spectrum” result: Bragg order forces arithmetic discreteness.

**Captures:** robust algebraic discreteness and near-model-set behavior. **Fails to capture:** full Bragg intensities and internal window geometry unless extra hypotheses are added.

### 2.6 Quasiperiodic potentials and operators

Quasiperiodic order can be encoded not by a point set but by a potential.
The almost Mathieu operator is \\[ (H_{\lambda,\alpha,\theta}\psi)_n =\psi_{n+1}+\psi_{n-1}+2\lambda\cos(2\pi(n\alpha+\theta))\psi_n, \qquad \alpha\notin\mathbb Q. \\] The parameter \\(\lambda\\) tunes spectral regimes; the arithmetic of \\(\alpha\\) and phase \\(\theta\\) matters.
More general analytic quasiperiodic Schrödinger operators have \\[ (H\psi)_n=\psi_{n+1}+\psi_{n-1}+V(\theta+n\alpha)\psi_n, \qquad V:\mathbb T^r\to\mathbb R. \\] This class is central to localization, transport, Cantor spectrum, gap labeling, and topological pumping [@jitomirskaya_marx2015; @jitomirskaya2022; @avila_you_zhou2017; @wang_you_zhou2026].

**Captures:** wave/electron spectral effects of quasiperiodicity; localization transitions; topological gap labels; experimentally realizable cold-atom, photonic, and superconducting circuits. **Fails to capture:** the geometry of atomic tilings unless the potential is derived from a Delone set; many-body interactions except in specialized extensions; realistic relaxation in intermetallic quasicrystals.

## 3. Geometry and symmetry

### 3.1 Forbidden rotational symmetry and noncrystallographic order

The classical crystallographic restriction theorem says that a periodic lattice in \\(\mathbb R^2\\) or \\(\mathbb R^3\\) cannot have exact rotational symmetries such as 5-fold, 8-fold, 10-fold, or 12-fold symmetry.
Shechtman, Blech, Gratias, and Cahn’s 1984 diffraction data forced the community to separate rotational long-range order from lattice periodicity [@shechtman1984].

The mathematical reason is simple: if \\(R\\) preserves a rank- \\(d\\) lattice \\(L\\), then \\(R\\) acts as an integral matrix on \\(L\\); its eigenvalues must be roots of monic integer polynomials of degree at most \\(d\\).
In \\(d=2\\), this restricts rotational orders to \\(1,2,3,4,6\\).
Quasicrystals avoid the theorem by replacing a rank- \\(d\\) translation lattice with a higher-rank module projected to physical space.

### 3.2 Higher-dimensional projection and acceptance windows

Cut-and-project schemes convert a periodic lattice in \\(\mathbb R^{d+m}\\) into a nonperiodic set in \\(\mathbb R^d\\).
Physical displacement in internal space corresponds to **phason** degrees of freedom.
The window \\(W\\) determines which lattice points are visible in physical space.
Boundary points of \\(W\\) create singular choices: moving the cut can flip local configurations.
This is why the boundary regularity of \\(W\\) controls diffraction and local statistics.

For regular model sets, the window boundary has Haar measure zero; then almost every internal shift gives the same patch frequencies and pure point diffraction.
For weak model sets or windows with positive-measure boundary, diffuse components and more delicate entropy phenomena can arise [@moody2000; @baake_grimm2017; @richard_strungaru2017].

### 3.3 Local matching rules

A finite set of local rules **forces** a tiling class if every tiling satisfying the rules lies in the desired hull, often up to mutual local derivability.
Penrose matching arrows are the classical example.
The hard question is whether a global projection or substitution structure can be enforced by local constraints.
Local rules are not only combinatorial: they are the mathematical analogue of finite-range interactions in statistical mechanics.

The hat and Spectre work is important because it shows that a single planar shape can force nonperiodicity; but subsequent diffraction papers show that the observable point set extracted from such a tiling may not automatically display nonperiodic Bragg order unless the correct decorations/control points are used [@smith_myers_kaplan_goodmanstrauss2024_hat; @smith_myers_kaplan_goodmanstrauss2024_spectre; @kaplan_okeeffe_treacy2024_hat; @baake_gahler_mazac_mitchell2025].

### 3.4 Inflation, deflation, and tiling spaces

Inflation/deflation makes nonperiodicity transparent.
If a tiling is invariant under a substitution with expansive map \\(Q\\), then a translational period would inflate to arbitrarily large periods while remaining constrained by finite local complexity; under recognizability and primitivity, this forces the period to vanish.
The rigorous form of such arguments depends on recognizability, FLC, primitivity, and border-forcing hypotheses [@solomyak1997; @queffelec2010; @sadun2008].

The **tiling space** or **hull** is \\[ \Omega_{\mathcal T}=\overline{\{\mathcal T-t:t\in\mathbb R^d\}}, \\] with the local topology.
It contains all tilings locally indistinguishable from \\(\mathcal T\\).
Repetitivity corresponds to minimality of the translation action; unique patch frequencies correspond to unique ergodicity.
Thus “the quasicrystal” is often not one tiling but the dynamical system \\((\Omega_{\mathcal T},\mathbb R^d)\\) [@sadun_williams2003; @sadun2008].

### 3.5 Why ordered but nonperiodic?

A concise answer: the hull is compact and highly constrained, but the translation orbit is not closed as a finite torus.
In a crystal, \\(\Omega\\cong \mathbb R^d/L\\).
In a model-set quasicrystal, the maximal equicontinuous factor is typically a higher-dimensional torus or solenoid; physical translation winds densely through it.
Order comes from the compact factor; nonperiodicity comes from irrational winding.

## 4. Statistical mechanics and dynamics

### 4.1 Ensembles: deterministic hulls versus random tilings

There are two statistically distinct notions of a quasicrystal.

A **deterministic quasicrystal** is represented by a minimal uniquely ergodic hull.
Entropy is often zero, patch frequencies exist, and diffraction is frequently pure point.
Penrose, Ammann–Beenker, and many primitive substitution/model-set examples belong here.

A **random tiling quasicrystal** is an ensemble of tilings with a macroscopic order parameter but positive configurational entropy.
It may display sharp Bragg peaks plus diffuse scattering.
Random-tiling theory treats phason strain fields and entropic stabilization; this is closer to many soft-matter and finite-temperature physical systems.

The topological entropy of an FLC tiling hull can be written as \\[ h_{\mathrm{top}}=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\log N(R)}{\operatorname{vol}(B_R)}, \\] when the limit exists, where \\(N(R)\\) is the number of radius- \\(R\\) patches up to translation.
Deterministic substitutions often have \\(h_{\mathrm{top}}=0\\); random tiling ensembles can have \\(h_{\mathrm{top}}>0\\).

### 4.2 Correlation functions and structure factors

For a weighted point set with \\(N\\) points in a window, the finite structure factor is \\[ S_N(k)=\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{j=1}^N w_j e^{-i k\cdot x_j}\right|^2. \\] The infinite-volume limit, when suitably normalized, is the diffraction measure.
Pair correlations are encoded by the autocorrelation coefficients \\[ \eta(z)=\operatorname{freq}\{(x,y)\in\Lambda^2:y-x=z\}, \qquad \gamma=\sum_{z\in\Lambda-\Lambda}\eta(z)\delta_z \\] for FLC sets with uniform cluster frequencies.
Higher-order correlations may distinguish structures with identical two-point diffraction; the **homometry** problem is therefore fundamental for inverse diffraction and phase retrieval [@baake_grimm2012_diffraction; @baake_grimm2017].

### 4.3 Phonons, phasons, and free energy

In a cut-and-project picture, small deformations split into physical-space displacement \\(u_\parallel(x)\\) and internal-space displacement \\(u_\perp(x)\\).
The latter is a **phason** field.
A continuum elastic free energy has the schematic quadratic form \\[ F[u,w]=\frac12\int \left( C_{ijkl}\,\partial_i u_j\partial_k u_l +K_{i\alpha j\beta}\,\partial_i w_\alpha\partial_j w_\beta +2R_{ij k\alpha}\,\partial_i u_j\partial_k w_\alpha \right)\,dx, \\] where \\(w=u_\perp\\).
The precise tensor constraints depend on point symmetry.
Phasons are not a poetic name for “complexity”; they are internal-space degrees of freedom with observable diffuse scattering, relaxation dynamics, and elastic constants [@deboissieu2012].

### 4.4 Stability: energetic and entropic routes

Physical quasicrystals may be stabilized by energetic matching rules, entropic random tiling effects, or competition between length scales.
The current computational/statistical frontier has especially active soft-matter examples.
Binary hard spheres confined to a plane can form dodecagonal quasicrystals stabilized by configurational entropy [@fayen2024].
Related simulations find octagonal/dodecagonal order in hard-sphere monolayers [@fayen_smallenburg2023], cyclic shear can assist quasicrystal assembly in model systems [@maire2026], and inverse-design studies tune pair or hard-particle interactions toward target rotational symmetries [@bedolla_montiel2024; @ratliff2026].

For intermetallic systems, stability is much less reducible to a single mathematical functional.
Modern structure prediction combines density-functional theory, empirical oscillating pair potentials, approximant searches, and thermodynamic sampling [@widom_mihalkovic2024].
Recent Al–Co–Cu simulations explicitly argue for temperature-dependent stabilization and near-degeneracy with approximants, illustrating why exact tiling models are starting points rather than complete thermodynamics [@huang_widom_mihalkovic2024].

### 4.5 Approximants

A **periodic approximant** replaces irrational slope data by rational approximations.
If \\(\alpha\\) is an irrational slope and \\(p_n/q_n\\) are continued-fraction convergents, one obtains periodic cells of size \\(q_n\\) approximating the quasiperiodic structure.
Approximants are indispensable computationally because finite simulation and electronic-structure codes require periodic boundary conditions.
Mathematically, they are subtle: convergence of spectra, gap labels, diffraction intensities, and thermodynamic quantities can depend on Diophantine properties and on whether limits commute with volume, temperature, and disorder [@jitomirskaya_marx2015; @hege2022].

### 4.6 Quasiperiodic order parameters

Landau-style theories represent a density by amplitudes on a star of wavevectors: \\[ \rho(x)=\rho_0+\sum_{k\in\mathcal S}\left(A_k e^{ik\cdot x}+\overline{A_k}e^{-ik\cdot x}\right)+\cdots. \\] For quasiperiodic order, the module generated by \\(\mathcal S\\) has rank greater than \\(d\\).
Order parameters therefore live on phase variables associated with a higher-dimensional torus.
Phason shifts are relative phase changes not induced by physical translations.
This formalism is powerful for symmetry and elasticity, but it may miss discrete tiling constraints and nonperturbative defects.

## 5. Topology and modern mathematical structure

### 5.1 Tiling hulls and Čech cohomology

For an FLC repetitive tiling, the hull \\(\Omega\\) is compact and locally resembles a product of a Euclidean patch and a Cantor transversal.
Sadun and Williams formalized this as a Cantor-set fiber-bundle structure under standard hypotheses [@sadun_williams2003].
The Čech cohomology \\(\check H^*(\Omega;\mathbb Z)\\) is computable for many substitution tilings via inverse limits of Anderson–Putnam complexes or collared-tile complexes [@sadun2008].

Cohomology is not ornamental.
It classifies shape deformations, detects asymptotically negligible classes, controls pattern-equivariant forms, and enters gap-labeling through the Ruelle–Sullivan map.
In substitution tilings, eigenvalues and substitution action on cohomology can encode inflation, return vectors, and deformation moduli.

### 5.2 Groupoids and noncommutative geometry

The canonical noncommutative algebra of an aperiodic solid is often a crossed product or groupoid C*-algebra.
For a tiling hull \\(\Omega\\) with translation action, one studies \\[ C(\Omega)\rtimes \mathbb R^d \\] or a transversal groupoid algebra.
This plays the role of a noncommutative Brillouin zone.
For periodic crystals, Bloch theory decomposes operators over a torus; for aperiodic solids, there is no finite Brillouin torus, but the crossed product retains translation covariance and supports K-theoretic invariants [@bellissard1995; @bellissard_benedetti_gambaudo2006].

### 5.3 Gap labeling

Given an ergodic family of finite-range Hamiltonians over \\(\Omega\\), the integrated density of states (IDS) is constant almost surely.
If \\(E\\) lies in a spectral gap, the Fermi projection defines a K-theory class.
The possible IDS values in gaps lie in \\[ \tau_*\bigl(K_0(C(\Omega)\rtimes \mathbb R^d)\bigr), \\] where \\(\tau\\) is the canonical trace induced by the invariant measure.
In many FLC tiling cases this group is the frequency module: the additive group generated by patch frequencies.
This is the Bellissard gap-labeling framework, proved in broad settings by K-theoretic and index-theoretic methods [@bellissard1995; @kaminker_putnam2003; @benameur_oyono2003; @bellissard_benedetti_gambaudo2006].

The frontier is not settled.
Recent work on rotational tiling spaces shows that naive extensions of gap-labeling to Euclidean-motion hulls can fail in higher dimensions because torsion and rotational structure matter [@liu2026_gap].
This is a good example of topology doing real mathematical work rather than supplying vocabulary.

### 5.4 Topological phases in quasicrystals

Topological quasicrystal studies ask whether protected boundary states, Chern numbers, real-space invariants, or many-body topological responses survive without translation symmetry.
There are two rigorous routes:

1. **Higher-dimensional ancestor models.** A \\(d\\)-dimensional quasicrystal may be a slice of a periodic \\(D>d\\) system; topological pumping and gap labels descend from Chern data in the ancestor torus.
  This is common in Aubry–André/Harper and photonic quasicrystal systems [@zilberberg2021].
2. **Noncommutative real-space invariants.** Chern characters, spectral localizers, and K-theory of aperiodic operator algebras define invariants without Bloch momentum.
  These are the natural tools for disordered and quasiperiodic systems [@bellissard1995; @else_thorngren_pretko_vishwanath2021].

Recent papers on three-dimensional topological quasicrystals and quasiperiodic superconductors are mathematically interesting because they force real-space topology to interact with fractal boundary geometry, singular-continuous spectra, and nonperiodic local environments [@chen_lou_hu_lim2024; @saito2024].
The caution is that “topological” in a physics paper may mean a numerically observed boundary mode; a mathematical theorem requires a spectral gap or mobility gap, a stable invariant, and robustness under perturbations.

### 5.5 Tiling spaces over irrational tori

Recent work identifying tiling spaces as covering spaces over irrational tori refines the relation between hulls and maximal equicontinuous factors [@alatorre2024].
This is relevant to classification: many model sets and substitution tilings have torus parametrizations that are almost one-to-one except over singular fibers.
The frontier question is how much of the tiling space is determined by this factor plus singular-fiber data.

## 6. Rigorous mathematical results

This section separates theorem-level statements from physically motivated belief.

### 6.1 Regular model sets have pure point diffraction

**Theorem schema.** Let \\(\Lambda(W)\\) be a regular model set in a cut-and-project scheme with compact window \\(W\\), nonempty interior, and Haar-null boundary.
Then \\(\Lambda(W)\\) is pure point diffractive.
For weighted model sets, the Bragg amplitudes are Fourier transforms of the window or weight function on internal space [@hof1995; @moody2000; @baake_moody2004; @richard_strungaru2017].

**Assumptions:** regular window, translation-bounded comb, existence of autocorrelation, locally compact Abelian setting. **What is not included:** positive-boundary windows, strong disorder, random decorations, and finite-sample broadening.

### 6.2 Diffraction–dynamical spectrum equivalence

For FLC Delone sets or tilings with uniform cluster frequencies, pure point diffraction is closely related to pure point dynamical spectrum of the hull.
Lee, Moody, and Solomyak proved a central equivalence between pure point diffraction and pure point dynamical spectrum under appropriate hypotheses [@lee_moody_solomyak2002].
Baake and Lenz extended the measure-dynamical formulation [@baake_lenz2004].

**Assumptions:** compact hull, invariant ergodic/uniquely ergodic measure, finite local complexity or translation-bounded measures, and uniform frequencies in common formulations. **Subtlety:** diffraction is a two-point statistic; dynamical spectrum may detect additional eigenfunctions, often via factors or weighted versions.

### 6.3 Pure point diffraction and almost periodicity

Lenz, Spindeler, and Strungaru characterized pure point diffraction using mean, Besicovitch, and Weyl almost periodicity for measures [@lenz_spindeler_strungaru2020].
Lenz’s 2024 survey clarifies the hierarchy: Bohr almost periodicity is too restrictive for standard examples such as the Fibonacci chain, while mean/Besicovitch/Weyl almost periodicity captures the physically relevant pure point diffraction class [@lenz2024_pure_point].

**Frontier:** Fourier quasicrystal measures are now an active zone of measure theory.
Unit-mass Fourier quasicrystals, crystalline measures that fail to be Fourier quasicrystals, and recent almost-periodicity resolutions show that “discrete support plus discrete spectrum” is more delicate than model-set intuition suggests [@olovskii_ulanovskii2020; @favorov2024; @mazac_richard_strungaru2026].

### 6.4 Substitution tilings: unique ergodicity, spectra, Meyer property

Primitive self-similar substitution tilings under FLC hypotheses are often minimal and uniquely ergodic; Solomyak developed core dynamical results and spectral criteria [@solomyak1997].
In symbolic dynamics, substitution systems form a mature theory with spectral analysis through incidence matrices, return words, and eigenvalue criteria [@queffelec2010].

A key rigorous bridge says that pure point diffractive primitive substitution Delone sets are Meyer, and that in this class the Meyer property is equivalent to relatively dense Bragg peaks [@lee_solomyak2008].
This converts spectral evidence into arithmetic geometry.

**Limitations:** substitution rules are special.
Recognizability, border forcing, Pisot-family conditions, and coincidence conditions are not automatic.
The general “Pisot substitution conjecture” and higher-dimensional analogues remain difficult.

### 6.5 Local indistinguishability and hull classification

Two tilings are **locally indistinguishable** if every finite patch of either appears in the other.
For repetitive FLC tilings this relation corresponds to belonging to the same hull.
Local indistinguishability is weaker than global congruence; it is the correct equivalence for diffraction and thermodynamic infinite-volume limits.
Tiling cohomology, shape conjugacy, and maximal equicontinuous factors are the main tools for classification [@sadun2008; @bellissard_benedetti_gambaudo2006].

### 6.6 Quasiperiodic Schrödinger operators

The almost Mathieu operator and analytic one-frequency quasiperiodic Schrödinger operators have a deep global theory: Aubry duality, Lyapunov exponents, arithmetic transitions, reducibility, localization, absolutely continuous spectra, and Cantor spectrum/gaps.
The sharp phase transition for the almost Mathieu operator and Avila’s global theory reorganized the field [@avila_you_zhou2017; @jitomirskaya2022].

For substitution models such as Fibonacci/Sturmian Hamiltonians, spectra are typically Cantor and transport is anomalous, but proofs often rely on specific trace-map or symbolic machinery [@damanik_embree_gorodetski2012].
Current problem lists emphasize higher-frequency, long-range, singular, and multidimensional quasiperiodic operators, where many one-dimensional techniques fail [@wang_you_zhou2026].

### 6.7 Aperiodic monotiles and diffraction

The hat and Spectre results prove aperiodicity of specific monohedral prototiles in the plane [@smith_myers_kaplan_goodmanstrauss2024_hat; @smith_myers_kaplan_goodmanstrauss2024_spectre].
The diffraction story is more nuanced.
Kaplan, O’Keeffe, and Treacy found that certain vertex sets associated with the hat can produce periodic diffraction, despite the underlying tilings being aperiodic [@kaplan_okeeffe_treacy2024_hat].
Subsequent mathematical work analyzes diffraction of hat/Spectre relatives and the long-range order of Spectre tilings, including pure point dynamical spectrum and cut-and-project/Rauzy-window structures for enriched systems [@kaplan_okeeffe_treacy2024_spectre; @baake_gahler_mazac_sadun2025; @baake_gahler_mazac_mitchell2025; @baake_gahler_klick_manibo_mazac2026].

**Lesson:** aperiodicity of tilings, nonperiodic diffraction, and pure point spectrum are logically distinct.

### 6.8 Periodic tiling conjecture and decidability context

Greenfeld and Tao disproved the periodic tiling conjecture in sufficiently high dimension: there are finite translational tile sets that tile but admit no periodic tiling [@greenfeld_tao2024].
Recent work on undecidability in four-dimensional translational tiling further shows that algorithmic classification of tiling behavior can be fundamentally hard [@yang_zhang2024].
These results are not quasicrystal theorems in the diffraction sense, but they matter for the frontier of aperiodic order: local rules, decidability, and periodic approximability are not tame combinatorial problems.

## 7. Computational frontier

### 7.1 Tiling generation and databases

Tiling generation now ranges from exact substitution/cut-and-project algorithms to combinatorial search over local rules and monotiles.
The Tilings Encyclopedia is a practical research tool because it records substitutions, mutual local derivations, inflation factors, and known model-set realizations for many canonical tilings [@tilings_encyclopedia].
For research, the database role is not archival only: it enables pattern recognition across substitutions, windows, and cohomological invariants.

### 7.2 Diffraction simulation and exact renormalization

Naive diffraction simulation computes \\(S_N(k)\\) for large patches.
The mathematical frontier uses renormalization and exact pair-correlation recursions to identify spectral components.
The 2026 Acta Crystallographica paper on renormalization techniques for inflation systems develops exact methods for diffraction and window covariograms, with applications to monotile systems [@baake_gahler_klick_manibo_mazac2026].

Where computation produces insight: it can expose a hidden periodic diffraction component, suggest a missing decoration needed for pure point order, or reveal continuous spectral components before theorem-level decomposition exists.

### 7.3 Spectral computation for quasiperiodic operators

Computational spectral theory uses rational approximants, transfer matrices, Lyapunov exponents, trace maps, finite-section methods, and certified interval arithmetic.
A key modern direction is not simply plotting Hofstadter butterflies; it is **proving gaps** or certifying their persistence.
The 2022 work on finding spectral gaps in quasicrystals gives systematic numerical methods aimed at gap certification and is directly connected to gap labeling [@hege2022].

Hard numerical issues include small divisors, exponentially narrow gaps, singular-continuous spectral measures, boundary-condition sensitivity, and noncommutation of thermodynamic and quasiperiodic limits.

### 7.4 Molecular dynamics and structure prediction

Atomistic computations for intermetallic quasicrystals require large approximants, alloy chemistry, phonon/phason entropy, and comparison with experiment.
Widom and Mihalkovič survey modern structure-prediction strategies, including empirical oscillating pair potentials and DFT-informed searches [@widom_mihalkovic2024].
The Al–Co–Cu simulations illustrate a frontier pattern: the target is not a single perfect tiling but a competition among approximants, phason disorder, temperature, and composition [@huang_widom_mihalkovic2024].

### 7.5 Inverse design and machine learning

Inverse design asks for interactions or particle shapes that assemble a target quasicrystal.
Recent hard-disk work combines diffraction-based symmetry objectives, evolutionary search, and machine-learning classification to find non-additive binary mixtures favoring crystals and quasicrystals [@bedolla_montiel2024].
Automated DNA-origami/colloidal design has been proposed for icosahedral quasicrystal assembly [@pinto_sulc_sciortino_russo2024], and computational self-assembly has produced candidate six-fold chiral quasicrystal models [@varela_engel2024].
Automated recognition of quasicrystalline motifs from microscopy images is emerging as a separate ML task, with current work focused on distinguishing 8-, 10-, and 12-fold quasiperiodic patterns from noisy configurations [@automatic_detection2025].

Mathematically interesting inverse-design questions include:

- Can a finite-range potential enforce a prescribed model-set hull?
- Can a differentiable loss based on diffraction distinguish homometric structures?
- Can learned descriptors converge to provable local derivation invariants?
- Can generative search find new substitution or local-rule systems rather than merely parameter-tuning known patterns?

### 7.6 Topological data analysis and real-space invariants

Persistent homology, local patch complexes, and graph neural descriptors can detect mesoscale order without assuming periodic coordinates.
Their mathematical value depends on stability: a TDA descriptor should be invariant under small perturbations and sensitive to changes in hull, window, or defect class.
At present, TDA is promising but less theorem-integrated than K-theory and cohomology.
A useful research direction is to relate persistent classes of finite samples to Čech cohomology or pattern-equivariant cohomology of the limiting hull.

### 7.7 Computation as theorem generator

The best computational work in quasicrystals produces conjectural exact statements: window shapes, substitution matrices, spectral gap labels, renormalization identities, or local rules.
The hat/Spectre diffraction sequence is exemplary: computational diffraction exposed a mismatch between aperiodicity and visible Bragg order, prompting more refined mathematical models [@kaplan_okeeffe_treacy2024_hat; @baake_gahler_mazac_mitchell2025].

## 8. Experimental or physical interface

### 8.1 Diffraction as the central bridge

The defining empirical signature of quasicrystals is sharp diffraction inconsistent with lattice periodicity.
Shechtman et al. observed sharp spots with icosahedral symmetry in rapidly solidified Al–Mn, incompatible with any ordinary lattice [@shechtman1984].
The IUCr definition then made discrete diffraction, not periodicity, the criterion of crystallinity [@iucr1992].
Mathematically, this makes autocorrelation and diffraction measures central rather than optional.

### 8.2 Electron microscopy and atomic models

Microscopy constrains local motifs, defects, and approximant relations.
However, microscopy rarely gives an infinite Delone set.
The mathematical task is inverse: infer a hull, model set, decoration rule, or ensemble from finite noisy data.
Homometry and phase retrieval mean diffraction alone is insufficient; local imaging and chemical constraints are needed [@baake_grimm2012_diffraction; @steurer2018].

### 8.3 Soft matter

Soft-matter quasicrystals are fertile because interactions are tunable and entropy is visible.
Hard-sphere monolayers and binary mixtures show how excluded-volume entropy can stabilize octagonal and dodecagonal order [@fayen_smallenburg2023; @fayen2024].
Nanocrystal superlattice work shows partial matching rules and experimentally reconstructed random-tiling geometry in mesoscopic assemblies [@ye_chen_irrgang_engel_dong_glotzer_murray2023].
These systems push mathematics toward random tilings, phason elasticity, finite-size scaling, and inverse statistical mechanics rather than perfect model sets.

### 8.4 Photonic quasicrystals

Photonic quasicrystals implement quasiperiodic refractive-index landscapes.
They allow direct observation of localization, edge modes, and topological pumps.
Recent Nature Photonics work observed localization of light in linear photonic quasicrystals with multiple rotational symmetries [@wang2024_photonic].
The mathematical interface is the spectrum of Maxwell/Schrödinger-type operators on quasiperiodic media: gaps, critical states, and topological invariants.

### 8.5 Cold atoms, superconducting processors, and programmable models

Cold-atom and superconducting platforms implement Aubry–André–Harper-type models with tunable quasiperiodic potentials.
A 2023 superconducting-processor experiment observed a critical phase transition in a generalized Aubry–André–Harper model [@li2023_aah].
These platforms do not reproduce atomic quasicrystals; rather, they realize operator-theoretic quasicrystal models where localization, topology, and interaction effects can be tested.

### 8.6 Moiré quasicrystals

Moiré quasicrystals arise when multiple incommensurate moiré patterns superpose.
Twisted trilayer graphene has produced tunable moiré quasicrystal behavior with superconductivity and strong interactions [@uri2023].
Large-area quasiperiodic moiré superlattices and dodecagonal structures in twisted bilayer graphene have also been reported [@lai2025].
These systems challenge ideal cut-and-project models because relaxation, strain, electron interactions, and finite domains all matter.

### 8.7 Empirical constraints on mathematics

Experiments impose constraints often absent from ideal models:

- finite coherence length and peak broadening;
- phason strain and diffuse scattering;
- chemical decoration and multiple scattering strengths;
- defect kinetics and growth history;
- approximant competition;
- finite temperature and entropy;
- observables that may ignore tile labels or internal coordinates.

A mathematically useful model should say which of these are perturbations and which change the invariant.

## 9. Current frontier, 2020–2026

### 9.1 Established advances

**Aperiodic monotiles.** The hat and Spectre prove that single planar shapes can force nonperiodic tilings, resolving a famous combinatorial-geometry problem in a strong form [@smith_myers_kaplan_goodmanstrauss2024_hat; @smith_myers_kaplan_goodmanstrauss2024_spectre].
Follow-up diffraction and long-range-order work clarified that aperiodicity, pure point diffraction, and physical observability depend on labels/control points/decorations [@kaplan_okeeffe_treacy2024_hat; @kaplan_okeeffe_treacy2024_spectre; @baake_gahler_mazac_sadun2025; @baake_gahler_mazac_mitchell2025].

**Almost-periodicity classification of diffraction.** Pure point diffraction is now connected to precise almost-periodicity classes.
The frontier has shifted to crystalline measures, Fourier quasicrystals, and the exact boundary between discrete support/discrete spectrum and translation-bounded almost periodicity [@lenz_spindeler_strungaru2020; @lenz2024_pure_point; @favorov2024; @mazac_richard_strungaru2026].

**Topological gap labeling beyond standard hulls.** Classical gap-labeling theorems are mature for many aperiodic solids, but recent rotational tiling-space work shows that extensions are not automatic and can fail in higher-dimensional rotational settings [@kaminker_putnam2003; @benameur_oyono2003; @liu2026_gap].

**Quasiperiodic quantum systems.** The global theory of one-dimensional analytic quasiperiodic Schrödinger operators continues to sharpen, while problem lists identify multidimensional, multifrequency, singular, and interacting cases as open frontiers [@jitomirskaya2022; @wang_you_zhou2026].

**Soft-matter entropy and inverse design.** Entropy-stabilized hard-sphere quasicrystals, inverse-designed hard-disk mixtures, and cyclic-shear assembly show that quasicrystals are not only low-temperature quasiperiodic ground states [@fayen2024; @bedolla_montiel2024; @maire2026].

### 9.2 Emerging but less settled directions

**Machine-learned aperiodic order.** Automated quasicrystal recognition and inverse design are active, but mathematical guarantees remain limited.
The frontier is to turn descriptors into provable invariants or certified local-rule extraction [@automatic_detection2025; @bedolla_montiel2024; @pinto_sulc_sciortino_russo2024].

**Pattern-forming PDE quasicrystals.** Recent work by Melbourne, Rademacher, Rink, and Zelik constructs quasiperiodic pattern classes for Euclidean-equivariant PDEs such as Swift–Hohenberg/Brusselator models, emphasizing local existence, spatial almost periodicity, and global well-posedness rather than Delone-set order.
These papers explicitly note that their diffraction diagrams need not be Delone sets, so they are adjacent to—but not identical with—the mathematical quasicrystal class used in model-set diffraction [@melbourne_rademacher_rink_zelik2024_part1; @melbourne_rademacher_rink_zelik2025_part2].

**Moiré quasicrystals and interacting electrons.** Experiments are moving quickly, especially in graphene-based systems [@uri2023; @lai2025].
Mathematical models are still catching up because relaxation, strong correlations, finite domains, and quasiperiodic geometry interact.

**Topological superconductivity and fractal boundary states.** Recent topological-quasicrystal papers propose robust real-space phenomena [@chen_lou_hu_lim2024; @saito2024].
Some results are numerical or model-specific; rigorous mobility-gap and interaction-stability theorems remain sparse.

**Aperiodic tiling undecidability and local rules.** High-dimensional counterexamples and undecidability results show that local-rule classification may be algorithmically wild [@greenfeld_tao2024; @yang_zhang2024].
Their direct physical relevance is uncertain, but their mathematical relevance to aperiodicity is clear.

## 10. Open problems

### Problem 1. Classify mathematically natural notions of quasicrystal

**Question.** How do pure point diffraction, pure point dynamical spectrum, Meyer property, model-set structure, finite local complexity, repetitivity, almost periodicity, and topological gap labels imply or fail to imply one another?

**Why hard.** The implications depend on hypotheses: FLC, unique ergodicity, translation-boundedness, window regularity, weights, randomness, and support discreteness.
Recent crystalline-measure counterexamples show that naive equivalences fail [@favorov2024; @mazac_richard_strungaru2026].

**Possible advance.** A hierarchy theorem with explicit counterexamples at each missing implication, plus stability results under bounded displacement, random thinning, and weak disorder.

### Problem 2. Characterize which Meyer sets are close to regular model sets

**Question.** Given a repetitive Meyer set with pure point diffraction, when is it mutually locally derivable, topologically conjugate, or measure-theoretically isomorphic to a regular model set?

**Why hard.** Meyer structure gives arithmetic discreteness but not a regular window.
Singular windows, non-Euclidean internal groups, and deformations complicate classification [@lagarias1996; @moody2000; @baake_grimm2017].

**Possible advance.** Reconstruct internal space and window from dynamical eigenfunctions plus patch-frequency data.

### Problem 3. Local rules for projection tilings and physical interactions

**Question.** Which cut-and-project tilings can be forced by finite local matching rules?
Which can be ground states of finite-range or rapidly decaying potentials?

**Why hard.** Local constraints must encode a global irrational cut.
Penrose succeeds, but general windows and higher codimension create singular-boundary and undecidability issues.

**Possible advance.** A computable criterion in terms of window polyhedrality, algebraic slope, and cohomological obstruction classes.

### Problem 4. Diffraction beyond pure point

**Question.** Can we classify mixed diffraction \\(\widehat\gamma=\widehat\gamma_{pp}+\widehat\gamma_{sc}+\widehat\gamma_{ac}\\) for substitution, random tiling, and weak model-set systems?

**Why hard.** Continuous components depend on long-range fluctuations invisible to finite patch frequencies.
Singular continuous diffraction is especially difficult to diagnose numerically.

**Possible advance.** Exact renormalization schemes for higher-dimensional substitutions and weak model sets, generalizing recent inflation-diffraction methods [@baake_gahler_klick_manibo_mazac2026].

### Problem 5. Homometry and inverse diffraction

**Question.** When does diffraction determine a quasicrystal up to local derivability, measure isomorphism, or decoration ambiguity?

**Why hard.** Diffraction is two-point data; different structures can share autocorrelation.
Physical diffraction also loses phases and has finite resolution [@baake_grimm2012_diffraction].

**Possible advance.** Combine higher-order correlations, constrained chemical decorations, and topological hull invariants into a provably identifiable inverse problem.

### Problem 6. Spectral theory of higher-dimensional quasiperiodic operators

**Question.** What replaces one-dimensional analytic global theory for multidimensional, multifrequency, long-range, or singular quasiperiodic operators?

**Why hard.** Transfer matrices, Aubry duality, and rotation-number methods are one-dimensional or require analyticity.
Small divisors and resonances proliferate [@jitomirskaya2022; @wang_you_zhou2026].

**Possible advance.** Multiscale analysis integrated with noncommutative K-theory and Diophantine geometry, yielding robust localization/delocalization and gap-opening theorems.

### Problem 7. Quasiperiodic transport

**Question.** How do wave packets spread in quasicrystal Hamiltonians with singular-continuous spectra, critical eigenstates, or topological boundary modes?

**Why hard.** Spectral type alone does not determine transport exponents.
Fibonacci-type trace-map methods are special [@damanik_embree_gorodetski2012].

**Possible advance.** A general theory connecting local dimension of spectral measures, hull dynamics, and transport bounds.

### Problem 8. Gap-label realizability

**Question.** Which abstract gap labels correspond to actual open spectral gaps for natural Hamiltonians on a given hull?

**Why hard.** K-theory gives possible labels, not gap opening.
Small gaps may close under perturbation, and rotational hulls introduce additional torsion subtleties [@bellissard1995; @liu2026_gap].

**Possible advance.** Certified numerical gap algorithms plus perturbative and nonperturbative gap-opening theorems [@hege2022].

### Problem 9. Growth mechanisms and local kinetics

**Question.** How can a finite-temperature local growth process produce a low-defect quasicrystal rather than a glass, approximant, or heavily phason-strained random tiling?

**Why hard.** Growth is nonequilibrium, local, and defect-prone.
Infinite-hull equilibrium models do not predict kinetics.

**Possible advance.** Coupled stochastic tiling dynamics with provable convergence rates, defect measures, and phason-strain hydrodynamics.

### Problem 10. Entropy versus energy in physical stability

**Question.** Can one derive quantitative stability criteria separating energetic matching-rule quasicrystals from entropic random tiling quasicrystals?

**Why hard.** Free energy needs phonons, phasons, chemical disorder, approximants, finite temperature, and finite-size effects.
Current successes are model-specific [@fayen2024; @widom_mihalkovic2024; @huang_widom_mihalkovic2024].

**Possible advance.** Rigorous coarse-graining from microscopic interactions to phason elastic free energy and entropy functionals.

### Problem 11. Physical realizability of monotiles

**Question.** Can the hat or Spectre be realized as a robust physical self-assembling system with observable nonperiodic long-range diffraction?

**Why hard.** Aperiodic tiling rules do not automatically translate into particle interactions; visible diffraction can be periodic for natural control points [@kaplan_okeeffe_treacy2024_hat; @baake_gahler_mazac_mitchell2025].

**Possible advance.** Decorated monotile particles whose interaction potentials force the mathematically enriched hull and whose scattering weights reveal nonperiodic Bragg modules.

### Problem 12. Complexity and decidability

**Question.** What aspects of aperiodicity, local-rule forcing, spectral gaps, and model-set recognition are decidable?

**Why hard.** Tiling problems are algorithmically wild in high dimension, and even low-dimensional local-rule spaces can encode computation [@greenfeld_tao2024; @yang_zhang2024].

**Possible advance.** Complexity classifications for restricted but physically relevant classes: polygonal monotiles, substitution rules, algebraic cut-and-project windows, and finite-range interaction ground states.

### Problem 13. Robust topology without periodicity

**Question.** Which topological phases persist in quasiperiodic systems under disorder, phason flips, interactions, and finite-size approximants?

**Why hard.** Bloch momentum is absent, mobility gaps may replace spectral gaps, and interactions require many-body C*-algebraic or tensor-network methods [@else_thorngren_pretko_vishwanath2021; @chen_lou_hu_lim2024].

**Possible advance.** A unified real-space invariant framework with computable finite-sample estimators and thermodynamic-limit proofs.

### Problem 14. Data-to-hull reconstruction

**Question.** Given finite noisy atomic coordinates or images, can one infer the underlying hull, substitution, window, or ensemble?

**Why hard.** Data are finite, noisy, chemically decorated, and often homometric.
Learned classifiers may not identify mathematical invariants [@automatic_detection2025; @steurer2018].

**Possible advance.** Statistical estimators with consistency theorems for model-set parameters, patch-frequency modules, and cohomology proxies.

### Problem 15. Inverse design with proof certificates

**Question.** Can machine learning discover new quasicrystals together with a certificate: local rules, substitution, diffraction type, or ground-state proof?

**Why hard.** Optimization finds candidates, not proofs.
Energy landscapes can favor approximants or glasses close in objective value [@bedolla_montiel2024; @ratliff2026].

**Possible advance.** Hybrid pipelines: neural/generative search → exact symbolic tiling extraction → computer-assisted proof of aperiodicity/diffraction/gap labels.

## 11. Cross-regime comparison

| Regime | Mathematical object | Translational structure | Correlation/diffraction | Dynamics/statistics | What changes relative to quasicrystals |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Crystal | Periodic Delone set or lattice-periodic density | Full-rank period lattice \\(L\\); compact unit cell | Pure point diffraction on reciprocal lattice \\(L^*\\) | Bloch decomposition over Brillouin torus | Rank of Fourier module equals spatial rank; no phason degrees of freedom |
| Quasicrystal | FLC/repetitive Delone set, tiling hull, model set, substitution system, or quasiperiodic operator | No full-rank period lattice; often higher-rank \\(\mathbb Z\\)-module | Often pure point Bragg diffraction on dense or high-rank module; may include diffuse parts | Hull dynamics, internal torus/solenoid factors, phasons, approximants | Order remains long-range but periodic unit cell disappears |
| Random tiling quasicrystal | Probability measure on constrained tilings | No fixed period; ensemble has quasiperiodic order parameter | Bragg peaks plus diffuse scattering possible | Positive configurational entropy; phason elasticity | Deterministic hull becomes statistical ensemble |
| Glass/amorphous solid | Disordered point process with rigidity | No lattice and no quasiperiodic module | Broad peaks; short/intermediate-range order | Metastable energy landscape; aging | Local order may exist, but long-range Bragg/module structure is absent |
| Liquid | Translation/rotation-invariant point process in equilibrium | No persistent fixed sites | Pair correlation decays; structure factor has broad peaks/rings | Particles mobile; hydrodynamic modes | Static Delone geometry is replaced by time-dependent correlations |
| Gas | Dilute point process, often near Poisson | None | \\(g(r)\\approx1\\) outside interaction scale | Kinetic theory; Boltzmann/Vlasov limits | Correlations weak and sparse; no long-range positional order |
| Plasma | Charged particle distribution plus electromagnetic/self-consistent field | None generally | Screening; collective modes; dynamic structure factor | Vlasov–Poisson, Landau damping, Debye shielding | Dominant invariant is collective field behavior, not static point-set order |

The mathematically decisive transitions are: periodic lattice \\(\to\\) higher-rank/quasiperiodic module; deterministic FLC hull \\(\to\\) random ensemble; Bragg measure \\(\to\\) diffuse structure factor; fixed Delone geometry \\(\to\\) mobile point process; neutral correlations \\(\to\\) long-range charged collective fields.

## 13. Suggested research entry points

For a researcher designing new work, the highest-leverage entry points are:

1. **Diffraction/measure theory:** build examples separating pure point diffraction, Fourier quasicrystal definitions, Meyer structure, and almost-periodicity classes.
2. **Monotile long-range order:** determine which decorations/control points of monotile tilings yield nonperiodic pure point diffraction and whether those decorations are physically enforceable.
3. **Gap-label realizability:** combine certified numerical spectral gaps with K-theoretic labels and perturbation theory.
4. **Data-to-hull reconstruction:** develop statistically consistent estimators for windows, substitution rules, and cohomology from finite noisy samples.
5. **Entropy-stabilized quasicrystals:** derive random-tiling/phason elastic theories from microscopic simulations with explicit error control.
6. **Moiré/topological quasicrystals:** formulate continuum and tight-binding models whose quasiperiodic limits have rigorous invariants beyond periodic approximants.

The common methodological lesson is that “aperiodic” is too weak a property.
Research progress comes from specifying which invariant—diffraction, dynamical spectrum, Meyer geometry, topology, entropy, spectral gap, or transport exponent—is being preserved, computed, or classified.
