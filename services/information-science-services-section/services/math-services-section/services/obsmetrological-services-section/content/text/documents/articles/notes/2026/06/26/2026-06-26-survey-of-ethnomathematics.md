**Current as of:** 26 June 2026 **Scope:** Ethnomathematics as a field of research, pedagogy, historical reconstruction, philosophy, and political/ethical critique. **Important caution:** This survey treats cultural, Indigenous, diasporic, religious, craft, ecological, navigational, computational, and material practices as knowledge systems in their own right.
It does not treat them as primitive precursors to Western academic mathematics or as decorative examples to be mined for algebra, geometry, or symmetry.

## How to read this survey

Ethnomathematics is not one thing.
It is at least four overlapping projects:

1. the study of mathematical practices embedded in cultural life;
2. the academic reconstruction, comparison, and formalization of those practices;
3. a pedagogical and decolonial movement in mathematics education;
4. a philosophical challenge to what mathematics is, who gets to define it, and how mathematical authority is institutionalized.

The field is intellectually productive because these projects do not fully coincide.
The same beadwork pattern can be read as a symmetry group, a family practice, a sacred or ceremonial form, a classroom activity, a market commodity, a political assertion of continuity, or a formal design grammar.
A strong ethnomathematical account must hold these readings together without collapsing them into one another.

# I. Executive summary

Ethnomathematics emerged in the late twentieth century from converging dissatisfaction with the standard Eurocentric story of mathematics.
In that story, mathematics is imagined as a universal, culture-free body of formal truths whose historical arc passes from Greece to Europe to modern abstraction, while other peoples appear only as sources of examples, intuitive techniques, or pre-scientific approximations.
Ethnomathematics contests that story.
It asks how people count, measure, locate, design, infer, classify, optimize, calculate, encode, model, compare, remember, predict, and coordinate action in specific cultural, ecological, technological, and political settings.

Ubiratan D'Ambrosio's 1985 essay, usually treated as the founding manifesto of the field, defined ethnomathematics as a research program at the border of the history of mathematics and cultural anthropology.
His formulation did not merely add non-European examples to a European curriculum.
It asked how mathematical ideas arise from culturally organized ways of explaining, knowing, surviving, and acting.
Later accounts by Marcia Ascher, Alan Bishop, Paulus Gerdes, Claudia Zaslavsky, Ron Eglash, Bill Barton, Arthur Powell, Marilyn Frankenstein, Milton Rosa, Daniel Orey, and many others expanded this program into a field spanning anthropology, mathematics education, history of science, philosophy, design, craft studies, Indigenous studies, and science and technology studies.

The field remains contested because it touches the identity of mathematics itself.
One camp treats mathematics as universal but differentially expressed: every culture may count, pattern, or measure, but the resulting practices are ultimately partial expressions of one mathematics.
A pluralist camp treats mathematics as a family of practices rather than a single object: what counts as mathematics depends on forms of life, goals, notations, technologies, proof norms, institutions, and material constraints.
Between these poles are many intermediate positions.
Some researchers want to show that Indigenous or African or Pacific or diasporic practices contain "real mathematics" such as group theory, graph theory, recursion, modular arithmetic, optimization, topology, or combinatorics.
Others warn that this defense can reproduce the very hierarchy it rejects: a practice is granted legitimacy only after being translated into academic mathematics.

The contemporary frontier is therefore not just the discovery of more examples.
It is methodological and ethical.
Researchers now ask: Who has authority to identify a practice as mathematical?
What happens when sacred, restricted, or community-governed knowledge is turned into a dataset?
Can formalization be reciprocal rather than extractive?
Can mathematics education be transformed rather than merely diversified by adding culturally themed word problems?
How do Indigenous data sovereignty, community review, refusal, and Local Contexts labels reshape mathematical research?
What would AI trained on cultural pattern archives do to living traditions?
Can communities build their own formal archives, computational design tools, or curricula without surrendering control?

Recent scholarship shows several visible trends.
First, literature reviews and bibliometric studies indicate rapid growth, especially in mathematics education and especially in Indonesia, Latin America, Africa, and Indigenous education contexts.
A 2024 scoping review by Hongzhang Xu and Rowena Ball mapped 169 studies from 1984 to 2023, identifying two broad research orientations: identifying mathematical concepts in Indigenous and traditional practices, and systematizing those concepts into more explicit frameworks.
A 2026 bibliometric analysis of 210 Scopus-indexed publications from 2010 to 2024 similarly describes an expanding but uneven field.
Second, Indigenous data governance frameworks such as CARE, OCAP, and Local Contexts have begun to change the ethical baseline: not all knowledge should be open, digitized, or formalized.
Third, the rise of AI and machine learning has created a new extraction risk.
Pattern archives, khipu databases, textile datasets, language corpora, and image repositories can be modeled computationally, but without community governance this repeats colonial collection under a technical name.

Mathematically, the field has become richer and more precise.
Khipu research now uses corpus analysis and data science to test accounting relations among hundreds of knotted-string records.
Sona and related sand-drawing traditions are studied through Eulerian graph theory, drawing algorithms, and greatest-common-divisor constructions, while researchers also emphasize that the drawings are story-bearing performance practices.
Kolam research connects ritual drawing to graph traversal, picture grammars, one-stroke generation, modular structure, and, recently, community-sensitive data physicalization.
Pacific navigation involves star compasses, swell patterns, dead reckoning, embodied spherical reasoning, and relational orientation rather than map-centered abstraction.
African fractal architecture and design involve recursion, scaling, and generative procedures, but reductive "this is just fractals" readings miss ritual, social, settlement, and aesthetic meanings.
Beadwork, weaving, basketry, and textiles open research problems in symmetry, color constraints, braid and knot structures, topology, linear algebra, tiling, and embodied fabrication.
Games such as mancala connect counting, strategy, state spaces, invariants, combinatorial game theory, and social pedagogy.
Music and dance bring modular time, Euclidean rhythms, cyclic permutations, embodied timing, and gesture-space geometry into mathematical view.

The strongest current work is neither anti-mathematical nor naïvely celebratory.
It accepts that formalization can reveal deep structure, support education, and create new mathematics.
It also insists that formalization can distort, appropriate, or desacralize.
The frontier lies in methods that can do both: mathematical modeling that is precise enough to matter to mathematicians, and accountable enough not to turn living knowledge into raw material for institutional extraction.

The next decade will likely be shaped by five pressures.
First, Indigenous and community-led scholarship will demand stronger protocols for consent, authorship, compensation, data governance, and refusal.
Second, AI will intensify the stakes of pattern extraction, language modeling, and automated classification.
Third, mathematics education will continue to fight over whether culturally sustaining and decolonial mathematics is rigorous transformation or political dilution; the answer will depend on whether curricula change epistemic authority, not merely examples.
Fourth, material mathematics—textiles, beadwork, architecture, basketry, carving, ceramics, knotting, navigation tools—will become a major interdisciplinary frontier connecting topology, mechanics, computation, and craft.
Fifth, philosophy of mathematics will have to engage ethnomathematics more seriously: not as a sociological add-on, but as evidence that mathematical objects, proofs, models, and practices are stabilized through institutions, tools, languages, bodies, and lands.

# II. Definitions and scope

## 1. D'Ambrosio and the emergence of the term

D'Ambrosio's 1985 article, "Ethnomathematics and Its Place in the History and Pedagogy of Mathematics," named a research program that sits between the history of mathematics, anthropology, and mathematics education.
Later summaries often gloss ethnomathematics as the mathematical practices of identifiable cultural groups.
D'Ambrosio also parsed the word etymologically: *ethno* as cultural environment, *mathema* as explanation/understanding/knowing, and *tics* from techniques or arts.
This etymology matters because it shifts mathematics from a noun to an activity: ways of explaining, coping, ordering, designing, and knowing.

D'Ambrosio's program also had an ethical horizon.
He repeatedly linked ethnomathematics to peace, social justice, and critique of domination.
The point was not only to recognize hidden mathematics in cultural practices, but to contest the educational and political systems that position some peoples as mathematically capable and others as deficient.

## 2. Mathematics as cultural practice

A cultural-practice definition treats mathematics as something people do.
It includes counting systems, body-based numeration, land measurement, star navigation, calendrical calculation, trade accounting, craft design, kinship classification, game strategy, ritual patterning, ecological timing, and material fabrication.
The practices may not be separated from religion, law, kinship, subsistence, aesthetics, or governance.
Indeed, the separation of "mathematics" from those domains is itself historically specific.

This definition is attractive because it does not require a practice to resemble classroom mathematics before being studied.
Its risk is overextension: if every ordered practice is mathematics, the term loses discrimination.
Good ethnomathematics therefore needs criteria: not rigid external criteria, but careful attention to counting, measuring, relational ordering, invariants, transformations, proof-like justification, optimization, algorithmic repetition, reversible operations, pattern generation, error correction, and other features that make a practice mathematically consequential without forcing it into a predetermined academic template.

## 3. Ethnomathematics as education reform

In mathematics education, ethnomathematics has been used to make curricula culturally relevant, culturally responsive, culturally sustaining, anti-racist, Indigenizing, and decolonial.
The strongest versions do more than add cultural artifacts to worksheets.
They ask whose knowledge counts, which languages can carry mathematical reasoning, which problems matter to a community, how learners' existing practices can be treated as intellectual resources, and how mathematics can be taught without requiring cultural assimilation.

The weakest versions become tokenism: a Navajo rug pattern inserted into a geometry lesson, a mancala board used to teach arithmetic, or a beadwork image used to decorate a linear algebra assignment without community context, Indigenous authority, or attention to the politics of knowledge.
Contemporary education research increasingly distinguishes representational inclusion from epistemic transformation.

## 4. Ethnomathematics as anthropology and history of knowledge

Anthropological and historical ethnomathematics asks how mathematical ideas appear in archives, artifacts, oral traditions, everyday labor, performance, craft, ritual, and social organization.
It studies how knowledge is transmitted, how procedures are validated, how practitioners identify error, how expertise is distributed, and how practices change under trade, missionization, schooling, colonization, industrialization, tourism, digital archiving, and market commodification.

This strand is close to the anthropology of number, history of science, material culture studies, cognitive anthropology, and STS.
It is also vulnerable to classic anthropological problems: outsider authority, colonial archives, translation asymmetry, informant extraction, and publication of knowledge that communities may not want public.

## 5. Ethnomathematics as decolonial critique

As decolonial critique, ethnomathematics targets the coloniality of mathematical authority.
It asks why modern institutions often treat European formal mathematics as universal reason and other practices as culture.
It asks how schools, exams, development projects, technical standards, land surveys, census categories, and economic models imposed colonial order.
It also asks how communities can reclaim or continue mathematical knowledge without simply translating it into institutional terms.

This strand is strongly shaped by Indigenous research methodologies, critical mathematics education, antiracist education, and decolonial theory.
Its key terms include sovereignty, refusal, reciprocity, community accountability, epistemic justice, and knowledge governance.

## 6. Ethnomathematics as philosophy of mathematics

Philosophically, ethnomathematics challenges familiar positions:

- **Realism/Platonism:** If mathematics is a realm of abstract objects, then cultural practices are different routes to the same truths.
  Ethnomathematics becomes history and pedagogy, not ontology.
- **Formalism:** If mathematics is formal symbol manipulation, many cultural practices are proto-formal or informal until encoded.
  Ethnomathematics becomes a source of structures for formalization.
- **Constructivism/pragmatism:** Mathematical objects are constructed through activity, proof, tools, notation, and use.
  Ethnomathematics becomes evidence that different practices construct different stable objects.
- **Pluralism:** There may be many legitimate mathematical worlds, logics, or practices, not merely many examples of one mathematics.
- **Decolonial epistemology:** The issue is not only ontology but power: who can declare an object mathematical, what institutions enforce that declaration, and what forms of life are erased when mathematics is purified from context.

## 7. Universalist and pluralist tensions

The universalist impulse says: two plus two is four everywhere; a symmetry group is a symmetry group everywhere; if a craft pattern instantiates a wallpaper group, that fact is not culturally relative.
The pluralist response says: the statement may be formally true, but the practice is not exhausted by it.
A weaver may not be "doing wallpaper groups" in the social sense, even if a mathematician can construct a wallpaper-group model from the cloth.
Conversely, a community may possess rich mathematical knowledge even where no Western theorem is immediately visible.

A mature position distinguishes **formal equivalence**, **practical equivalence**, **epistemic equivalence**, and **political equivalence**.
A model may be formally correct while practically irrelevant, epistemically distorting, or politically extractive.
The task is not to abandon formalization, but to situate it.

# III. Historical foundations

## 1. Colonial encounters and the classification of knowledge

Modern mathematics developed alongside colonial classification: mapping, surveying, accounting, taxation, navigation, logistics, astronomy, ballistics, census-making, and racialized schooling.
Colonial institutions frequently treated Indigenous and local mathematical practices as superstition, craft habit, or practical rule-of-thumb, while extracting land knowledge, navigational knowledge, calendrical knowledge, agricultural timing, architecture, and design.

The colonial archive is therefore both indispensable and dangerous.
It contains records of practices disrupted by colonization, but those records are filtered through missionaries, administrators, ethnographers, traders, and school officials.
Errors in transcription, mistranslation, and missing protocol context are common.
Contemporary work in Indigenous astronomy, for example, warns that early records may misidentify stars or omit cultural restrictions.
Similar caution applies to khipu, sand drawings, beadwork, ecological calendars, and kinship systems.

## 2. Anthropology of number, measure, space, time, and pattern

Long before ethnomathematics was named, anthropologists and historians studied numeration, counting gestures, body-based systems, calendrical cycles, spatial orientation, and trade calculation.
These studies often oscillated between fascination and evolutionism.
Some treated non-European systems as evidence of "primitive mentality" or cognitive limitation.
Later scholarship reversed that framing, showing that numeration and calculation are technologies embedded in practical life.
A body-counting system, for example, is not a failed place-value system; it is a culturally organized mapping between body, gesture, memory, and quantity.

## 3. History of mathematics beyond Greece and Europe

Ethnomathematics overlaps with the broader project of writing world histories of mathematics: Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Chinese, Indian, Islamic, African, Mesoamerican, Andean, Pacific, Arctic, and other traditions.
The distinction is that history of mathematics often focuses on texts, proofs, astronomies, algorithms, and institutions that can be placed in a global academic genealogy.
Ethnomathematics more readily includes craft, oral knowledge, embodied practice, local computation, and living tradition.
The two fields are complementary when both avoid civilizational ranking.

## 4. Foundational figures

### Ubiratan D'Ambrosio D'Ambrosio gave the field its name and its political ambition.
His work connected mathematics education, cultural history, and critique of domination.
He treated mathematics not as a neutral universal possession but as a set of techniques for knowing and acting that are historically and culturally located.

### Marcia Ascher Ascher's *Ethnomathematics: A Multicultural View of Mathematical Ideas* (1991) remains one of the clearest mathematical introductions.
Her examples include kinship systems, khipu, games, graph-like drawings, and mathematical ideas outside written proof traditions.
Her strength is mathematical clarity; the limitation, from a contemporary view, is that the field now demands deeper community governance and sovereignty analysis than early synthetic work usually provided.

### Alan Bishop Bishop's influential account identified six mathematical activities widely found across cultures: counting, locating, measuring, designing, playing, and explaining.
This taxonomy helped educators see mathematics as activity rather than only curriculum content.
Its limitation is that a universal activity list can still impose an external frame if not handled carefully.

### Claudia Zaslavsky Zaslavsky's *Africa Counts* (1973; later editions) documented number, pattern, games, music, architecture, and other mathematical practices across sub-Saharan Africa.
It was foundational for both African mathematics history and multicultural mathematics education.
It also represents an early effort to challenge the absence of African intellectual traditions from school mathematics.

### Paulus Gerdes Gerdes developed some of the most mathematically detailed work in ethnomathematics, especially on African geometry, sona drawings, basketry, mat weaving, and design.
His work often reconstructs geometric reasoning from craft procedures and shows how it can generate mathematical problems.
Contemporary readers should pair his formal reconstructions with community-centered and anti-extractive protocols.

### Ron Eglash Eglash's *African Fractals* (1999) brought recursion, scaling, and computational design into public view.
It showed fractal-like organization in architecture, settlement layout, hairstyling, textiles, sculpture, and symbolic systems.
Its importance is enormous, but the slogan "African fractals" can become reductive when detached from local meanings and practices.

### Gloria Ford Gilmer and ISGEm Gloria Ford Gilmer was a major organizer of ethnomathematics in the United States and a co-founder of the International Study Group on Ethnomathematics in 1985.
ISGEm helped institutionalize the field through networks, conferences, and publications connecting mathematics, culture, social justice, and decolonization.

### Bill Barton, Arthur Powell, Marilyn Frankenstein, Milton Rosa, Daniel Orey, and others These scholars expanded ethnomathematics in mathematics education, critical pedagogy, curriculum design, teacher education, and theoretical clarification.
Barton sharpened definitional issues; Powell and Frankenstein connected mathematics education to democracy and social justice; Rosa and Orey developed curricula, modeling frameworks, and global syntheses.

## 5. Practices historically studied

Ethnomathematics has studied mathematical reasoning in:

- craft: weaving, basketry, beadwork, carving, ceramics, textiles, knotting;
- architecture: settlement plans, house forms, sacred buildings, Islamic geometric patterns;
- navigation: star paths, swell reading, wayfinding, orientation systems;
- music and dance: rhythm cycles, modular timing, floor patterns, gesture sequences;
- games: mancala, strategy games, puzzles, race games, gambling and probability;
- agriculture and ecology: seasonal calendars, phenology, irrigation, land division;
- astronomy and calendars: lunar/solar cycles, ritual calendars, star knowledge;
- trade and accounting: numeration, tallying, khipu, weights, measures, exchange;
- language and kinship: classifiers, relation systems, taxonomies, spatial deixis.

## 6. Nation-states, colonial schools, and mathematics education

Colonial and national schooling often used mathematics to rank populations and train bureaucratic subjects.
Standard curricula displaced local knowledge by treating school mathematics as the only real mathematics.
Ethnomathematics entered teacher education partly as a repair: it sought to reconnect students' lived knowledge with formal learning.
The danger is that schools can absorb ethnomathematics as multicultural decoration while preserving the hierarchy that caused the harm.
The frontier is therefore not inclusion alone but authority: who designs the curriculum, who benefits, who reviews it, and what knowledge remains outside the classroom by community choice.

# V. Current frontier clusters

## A. Ethnomathematics and Indigenous knowledge sovereignty

### Core questions

- Who owns or governs mathematical knowledge embedded in cultural practice?
- What does consent mean when knowledge is collective, intergenerational, sacred, restricted, gendered, seasonal, clan-based, or land-based?
- Can a mathematical model be published if the underlying practice is not public knowledge?
- How should citations, authorship, compensation, review, and withdrawal rights work?
- What does refusal mean in mathematical research?

### Current state

The strongest contemporary work treats sovereignty not as an optional ethics appendix but as a condition of knowledge production.
Indigenous data governance frameworks have become central.
The CARE Principles require Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics.
OCAP articulates First Nations' rights to Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession of data.
Local Contexts' Traditional Knowledge and Biocultural Labels offer digital mechanisms for communicating Indigenous protocols around access, use, attribution, and circulation.
Indigenous AI protocols extend these concerns to machine learning and automated systems.

For ethnomathematics, these frameworks change the default.
A beadwork pattern is not simply an image; a star story is not simply an astronomical dataset; a khipu is not simply a string encoding; a seasonal calendar is not simply a table.
Mathematical research must ask whether formalizing, digitizing, or publishing the structure violates community governance.

### Methodological difficulties

- Many mathematical practices are transmitted orally or through apprenticeship, not through public texts.
- Some knowledge is public in one context but restricted in another.
- Academic publication presumes permanence and circulation; community protocols may require contextual, seasonal, or relational limits.
- Existing IP law often fails to protect collective and intergenerational knowledge.
- Data repositories favor openness, while Indigenous data sovereignty may require closure, tiered access, or deletion.

### Ethical issues

The key ethical shift is from **informed consent** to **relational accountability**.
Consent from one individual may not authorize publication of collective knowledge.
Peer review by academic strangers may not substitute for community review.
Mathematical elegance is not a justification for extraction.
Some results should remain unpublished.

### Unresolved problems

- Community-controlled formalization protocols for mathematics.
- Mathematical archives with tiered access, Local Contexts labeling, and withdrawal mechanisms.
- Authorship standards for collective and intergenerational knowledge.
- Standards for AI training exclusions and cultural pattern opt-outs.
- Methods for citing knowledge without exposing restricted details.

### Likely next 5–10 years

Expect stronger norm-setting around community review, Local Contexts labels in digital math/craft archives, Indigenous-led journals or review boards, and explicit refusal statements in publications.
AI will make these protocols urgent.

## B. Decolonizing mathematics education

### Core questions

- Is ethnomathematics a way to make standard mathematics more accessible, or a way to transform what counts as mathematics?
- How can curricula sustain students' cultural and linguistic knowledge rather than assimilating it?
- How should rigor be evaluated when mathematical reasoning is oral, embodied, relational, or land-based?
- What is the difference between culturally relevant, culturally responsive, culturally sustaining, Indigenizing, and decolonial mathematics education?

### Current state

Mathematics education is the largest and fastest-moving area of ethnomathematics.
Contemporary work includes culturally sustaining pedagogy, Indigenous STEM, Hawaiian ethnomathematics, Māori-medium teacher education, Turtle Mountain College's Indigenous Math sequence, Indigenizing university mathematics, and anti-racist curriculum debates.
The 2024--2026 literature includes systematic reviews, bibliometric studies, edited volumes, and many local curriculum cases.

### Transformative vs representational approaches

A representational approach adds cultural examples to existing units.
A transformative approach changes the epistemic architecture: the language of instruction, the authority of elders and makers, the problems deemed worthy, the forms of proof accepted, the relation to land and community, and the purposes of mathematical knowledge.

The latter is harder to assess because standard tests often measure assimilation into existing school mathematics.
A curriculum may be mathematically and culturally strong while not immediately visible through standardized metrics.

### Political backlash

Culturally responsive and decolonial mathematics education has become part of wider culture-war debates.
Critics often claim that such work lowers standards, politicizes mathematics, or replaces algebra with identity.
Internal critics reply that mathematics has always been politically situated through tracking, testing, language, access, and the privileging of some forms of abstraction.
The serious question is not whether politics is present, but whether the curriculum produces deeper mathematical agency while respecting community knowledge.

### Unresolved problems

- Longitudinal evidence for culturally sustaining mathematics curricula.
- Assessment models that recognize embodied, oral, collaborative, and land-based reasoning.
- Teacher preparation without shallow artifact use.
- University mathematics courses that integrate Indigenous knowledge without appropriation.
- Curriculum governance by communities rather than institutions alone.

## C. Philosophy of mathematics and mathematical pluralism

### Core questions

- Is there one mathematics discovered in many cultures, or many mathematical practices stabilized differently?
- Does formal equivalence imply epistemic equivalence?
- Can a practice count as mathematical without proof, notation, abstraction, or generality in the academic sense?
- How should philosophy of mathematics treat oral, embodied, material, and ecological reasoning?

### Current state

Mainstream philosophy of mathematics has begun to take pluralism more seriously, especially in logic and set theory, where multiple legitimate foundations or universes are debated.
Ethnomathematics pushes pluralism further: the plurality is not only formal systems but practices, institutions, bodies, tools, land relations, and community protocols.
A kolam and a graph-theoretic Eulerian circuit may be formally connected, but they are not the same object in every epistemic sense.

### Productive distinctions

- **Object pluralism:** different mathematical objects or structures.
- **Practice pluralism:** different ways of making, validating, transmitting, and using mathematics.
- **Norm pluralism:** different standards of correctness, elegance, sufficiency, proof, or responsibility.
- **Ontological pluralism:** different worlds or relations in which mathematical entities are meaningful.
- **Political pluralism:** different authorities over what may be known, named, or published.

### Unresolved problems

Philosophy still lacks a robust account of **formalization-with-loss**.
We can often say what a model gains: precision, generalization, proof, computation.
We less often have technical language for what is lost: obligation, secrecy, apprenticeship, land relation, gesture, rhythm, sacredness, humor, social pedagogy, and the maker's authority.

## D. Material culture and embodied mathematics

### Core questions

- How is mathematical reasoning carried by hand, eye, tool, material, rhythm, error correction, and apprenticeship?
- What happens when a finished artifact is analyzed apart from its making process?
- Can topology, geometry, mechanics, and computation model craft without extracting it?

### Current state

Material culture is one of the richest frontiers.
Textiles, beadwork, weaving, basketry, ceramics, architecture, carving, and knotting are not merely patterned objects.
They are constrained production systems.
A weaver reasons through warp, weft, tension, sequence, edge conditions, repair, repetition, color, available materials, inherited designs, and social protocols.
A beadworker reasons through grid, thread path, color adjacency, symmetry, motif transformation, count, and technique.
A basketmaker reasons through curvature, stiffness, local over-under relations, growth patterns, and embodied force.

### Mathematical content

Relevant mathematics includes:

- wallpaper, frieze, and layer groups;
- color symmetry and color constraints;
- knot, braid, and textile topology;
- graph embeddings and traversal;
- tilings and substitution systems;
- linear algebra of pattern vectors and transformations;
- mechanics of flexible materials;
- algorithmic and generative grammars;
- constraint satisfaction and inverse design.

### Ethical issues

Material culture is especially vulnerable to extraction because images circulate easily.
A photograph of beadwork or a basket can become a dataset, a generated design, or a commercial product.
Formal publication should not make motifs available for appropriation.

### Next directions

Community-governed design tools, craft-led mathematical publications, maker co-authorship, protected datasets, and mathematical models of process rather than only finished pattern.

## E. Pattern, symmetry, computation, and generative systems

### Core questions

- Which cultural pattern systems are algorithmic, recursive, graph-theoretic, grammatical, or computational?
- How do formal generative models relate to practitioners' own rules?
- Can computational reconstruction support revitalization rather than appropriation?

### Current state

This is the most visibly mathematical frontier.
Sona drawings, kolam, African fractal design, Islamic geometric patterns, khipu, weaving drafts, and beadwork patterns have all been modeled computationally.
Recent kolam work includes graph-theoretic theses, algorithmic one-stroke generation, modular/Eulerian constructions, and data physicalization.
Khipu work increasingly uses corpus analysis.
Sona work connects line drawings to Eulerian circuits, Gaussian graphs, and gcd-based algorithms.

### Methodological difficulties

- A formal grammar may generate valid-looking patterns that are culturally meaningless.
- A dataset may contain designs collected without consent.
- Algorithmic simplicity may be an artifact of the model, not the practice.
- Pattern similarity may ignore lineage, clan, ceremony, gender, or maker identity.

### Next directions

Community-owned pattern engines, protocol-aware generative systems, non-extractive computational archives, and formal models that include use restrictions as first-class metadata.

## F. Language, logic, classification, and relational reasoning

### Core questions

- How do languages organize number, measure, space, time, relation, and category?
- Can kinship, ecological classification, and ceremonial taxonomy be modeled mathematically without reducing them to abstract sets?
- What alternative logics or relation systems are embedded in non-Western languages and practices?

### Current state

This area is underdeveloped relative to pattern and education research.
Counting-word studies are mature, but classification, kinship, relationality, and alternative inferential structures are less systematically connected to mathematics.
Ascher's work on kinship systems remains important.
Indigenous language revitalization and ontology projects now create possibilities for community-controlled formal models, but also significant risk of semantic extraction.

### Mathematical content

Potential structures include directed graphs, relations, partial orders, equivalence relations, Galois connections, algebraic kinship models, category-like relation composition, mereology, modal logics, and non-classical logics.
But the technical fit must be demonstrated, not assumed.

### Next directions

Collaborative formal ontologies governed by communities; relation-aware language tools; mathematical models of ecological taxonomies; and philosophical accounts of relational reasoning that do not treat relation as merely a binary predicate over individuals.

## G. Ethnomathematics, AI, and computation

### Core questions

- What happens when cultural patterns become training data?
- Can AI support community-led revitalization, pedagogy, or reconstruction without extracting knowledge?
- How should models respect Local Contexts labels, CARE, OCAP, refusal, and restricted knowledge?
- Can mathematical formalization include access protocols and cultural governance as part of the model?

### Current state

AI has entered ethnomathematics through three routes: educational technology, computational modeling of cultural practices, and broader debates about decolonial AI.
Emerging work asks whether AI can help revitalize African ethnomathematics or support Indigenous education.
At the same time, AI intensifies every extraction problem.
Image-generation models can imitate beadwork, textiles, tattoos, and sacred motifs.
Pattern-classification systems can infer structures from digitized archives.
Language models can reproduce restricted stories from scraped sources.

### Ethical requirements

- Datasets should record provenance, consent, restrictions, and community authority.
- Some materials should be excluded from training.
- Communities should be able to audit, withdraw, or relabel data.
- AI outputs should not be treated as cultural authority.
- Models should not generate sacred, clan-specific, or restricted designs.

### Next directions

Protocol-aware AI systems, community-controlled model training, cultural-pattern opt-out registries, mathematical metadata for restrictions, and AI tools designed for internal community use rather than public extraction.

## H. Land, navigation, ecology, and environmental knowledge

### Core questions

- How are spatial, temporal, and ecological relations mathematically organized outside map-centered abstraction?
- How do seasonal calendars, star knowledge, water knowledge, fire knowledge, and animal/plant indicators coordinate action?
- How can mathematical ecology learn from Indigenous knowledge without reducing it to data points?

### Current state

Pacific navigation, Indigenous astronomy, seasonal calendars, and ecological indicators are central.
Community co-developed seasonal calendars show how cycles are not merely dates but relations among species, weather, water, fire, ceremony, harvesting, and responsibility.
Navigation knowledge often uses stars, swells, birds, clouds, currents, and embodied movement.
It is mathematical, but not primarily because it secretly contains classroom trigonometry; rather, it organizes relational space, uncertainty, timing, correction, and collective memory.

### Difficulties

- Climate change disrupts ecological indicators.
- Colonial maps and property regimes conflict with relational land knowledge.
- Some star or place knowledge is restricted.
- GIS can both support and distort Indigenous spatial knowledge.

### Next directions

Mathematical models of seasonal uncertainty, community-controlled ecological data systems, land-based mathematics curricula, and careful work on relational spatial reasoning.

## I. Critiques of ethnomathematics

### 1. Romanticization

Ethnomathematics can romanticize cultural practices as harmonious, ancient, or spiritually pure.
This freezes living communities and ignores change, conflict, gender, class, market pressures, and colonial disruption.

### 2. Essentialism

The phrase "Indigenous mathematics" or "African mathematics" can hide enormous internal diversity.
There is no single Indigenous mathematics, African mathematics, Asian mathematics, or Pacific mathematics.

### 3. Tokenism

Curricula can decorate standard lessons with cultural artifacts while leaving authority unchanged.
This is especially common when educators lack community relationships.

### 4. "Everything is math" overreach

If any pattern, story, or classification is called mathematics, the concept becomes too broad.
Ethnomathematics needs precise but flexible criteria.

### 5. Academic extraction

Researchers can convert community practices into publications, grants, datasets, and careers without reciprocity.
This critique is central, not peripheral.

### 6. Misrepresentation

External formalization may identify a symmetry, algorithm, or equation that practitioners do not recognize as meaningful.
The model may be formally true but culturally misleading.

### 7. Universalism vs relativism

External critics sometimes accuse ethnomathematics of relativism.
Internal critics reply that recognizing cultural practice does not deny formal truth; it denies that institutional Western mathematics exhausts mathematical knowledge.

### 8. Political backlash

Culturally responsive mathematics is frequently attacked as lowering standards.
The stronger reply is not defensive tokenism but rigorous curriculum design demonstrating mathematical depth, cultural accountability, and student agency.

### 9. Legibility danger

The most subtle danger is translating knowledge into mathematics only to make it legible to institutions.
Once legible, knowledge can be governed, extracted, patented, sold, or used against communities.

# VI. Mathematical content: examples with gains and losses of formalization

## 1. Khipu: cords, knots, decimal accounting, and information encoding

### Practice

Inka and Andean khipu are knotted-cord devices used for recordkeeping, administration, accounting, and possibly narrative or other nonnumeric communication.
A khipu typically consists of a primary cord with pendant cords, subsidiaries, knots, colors, ply directions, attachment types, and spatial order.
The Aschers showed that many khipu encode decimal numerical information through knot type and position.
Urton and later researchers argued that additional binary or multivalent features may encode nonnumeric signs.
Recent corpus work by Manuel Medrano applies data science to hundreds of khipu to test accounting relations.

### Mathematical structures

- decimal positional notation through knot placement;
- hierarchical tree or graph structure of cords;
- summation and accounting relations among pendants and top cords;
- combinatorial sign systems using color, knot type, ply, attachment, and direction;
- data-structure analysis of ordered strings with metadata;
- statistical inference over corpora.

### What formalization gains

Formalization can identify accounting relations, reconstruct missing totals, compare khipu families, test hypotheses about cord groups, and distinguish numerical from possibly nonnumeric features.
Data science can generalize beyond famous individual artifacts.

### What formalization loses or risks

It can treat khipu as inert encodings without khipukamayuq expertise, Andean social organization, colonial rupture, and ritual or administrative context.
It may privilege decipherment over living Andean authority.
A statistically plausible code is not automatically a culturally grounded interpretation.

### Grounding

The Aschers' work is mathematically careful but largely external.
Contemporary khipu research increasingly uses corpus methods, but community-grounded interpretation remains a major frontier.

## 2. Pacific navigation: star compasses, swells, and relational space

### Practice

Pacific navigators have crossed open ocean without modern instruments by using stars, sun, moon, swells, winds, birds, clouds, color of water, and embodied dead reckoning.
The revival of Hōkūleʻa and other voyaging traditions demonstrates that this is living knowledge, not merely historical memory.

### Mathematical structures

- angular orientation and horizon star paths;
- vector-like accumulation of direction, speed, and drift;
- spherical and geodesic reasoning;
- error correction from multiple cues;
- probabilistic inference under uncertainty;
- relational coordinate systems tied to canoe, sea, sky, and islands.

### What formalization gains

Formal models can support teaching, simulation, and comparison with spherical geometry or navigation theory.
They can also show that wayfinding involves sophisticated dynamic inference.

### What formalization loses or risks

A map-and-vector model may erase embodied apprenticeship, chant, genealogy, spiritual relation, and authority of navigators.
Some knowledge may be restricted.
Treating navigation as pre-GPS engineering misses its cultural and political role in Pacific resurgence.

### Grounding

The best work is connected to voyaging communities, Indigenous educators, and cultural practitioners; weak work abstracts the star compass as a technical object alone.

## 3. African fractal architecture and design

### Practice

Ron Eglash documented recursive and fractal-like forms in African settlement plans, architecture, hairstyles, textiles, sculpture, and symbolic systems.
Examples include self-similar arrangements across scales and iterative design procedures.

### Mathematical structures

- recursion and self-similarity;
- scaling relations;
- generative algorithms;
- fractal dimension in some cases;
- nested spatial organization;
- iterative design rules.

### What formalization gains

It reveals that recursion and scaling are not modern computer-era inventions and that African design systems can embody algorithmic sophistication.
It opens connections to computation, architecture, and STEM pedagogy.

### What formalization loses or risks

The phrase "African fractals" can flatten a continent and reduce diverse practices to a single visual property.
Not every recursive-looking form is fractal in the strict sense.
Ritual, social, cosmological, and settlement meanings may matter more than dimension estimates.

### Grounding

Eglash combined mathematical analysis with interviews and cultural interpretation.
Future work needs stronger local authorship and protection against commercial pattern extraction.

## 4. Sona and sand drawings: graph algorithms with stories

### Practice

Among Chokwe and related traditions in Angola and neighboring regions, sona are line drawings made in sand, often around arrays of dots, connected to stories, proverbs, initiation, and social knowledge.
Similar sand-drawing traditions occur elsewhere, including Vanuatu and other Oceanic contexts.

### Mathematical structures

- Eulerian circuits and graph traversal;
- planar curves and knot-like paths;
- symmetry and repetition;
- greatest-common-divisor algorithms for some pattern classes;
- graph embeddings and parity conditions;
- algorithmic generation from dot arrays.

### What formalization gains

Graph theory explains why one-line drawings work, how families can be generated, and which arrays produce closed paths.
It creates rich problems in discrete mathematics and topology.

### What formalization loses or risks

The drawing is not merely a graph.
It is performance, pedagogy, story, memory, and social interaction.
Publishing algorithms without context can detach designs from their narrative and initiatory settings.

### Grounding

Gerdes' work is mathematically deep; recent comparative work continues the formal line.
A frontier is collaboration with living practitioners and careful attention to which drawings are appropriate to publish.

## 5. Kolam: one-stroke ritual drawing, grammars, and data physicalization

### Practice

Kolam are South Indian drawing practices, often made by women at thresholds using rice flour or powder.
Pulli kolam are drawn around dot grids.
They involve daily practice, ritual, aesthetics, hospitality, auspiciousness, ecological relation, and household space.

### Mathematical structures

- Eulerian paths and circuits;
- graph traversal around dot arrays;
- planar symmetry;
- array grammars and formal languages;
- modular constructions;
- local rewrite rules;
- data physicalization through dot, color, line, fill, and pattern mappings.

### What formalization gains

It can generate families of kolam, analyze one-stroke properties, classify designs, and support mathematical learning.
Recent work extends kolam into data representation while trying to preserve cultural meaning.

### What formalization loses or risks

A graph-theoretic kolam may omit ritual temporality, gendered labor, threshold space, materiality, and daily repetition.
A generated kolam can look plausible while being culturally empty.

### Grounding

Older formal-language work by Siromoney and collaborators is foundational.
Newer work should be judged by whether it attends to practitioners and the social life of kolam, not only algorithmic novelty.

## 6. Basketry, weaving, and beadwork: symmetry, constraint, and making

### Practice

Textile and beadwork practices involve patterned production under material constraints.
Indigenous beadwork, Batak weaves, baskets, Navajo weaving, Andean textiles, African strip weaving, Islamic textiles, and many other traditions carry mathematical reasoning in pattern generation, color placement, edge conditions, motif transformation, and embodied making.

### Mathematical structures

- frieze, wallpaper, and layer groups;
- color symmetry;
- grid graphs and adjacency constraints;
- modular arithmetic in repeats;
- linear transformations and pattern vectors;
- braid/knot/textile topology;
- tiling, substitution, and combinatorial design;
- mechanics of flexible materials.

### What formalization gains

It can support design analysis, curriculum, preservation, and new mathematical questions about pattern constraints and fabrication.
Beadwork can make linear algebra tangible through vectors, transformations, bases, and symmetry operations.

### What formalization loses or risks

Motifs may be clan-specific, sacred, market-sensitive, or governed by community protocols.
Static images miss the kinesthetic and social process.
Commercial extraction is a serious risk.

### Grounding

Recent work on Indigenous beadwork in linear algebra is promising because it situates curriculum and collaboration explicitly.
Future work should increase maker authorship and data protection.

## 7. Indigenous seasonal calendars and ecological mathematics

### Practice

Seasonal calendars encode relations among plants, animals, weather, water, fire, ceremony, harvesting, and social responsibilities.
Indigenous groups have co-developed calendars for education, environmental management, and knowledge transmission.

### Mathematical structures

- cyclic time and modular arithmetic;
- multi-cycle synchronization;
- phenological indicators;
- qualitative thresholds;
- temporal logic;
- probabilistic ecological inference;
- networks of species and events.

### What formalization gains

Models can support climate adaptation, education, and comparison of ecological indicators.
They can show that calendars are dynamic predictive systems, not lists of dates.

### What formalization loses or risks

Calendars may become extractive environmental data.
Some knowledge may be restricted.
Climate change may alter indicators in ways that require community-led revision.
A Gregorian table can erase relational time.

### Grounding

Best practice is co-development with language groups and community control over what is shared.

## 8. Islamic geometric design: construction, tiles, and quasi-periodic debates

### Practice

Islamic geometric design includes star polygons, rosettes, interlacing, girih patterns, muqarnas, tilework, manuscript diagrams, architecture, and craft lineages across many regions and centuries.

### Mathematical structures

- straightedge-and-compass construction;
- polygonal design methods;
- tessellations and girih tile sets;
- symmetry groups;
- quasiperiodic order in debated cases;
- proportional systems and radial grids.

### What formalization gains

It can reconstruct design methods, compare manuscripts and buildings, and connect historical craft to geometry and computation.

### What formalization loses or risks

Islamic design is not a single tradition; claims about quasi-periodicity or advanced geometry can be overgeneralized.
Artisans' practical geometry, workshop transmission, theology, patronage, and material constraints matter.

### Grounding

Historical work must distinguish documented construction methods from modern mathematical reconstructions.

## 9. Mancala and sowing games

### Practice

Mancala-family games occur across Africa and the diaspora and beyond, with many local variants.
They involve sowing counters through pits, capturing, planning, and social play.

### Mathematical structures

- modular counting;
- state spaces and transitions;
- invariants;
- combinatorial game theory;
- search algorithms;
- strategy and optimization;
- solvable solitaire variants such as Tchoukaillon.

### What formalization gains

Game analysis provides discrete mathematics problems and classroom entry points.
It reveals strategic depth beyond arithmetic practice.

### What formalization loses or risks

There is no single mancala.
Treating the family as one abstract game erases local rules and social contexts.
Classroom use may detach games from cultural pedagogy.

### Grounding

Good work specifies variants, rule histories, play contexts, and local names.

## 10. Kinship and relational systems

### Practice

Kinship systems classify relations, obligations, marriage rules, inheritance, joking/avoidance relations, clan structures, and social positions.
They are not merely names for biological relations; they are rule-governed social logics.

### Mathematical structures

- directed labeled graphs;
- relation composition;
- equivalence classes and partitions;
- algebraic kinship systems;
- constraints and allowed transformations;
- category-like structures of relation and obligation.

### What formalization gains

Formal models can clarify relation composition and reveal structural constraints.
They can connect anthropology to algebra and logic.

### What formalization loses or risks

Obligation, respect, joking, taboo, land, ceremony, and lived relation can disappear when kinship becomes a graph.
Some relations may not be public.

### Grounding

Models should be built with community authority and should not publish sensitive relational rules.

## 11. Rhythm and modular time

### Practice

Many musical traditions use cyclic timelines, interlocking rhythms, syncopation, call-and-response, dance coordination, and embodied timing.
African and Afro-diasporic rhythms are often studied through modular and Euclidean structures.

### Mathematical structures

- cyclic groups;
- modular arithmetic;
- Euclidean algorithm distributions;
- necklace equivalence classes;
- metric spaces on rhythms;
- syncopation measures;
- geometric distances on circular time.

### What formalization gains

It explains why certain rhythms distribute beats evenly or generate recognizable families.
It supports algorithmic composition and comparative music theory.

### What formalization loses or risks

A timeline is not a culture.
Feel, swing, embodiment, dance, social occasion, and improvisation are not captured by binary onset vectors.

### Grounding

Formal rhythm analysis should be paired with ethnomusicology and practitioner accounts.

## 12. Body-based and limited numeral systems

### Practice

Oksapmin counting maps numbers to a sequence of twenty-seven body parts.
Some communities use small exact numerals, approximate quantity expressions, or one-to-one matching without extensive symbolic numerals.

### Mathematical structures

- ordered finite sequences;
- body-coordinate mappings;
- successor structure without place value;
- one-to-one correspondence;
- exact vs approximate number;
- cognitive technology of numeration.

### What formalization gains

It shows that number is supported by language, body, tools, and practice.
It challenges the assumption that place-value notation is the natural baseline of cognition.

### What formalization loses or risks

Studies of limited numerals have often been used to imply cognitive deficiency.
A careful account distinguishes numeral vocabulary from quantitative competence and examines ecological need.

### Grounding

Cognitive studies should be read with linguistic and anthropological caution.

# VII. Methodology

## 1. Archival research

Archival work uses colonial records, missionary grammars, notebooks, museum catalogs, photographs, diagrams, manuscripts, and old ethnographies.
It is essential where practices were disrupted or suppressed.
It is also risky because archives often misname peoples, mistranslate terms, omit restrictions, and reproduce colonial categories.

Best practice: read archives against the grain, consult descendant communities, mark uncertainty, and do not treat archival visibility as community consent.

## 2. Fieldwork and participant observation

Fieldwork observes practice in context: making, teaching, counting, navigating, playing, farming, singing, trading, building.
Participant observation is especially important for embodied and craft mathematics because finished artifacts rarely reveal the decision process.

Best practice: long-term relation, language work, compensation, community-defined questions, and review before publication.

## 3. Community-based participatory research

CBPR treats community members as co-researchers, not informants.
Questions, methods, outputs, ownership, and dissemination are negotiated.
This is increasingly necessary for Indigenous mathematics research.

Best practice: formal agreements, shared authorship, community review boards, benefit plans, and the right to refuse publication.

## 4. Oral history

Oral history records practitioner memory, teaching lineages, origin stories, technical vocabulary, and changes over time.
It requires protocol because stories may be restricted by season, gender, age, clan, or ceremonial status.

Best practice: record permission levels, allow withdrawal, preserve language, and avoid extracting technical fragments from narrative form.

## 5. Linguistic analysis

Counting words, classifiers, spatial terms, temporal terms, kinship terms, and relational verbs can reveal mathematical structure.
But translation is not neutral.
A term translated as "number," "measure," "time," or "space" may carry different ontological assumptions.

Best practice: collaborate with fluent speakers and language authorities; include morphology, use contexts, and limits of translation.

## 6. Artifact analysis

Artifact analysis studies textiles, baskets, beadwork, architecture, ceramics, tools, records, and games.
It may involve measurement, photography, symmetry classification, material reconstruction, or comparison across collections.

Best practice: provenance research, cultural permissions, maker attribution, repatriation awareness, and caution with museum objects obtained through colonial violence.

## 7. Formal mathematical modeling

Formalization can use graph theory, group theory, topology, algebra, combinatorics, probability, information theory, formal languages, optimization, dynamical systems, geometry, or computation.

Best practice: state what the model captures, what it excludes, who recognizes it, and what authority it has.
Never confuse model fit with cultural truth.

## 8. Curriculum design and classroom study

Educational ethnomathematics develops lessons, units, teacher training, and assessment.
Methods include design-based research, classroom observation, interviews, student work analysis, and longitudinal studies.

Best practice: avoid artifact tokenism, involve communities, assess mathematical depth, and examine identity, belonging, language, and agency.

## 9. Digital and computational reconstruction

Computational work reconstructs patterns, simulates practices, builds databases, trains classifiers, generates designs, or creates interactive tools.

Best practice: provenance metadata, access controls, Local Contexts labels, non-public datasets when needed, community ownership, and AI restrictions.

## 10. Collaborative authorship and community review

A serious ethnomathematics paper should specify who participated, who reviewed, what knowledge was withheld, what benefits return, and how disputes are handled.
Community review is not merely a courtesy; it can be epistemically necessary.

## Methodological tensions

### Translation vs distortion

Translation makes comparison possible but can force local concepts into foreign categories.

### Formalization vs reduction

Formalization reveals structure but can strip practice of meaning.

### Public knowledge vs restricted knowledge

A researcher may be able to describe something but not have the right to publish it.

### Researcher authority vs community authority

Academic credentials do not override community governance.

### Proof vs practice

A proof can certify a property of a model.
It does not certify the cultural interpretation.

### Mathematics as noun vs mathematics as verb

Ethnomathematics often makes more sense when mathematics is treated as activity: counting, designing, navigating, patterning, explaining, coordinating, and remembering.

# VIII. Ethics and politics

## 1. Colonial knowledge extraction

Mathematical knowledge has been extracted through surveying, mapping, navigation, agricultural science, statistics, census-making, linguistics, museum collecting, and schooling.
Ethnomathematics can resist this by recognizing suppressed knowledge, but it can also reproduce extraction by translating cultural practices into publishable structures.

## 2. Intellectual property

Conventional copyright and patent systems often fail to protect collective designs, ceremonial motifs, ecological knowledge, or intergenerational practices.
A community may have rules governing a pattern even if state IP law treats it as public domain.

## 3. Sacred and restricted knowledge

Some knowledge should not be published, modeled, photographed, digitized, or taught outside specific contexts.
This includes some star knowledge, ceremonial designs, kinship rules, place names, songs, and ecological practices.

## 4. Indigenous data sovereignty

CARE, OCAP, and Local Contexts shift data ethics from openness to governance.
A khipu database, beadwork image set, seasonal calendar, or language corpus should include authority, access, use, and restriction metadata.

## 5. Repatriation and digital archives

Digitization can increase access, but it can also intensify dispossession.
Digital repatriation must include control, not merely copies.
Mathematical metadata should not expose restricted structures.

## 6. Citation ethics

Cite communities, makers, elders, language authorities, and local scholars.
Do not cite only the outsider who translated or formalized a practice.
Where knowledge is collectively held, ask how credit should appear.

## 7. Compensation and reciprocity

Payment, co-authorship, equipment, curriculum materials, archival copies, community presentations, and training can be forms of reciprocity.
Reciprocity should be negotiated, not assumed.

## 8. Authorship and community credit

Community co-authors should have real editorial authority.
Some outputs may need collective authorship, institutional authorship, or anonymous attribution for protection.

## 9. When not to publish

Do not publish when the knowledge is restricted, when community review is absent, when publication increases appropriation risk, when the mathematical result is trivial but cultural exposure is high, or when the community asks for refusal.

## 10. Resistance and reproduction

Ethnomathematics resists colonial power when it shifts authority, supports sovereignty, changes pedagogy, protects knowledge, and recognizes living mathematical worlds.
It reproduces colonial power when it mines culture for formal novelty, grants legitimacy only through Western abstraction, or turns community knowledge into open data for institutions.

### D'Ambrosio, Ubiratan.
1985.

# XI. Glossary

**Ethnomathematics** — The study of mathematical practices, ideas, techniques, and ways of knowing as they arise in cultural, social, historical, ecological, material, and political contexts.
Also a pedagogical and decolonial movement.

**Mathematical pluralism** — The view that mathematics may include multiple legitimate structures, practices, logics, or worlds rather than a single monolithic object.

**Decolonial mathematics** — Approaches that examine and resist colonial hierarchies in mathematical knowledge, education, institutions, data, and abstraction.

**Culturally sustaining pedagogy** — An educational approach that sustains and develops students' cultural and linguistic practices rather than merely using culture as a bridge to dominant norms.

**Indigenous knowledge sovereignty** — The authority of Indigenous peoples to govern knowledge, data, protocols, access, interpretation, and circulation connected to their communities, lands, waters, and ancestors.

**Situated knowledge** — Knowledge understood as produced from particular social, historical, material, embodied, and political positions rather than from nowhere.

**Embodied mathematics** — Mathematical reasoning carried by gesture, movement, perception, tool use, rhythm, making, and bodily practice.

**Material mathematics** — Mathematical structure and reasoning embedded in materials and fabrication processes such as weaving, beadwork, basketry, architecture, ceramics, knotting, and carving.

**Recursive design** — Design generated by repeated application of rules, often producing self-similar or nested patterns.

**Pattern logic** — The rule-governed organization of motifs, symmetries, transformations, constraints, and repetitions in a pattern system.

**Formalization** — The translation or reconstruction of a practice into explicit mathematical notation, definitions, models, algorithms, or proofs.

**Extraction** — Taking knowledge, data, images, designs, or practices from communities for external academic, commercial, or institutional benefit without proper authority, reciprocity, or governance.

**Refusal** — A methodological and political stance in which communities or researchers withhold knowledge, reject research terms, or decline publication as an assertion of sovereignty and care.

**Community accountability** — The requirement that research answer to the people whose knowledge, practices, land, or cultural materials are involved, not only to academic peers.

**CARE Principles** — Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics; a framework for Indigenous data governance.

**OCAP** — Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession; First Nations data governance principles.

**Local Contexts Labels** — Digital labels and notices that communicate Indigenous protocols for access, use, attribution, and circulation of traditional knowledge and biocultural data.

**Tokenism** — Superficial inclusion of cultural examples without changing epistemic authority, pedagogy, or community benefit.

**Mathematics as verb** — A phrase used here to emphasize mathematical activity: counting, locating, measuring, designing, playing, explaining, patterning, navigating, encoding, and coordinating action.

# Concluding synthesis

The central lesson of ethnomathematics is not that every culture secretly discovered the same classroom mathematics.
Nor is it that formal mathematics is merely one arbitrary culture among others.
The stronger claim is more demanding: mathematical knowledge is real, powerful, and plural; it is made durable through practices, tools, bodies, languages, institutions, lands, and relations.
Academic formalization can reveal genuine structures, but it is not the only judge of mathematical life.

For mathematicians, ethnomathematics offers new objects, algorithms, constraints, examples, and philosophical problems.
For educators, it offers ways to teach without requiring cultural erasure.
For Indigenous and community scholars, it can be a tool of continuity, sovereignty, and critique, but only when governed by the people whose knowledge is at stake.
For AI and computational researchers, it is a warning: cultural pattern is not free data.

The field's future depends on whether it can hold mathematical rigor and ethical refusal together.
The best ethnomathematics of the next decade will not simply find more hidden algebra in cultural artifacts.
It will build accountable forms of mathematical research in which communities decide what may be known, mathematicians say precisely what their models do and do not capture, and education becomes a site not of assimilation but of plural mathematical futures.
