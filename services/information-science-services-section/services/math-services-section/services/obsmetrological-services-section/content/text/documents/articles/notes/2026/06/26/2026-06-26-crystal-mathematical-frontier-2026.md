# The Mathematical Frontier of Crystals

## Orientation

A crystal is no longer best treated as merely a visually regular solid. At the mathematical frontier, a crystal is a meeting point of at least five structures:

1. a **locally finite geometric measure** in physical space, usually a decorated periodic set or a thermodynamic phase close to one;
2. a **group-theoretic object** whose Euclidean symmetry group contains a lattice of translations with finite quotient;
3. a **Fourier/diffraction object**, whose autocorrelation has a Fourier transform with distinguished Bragg peaks;
4. a **dynamical/statistical object**, namely a Gibbs, ground-state, or nonequilibrium measure with broken continuous translation symmetry;
5. a **spectral-topological object**, where the periodicity of the atomic background turns differential or tight-binding operators into Bloch bundles over a torus or an orbifold quotient of the Brillouin zone.

The older crystallographic definition emphasizes a periodic array of atoms. The modern IUCr definition is intentionally diffraction-based: a crystal is a solid having an essentially discrete diffraction diagram, while an aperiodic crystal has sharp diffraction but no three-dimensional lattice periodicity [@iucr-crystal-2021; @iucr-aperiodic-2024]. This broadened definition matters: it admits incommensurately modulated phases and quasicrystals, but it also forces mathematicians to distinguish **periodicity**, **pure point diffraction**, **long-range order**, and **thermodynamic stability** as distinct predicates.

This memo surveys the formal core, canonical models, rigorous theorems, computational methods, 2020--2026 directions, and open problems. It is intentionally research-facing rather than pedagogical: I state idealizations, assumptions, and places where the literature remains physically motivated rather than theorem-level.

## 1. Core mathematical object

### 1.1 Periodic crystal as a decorated lattice

Let $d\in\{2,3\}$ for physical crystals, though most formalism extends to arbitrary dimension. A **lattice** in $\mathbb R^d$ is a discrete co-compact subgroup
\[
\Lambda = A\mathbb Z^d,\qquad A\in GL(d,\mathbb R).
\]
Its **fundamental cell** or **unit cell** may be taken as
\[
C_A=\{A t: t\in[0,1)^d\},
\]
with volume $|\det A|$. A periodic atomic crystal with species labels and scattering weights can be modeled as a translation-bounded measure
\[
\omega = \sum_{R\in\Lambda}\sum_{j=1}^m b_j\,\delta_{R+x_j}
      = \delta_{\Lambda} * \mu_0,
\qquad \mu_0=\sum_{j=1}^m b_j\delta_{x_j},
\]
where $x_j\in C_A$ are basis positions and $b_j$ are form factors or scattering lengths. The set $M=\{x_1,\dots,x_m\}$ is the **motif** or **basis**. A Bravais lattice is the special case $m=1$ with no nontrivial motif, up to physical labels [@hahn-2005-ita; @muller-2013-math-crystallography; @ashcroft-mermin-1976].

This construction captures perfect translational order. It omits thermal displacements, occupational disorder, local defects, quantum nuclei, finite size, surfaces, twinning, and growth history. Much of modern crystal mathematics studies how far one can perturb this ideal object and still retain meaningful order, spectra, topology, or elasticity.

### 1.2 Symmetry group: point, space, and Bravais structure

The Euclidean group is $E(d)=\mathbb R^d\rtimes O(d)$. A **space group** is a discrete subgroup $G\subset E(d)$ with compact quotient $\mathbb R^d/G$. For a periodic crystal measure $\omega$, its exact symmetry group is
\[
G_\omega=\{g\in E(d): g_*\omega=\omega\}.
\]
The subgroup of pure translations
\[
T_G=G\cap\mathbb R^d
\]
is a lattice when $G$ is crystallographic. The quotient
\[
P_G=G/T_G
\]
is finite and is called the **point group**. Thus crystallographic symmetry fits into
\[
0\longrightarrow \Lambda \longrightarrow G \longrightarrow P \longrightarrow 1.
\]
In three dimensions there are 14 Bravais lattice types, 32 crystallographic point groups, and 230 space-group types; in two dimensions there are 5 Bravais lattice types, 10 crystallographic point groups, and 17 wallpaper groups [@hahn-2005-ita; @muller-2013-math-crystallography]. The classification is group-theoretic but constrained by geometry: point-group rotations preserving a lattice can have only orders $1,2,3,4,6$ in dimensions two and three.

### 1.3 Reciprocal lattice and diffraction

The **reciprocal lattice** of $\Lambda=A\mathbb Z^d$ is
\[
\Lambda^*=\{k\in\mathbb R^d: k\cdot R\in 2\pi\mathbb Z\text{ for all }R\in\Lambda\}
       =2\pi A^{-T}\mathbb Z^d.
\]
For the ideal measure $\omega=\delta_\Lambda *\mu_0$, the autocorrelation is
\[
\gamma_\omega=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\omega|_{B_n}*\widetilde{\omega|_{B_n}}}{\operatorname{vol}(B_n)},
\]
when the volume-averaged limit exists. Here $\widetilde\mu(f)=\overline{\mu(\widetilde f)}$ with $\widetilde f(x)=\overline{f(-x)}$. Its Fourier transform $\widehat\gamma_\omega$ is the **diffraction measure**. For a lattice comb, Poisson summation gives
\[
\widehat{\delta_\Lambda}=\operatorname{dens}(\Lambda)\,\delta_{\Lambda^*}
\]
with a convention-dependent $2\pi$ normalization; the decorated motif multiplies Bragg intensities by the structure factor
\[
S(k)=\left|\sum_{j=1}^m b_j e^{-i k\cdot x_j}\right|^2.
\]
Thus ideal periodic crystals have pure point diffraction supported on $\Lambda^*$, with systematic absences and intensities controlled by motif and symmetry [@baake-grimm-2012-diffraction; @richard-2017].

The physically observed diffraction pattern is not the Fourier transform of positions alone. It also contains atomic form factors, thermal Debye--Waller attenuation, finite-size broadening, mosaicity, strain, diffuse scattering from correlations, instrument resolution, and phase-loss constraints. The mathematical core nevertheless remains: periodicity in direct space becomes discreteness in reciprocal space.

### 1.4 Unit cells, primitive cells, conventional cells

A **primitive cell** has volume equal to the covolume of the translation lattice and contains one lattice point. A **conventional cell** may be larger but exhibits more of the point symmetry; e.g. face-centered cubic and body-centered cubic conventional cells contain multiple lattice points. Choice of cell is a gauge-like redundancy: the physical periodic set is invariant under unimodular changes $A\mapsto AU$, $U\in GL(d,\mathbb Z)$, plus origin shifts and basis transformations. Computational crystallography must quotient by these redundancies, a nontrivial source of numerical ambiguity in structure matching and generative models.

### 1.5 Correlation, order, and constraints

Several non-equivalent mathematical notions of order are used:

- **Long-range translational order:** non-decaying correlations of density Fourier components $\rho_G$ at reciprocal vectors $G$.
- **Orientational order:** nonzero expectation of point-group-sensitive tensors or bond-order parameters.
- **Pure point diffraction:** Bragg peaks in $\widehat\gamma$; this is stronger than local motif regularity but does not always imply periodicity.
- **Finite local complexity:** for uniformly discrete point sets, finitely many patches up to translation in every bounded radius.
- **Dynamical pure point spectrum:** pure point spectrum of the translation action on the hull of the structure; for many uniquely ergodic Delone systems, this is deeply related to pure point diffraction [@dworkin-1993; @lenz-2009-diffraction; @baake-lenz-2017].
- **Topological order/band topology:** invariants of occupied Bloch bands or many-body ground states; this is not equivalent to geometric crystallinity.
- **Mechanical rigidity/order:** positivity of elastic moduli, phonon stability, absence of floppy modes, or constraints encoded by rigidity matrices.

Crystalline matter is therefore not defined by one scalar order parameter. It is a nexus of constraints: metric discreteness, group symmetry, correlation decay or non-decay, reciprocal-space singularity, spectral band structure, and thermodynamic stability.

### 1.6 Crystals versus quasicrystals, glasses, liquids, gases, and plasmas

A periodic crystal has a rank-$d$ translation lattice in physical space. A quasicrystal has sharp diffraction but generally no rank-$d$ lattice of translations; its Bragg peaks often lie on a finitely generated Fourier module of rank greater than $d$, as in cut-and-project sets [@baake-grimm-2013; @iucr-aperiodic-2024]. A glass has no exact long-range periodicity and typically shows diffuse diffraction with short- and medium-range order; modern local-structure mathematics uses pair distribution functions, persistent homology, and network topology [@terban-2021-pdf; @onodera-2020-glass-topology]. A liquid has translation-invariant equilibrium statistics but no static lattice; its structure is encoded in correlation functions such as $g(r)$ and $S(k)$, with many-body correlations becoming essential in dense regimes [@pihlajamaa-2023-liquids]. Gases are dilute kinetic systems, and plasmas are charged kinetic-field systems with collective screening and long-range electromagnetic coupling rather than static crystalline order.

## 2. Foundational models

The models below are not mutually exclusive. A single research problem may require crystallographic group theory for symmetry, DFT for energetics, phonons for stability, $K$-theory for band topology, and stochastic dynamics for defects.

### 2.1 Decorated-lattice model

**Object.** A periodic measure or labeled Delone set
\[
X=\bigcup_{j=1}^m (x_j+\Lambda),\qquad \omega=\sum_{x\in X}b(x)\delta_x.
\]

**Assumptions.** Infinite, perfect periodic order; fixed sites; no thermal motion; exact species occupancy.

**Captures.** Space-group symmetry, Bravais lattice, reciprocal lattice, Bragg peak locations, systematic absences, unit-cell equivalence, and many crystallographic classification questions.

**Fails.** Thermal diffuse scattering, disorder, defects, surfaces, incommensurability, nonlinear elasticity, metastability, and growth kinetics.

This is the base model behind the International Tables, symmetry databases, structure refinement, and much of computational materials representation [@hahn-2005-ita; @aroyo-2006-bilbao; @de-la-flor-2021-bz; @togo-2024-spglib].

### 2.2 Crystallographic groups and quotient geometry

**Object.** A space group $G\subset E(d)$ acting properly discontinuously and co-compactly on $\mathbb R^d$. The crystal is a decorated orbifold quotient $\mathbb R^d/G$ plus Wyckoff-position data.

**Equations and structure.** Each $g\in G$ has form $x\mapsto Qx+t$, with $Q\in O(d)$ and $t\in\mathbb R^d$. The point group $P$ acts on $\Lambda$ by automorphisms, giving $P\subset GL(d,\mathbb Z)$ in a lattice basis. Wyckoff positions are orbits stratified by site-symmetry groups.

**Captures.** Classification, symmetry constraints on tensors, selection rules, band irreducible representations, and symmetry-aware generative design.

**Fails.** It is kinematic rather than energetic. It says which structures are symmetry-allowed, not whether they are stable, synthesizable, or dynamically reachable.

### 2.3 Lattice spin, lattice gas, and configurational models

A lattice spin model assigns variables $s_i$ to sites $i\in\Lambda$ or to finite basis sites. Canonical examples are Ising, Potts, clock, lattice-gas, and cluster-expansion models:
\[
H(s)=-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}J_{ij}s_is_j-h\sum_i s_i,
\]
or, for alloys,
\[
E(\sigma)=J_0+\sum_\alpha J_\alpha \Phi_\alpha(\sigma),
\]
where $\Phi_\alpha$ are cluster functions. The Gibbs measure is
\[
\mu_{\beta,\Lambda_N}(s)=Z_N^{-1}e^{-\beta H_N(s)}.
\]

**Captures.** Phase transitions, symmetry breaking, occupational disorder, alloy ordering, adsorbate order, and simplified defect statistics.

**Fails.** Off-lattice relaxation, electronic structure, phonons unless added, and realistic many-body forces. Lattice models can also hide the mathematical difficulty of proving that the lattice itself emerges from continuum interacting particles.

### 2.4 Periodic potentials and Bloch Hamiltonians

An electron in a periodic background is modeled by
\[
H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Delta+V(x),\qquad V(x+R)=V(x),\ R\in\Lambda.
\]
Bloch theory decomposes $H$ as a direct integral over the Brillouin torus $\mathbb T^d=\mathbb R^d/\Lambda^*$:
\[
H\cong \int_{\mathrm{BZ}}^{\oplus} H(k)\,dk,
\]
where $H(k)$ acts on periodic functions with quasi-periodic boundary condition $\psi_k(x+R)=e^{ik\cdot R}\psi_k(x)$ [@kuchment-1993; @kuchment-2016]. The spectrum is a union of bands
\[
\sigma(H)=\bigcup_{n\ge 1}\{E_n(k): k\in\mathrm{BZ}\}.
\]

**Captures.** Electronic band structure, spectral gaps, effective mass, Fermi surfaces, and Bloch bundles.

**Fails.** Strong correlation, finite-temperature electron-phonon coupling, disorder localization, nonadiabatic nuclear motion, and exchange-correlation exactness unless embedded in more sophisticated many-body or DFT models.

### 2.5 Density functional theory and periodic Kohn--Sham models

DFT reduces the interacting electronic ground-state problem to a variational problem over electron density $\rho$; Kohn--Sham DFT replaces it by self-consistent one-particle equations with exchange-correlation approximation [@hohenberg-kohn-1964; @kohn-sham-1965; @jones-2015-dft]. In a periodic cell one solves
\[
\left[-\frac12(\nabla+ik)^2+V_{\mathrm{ion}}+V_H[\rho]+V_{xc}[\rho]\right]u_{n,k}=\varepsilon_{n,k}u_{n,k}.
\]

**Captures.** Formation energies, relaxed structures, elastic tensors, phonons via force constants, and high-throughput screening.

**Fails.** Exact many-body correlations, dispersion without corrections, finite-temperature kinetics, strongly correlated materials without special functionals, metastable synthesis pathways, and sometimes band gaps. Mathematically, most predictive DFT workflows rest on controlled numerical practice rather than fully rigorous error bars for all approximations.

### 2.6 Harmonic crystal and phonon models

Let $u_{\kappa}(R)$ be displacement of basis atom $\kappa$ in cell $R$. Expanding potential energy around equilibrium gives
\[
U=U_0+\frac12\sum_{R,R'}\sum_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}
\Phi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(R-R')u_{\kappa\alpha}(R)u_{\kappa'\beta}(R')+O(u^3).
\]
The equations of motion become
\[
M_\kappa \ddot u_{\kappa\alpha}(R)=
-\sum_{R',\kappa',\beta}\Phi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(R-R')u_{\kappa'\beta}(R').
\]
Using $u_{\kappa}(R,t)=e^{i(q\cdot R-\omega t)}e_\kappa(q)$ yields the dynamical matrix eigenproblem
\[
D(q)e(q)=\omega^2(q)e(q).
\]
The acoustic sum rule enforces zero-frequency translational modes at $q=0$ [@born-huang-1954; @togo-2023-phonopy].

**Captures.** Lattice vibrations, elastic constants at long wavelength, thermodynamic free energy in harmonic/quasi-harmonic approximations, neutron/X-ray inelastic scattering, and dynamical stability.

**Fails.** Strong anharmonicity, melting, diffusion, plastic flow, thermal expansion beyond quasi-harmonic approximation, and phonon-phonon scattering unless higher-order force constants are included.

### 2.7 Anharmonic and statistical lattice dynamics

With cubic and quartic force constants,
\[
U=U_0+\frac12\Phi^{(2)}u^2+\frac1{3!}\Phi^{(3)}u^3+\frac1{4!}\Phi^{(4)}u^4+\cdots,
\]
one obtains phonon lifetimes, thermal conductivity, nonlinear response, and temperature-dependent renormalization. Mathematical models include stochastic lattice dynamics, Green--Kubo formulas, Boltzmann--Peierls phonon transport, and nonlinear oscillator chains. Rigorous derivations of macroscopic Fourier heat conduction from deterministic anharmonic crystals remain limited and model-dependent.

### 2.8 Elasticity and Cauchy--Born models

Let $y_i$ be atom positions in a deformed lattice. A pair or many-body atomistic energy may be written
\[
E_\varepsilon(y)=\varepsilon^d\sum_i V\left(\left\{\frac{y_j-y_i}{\varepsilon}:j\in\mathcal N(i)\right\}\right).
\]
The Cauchy--Born rule posits that under a smooth deformation $y(x)$, local atomic neighborhoods deform by the deformation gradient $F=\nabla y$, giving a continuum energy
\[
E(y)=\int_\Omega W(\nabla y(x))\,dx,
\qquad W(F)=\frac1{\operatorname{vol}C}\,V(F\mathcal N).
\]

**Captures.** Long-wavelength strain energy, acoustic phonons, elastic tensors, and mechanical stability [@landau-lifshitz-elasticity-1986].

**Fails.** Defect cores, fracture, plasticity, microstructure, phase transformations, nonlocality, and instabilities where Cauchy--Born is invalid. Rigorous atomistic-to-continuum limits require hypotheses on stability, interactions, boundary conditions, and scaling [@blanc-2007; @blanc-2010-cauchy-born; @schmidt-2009; @friesecke-james-muller-2002].

### 2.9 Defect models

Defects are controlled failures of perfect periodicity:

- **Vacancy/interstitial/substitutional defect:** local change in occupation or species.
- **Dislocation:** line defect with Burgers vector
  \[
  b=\oint_\Gamma F^p\,dx
  \]
  or, in continuum notation, dislocation density $\alpha=\operatorname{curl}\beta^p$.
- **Disclination:** rotational mismatch with Frank vector or curvature-like measure.
- **Grain boundary:** interface between regions with different orientation, often modeled as an array of dislocations at low angle.

Geometric theories model dislocations via torsion and disclinations via curvature in a Riemann--Cartan or related geometry [@katanaev-2005-defects; @yavari-goriely-2012; @kobayashi-2025-volterra]. Variational theories derive continuum plastic energies from discrete dislocation ensembles in specific asymptotic regimes [@garroni-2008-dislocations]. Atomistic defect simulation requires boundary truncation and far-field correction; rigorous error estimates exist under restrictive conditions [@ortner-shapeev-2016-defects].

## 3. Geometry and symmetry

### 3.1 Bravais lattices and metric classification

A Bravais lattice is an orbit $\Lambda=A\mathbb Z^d$ under translations. Classification up to Euclidean isometry is classification of positive-definite Gram matrices
\[
G=A^TA
\]
modulo $G\mapsto U^TGU$, $U\in GL(d,\mathbb Z)$. Crystallographic types further stratify lattices by holohedry, i.e. the finite point group preserving the lattice. In $d=3$, the 14 Bravais lattices are distributed among seven crystal systems: triclinic, monoclinic, orthorhombic, tetragonal, trigonal/rhombohedral, hexagonal, and cubic [@hahn-2005-ita; @muller-2013-math-crystallography].

The moduli space viewpoint is useful for modern computation. A lattice is not a raw matrix $A$ but an equivalence class under rotations, scaling conventions, and $GL(d,\mathbb Z)$ basis changes. Machine-learning models that generate lattice parameters without quotient awareness can learn coordinate artifacts rather than geometry.

### 3.2 Point groups and the crystallographic restriction

A point group $P\subset O(d)$ is crystallographic if it preserves a lattice: $Q\Lambda=\Lambda$. In a lattice basis, $Q$ is an integral matrix. If $Q$ has finite order $n$, its eigenvalues are roots of unity and its characteristic polynomial has integer coefficients. In dimensions two and three this restricts rotation orders to $1,2,3,4,6$. This is the crystallographic restriction theorem, a concise bridge between algebraic number theory and physical symmetry [@muller-2013-math-crystallography].

Quasicrystals evade this theorem because they are not periodic lattices in physical space; fivefold, eightfold, tenfold, and twelvefold diffraction symmetries can occur through higher-dimensional periodicity or inflation symmetries projected to physical space [@baake-grimm-2013; @baake-grimm-2020-inflation].

### 3.3 Space groups, nonsymmorphicity, and Wyckoff positions

A space group element is $(Q,t)$ with composition
\[
(Q,t)(Q',t')=(QQ',Qt'+t).
\]
A group is **symmorphic** if it is a semidirect product $\Lambda\rtimes P$ after a choice of origin. It is **nonsymmorphic** if it contains glide planes or screw axes whose fractional translations cannot be removed by shifting the origin. Nonsymmorphic symmetries force band degeneracies, sticking, and compatibility constraints in reciprocal space; they are central to modern topological-materials classification [@po-vishwanath-watanabe-2017; @bradlyn-2017-tqc; @wieder-2022-discovery].

Wyckoff positions partition the unit cell into orbits with common site-symmetry type. They are the natural discrete strata for crystal generation, substitution, and elementary band representations. A crystal structure can often be represented compactly by its space group, lattice parameters, occupied Wyckoff letters, and internal coordinates.

### 3.4 Wallpaper groups and layer groups

Two-dimensional periodic patterns have 17 wallpaper groups. They serve as exactly solvable laboratories for symmetry, orbifold notation, tilings, and 2D materials. Layer groups and rod groups interpolate between 2D periodicity embedded in 3D and lower-dimensional periodic structures; modern Brillouin-zone and irreducible-representation databases now include layer-group data relevant to van der Waals materials and surfaces [@de-la-flor-2021-bz].

### 3.5 Reciprocal lattices and Brillouin zones

The first Brillouin zone is the Voronoi cell of $0$ in reciprocal space:
\[
\mathrm{BZ}=\{k\in\mathbb R^d: |k|\le |k-G|\text{ for all }G\in\Lambda^*\}.
\]
As a domain it is not unique; as a torus $\mathbb R^d/\Lambda^*$ it is canonical. High-symmetry points, lines, and planes are stabilizer strata of the reciprocal-space action of the point group. The Bilbao Crystallographic Server provides standardized Brillouin-zone paths, $k$-vector tables, irreducible representations, and compatibility data used in band topology and computational materials science [@aroyo-2006-bilbao; @de-la-flor-2021-bz].

### 3.6 Bloch theory and symmetry constraints

If $H$ commutes with lattice translations, eigenfunctions decompose into irreducible characters of $\Lambda$:
\[
\psi_{n,k}(x)=e^{ik\cdot x}u_{n,k}(x),\qquad u_{n,k}(x+R)=u_{n,k}(x).
\]
Space-group symmetries act on fibers $H(k)$ by little groups
\[
G_k=\{g\in G: gk\equiv k\pmod{\Lambda^*}\}.
\]
Band eigenstates at high-symmetry momenta transform under irreducible representations of $G_k$. Compatibility relations along lines and planes constrain which irreps can connect into isolated band groups [@bradlyn-2017-tqc; @cano-2018-ebr; @wieder-2022-discovery].

Symmetry also constrains macroscopic tensors. Neumann's principle states that the symmetry group of any physical property tensor contains the point group of the crystal. Thus elasticity, piezoelectricity, dielectric response, conductivity, and optical tensors are reduced by group representation theory [@nye-1985].

### 3.7 Broken symmetry

A homogeneous liquid equilibrium measure is invariant under continuous translations and rotations. A crystal phase breaks this continuous Euclidean symmetry to a discrete space group. The broken translations produce acoustic Goldstone modes, i.e. phonons, in the long-wavelength limit. In finite systems and in two dimensions at finite temperature, this statement must be interpreted with care: true long-range translational order can be destroyed by fluctuations, leaving quasi-long-range order and algebraic Bragg peaks in idealized 2D models [@mermin-wagner-1966; @halperin-nelson-1978; @nelson-halperin-1979; @young-1979].

## 4. Statistical mechanics and dynamics

### 4.1 Equilibrium measures and order parameters

For $N$ interacting particles with positions $q_i$ and momenta $p_i$, the classical canonical partition function is
\[
Z_N(\beta,V)=\frac1{N!h^{dN}}\int_{V^N}\int_{\mathbb R^{dN}}e^{-\beta H(p,q)}\,dp\,dq.
\]
Crystallization would mean that an infinite-volume Gibbs state has density correlations reflecting a lattice. A density order-parameter expansion writes
\[
\rho(x)=\rho_0+\sum_{G\in\Lambda^*\setminus\{0\}}\rho_G e^{iG\cdot x},
\]
where nonzero $\rho_G$ indicate broken translational symmetry. Landau theory then studies invariant polynomials in the complex amplitudes $\rho_G$ under the liquid symmetry group [@chaikin-lubensky-1995].

For a finite configuration $\{r_j\}_{j=1}^N$, the static structure factor is
\[
S(k)=\frac1N\left\langle\left|\sum_{j=1}^N e^{-ik\cdot r_j}\right|^2\right\rangle.
\]
In a perfect crystal $S(k)$ has Bragg peaks at reciprocal vectors. In liquids and glasses it is mostly continuous, with peaks indicating preferred length scales but not exact periodic order.

### 4.2 Stability and thermodynamic phases

Thermodynamic stability has two layers. First, **stability of matter** asks for a lower bound $E_N\ge -CN$ and existence of a thermodynamic limit. For Coulomb quantum matter these are deep theorems of Dyson--Lenard, Lieb--Thirring, Lieb--Lebowitz, and successors [@lieb-lebowitz-1972; @lieb-1976-stability; @lieb-seiringer-2010]. Second, **crystalline phase stability** asks whether the minimizing or equilibrium state actually has periodic order. This is much harder. Rigorous crystallization has been proved only for special potentials, low dimensions, or restricted settings; realistic three-dimensional Coulomb crystallization from first principles is not a solved theorem.

### 4.3 Harmonic thermodynamics

In the harmonic approximation, independent phonon modes give quantum free energy
\[
F(T)=E_0+\sum_{q,\nu}\left[\frac12\hbar\omega_{\nu}(q)+k_BT\log\left(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_\nu(q)}\right)\right].
\]
Classically, each quadratic mode contributes $k_BT$ to energy. The Debye model replaces the acoustic phonon density of states by a continuum approximation. These models explain heat capacity and low-temperature thermodynamics but miss strong anharmonicity and defect-mediated processes [@born-huang-1954; @ashcroft-mermin-1976].

### 4.4 Anharmonicity, transport, and nonequilibrium

Anharmonic terms produce finite phonon lifetimes and thermal resistance. The Green--Kubo formula expresses thermal conductivity as an integrated heat-current autocorrelation:
\[
\kappa_{ij}=\frac{1}{k_BT^2V}\int_0^\infty \langle J_i(t)J_j(0)\rangle\,dt.
\]
The phonon Boltzmann equation approximates mode occupations $n_{q\nu}$ by scattering integrals computed from higher-order force constants. Its mathematical status is asymptotic and delicate: one needs weak nonlinearity, separation of time scales, and control of resonances. Rigorous nonequilibrium statistical mechanics of anharmonic crystals remains a frontier.

### 4.5 Defects in statistical mechanics

Defects have entropic and energetic roles. A vacancy concentration satisfies approximately
\[
c_v\sim \exp[-\beta G_f]
\]
where $G_f$ is vacancy formation free energy. Dislocations in 2D can unbind through a Kosterlitz--Thouless-type mechanism. The KTHNY scenario predicts a solid-to-hexatic transition by dislocation unbinding and a hexatic-to-liquid transition by disclination unbinding [@kosterlitz-thouless-1973; @halperin-nelson-1978; @nelson-halperin-1979; @young-1979]. Simulations and experiments reveal multiple pathways; for example, hard disks show a first-order liquid--hexatic transition with a continuous hexatic--solid transition, while recent studies continue to find one-step or protocol-dependent melting in related 2D systems [@bernard-krauth-2011; @zhu-2025-melting; @galliano-2023-nonequilibrium-crystals].

### 4.6 Melting and why it is difficult

Melting is hard mathematically because it joins long-range order, topological defects, entropy, metastability, hydrodynamics, and finite-size effects. A rigorous theory would need to identify infinite-volume Gibbs states, prove symmetry breaking at low temperature, prove loss of crystalline order at high temperature, and characterize transition order. For continuum particles in 3D with realistic interactions, this remains beyond current methods. Existing rigorous results often concern special potentials, lattice approximations, or perturbative low-temperature expansions.

## 5. Topology and modern mathematical structure

### 5.1 Bloch bundles and Berry geometry

For an insulator with occupied Bloch eigenstates $\{|u_{n,k}\rangle\}_{n=1}^N$, the occupied subspaces define a vector bundle $E\to\mathbb T^d$ over the Brillouin torus. The Berry connection and curvature are
\[
\mathcal A=-i\,P(k)dP(k),\qquad \mathcal F=d\mathcal A+i\mathcal A\wedge\mathcal A,
\]
where $P(k)$ is the occupied-band projector. In two dimensions the first Chern number is
\[
C_1(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb T^2}\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal F\in\mathbb Z.
\]
This integer controls quantized Hall conductance in noninteracting band theory and survives perturbations preserving the spectral gap.

### 5.2 Topological crystalline phases

A topological crystalline phase is protected or constrained by crystalline symmetry, not merely by internal symmetries such as time reversal or charge conservation. Fu's mirror-symmetry construction introduced topological crystalline insulators with mirror Chern numbers [@fu-2011-tci], and SnTe-class materials provided a canonical early materials realization [@hsieh-2012-snte].

Mathematically, a space group $G$ acts on $\mathbb T^d$ and on Bloch fibers. Classification problems become equivariant: vector bundles, projective representations, twists from spin-orbit coupling and magnetic symmetry, and stable equivalence under adding atomic bands all enter. For noninteracting fermions, equivariant $K$-theory and related spectral-sequence methods give classification frameworks, though mapping these invariants to experimentally accessible symmetry eigenvalues is another layer [@stehouwer-2018-k-theory; @kruthoff-2017].

### 5.3 Symmetry indicators and topological quantum chemistry

Topological quantum chemistry (TQC) compares bands induced from localized orbitals at Wyckoff positions with actual band representations. An **elementary band representation** (EBR) is induced from an irreducible representation of a site-symmetry group. If an isolated set of bands cannot be expressed as a positive sum of EBRs, or decomposes in a way incompatible with symmetric localized Wannier functions, it is topological or fragile depending on the precise obstruction [@bradlyn-2017-tqc; @cano-2018-ebr].

Symmetry indicators compress this into finite abelian groups derived from irreducible-representation multiplicities at high-symmetry momenta. Po, Vishwanath, and Watanabe constructed indicators for all 230 space groups [@po-vishwanath-watanabe-2017]. Large-scale catalogues then used symmetry data and DFT to classify candidate topological materials [@vergniory-2019-catalogue; @tang-2019-natphys; @wieder-2022-discovery]. Magnetic topological quantum chemistry extends this to magnetic space groups; Elcoro et al. treat the 1651 magnetic space groups [@elcoro-2021-mtqc].

The mathematical warning is important: symmetry indicators are neither the whole classification nor a direct measurement of all invariants. They can miss topological phases invisible to symmetry eigenvalues and can diagnose only stable or symmetry-detectable obstructions under specified assumptions.

### 5.4 Higher-order topology and boundary stratification

Higher-order topological phases have protected modes on boundaries of codimension greater than one: hinges in 3D, corners in 2D, etc. The real-space topological-crystal approach builds phases by decorating cells of a spatial decomposition with lower-dimensional topological states subject to symmetry and anomaly cancellation [@song-2019-topological-crystals; @song-2020-realspace]. This is mathematically close to equivariant obstruction theory: one assembles local data on cells and checks extension conditions. Higher-order topological insulators and superconductors are now a large subfield, but the classification depends sensitively on interactions, crystalline symmetry, boundary termination, and disorder [@schindler-2018-hoti; @yang-2024-hoti-review].

### 5.5 Topological defects

Crystalline defects are topological in a precise homotopy/geometric sense. A dislocation is detected by a Burgers circuit: walking around a defect line fails to close by $b\in\Lambda$. A disclination is detected by rotational holonomy. In continuum geometry, translational holonomy corresponds to torsion and rotational holonomy to curvature [@katanaev-2005-defects; @yavari-goriely-2012; @kobayashi-2025-volterra].

Defects also interact with band topology. Dislocations in weak topological insulators can bind protected modes when the Burgers vector pairs nontrivially with reciprocal topological indices. More generally, crystalline gauge fields couple elastic deformations to electronic topology. This is a genuinely mathematical use of topology, not a metaphor: it involves homotopy classes, characteristic classes, holonomy, and index-like protected boundary states.

### 5.6 What is not topology

Several uses of “topological” in materials writing are loose. A network may have a “topology” meaning graph connectivity; a microstructure may have a “topology” meaning morphology; an optimization may perform “topology optimization” of shape. These may be mathematically meaningful, but they are distinct from topological phases classified by invariants stable under gapped homotopy. A research memo should keep these notions separate.

## 6. Rigorous mathematical results

### 6.1 Crystallographic classification

The classification of crystallographic groups is one of the cleanest theorem-level parts of crystal mathematics. A crystallographic group is a discrete co-compact subgroup of $E(d)$. Bieberbach's theorems imply, among other things, that such a group contains a maximal translation lattice of finite index and that in each dimension there are finitely many affine-equivalence classes. In dimension 2 this gives 17 wallpaper groups; in dimension 3 it gives 230 space groups [@hahn-2005-ita; @muller-2013-math-crystallography].

What is proved: the group classification, Wyckoff-position structure, point-group restrictions, reciprocal-space symmetry classification, and representation-theoretic tables. What is not proved by crystallography alone: existence of a stable material realizing each group under given interactions.

### 6.2 Diffraction theory

For a lattice comb, Poisson summation gives pure point diffraction on the reciprocal lattice. For more general Delone sets and measures, diffraction is formulated through autocorrelation and Fourier transform of measures. The theory distinguishes pure point, singular continuous, and absolutely continuous diffraction [@baake-grimm-2012-diffraction; @baake-grimm-2013].

Aperiodic order has rigorous classes with pure point diffraction, especially regular model sets from cut-and-project schemes. Dworkin-type arguments connect diffraction to dynamical spectrum under ergodicity assumptions [@dworkin-1993; @lenz-2009-diffraction; @baake-lenz-2017]. This area is mathematically mature but still has open inverse problems: diffraction does not uniquely determine structure, and homometric structures can share autocorrelations.

### 6.3 Spectral theory of periodic operators

For elliptic periodic operators, Floquet--Bloch theory gives a direct integral decomposition over quasi-momentum. Band functions are analytic away from crossings, spectra have band structure, and many operators have absolutely continuous spectrum [@kuchment-1993; @kuchment-2016]. Spectral gaps, effective masses, edge states, and Fermi surfaces are central. The rigorous literature addresses integrated density of states, perturbation by defects, photonic crystals, quantum graphs, and inverse spectral questions.

What remains difficult includes: proving or excluding spectral gaps in high-dimensional periodic operators, controlling band crossings under symmetry, handling interacting electrons beyond mean-field, and deriving topological response coefficients for realistic disordered interacting crystals.

### 6.4 Stability of matter and thermodynamic limits

Theorems on stability of matter prove that quantum Coulomb systems have energy bounded below linearly in particle number and have a thermodynamic limit under suitable neutrality and boundary assumptions [@lieb-lebowitz-1972; @lieb-1976-stability; @lieb-seiringer-2010]. Mean-field models such as Hartree--Fock and reduced Hartree--Fock also have thermodynamic-limit and defect theories for crystals [@catto-2001; @cances-2008-defects; @hainzl-lewin-solovej-2008].

These results justify that macroscopic matter is stable. They do not generally prove that the thermodynamic minimizer is a periodic crystal of a prescribed space group. That “crystallization problem” remains one of the most important gaps between mathematical physics and materials reality.

### 6.5 Rigidity, elasticity, and homogenization

The Friesecke--James--Müller geometric rigidity theorem controls maps whose gradients are close to rotations and underlies rigorous derivations of plate and shell theories [@friesecke-james-muller-2002]. Atomistic-to-continuum results use $\Gamma$-convergence, discrete-to-continuum compactness, and stability hypotheses to derive nonlinear elasticity and Cauchy--Born energy densities [@blanc-2007; @blanc-2010-cauchy-born; @schmidt-2009].

Elastic stability criteria reduce positivity of strain energy to inequalities on elastic constants, with symmetry reducing the number of independent constants. Standard criteria for crystal systems were clarified by Mouhat and Coudert; recent work revisits necessary and sufficient criteria for single crystals [@mouhat-coudert-2014; @knowles-2025-elastic-stability].

Open mathematical issues include finite-temperature elasticity, defects and plasticity, fracture, phase-transforming crystals, and error estimates for multiscale coupling.

### 6.6 Defects and singular geometry

Dislocations and disclinations are singularities in the order parameter. Rigorous treatments include discrete dislocation homogenization [@garroni-2008-dislocations], atomistic boundary-condition analysis [@ortner-shapeev-2016-defects], nonlinear dislocation dynamics variational principles [@acharya-2023-dislocation], and Riemann--Cartan geometric formulations [@katanaev-2005-defects; @yavari-goriely-2012; @kobayashi-2025-volterra].

What is proved is often asymptotic: dilute defects, prescribed defect topology, linearized elasticity, or continuum limits under scaling. Fully predictive defect dynamics in real crystals remains heavily computational and empirical.

### 6.7 Rigorous crystallization and its limits

The dream theorem would say: for a natural interaction potential in $\mathbb R^3$, low-temperature Gibbs states break Euclidean symmetry to a particular lattice and motif. This is open for most physically realistic systems. Results exist for special potentials, sphere packings, one-dimensional systems, certain two-dimensional models, and perturbative regimes. The hard part is not guessing the lattice; it is controlling all competing configurations, entropy, defects, and long-range interactions in the thermodynamic limit.

## 7. Computational frontier

### 7.1 Computational crystallography

Computational crystallography implements the quotient structure of crystals: lattice reduction, space-group detection, Wyckoff assignment, symmetry tolerances, conventional-cell selection, and structure matching. `spglib` is the de facto open library for symmetry search and has a 2024 methods paper emphasizing robust symmetry detection under numerical distortions [@togo-2024-spglib]. The Bilbao Crystallographic Server provides symmetry databases, irreducible representations, and Brillouin-zone tools [@aroyo-2006-bilbao; @de-la-flor-2021-bz]. `pymatgen` supplies Python data structures and analysis tools for crystals and high-throughput workflows [@ong-2013-pymatgen].

The mathematical issue is not merely software engineering. Finite-precision structures live near singular strata of a quotient by Euclidean motions, lattice-basis changes, permutations, and space-group actions. A small perturbation can change detected symmetry. Thus “the” symmetry of a computed structure is often a tolerance-dependent inference problem.

### 7.2 DFT and high-throughput databases

DFT remains the main computational bridge between quantum mechanics and crystal-scale prediction [@hohenberg-kohn-1964; @kohn-sham-1965; @jones-2015-dft]. High-throughput infrastructures generate, relax, and analyze thousands to millions of candidate structures. Major databases and platforms include the Materials Project, OQMD, AFLOW, COD, CSD, ICSD, and NOMAD [@horton-2025-mp-api; @kirklin-2015-oqmd; @curtarolo-2012-aflow; @grazulis-2009-cod; @groom-2016-csd; @hellenbrandt-2004-icsd; @scheidgen-2023-nomad]. Workflow engines such as AiiDA, atomate, and atomate2 encode provenance, reproducibility, and automated calculations [@huber-2020-aiida; @mathew-2017-atomate; @ganose-2025-atomate2].

The central mathematical construct is the **convex hull of formation energies**. A compound is thermodynamically stable at zero temperature if its formation energy lies on the lower convex hull in composition space. Metastability requires additional kinetic and finite-temperature information; large databases can suggest candidates but cannot by themselves prove synthesizability [@therrien-2021-metastable].

### 7.3 Phonon and finite-temperature computation

Finite-displacement and density-functional perturbation theory compute harmonic force constants and phonon dispersions. `phonopy` and `phono3py` are widely used for harmonic and anharmonic phonon calculations, including thermodynamic quantities and thermal transport [@togo-2023-phonopy]. Imaginary phonon frequencies indicate instability of a proposed structure with respect to a distortion at wave vector $q$; however, finite-temperature anharmonic stabilization can make a harmonically unstable phase physically relevant.

### 7.4 Molecular dynamics, Monte Carlo, and finite elements

Classical molecular dynamics integrates
\[
M_i\ddot r_i=-\nabla_{r_i}U(r_1,\dots,r_N),
\]
with empirical, DFT-fitted, or machine-learned potentials. Monte Carlo samples equilibrium distributions by stochastic moves. Finite element methods solve continuum elasticity, phase-field, fracture, and multiphysics models. Coupled atomistic-continuum methods attempt to pass defect-core information to macroscopic fields. Their mathematical edge is error estimation across scales and interfaces.

### 7.5 Crystal graph representations

A crystal graph represents atoms as nodes and neighbor relations as edges under periodic boundary conditions. CGCNN demonstrated graph convolution for property prediction [@xie-grossman-2018-cgcnn]. MEGNet generalized molecular/crystal graph networks with global state variables [@chen-2019-megnet]. These models shifted the mathematical representation problem from fixed descriptors to learned equivariant or invariant functions on periodic labeled metric graphs.

However, a crystal is not simply a finite graph. It is an infinite periodic graph plus an embedding and lattice. Neighbor cutoffs can break symmetry, and graph isomorphism does not imply geometric equivalence. Frontier methods increasingly enforce Euclidean equivariance, periodicity, and sometimes space-group constraints.

### 7.6 Machine-learned interatomic potentials

Universal machine-learned interatomic potentials approximate energies, forces, stresses, and sometimes magnetic moments over large chemical spaces. M3GNet is a graph deep-learning interatomic potential trained on Materials Project relaxations and used for large-scale hypothetical screening [@chen-2022-m3gnet]. CHGNet is a pretrained charge-informed neural network potential trained on large inorganic datasets including energies, forces, stresses, and magnetic moments [@deng-2023-chgnet]. These models enable molecular dynamics and relaxation at scales far beyond direct DFT.

The mathematical frontier is uncertainty and extrapolation. A neural potential is a learned function on a high-dimensional quotient space of local atomic environments. Stability of long-time MD, conservation laws, symmetry, transferability, and error accumulation are not guaranteed merely by low test error on random train/test splits.

### 7.7 Generative crystal models and inverse design

Generative models attempt to sample new crystal structures conditional on composition or target properties. CDVAE introduced diffusion/variational generation for periodic materials [@xie-2022-cdvae]. DiffCSP uses joint equivariant diffusion for lattice and fractional coordinates [@jiao-2023-diffcsp]. DiffCSP++ imposes space-group constraints and Wyckoff structure [@jiao-2024-diffcsppp]. MatterGen uses diffusion for inorganic materials design, including property conditioning [@zeni-2025-mattergen]. GNoME scaled deep learning for materials discovery and reported millions of predicted stable structures, followed by DFT validation of large subsets [@merchant-2023-gnome]. Reviews now frame generative AI for crystals as a distinct subfield [@de-breuck-2025-generative-review].

The mathematical insight is that inverse crystal design is generation on a stratified quotient space:
\[
\frac{\{\text{lattices, motifs, species, occupancies}\}}{E(d)\times GL(d,\mathbb Z)\times S_m\times G_{\mathrm{space}}},
\]
subject to non-overlap, charge balance, stoichiometry, Wyckoff constraints, and stability inequalities. Current models approximate this geometry; none fully solve it. Key failure modes include duplicate rediscovery, invalid local coordination, DFT-functional bias, poor synthesizability, and under-modeled disorder or finite-temperature effects. By 2026 the generative-crystal literature had matured enough for dedicated reviews emphasizing representations, datasets, evaluation metrics, disorder/defects, and synthesis constraints, while specialized workflows such as BOMLIP-CSP integrated MLIPs with batched optimization for molecular crystal structure prediction [@metni-2026-generative; @zhao-2026-bomlip].

### 7.8 Where computation creates new mathematics

Computation is not merely approximation when it forces new formal questions:

- How should one metrize the moduli space of periodic point sets with species labels?
- What is a statistically consistent estimator of space group from noisy atomic coordinates?
- Can generative models be made exactly equivariant under $GL(d,\mathbb Z)$ and space-group actions?
- Can convex-hull stability be integrated with kinetic accessibility in a mathematically controlled way?
- What are rigorous uncertainty bounds for learned interatomic potentials along rare-event pathways?
- Can topological band invariants be computed with certified error from finite $k$-meshes and approximate DFT Hamiltonians?

These are mathematical research problems generated by computational practice.

## 8. Experimental and physical interface

### 8.1 Diffraction as Fourier measurement

X-ray, neutron, and electron diffraction measure intensities related to $|\widehat\rho(k)|^2$, not phases. Bragg peak positions determine lattice parameters; intensities constrain motif and electron/nuclear density; systematic absences encode space-group elements such as glides and screws. The phase problem is inverse Fourier retrieval under positivity, atomicity, chemical constraints, and symmetry.

Neutron diffraction is sensitive to nuclei and magnetic order; X-rays primarily probe electron density; electron diffraction has strong interaction with matter and is central for nanoscale crystals. All are reciprocal-space probes that convert geometric order into constraints on Fourier amplitudes.

### 8.2 Diffuse scattering and local structure

Real crystals have diffuse scattering from phonons, occupational disorder, stacking faults, defects, and local correlations. Pair distribution function analysis uses total scattering to infer real-space pair correlations, especially for nanocrystalline, amorphous, and locally distorted materials [@terban-2021-pdf]. Mathematically, this is a partial inverse problem: two-point correlations do not generally determine a structure uniquely.

### 8.3 Spectroscopy and dynamics

Raman, infrared, inelastic neutron, inelastic X-ray, and ultrafast spectroscopies probe phonons, magnons, excitons, polarons, and electron-phonon coupling. These measurements constrain dynamical matrices, selection rules, lifetimes, and symmetry breaking. Symmetry determines which modes are active; anharmonicity determines linewidths and transport.

### 8.4 Imaging and atomistic reconstruction

Transmission electron microscopy, scanning probe microscopy, and atomic electron tomography can reconstruct local atomic configurations, including defects and amorphous structures. The mathematical interface includes inverse scattering, compressed sensing, tomographic reconstruction, graph matching, point-cloud statistics, and uncertainty quantification. Imaging has pushed crystal mathematics toward finite, defective, nonperiodic structures rather than only ideal infinite lattices.

### 8.5 Databases as empirical boundary conditions

Crystal databases encode experimentally determined and computationally predicted structures. The CSD, ICSD, COD, Materials Project, OQMD, AFLOW, and NOMAD provide empirical priors over plausible coordination environments, prototypes, Wyckoff occupancies, and energy scales [@groom-2016-csd; @hellenbrandt-2004-icsd; @grazulis-2009-cod; @horton-2025-mp-api; @kirklin-2015-oqmd; @curtarolo-2012-aflow; @scheidgen-2023-nomad]. For machine learning, these are not neutral samples from “the distribution of all crystals.” They are historically and technologically biased archives shaped by synthesis methods, publication practices, DFT settings, and database curation.

## 9. Current frontier, 2020--2026

### 9.1 Topological crystalline materials and magnetic symmetry

**Established advance.** Symmetry indicators and TQC have become operational tools for screening real materials. The pipeline from space group to irreducible representations to EBR decomposition to topological diagnosis is now embedded in databases and high-throughput searches [@po-vishwanath-watanabe-2017; @bradlyn-2017-tqc; @cano-2018-ebr; @vergniory-2019-catalogue; @wieder-2022-discovery]. Magnetic TQC extends the formalism to Shubnikov groups, crucial for antiferromagnets and magnetic topological materials [@elcoro-2021-mtqc].

**Active frontier.** Symmetry-breaking perturbations, magnetic order, correlated bands, fragile topology, higher-order topology, and disorder challenge purely symmetry-eigenvalue diagnostics. A 2024 direction studies how topological states evolve under symmetry breaking and how to identify descendants across magnetic and nonmagnetic group relations [@tang-wan-2024-symmetry-breaking].

**Open mathematical need.** A unified classification that handles crystalline symmetry, interactions, disorder, magnetic order, and realistic boundary terminations without collapsing to purely noninteracting band representations.

### 9.2 High-throughput and AI-accelerated crystal discovery

**Established advance.** Automated DFT workflows and databases have transformed crystal discovery into a large-scale discrete-continuous search over compositions, prototypes, and structures [@horton-2025-mp-api; @kirklin-2015-oqmd; @curtarolo-2012-aflow; @huber-2020-aiida; @ganose-2025-atomate2]. Machine-learned potentials and graph networks have made relaxations, property prediction, and screening much faster [@xie-grossman-2018-cgcnn; @chen-2019-megnet; @chen-2022-m3gnet; @deng-2023-chgnet]. GNoME is a landmark in scaling graph deep learning and DFT validation for materials discovery [@merchant-2023-gnome].

**Emerging proposal.** Foundation-like generative models such as MatterGen and diffusion-based CSP systems claim direct generation of stable, property-conditioned inorganic crystals [@xie-2022-cdvae; @jiao-2023-diffcsp; @jiao-2024-diffcsppp; @zeni-2025-mattergen; @de-breuck-2025-generative-review].

**Caution.** “Predicted stable” usually means stable or near-stable relative to a chosen DFT functional and database hull, not experimentally synthesizable. Kinetic accessibility, defects, finite-temperature entropy, decomposition pathways, and disorder can overturn zero-temperature hull predictions [@therrien-2021-metastable].

### 9.3 Symmetry-aware generative modeling

A key 2020--2026 movement is the shift from generic coordinate generation to symmetry-aware generation. The mathematical task is to sample valid points in a stratified space: choose composition, space group, lattice stratum, Wyckoff orbits, species assignment, and internal coordinates while respecting non-overlap and chemistry. DiffCSP++ is representative because it explicitly constrains generation by space group and Wyckoff structure [@jiao-2024-diffcsppp].

This is a frontier where crystallography becomes machine-learning geometry. A fully satisfactory model would be invariant or equivariant under all representation redundancies, would assign calibrated probabilities to structures, and would couple generation to thermodynamic and kinetic validation.

### 9.4 Defects, microstructure, and multiscale simulation

Defects are moving from afterthoughts to primary mathematical objects. ML potentials now permit simulations of dislocations, grain boundaries, diffusion, and phase transformations at scales not accessible to DFT, but they raise questions about rare-event reliability and core extrapolation. Rigorous work continues on atomistic boundary conditions, continuum defect geometry, and variational dislocation dynamics [@ortner-shapeev-2016-defects; @acharya-2023-dislocation; @kobayashi-2025-volterra].

The frontier is coupling: electronic structure near defect cores, elastic far fields, thermally activated motion, and topological constraints must be integrated.

### 9.5 Soft, active, and programmable crystals

Soft crystals include colloidal crystals, polymer crystals, liquid-crystal elastomers, DNA-programmed assemblies, and granular/active lattices. DNA-mediated colloids make interactions programmable by sequence and geometry [@zhang-2021-dna-colloids], while DNA origami and colloidal design expand the repertoire of finite motifs and target lattices [@lee-2025-dna-origami]. Active colloids and programmable active matter introduce nonequilibrium driving, nonreciprocal interactions, and self-propulsion [@bishop-2023-active-colloids; @yu-2023-programmable-active; @te-vrugt-2025-active-matter].

Mathematically, these systems challenge equilibrium crystallography. The “unit cell” may be a limit cycle, a driven state, or an emergent pattern with non-Hamiltonian dynamics. Discrete time crystals are a separate but conceptually adjacent example: they break discrete time-translation symmetry under periodic driving, not spatial translation symmetry [@else-2020-time-crystals; @zaletel-2023-time-crystals].

### 9.6 Aperiodic order and quasicrystal topology

Aperiodic crystals continue to motivate rigorous diffraction, higher-dimensional embedding, tiling cohomology, and spectral theory [@baake-grimm-2013; @baake-lenz-2017; @baake-grimm-2020-inflation]. The frontier includes topology of quasicrystalline Hamiltonians, gap labeling, noncommutative geometry, and robustness under disorder. This memo focuses on periodic crystals, but any mathematically honest frontier must treat quasicrystals as neighboring, not marginal.

### 9.7 Research groups and infrastructure map

This list is deliberately selective rather than canonical. The major mathematical and computational nodes include: the IUCr/International Tables/Bilbao ecosystem for crystallographic classification and representations [@hahn-2005-ita; @aroyo-2006-bilbao; @de-la-flor-2021-bz]; the Bielefeld/aperiodic-order community around Baake, Grimm, Lenz, Richard, and collaborators for diffraction and dynamical spectra [@baake-grimm-2013; @baake-lenz-2017; @richard-2017]; periodic-operator and Floquet-analysis programs represented by Kuchment and related spectral-theory groups [@kuchment-1993; @kuchment-2016]; rigorous atomistic-to-continuum and defect programs around Le Bris, Lions, Blanc, Ortner, Shapeev, Acharya, Yavari, and Goriely [@blanc-2010-cauchy-born; @ortner-shapeev-2016-defects; @acharya-2023-dislocation; @yavari-goriely-2012]; topological quantum chemistry and symmetry-indicator groups involving Bernevig, Bradlyn, Cano, Aroyo, Elcoro, Vergniory, Felser, Regnault, Po, Vishwanath, Watanabe, Tang, and Wan [@po-vishwanath-watanabe-2017; @bradlyn-2017-tqc; @wieder-2022-discovery; @tang-wan-2024-symmetry-breaking]; high-throughput materials platforms including the Materials Project, AFLOW, OQMD, NOMAD, AiiDA, atomate, and atomate2 communities [@horton-2025-mp-api; @curtarolo-2012-aflow; @kirklin-2015-oqmd; @scheidgen-2023-nomad; @huber-2020-aiida; @ganose-2025-atomate2]; and AI/materials-design groups around CGCNN/MEGNet/M3GNet/CHGNet, Google DeepMind's GNoME, and Microsoft Research's MatterGen [@xie-grossman-2018-cgcnn; @chen-2019-megnet; @chen-2022-m3gnet; @deng-2023-chgnet; @merchant-2023-gnome; @zeni-2025-mattergen].

The mathematical significance of this map is that many frontier problems are now infrastructural: standardization of symmetry data, reproducible workflows, robust quotient representations, and certified interfaces between theorem-level mathematics and database-scale computation.

### 9.8 Data standards, provenance, and reproducibility

As workflows become autonomous, provenance becomes mathematical infrastructure. AiiDA and NOMAD emphasize reproducible workflows and FAIR data [@huber-2020-aiida; @scheidgen-2023-nomad]. For theory, this matters because computational claims often depend on pseudopotentials, exchange-correlation functionals, convergence thresholds, magnetic initializations, symmetry tolerances, and database versions. A theorem has hypotheses; a high-throughput result needs equally explicit computational hypotheses.

## 10. Open mathematical problems

| Problem | Why it is hard | What kind of advance may resolve it |
|---|---|---|
| **Crystallization from continuum interactions** | Need to prove that low-temperature or ground-state continuum particles select a lattice/motif among infinitely many competitors. Entropy, defects, long-range forces, and boundary effects all matter. | New compactness and energy-localization methods; sharper universal optimality results; perturbative schemes around candidate lattices; computer-assisted proofs with certified bounds. |
| **Rigorous solid--liquid transition in realistic 3D systems** | Requires controlling two infinite-volume phases and the transition between them. Existing methods are strong for special lattice models but weak for continuum molecular potentials. | A synthesis of Pirogov--Sinai theory, continuum cluster expansions, defect gas methods, and verified numerical free-energy bounds. |
| **Finite-temperature elasticity from atoms** | $W(F,T)$ should include phonons, defects, anharmonicity, and entropy. Cauchy--Born is mostly zero-temperature/local-stability theory. | Rigorous thermodynamic-limit definitions of strain free energy; quantitative equivalence of ensembles under deformation; defect-aware homogenization. |
| **Defect dynamics and plasticity** | Dislocation motion couples singular geometry, core physics, long-range elasticity, thermal activation, and multiplication reactions. | Variational/PDE limits for moving defect networks; calibrated atomistic-to-continuum error estimates; geometric measure theory for evolving line defects. |
| **Classification of interacting topological crystalline phases** | Noninteracting band theory is mature, but interactions, disorder, magnetic order, and crystalline symmetry make classification much harder. | Equivariant generalized cohomology/K-theory plus anomaly inflow; real-space cell decompositions with interactions; computable invariants linked to observables. |
| **Topology with defects and strain** | Real crystals have dislocations, disclinations, boundaries, and strain fields that break exact translations. | Index theorems or bulk-defect correspondences for crystalline gauge fields; noncommutative geometry for aperiodic/defective media. |
| **Certified inverse crystal design** | Search space is combinatorial, continuous, symmetric, and chemically constrained. ML models lack guarantees of validity, novelty, and synthesizability. | Exact quotient-aware generative models; probabilistic programming over Wyckoff strata; certified DFT/ML uncertainty; integration of thermodynamics with kinetic path planning. |
| **Metric geometry of crystal-structure space** | Structure comparison must quotient by Euclidean motions, cell choices, atom permutations, species, and sometimes symmetry. | Stable metrics on periodic labeled point sets; optimal transport on quotient spaces; stratified statistical theory for space-group inference. |
| **Rigorous ML interatomic potentials** | Learned potentials can be accurate in-distribution but unstable out-of-distribution, especially for defects and rare events. | Error bounds tied to local-environment coverage; active-learning certificates; symplectic/conservative architectures; long-time stability estimates. |
| **Melting in 2D and quasi-2D materials** | KTHNY, first-order, and one-step pathways compete depending on potential, density, substrate, hydrodynamics, and finite size. | Universality classes for defect-mediated melting beyond ideal models; rigorous scaling theory plus high-precision simulation with finite-size certification. |
| **Mathematics of diffuse scattering** | Diffuse intensity encodes correlations but inverse problems are nonunique. | Reconstruction theory for higher-order correlations; compressed sensing with physical priors; uncertainty quantification for local-structure ensembles. |
| **Active and programmable crystals** | No equilibrium free energy may exist; detailed balance can fail; order may be dynamical rather than static. | Nonequilibrium pattern classification; stochastic PDE limits; Floquet/spatiotemporal group theory; control theory for programmable assembly. |

## 11. Cross-regime comparison

| Regime | Mathematical order parameter / object | Fourier or spectral signature | Symmetry status | Typical equations | What changes relative to periodic crystals |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Periodic crystal** | Lattice $\Lambda$, motif $\mu_0$, space group $G$, nonzero $\rho_G$ | Bragg peaks on $\Lambda^*$; band spectrum over $\mathbb T^d$ | Continuous Euclidean symmetry broken to discrete space group | Bloch Hamiltonians, phonons, elasticity, Gibbs states | Exact translation lattice and compact unit cell exist. |
| **Quasicrystal / aperiodic crystal** | Delone set, cut-and-project set, tiling hull; finite local complexity often | Sharp Bragg peaks on higher-rank Fourier module; possible singular spectra | No rank-$d$ lattice translations; noncrystallographic rotations possible | Diffraction measures, tiling dynamical systems, noncommutative Hamiltonians | Periodicity is replaced by pure point diffraction or dynamical order. |
| **Glass / amorphous solid** | Metastable disordered network; pair and higher correlations; persistent homology | Diffuse scattering, broad peaks; no reciprocal lattice | Statistically isotropic or locally anisotropic; no exact space group | Energy landscapes, inherent structures, mode-coupling, elastoplastic models | Static rigidity persists without periodic long-range order. |
| **Liquid** | Translation-invariant Gibbs state; $g(r)$, $S(k)$, many-body correlations | Continuous structure factor with peaks but no Bragg lattice | Continuous translations/rotations unbroken in equilibrium | Navier--Stokes, fluctuating hydrodynamics, liquid-state integral equations | Flow destroys static shear rigidity at long time; correlations decay. |
| **Gas** | Dilute point process/distribution function $f(x,v,t)$ | Weak structure factor except collisions/correlations | Continuous symmetry statistically intact | Boltzmann equation, kinetic theory | Mean free path and collision statistics replace lattice constraints. |
| **Plasma** | Charged distribution functions plus electromagnetic fields | Collective modes, Debye screening, plasma oscillations | No static lattice unless strongly coupled plasma crystallizes | Vlasov--Poisson, Vlasov--Maxwell, magnetohydrodynamics | Long-range Coulomb fields and screening dominate; quasineutral collective dynamics replace unit cells. |
| **Active/programmable crystal** | Ordered state of driven units; possibly time-periodic or controlled pattern | Bragg-like peaks plus nonequilibrium fluctuations; spatiotemporal spectra | Spatial and/or temporal symmetries may be broken dynamically | Langevin active matter, hydrodynamics, Floquet maps, control laws | Detailed balance can fail; “free energy minimizer” may not exist. |

The main mathematical transition is from an action of a co-compact translation lattice to weaker or different structures: a higher-rank Fourier module in quasicrystals, short/medium-range correlation complexes in glasses, time-dependent correlation fields in liquids, kinetic distributions in gases, and self-consistent long-range fields in plasmas.

## 12. Annotated bibliography

### Foundational crystallography and solid-state structure

**IUCr Online Dictionary: “Crystal” and “Aperiodic crystal”** [@iucr-crystal-2021; @iucr-aperiodic-2024]. These entries state the modern diffraction-oriented definitions. They are concise but conceptually important because they separate crystalline order from strict lattice periodicity.

**International Tables for Crystallography, Volume A** [@hahn-2005-ita]. The canonical reference for space-group symmetry, Wyckoff positions, conventional settings, and crystallographic notation. Essential for any mathematically serious treatment of 3D crystallographic classification.

**Müller and Schwarzenberger, _Mathematical Crystallography_** [@muller-2013-math-crystallography]. A compact mathematical route to point groups, Bravais lattices, and space groups. Useful for readers who want the group theory rather than only tables.

**Ashcroft--Mermin, Kittel, Born--Huang, Chaikin--Lubensky, Marder** [@ashcroft-mermin-1976; @kittel-2005; @born-huang-1954; @chaikin-lubensky-1995; @marder-2010]. These remain standard references for reciprocal lattices, phonons, band theory, and condensed-matter statistical mechanics. Born--Huang is still the classic mathematical source for lattice dynamics.

**Nye, _Physical Properties of Crystals_** [@nye-1985]. The classical tensor/symmetry reference. It is indispensable for understanding how point-group symmetry constrains measurable response tensors.

### Diffraction, quasicrystals, and aperiodic order

**Baake and Grimm, _Aperiodic Order_ and mathematical diffraction papers** [@baake-grimm-2013; @baake-grimm-2012-diffraction; @baake-grimm-2020-inflation]. These sources provide the measure-theoretic formulation of diffraction and the rigorous bridge from periodic crystals to quasicrystals and model sets.

**Dworkin and Lenz** [@dworkin-1993; @lenz-2009-diffraction; @baake-lenz-2017]. These works connect diffraction to dynamical spectra. This is one of the mathematically deepest interfaces between crystallography, ergodic theory, and harmonic analysis.

**Richard, “Pure point diffraction and Poisson summation”** [@richard-2017]. A modern rigorous treatment of Poisson summation and pure point diffraction, useful for understanding exactly when lattice-like diffraction statements hold.

### Spectral theory and electronic structure

**Kuchment, _Floquet Theory_ and periodic elliptic-operator overview** [@kuchment-1993; @kuchment-2016]. These are the right mathematical references for Bloch decomposition, band spectra, Fermi varieties, and spectral problems of periodic operators.

**Hohenberg--Kohn, Kohn--Sham, Jones review** [@hohenberg-kohn-1964; @kohn-sham-1965; @jones-2015-dft]. Foundational and reflective DFT sources. They explain why DFT is both indispensable and approximation-laden for crystal prediction.

### Rigorous matter, elasticity, and defects

**Lieb--Lebowitz; Lieb; Lieb--Seiringer** [@lieb-lebowitz-1972; @lieb-1976-stability; @lieb-seiringer-2010]. The rigorous stability-of-matter tradition. These results justify thermodynamic matter but do not solve crystallization.

**Catto--Le Bris--Lions; Cancès--Deleurence--Lewin; Hainzl--Lewin--Solovej** [@catto-2001; @cances-2008-defects; @hainzl-lewin-solovej-2008]. Mean-field and reduced Hartree--Fock approaches to thermodynamic limits and defects in crystals. Useful for mathematicians entering quantum crystal models.

**Blanc--Le Bris--Lions; Schmidt; Friesecke--James--Müller** [@blanc-2007; @blanc-2010-cauchy-born; @schmidt-2009; @friesecke-james-muller-2002]. Core references for discrete-to-continuum limits, Cauchy--Born-type reasoning, and geometric rigidity.

**Ortner--Shapeev and defect boundary conditions** [@ortner-shapeev-2016-defects]. Important for rigorous numerical analysis of atomistic simulations with defects. It exemplifies the kind of theorem needed to make multiscale defect computation trustworthy.

**Katanaev; Yavari--Goriely; Kobayashi--Cirak--Yavari** [@katanaev-2005-defects; @yavari-goriely-2012; @kobayashi-2025-volterra]. These works frame defects using differential geometry: torsion, curvature, Burgers vectors, and Volterra constructions. They are central for topological/geometric defect theory.

**Mouhat--Coudert and Knowles** [@mouhat-coudert-2014; @knowles-2025-elastic-stability]. References for elastic stability criteria by crystal class. Useful for connecting symmetry-reduced tensors with mechanical positivity.

**KTHNY and modern melting work** [@kosterlitz-thouless-1973; @halperin-nelson-1978; @nelson-halperin-1979; @young-1979; @bernard-krauth-2011; @zhu-2025-melting; @galliano-2023-nonequilibrium-crystals]. The KTHNY papers define the classic defect-mediated 2D melting theory. Later simulation/experiment work shows that real or model-dependent pathways remain subtle.

### Topological crystalline phases

**Fu and SnTe-class TCI work** [@fu-2011-tci; @hsieh-2012-snte]. Foundational sources for topological crystalline insulators protected by mirror symmetry.

**Po--Vishwanath--Watanabe; Bradlyn et al.; Cano et al.** [@po-vishwanath-watanabe-2017; @bradlyn-2017-tqc; @cano-2018-ebr]. The core symmetry-indicator and TQC framework. These papers made crystallographic representation data computationally actionable for topology.

**Vergniory et al.; Tang et al.; Wieder et al.** [@vergniory-2019-catalogue; @tang-2019-natphys; @wieder-2022-discovery]. Large-scale topological-material cataloguing and review. Good entry points to the database-driven topological materials program.

**Elcoro et al. magnetic TQC** [@elcoro-2021-mtqc]. Extends the TQC formalism to magnetic space groups. This is a major 2020s expansion of the symmetry/topology interface.

**Kruthoff et al.; Stehouwer et al.; Song et al.** [@kruthoff-2017; @stehouwer-2018-k-theory; @song-2019-topological-crystals; @song-2020-realspace]. These sources connect band combinatorics, $K$-theory, and real-space cell constructions. They are better mathematical guides than purely materials-oriented catalogues.

**Higher-order topology reviews and papers** [@schindler-2018-hoti; @yang-2024-hoti-review]. Important for boundary-stratified topology and crystalline protection beyond ordinary surface states.

### Computational crystallography, databases, and workflows

**Bilbao Crystallographic Server and spglib** [@aroyo-2006-bilbao; @de-la-flor-2021-bz; @togo-2024-spglib]. Essential infrastructure for symmetry computation, Brillouin zones, and representation data. They also expose the finite-precision nature of computational symmetry.

**pymatgen, phonopy/phono3py, AiiDA, atomate/atomate2** [@ong-2013-pymatgen; @togo-2023-phonopy; @huber-2020-aiida; @mathew-2017-atomate; @ganose-2025-atomate2]. Core open-source tools for modern computational materials science. They encode the practical mathematical objects: structures, workflows, provenance, force constants, and phase diagrams.

**Materials Project, OQMD, AFLOW, COD, CSD, ICSD, NOMAD** [@horton-2025-mp-api; @kirklin-2015-oqmd; @curtarolo-2012-aflow; @grazulis-2009-cod; @groom-2016-csd; @hellenbrandt-2004-icsd; @scheidgen-2023-nomad]. These databases define much of the empirical and computational prior for crystal informatics. A mathematical researcher should treat them as biased, curated samples with explicit provenance.

### Machine learning and generative crystal design

**CGCNN and MEGNet** [@xie-grossman-2018-cgcnn; @chen-2019-megnet]. Early, influential graph neural networks for crystal property prediction. They frame crystals as periodic graphs with learnable local-environment features.

**M3GNet and CHGNet** [@chen-2022-m3gnet; @deng-2023-chgnet]. Universal neural interatomic potentials trained on large computed datasets. They are central to the 2020s shift from property prediction to large-scale relaxation and dynamics.

**GNoME and MatterGen** [@merchant-2023-gnome; @zeni-2025-mattergen]. Landmark large-scale AI crystal discovery/generation works. They should be read with attention to validation criteria, DFT dependence, and synthesizability claims.

**CDVAE, DiffCSP, DiffCSP++** [@xie-2022-cdvae; @jiao-2023-diffcsp; @jiao-2024-diffcsppp]. Key methodological papers for diffusion/generative models of periodic structures. DiffCSP++ is especially relevant to mathematical crystallography because it builds in space-group/Wyckoff constraints.

**De Breuck et al.; Metni et al.; Zhao et al.** [@de-breuck-2025-generative-review; @metni-2026-generative; @zhao-2026-bomlip]. These recent sources show the rapid consolidation of generative crystal modeling into a reviewable field and the movement toward practical CSP workflows that combine learned potentials, symmetry/representation choices, and optimization. They are also useful for understanding why evaluation metrics and synthesizability remain unsettled.

**Therrien et al. metastable materials review** [@therrien-2021-metastable]. Essential cautionary reference: convex-hull stability is not the same as experimental synthesizability.

### Disordered, soft, active, and programmable regimes

**Terban--Billinge and persistent-homology glass work** [@terban-2021-pdf; @onodera-2020-glass-topology]. These sources show how mathematical structure persists beyond periodicity through pair distribution functions, topology, and local-network analysis.

**Pihlajamaa--Janssen** [@pihlajamaa-2023-liquids]. A modern source on many-body correlations in dense liquids. Useful for comparing crystalline order to liquid order beyond pair correlations.

**DNA-mediated, active, and programmable matter reviews** [@zhang-2021-dna-colloids; @lee-2025-dna-origami; @bishop-2023-active-colloids; @yu-2023-programmable-active; @te-vrugt-2025-active-matter]. These map the frontier where crystals become designable, driven, soft, or autonomous rather than passive equilibrium lattices.

**Discrete time crystal reviews** [@else-2020-time-crystals; @zaletel-2023-time-crystals]. Not spatial crystallography, but valuable for thinking carefully about symmetry breaking in time and about when the word “crystal” is being extended mathematically rather than metaphorically.

## Research-program takeaways

1. **The right object is not a unit cell alone.** It is a structured equivalence class: lattice, motif, symmetry group, correlation measure, energy functional, and sometimes Bloch bundle.
2. **Diffraction is the most robust definition of crystalline order, but not a complete inverse invariant.** Autocorrelation loses phase and can be homometric.
3. **Space-group representation theory is now computational topology.** TQC and symmetry indicators transformed crystallography into a high-throughput topological classifier, but noninteracting assumptions remain substantial.
4. **Defects are not perturbations to be ignored.** They are singular geometric/topological objects controlling plasticity, melting, growth, and many electronic phenomena.
5. **AI crystal discovery is a quotient-geometry problem.** Valid generation requires respecting Euclidean, lattice-basis, permutation, Wyckoff, and chemical constraints.
6. **The hardest rigorous gap is emergence.** We know how to analyze a crystal once periodicity is assumed. Proving that realistic matter crystallizes, melts, defects, and flows as observed remains a frontier.
