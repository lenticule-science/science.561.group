## Summary Answer:

**The shift from "murder being bad if your country says it is" to "murder being bad if the local place says it is" happened incrementally across the 17th to 19th centuries**, particularly in the context of settler colonialism, through the rise of **local jurisdictional authority**, **common law adaptation**, and **the doctrine of discovery**. The legal and philosophical ground for this shift was laid by figures like **John Locke**, **Hugo Grotius**, and later **Blackstone**, and it was institutionalized by **colonial charters**, **frontier courts**, and **state sovereignty** under U.S. federalism.

## Deeper Breakdown:

### 1. **Early Colonial Frameworks: Murder = Wrong if the Metropole Says So**

- In early English (and other European) colonial ventures, settlers were still subject to **the laws of the crown**. Murder was legally a matter for **imperial oversight**—in theory.
	
- However, enforcement was **logistically and politically limited**. Colonists often operated with de facto impunity, especially in "zones of exception" (Agamben), where imperial law was **present only as myth or claim**.

### 2. **Chartered Autonomy and Legal Pluralism**

- Colonies like Massachusetts Bay operated under **corporate charters**, which granted **quasi-sovereign power** to make local laws.
	
- These included the ability to define and adjudicate **criminal offenses**, including murder.
	
- This meant murder became "wrong" or "punishable" based on **local colonial codes**, not British common law per se.

> For instance, in early Plymouth, colonists hanged a white man for murdering a Native man in 1638, but such punishments were rare and often symbolic—enforcing intra-settler norms rather than cross-cultural justice.

### 3. **Frontier Zones as Legal Voids**

- The "frontier" in settler imagination functioned as a **legal terra nullius**, even though Indigenous nations had law and sovereignty.
	
- Settlers often did not consider killing Indigenous people to be murder **because local settler norms either ignored or justified it**. This was not a vacuum but a **redefinition of law through settler sovereignty**.

> **Example:** The 1782 Gnadenhutten massacre of 96 Christian Lenape by Pennsylvania militia was technically murder under U.S. law—but no one was tried, because local norms didn’t treat it as criminal.

### 4. **Post-Revolution Federalism: Local Sovereignty as Legal Foundation**

- After the U.S. formed, the federal system gave **states** considerable authority over criminal law.
	
- Murder was defined, prosecuted, and punished **at the state level**, reinforcing **local jurisdiction** over what constituted criminal violence.
	
- This is when the idea fully solidifies: **“Murder is bad if the place you're in says it is”—not because some distant capital does.**

## Theoretical Frame:

This transition reflects a move from **imperial verticality** to **settler horizontal sovereignty**. It wasn't just a decentralization—it was a **rearticulation of whose life counted as killable or sacred**, and **who got to say so**.

## Historical Development of “Lawless Zones”

### 1. **Settler Frontier: The American West**

- **"The West" was never truly lawless.** It was a **juridical imaginary** used to justify violence and retroactively impose order.
	
- The **violent acts of settler militias, land grabs, and massacres** were often described as "necessary taming" of a wild land—but this relied on the idea that the land had no legitimate law of its own (i.e. Indigenous sovereignty didn’t count).
	
- The absence of federal enforcement was not a gap but a **design**—a way to allow settlers to do the dirty work of dispossession.

> Lisa Ford shows how settler colonies actively structured their courts and legal codes to create *selective jurisdiction*—governing settlers rigorously while allowing impunity toward Indigenous people.

---

### 2. **High Seas / International Waters**

- Under **Grotius’s "Mare Liberum" (1609)**, the sea was imagined as **a space beyond sovereign claim**—but this was only true insofar as no other European power had claimed it.
	
- Today, **international waters** still function as **zones of suspended jurisdiction**, ideal for:
	
	- **Flags of convenience** (ships registered in countries with lax labor/environmental laws)
		
	- **Unregulated resource extraction** (deep-sea mining, overfishing)
		
	- **Military posturing** without triggering open war
		
- Legality here is **layered, outsourced, or ignored**, depending on what powerful actors want.

---

### 3. **The Legal Logic: Lawlessness as Exception, Not Absence**

This follows what **Carl Schmitt** and later **Giorgio Agamben** describe:

> "Sovereign is he who decides the exception."

- Zones deemed "lawless" are not law-free; they are **produced through the decision to suspend or displace law**.
	
- In settler states, this often allows **extra-legal acts (e.g., genocide, vigilante violence)** to be **reframed as pre-legal foundation**—as if these acts establish the possibility of law.
