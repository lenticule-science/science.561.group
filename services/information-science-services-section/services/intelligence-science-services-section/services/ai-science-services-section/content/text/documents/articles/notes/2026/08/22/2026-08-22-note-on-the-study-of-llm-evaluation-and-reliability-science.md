This study is read for one purpose: to establish, for this sub-literature, which claims trace to peer-reviewed research versus industry-lab preprints and technical reports versus blog and trade commentary, so this library's own agent-operated practice cites the right kind of source.
This field's peculiarity is that much of its load-bearing work is arXiv-preprint or industry-lab tier — frontier labs publish self-knowledge and red-teaming results directly to arXiv or their own technical-report pages, often without independent peer review, while the benchmark-construction and bias-documentation side of the field runs mostly through ICLR, NeurIPS, ACL-family venues, and TMLR — and this note labels tiers explicitly rather than flattening them into a uniform "the literature says" register.
Method: web-verified citations.
Every load-bearing claim below carries a markdown-linked source — a DOI, an arXiv ID, an ACL Anthology entry, or a stable publisher URL — and anything not verifiable to a primary source is marked **[UNVERIFIED]** inline.

## 1. The benchmark canon: MMLU, BIG-bench, HELM

Hendrycks et al. introduce MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding), a 57-subject multiple-choice test spanning elementary mathematics, US history, computer science, law, and more, built to require broad world knowledge and problem-solving rather than narrow task skill.
Source: [Hendrycks et al., "Measuring Massive Multitask Language Understanding," ICLR 2021](https://openreview.net/forum?id=d7KBjmI3GmQ) (also [arXiv:2009.03300](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.03300)).
**Tier: peer-reviewed (ICLR conference track).**
MMLU became the field's default multitask multiple-choice benchmark, cited in nearly every model release since; that ubiquity is exactly why its documented flaws (Section 2) matter disproportionately.

Srivastava et al. introduce BIG-bench (Beyond the Imitation Game benchmark), 204 tasks contributed by 450 authors across 132 institutions, spanning linguistics, childhood development, mathematics, common-sense reasoning, biology, physics, and social bias, explicitly designed to probe tasks believed to lie beyond then-current model capability and to track emergent behavior with scale.
Source: [Srivastava et al., "Beyond the Imitation Game: Quantifying and Extrapolating the Capabilities of Language Models," TMLR, May 2023](https://openreview.net/forum?id=uyTL5Bvosj) (first released as [arXiv:2206.04615](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04615) in June 2022).
**Tier: peer-reviewed (TMLR).**
BIG-bench's scale (204 tasks, 450 authors) makes it the closest thing this canon has to a crowd-sourced capability census rather than one lab's benchmark design, which is a distinct evidentiary posture from MMLU's fixed single-team construction.

Liang et al. introduce HELM (Holistic Evaluation of Language Models), a framework that taxonomizes the space of evaluation scenarios and metrics and then measures many models against a broad, explicitly multi-metric slate — accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity, and efficiency — across 16 core scenarios, arguing that accuracy-only leaderboards hide exactly these trade-offs.
Source: [Liang et al., "Holistic Evaluation of Language Models," TMLR, August 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.09110) (first released as arXiv:2211.09110 in November 2022).
**Tier: peer-reviewed (TMLR).**
HELM's own design is itself evidence for this study's central claim: calibration (Section 5) is one of HELM's seven named metrics, treated as co-equal with accuracy rather than an afterthought, which is the framework explicitly arguing against the single-scalar leaderboard culture this note is trying to place correctly by tier.

## 2. Documented limits of the canon: MMLU-Redux and ground-truth error

Gema et al. manually re-annotate 5,700 MMLU questions across all 57 subjects and estimate that 6.49% of MMLU questions contain some error — ambiguity, mislabeling, or multiple defensible correct answers — with the rate reaching 57% in the Virology subset, exceeding 20% in Logical Fallacies and College Chemistry, and exceeding 10% in several further subjects including Professional Law, Business Ethics, Formal Logic, and Human Aging.
Source: [Gema et al., "Are We Done with MMLU?," NAACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.262/) (also [arXiv:2406.04127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04127)).
**Tier: peer-reviewed (NAACL).**
This is a peer-reviewed correction of a peer-reviewed benchmark, and its implication is specific and load-bearing for gate design: at model accuracies now clustering in the high 80s and low 90s on MMLU, a meaningful fraction of remaining "errors" are almost certainly benchmark label errors rather than model failures, so any editorial-gate design that treats "passed benchmark X at Y%" as a settled reliability signal inherits whatever ground-truth error rate that benchmark's own community has (or has not) since audited — a check this study returns to in Section 9.

## 3. Benchmark contamination: measurement and detection

Sainz et al. argue that the worst form of data contamination — an LLM trained on a benchmark's own test split, then evaluated on that same benchmark — is common and, critically, not straightforward to measure after the fact, since most labs do not disclose full pretraining-corpus contents; they propose norms (disclosure of what was checked, and how) rather than a single detection algorithm.
Source: [Sainz et al., "NLP Evaluation in Trouble: On the Need to Measure LLM Data Contamination for Each Benchmark," EMNLP Findings 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-emnlp.722/).
**Tier: peer-reviewed (EMNLP Findings).**
The paper's own contribution beyond argument is empirical: the accompanying [LM Contamination Index](https://github.com/hitz-zentroa/lm-contamination) is a manually curated, continuously updated database of documented contamination evidence across models and benchmarks, which makes this the sub-literature's clearest peer-reviewed anchor for "contamination is a known, cataloged problem," not merely a suspected one.

Oren et al. supply a detection method that does not require access to a model's weights or training data at all: because contamination tends to make a model assign disproportionately high likelihood to a benchmark's canonical example ordering (an artifact of memorizing the order examples appeared in during training), a statistically significant likelihood gap between the canonical ordering and randomly shuffled orderings of an exchangeable benchmark is evidence of contamination.
Source: [Oren et al., "Proving Test Set Contamination in Black-Box Language Models," ICLR 2024 (Oral)](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2024/oral/19769).
**Tier: peer-reviewed (ICLR, oral presentation).**
This is the sub-literature's methodologically strongest contamination-detection result precisely because it is black-box: it gives an external auditor — not just the training lab — a way to test a contamination claim, which is the property any editorial-gate design borrowing this technique would need.

Zhou et al. survey benchmark-leakage risk more broadly and propose developer- and maintainer-facing guidelines — including detection heuristics based on performance-consistency checks across benchmark variants — for reducing inadvertent and deliberate benchmark leakage.
Source: [Zhou et al., "Don't Make Your LLM an Evaluation Benchmark Cheater," arXiv:2311.01964, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01964).
**Tier: arXiv preprint, not peer-reviewed as of this study's verification.**
This paper is treated as a lower-confidence anchor than Sainz et al. or Oren et al. for exactly that reason; its guidelines are useful as practice but should not be cited with the same evidentiary weight as the two peer-reviewed contamination papers above.

## 4. LLM-as-judge: MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena

Zheng et al. propose using a strong LLM (GPT-4, in their study) as an automated judge of other LLMs' open-ended chat responses, and validate this against two purpose-built resources: MT-Bench, an 80-question multi-turn benchmark targeting instruction-following and conversational ability, and Chatbot Arena, a crowdsourced platform where users blind-vote between anonymized model pairs.
Source: [Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena," NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks Track](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/91f18a1287b398d378ef22505bf41832-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks.html).
**Tier: peer-reviewed (NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks Track).**
The paper reports GPT-4 judge agreement with human preference above 80%, comparable to human-human agreement — the paper's own basis for treating LLM-as-judge as a viable, scalable substitute for exhaustive human evaluation — but the same paper is also the primary source for the bias findings in Section 5, so its validation and its warning arrive in the same peer-reviewed document rather than in tension across two separate literatures.

## 5. Documented judge biases: position, verbosity, self-preference

Zheng et al. (Section 4) document position bias (a judge's tendency to favor whichever response appears first or second, independent of quality), verbosity bias (a tendency to favor longer responses even when not more correct or helpful), and self-enhancement bias (an early framing of what Panickssery et al., below, would later isolate and name self-preference bias), and propose mitigations — swapping response position and averaging, and few-shot judge examples — that reduce but do not eliminate the effects.
Source: same as Section 4, [Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks Track](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/91f18a1287b398d378ef22505bf41832-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks.html).
**Tier: peer-reviewed (NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks Track).**

Panickssery et al. isolate self-preference bias specifically and connect it mechanistically to self-recognition: they show LLM judges (GPT-4, Llama 2, and others tested) score their own generations higher than a human-quality-matched alternative, that the same models have above-chance ability to recognize their own outputs as their own when asked directly, and — via controlled fine-tuning experiments that manipulate self-recognition ability — establish a linear correlation between how well a model recognizes its own text and how strongly it prefers it.
Source: [Panickssery, Bowman, and Feng, "LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations," NeurIPS 2024](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/7f1f0218e45f5414c79c0679633e47bc-Abstract-Conference.html) (also [arXiv:2404.13076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)).
**Tier: peer-reviewed (NeurIPS).**
The causal link this paper establishes — self-recognition drives self-preference, not merely correlates with it — is the strongest evidence in this study's scope that LLM-as-judge is not a neutral scoring instrument whenever the judge and a candidate response share a lineage (same model family, same fine-tuning lineage, or a judge grading its own earlier draft), which is a directly load-bearing warning for any editorial-gate design that would use one LLM to grade prose a related LLM produced (Section 9).

## 6. Calibration and self-knowledge

Guo et al. establish, for pre-LLM deep classifiers, that modern neural networks are systematically miscalibrated — usually overconfident — despite improved accuracy relative to older, smaller networks, and show depth, width, weight decay, and batch normalization as the main architectural drivers, with a simple post-hoc temperature-scaling correction recovering good calibration cheaply.
Source: [Guo, Pleiss, Sun, and Weinberger, "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks," ICML 2017](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/guo17a/guo17a.pdf).
**Tier: peer-reviewed (ICML).**
This is the field's peer-reviewed foundation for "calibration is a known, measurable, and only partially solved problem in modern deep networks" — a claim that predates LLMs entirely and against which LLM-specific calibration claims should be read as an extension, not a first discovery.

Kadavath et al. extend the calibration question to LLM self-knowledge specifically: they show larger models are reasonably well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false formats, and — for open-ended generation, where a direct confidence score is not naturally available — introduce a self-evaluation protocol in which a model is asked to assess "P(True)," the probability that its own previously generated answer is correct, finding this self-assessed probability itself carries real, if imperfect, calibration signal, improving somewhat with model scale and with access to a menu of the model's own candidate answers.
Source: [Kadavath et al., "Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know," Anthropic, arXiv:2207.05221, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221).
**Tier: industry-lab preprint (Anthropic), not peer-reviewed.**
This paper is the sub-literature's most-cited anchor for LLM self-knowledge, but its tier matters for gate design: "the model can partially predict its own errors" is an industry-reported result never subjected to independent peer review, and this study found no peer-reviewed replication of its central P(True) self-evaluation claim in the sources checked — a gap carried forward.

## 7. Red-teaming: automated adversarial elicitation and its scaling behavior

Perez et al. show that an LLM can itself be used to automatically generate test cases that elicit harmful behavior from a target LLM — including offensive language, private-information leakage, and other undesired outputs — at a scale and diversity human annotators writing test cases by hand cannot match, and they release the resulting test-case datasets alongside their classifiers.
Source: [Perez et al., "Red Teaming Language Models with Language Models," EMNLP 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.225/).
**Tier: peer-reviewed (EMNLP).**
This is the sub-literature's clearest peer-reviewed anchor for automated (not purely human) red-teaming as a method, which matters because most of what follows in adversarial LLM testing builds on exactly this "use a model to attack a model" pattern.

Ganguli et al. report Anthropic's internal red-teaming program at larger scale: roughly 38,900 human-written red-team attacks against models of varying size and RLHF training amount, finding that RLHF-trained models became harder to red-team as scale increased (a scaling-behavior claim the paper reports as somewhat surprising against a naive expectation that larger models would simply be more capable of harm), and they release the attack transcripts and rules for their own red-teaming process as a methods contribution.
Source: [Ganguli et al., "Red Teaming Language Models to Reduce Harms: Methods, Scaling Behaviors, and Lessons Learned," Anthropic, arXiv:2209.07858, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.07858).
**Tier: industry-lab technical report, not peer-reviewed.**
Its scaling-behavior claim (harder to red-team at larger RLHF-trained scale) is exactly the kind of result this study flags as needing independent replication before being treated as a settled property of RLHF rather than a property of Anthropic's particular models and training pipeline at the time of the study.

## 8. Jailbreak taxonomy: universal adversarial suffixes as the field's sharpest anchor

Zou et al. demonstrate a greedy-coordinate-gradient (GCG) search procedure that appends an adversarial suffix to a harmful prompt, optimized against open-source aligned models, and show the resulting suffixes transfer — inducing objectionable output — even against closed commercial systems the attack was never optimized against, including systems from multiple vendors at the time of testing.
Source: [Zou, Wang, Carlini, Nasr, Kolter, and Fredrikson, "Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models," arXiv:2307.15043, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043).
**Tier: arXiv preprint, not peer-reviewed as of this study's verification.**
This paper's transferability finding is treated in this study as the sub-literature's sharpest and most load-bearing jailbreak result precisely because it is falsifiable and mechanistic (an optimization procedure, not a hand-written prompt trick), but its tier still means the claim has not passed independent peer review, and readers of this note should not conflate "highly cited and mechanistically clear" with "peer-reviewed."

## 9. Production reliability: a documented gap, not an oversight

This study searched specifically for peer-reviewed work on LLM system reliability engineering — retry policies, fallback routing, output-validation guardrails, circuit-breaking around unreliable model calls — and found this literature to be genuinely thin relative to how central these practices are to production LLM deployment.
The one clear peer-reviewed anchor located is Rebedea et al.'s NeMo Guardrails: a runtime toolkit, modeled on dialogue-management state machines, for attaching user-defined, model-independent, inspectable "rails" to an LLM application — topical rails (stay on topic), safety rails (block disallowed content or actions), and execution rails (constrain what external calls a model-driven dialogue can trigger).
Source: [Rebedea, Dinu, Sreedhar, Parisien, and Cohen, "NeMo Guardrails: A Toolkit for Controllable and Safe LLM Applications with Programmable Rails," EMNLP 2023 System Demonstrations](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-demo.40/).
**Tier: peer-reviewed (EMNLP System Demonstrations track), industry-authored (NVIDIA).**
Note its own tier qualification: a system-demonstrations track is peer-reviewed for the demonstration's validity and clarity, not for a research claim being tested against baselines the way a main-track paper would be — so this is the closest thing to a peer-reviewed anchor for guardrail-style reliability engineering, not a fully general validation of the approach.
Beyond this one toolkit paper, this study found no peer-reviewed literature evaluating retry-and-fallback strategies, output-validation pipelines, or circuit-breaker patterns specifically for LLM API reliability as a systems-engineering discipline; what exists instead is vendor documentation, cloud-provider architecture guides, and practitioner blog posts, none of which this study treats as citable primary sources by its own stated method.

## Relating findings to this library's own practice

This library is maintained by LLM agents running multi-agent study-and-verify workflows — this study itself is one instance of that pattern — and its news program depends on the same evaluation instruments surveyed here for its editorial-gate designs, per the [accountable-agentic-newsrooms memo](../../../../../../../../../../../communication-science-services-section/services/media-science-services-section/services/news-media-science-services-section/content/text/triage/accountable-agentic-newsrooms.md), whose research question 15 asks what benchmark would show that an agentic newsroom system preserves attribution, uncertainty, correction state, and accountability, and whose "editorial gate algebra" artifact composes support, attribution, legal, harm, rights, correction, standards, and publication-readiness gates.
This study's findings map onto that design as follows.

**Strong enough to serve as one signal feeding an automated gate, not the gate itself:** the benchmark-canon instruments (Section 1), read together with their documented error rates (Section 2), can serve as a coarse, periodic capability check on which model version is doing the editorial work — but Section 2's finding (an estimated 6.49% ground-truth error overall, far worse in specific subjects) means a benchmark score alone cannot certify a specific claim or draft as reliable; it certifies a model version's general competence band, nothing more granular.
LLM-as-judge (Sections 4–5) is validated well enough (above 80% agreement with human preference on open-ended chat, per Zheng et al.) to serve as a first-pass, always-on flag for likely-low-quality or likely-unsupported drafts entering a human review queue — exactly the "cheap first-pass signal, not a gate" role the claim-extraction study (Section 8 there) assigns to NLI and faithfulness metrics.
Calibration self-assessment (Kadavath et al., Section 6) is, at best, a soft internal signal a drafting agent could log alongside a claim (its own estimated P(True)) for a human reviewer to weigh — not a pass/fail threshold, both because the underlying result is industry-preprint tier and because its own reported calibration is imperfect even at frontier scale.

**Too weak, or actively unsafe, to serve as an automated gate on its own:** LLM-as-judge must never be the sole gate when the judge shares model lineage with the drafting agent, per Panickssery et al.'s self-preference finding (Section 5) — a directly applicable warning, since an agentic newsroom that drafts with one Claude-family model and grades with another Claude-family model risks exactly the self-preference effect this paper demonstrates causally, not just correlationally.
Red-teaming and jailbreak literature (Sections 7–8) describe adversarial robustness of the underlying model, not editorial correctness of a specific news claim, and this study found no evidence in its scope that red-teaming results transfer into a claim-level or article-level gate signal at all; they belong instead in the "which model versions are safe to deploy as drafting or judging agents in the first place" decision that precedes gate design, not inside the gate.
Production-reliability engineering (Section 9) is the sharpest gap: the near-absence of peer-reviewed literature on retry, fallback, and guardrail system design means the editorial gate algebra's own runtime reliability — what happens when a judge call times out, returns malformed output, or silently degrades — is presently a systems-engineering problem this sub-literature does not yet answer, and the news program's gate design should treat NeMo Guardrails (Section 9) as one implementation pattern to study, not as a validated reliability guarantee.

The claim-extraction and grounded-generation engineering study in `news-media-engineering-services-section` reaches a structurally identical conclusion from the NLP-engineering side — see [Study of Claim Extraction and Grounded Generation Engineering for the Claim Infrastructure](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../engineering-services-section/services/media-engineering-services-section/services/news-media-engineering-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-the-study-of-claim-extraction-and-grounded-generation-engineering.md), Section 8: every surveyed automated faithfulness and citation metric is a fallible gate signal, not a gate, because none was validated on open-world, time-sensitive, corrected-and-recorrected newsroom claims.
This study's evaluation-and-reliability findings independently converge on the same architectural conclusion from the model-evaluation side: no surveyed benchmark, judge, calibration method, or red-teaming result in this study's scope was validated at newsroom-claim granularity either, which is two separate literatures agreeing that the accountable-agentic-newsrooms memo's human-governed, override-capable gate design is the position the evidence actually supports, not merely a cautious default chosen in the absence of evidence.

## Gaps carried forward

- Kadavath et al.'s P(True) self-evaluation result (Section 6) has no peer-reviewed independent replication found in this study's scope; it remains an industry-preprint-tier finding cited widely without that qualification in most secondary literature this study encountered.
- Ganguli et al.'s scaling-behavior claim — that RLHF-trained models become harder to red-team as scale increases (Section 7) — is a single industry lab's technical-report finding on its own model family and training pipeline; no independent cross-lab replication was found and none was exhaustively searched for.
- Zou et al.'s universal-suffix transferability result (Section 8) is arXiv-preprint tier; a 2026-08-22 adversarial verification pass checked the NeurIPS 2023 proceedings listing directly (two independent web-search summaries had wrongly claimed a NeurIPS 2023 venue for this paper, apparently conflating it with an unrelated same-author NeurIPS 2023 poster) and found no matching entry, so as of that check the paper remains unpublished at any peer-reviewed venue this study could locate; the fast-moving jailbreak-defense literature responding to it (patches, detection methods) remains out of this study's scope entirely.
- Production reliability engineering for LLM systems (Section 9) is this study's largest documented gap: essentially no peer-reviewed literature on retry, fallback, or circuit-breaker design for LLM API calls was found, and the search for one was not exhaustive across systems-conference venues outside NLP/ML — a targeted follow-up search of OSDI/NSDI-adjacent venues is owed and not yet done.
- This study did not verify whether Zheng et al.'s reported >80% judge/human agreement rate (Section 4) has been independently replicated on tasks outside open-ended chat evaluation (e.g., on structured news-claim grading), which is precisely the transfer question Section 9's mapping to the news program depends on.
- Per this library's practice, this study has not yet had the independent adversarial verification pass its own sibling study (the alignment-and-interpretability study, [2026-08-22-note-on-the-study-of-alignment-and-interpretability-science.md](2026-08-22-note-on-the-study-of-alignment-and-interpretability-science.md)) names as a standing debt for this study batch; that pass is still owed here as well.
