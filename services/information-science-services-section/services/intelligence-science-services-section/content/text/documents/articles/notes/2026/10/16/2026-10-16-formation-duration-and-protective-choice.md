# Formation Duration and Protective Choice
## Distinguishing temporal provenance, irreplaceability, and goal shielding

**Lenticule Science — Intelligence Science. Working research paper, 16 October 2026.**  
**Status:** Evidence synthesis and original, testable hypothesis. No new experiment has been conducted. This is not a discovery report, validated diagnostic scale, preregistered study, or independently accepted research result.

### Abstract

People sometimes prioritize protecting an object that required a long time to form, even when the object has lower prospective value than an alternative exposed to harm by the protective action. This paper proposes **formation-duration protective prioritization** (FDPP) as a descriptive research candidate: increased protective priority caused by information about how long an object took to come into being. The corresponding **formation-duration protection bias** would require an additional demonstration that priority persists under a stated decision objective for which formation duration has no legitimate incremental bearing on value, reversibility, or loss. The distinctions are necessary because slow formation can indicate real irreplaceability, historical information, scarcity, or nonmonetary values.

A reported everyday incident motivates the analysis. A mineral-bearing rock weighted a plate of drying seeds. The observer moved the rock to prevent possible water damage to the mineral while failing to consider that removing the weight might expose the seeds to wind. The incident demonstrates an introspectively reported omission, not the cause of that omission or the amount of either prospective loss. Existing empirical work documents effort-based value inferences, special valuation of object histories, and inhibition of alternative goals during focal goal pursuit. Experimental replication of the effort heuristic has been mixed, and none of these findings alone establishes formation-duration protection bias. We separate duration of formation, chronological age, personal effort, historical distinctiveness, replacement latency, hazard, and action-induced externalities. We provide a conditional decision model, competing causal explanations, and experiments designed to disconfirm the proposed distinct mechanism. The practical outcome sought is better protection of *all affected resources*, not unconditional preference for newer or older things.

**Keywords:** formation duration; value attribution; loss prevention; selective consideration; decision making; goal shielding; irreversibility; object history; conservation.

## 1. Research question and scientific ownership

**Question:** Holding the decision's legitimate consequences and alternative objects constant, does longer *perceived formation duration* independently increase the probability of protecting an object, and can it cause the decision maker to neglect harms to another object that the protective action affects?

The question concerns selection and revision of protective priorities by a bounded human intelligence. Its current scientific carrier is Intelligence Science, under Information Science; its experimental methods are those of cognitive psychology (OECD FORD 5.1). Behavioral economics supplies tests of prospective value and conservation economics supplies the rational case for irreversibility. Sociology may later study how institutions transmit and reinforce these preferences. Those fields are cross-disciplinary consumers, not substitutes for psychological evidence.

The bounded literature search was performed on 16 October 2026 using publisher and scholarly record surfaces for combinations of "effort heuristic," "formation time," "object history," "authenticity," "naturalness bias," "goal shielding," "sunk cost," "irreversibility," and "protective decisions." Selection prioritized original experimental or theoretical sources and direct replication, not a systematic census of every paper. Absence of an exact matching term in this search does not establish priority of discovery.

## 2. Index incident: a useful observation, not a causal demonstration

**Reported observation (one self-report, 16 October 2026):** Seeds were drying on a paper plate held down by a rock. The rock was said to contain water-sensitive zeolitic mineral material. The observer removed the rock to prevent potential mineral loss upon wetting. This removal increased the possibility that the seeds could blow away. The observer reports that the competing risk to the seeds did not enter conscious consideration during the action.

Known from the report: the rock was chosen for immediate protection, the seeds had depended on it as a weight, and the competing risk was overlooked. **Unknown:** whether any seed was lost; actual wind, wetting, and comparative damage probabilities; whether the specimen's reactive component was a zeolite framework, exchangeable ion, soluble accessory mineral, or another constituent; scarcity, cultural value, future biological value, and replacement costs. Zeolite chemistry cannot be inferred from the phrase "water-soluble zeolite" alone: framework dissolution and aqueous ion exchange are distinct processes; mineral-specific stability depends on the material and conditions [R13–R14].

The report is consistent with several mechanisms. Geological antiquity may have cued special value; long physical formation could have cued replacement difficulty; fear of irreversible mineral loss could have been rational; and activating "protect the rock" could have shielded awareness of the seeds. The rock also had a **second role**, restraining the plate. Removing a protection object can remove its protection of another object. This coupling is analytically central.

The event is not evidence that one person's stable preference, a species-wide bias, or a specific neurophysiological process has been established.

## 3. Definitions that must not be conflated

| Construct | Operational meaning | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formation duration, F | Time between onset and completion of the process yielding the particular object, with process and boundaries specified | Candidate cue causing prioritization |
| Chronological age, A | Time since the specified object's formation completed | A rock can be very old without having formed slowly |
| Lineage or system age, L | Age of a precursor, inherited history, species, mineral deposit, or ecosystem | A seed inherits a long evolutionary lineage while an individual seed can form quickly |
| Production effort, E | Human or other agent labor, intensity, and opportunity cost expended in manufacture | Core to effort-heuristic and sunk-investment alternatives; not synonymous with passive elapsed time |
| Replacement latency, R | Minimum feasible delay to obtain a functionally adequate replacement within the decision horizon | A potentially rational reason to prioritize a slowly regenerating resource |
| Historical identity, H | Individuating provenance, authenticity, original sequence, or historical information lost if this specimen is destroyed | Potentially legitimate intrinsic or instrumental value even when a functional duplicate exists |
| Prospective value, V | The values and services lost under the stated objective and horizon if the object is damaged | Determines stakes, but may be plural and nonmonetary |
| Preventable exposure, D(a) | Change in probability and severity of loss produced by action a, across *all* affected resources | Protecting X can increase hazard to Y |
| Salience / goal accessibility, S | Probability that a decision maker actively represents each relevant object, objective, and consequence | Separates priority preference from failure to consider an alternative |

A formation-time preference is **descriptive**: among matched alternatives, the person more often protects an item labeled as slower to form. A **bias relative to a specific objective** occurs when that preference demonstrably produces choices dominated on that objective despite equal or lower legitimate protective benefit. A person's warranted valuation of a unique history is not refuted simply by a different researcher's price list. An inference from historical duration to real replacement difficulty may also be reasonable under incomplete information. These cases must not be mislabeled as irrational.

### 3.1 A geological-timescale category error

An item's geological *age* and its time *spent forming* are different variables. Some objects may form rapidly and remain old; others may require extended growth but have formed recently. The seeds also contain several time levels: duration of individual seed development, parental growth, lineage history, and potential future growth. The original case does not say which timescale the observer was using. Manipulations that merely contrast "ancient rock" with "recent seeds" would confound F, A, L, material class, history, and future potential.

## 4. What existing scholarship actually establishes

The following are **evidence rows**, not assumed proof of the new construct. The empirical bridge to action selection remains open.

| Evidence | Source and finding in scope | Limit on inference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E1. Effort heuristic | Kruger et al. (2004) manipulated believed production time/effort for poems, paintings and armor; higher attributed effort sometimes increased quality, liking and monetary valuation, with ambiguity moderating results [R1]. | Judgments of human-made work do not show passive geological time changing preservation choices. |
| E2. Replication challenge | Ziano et al. (2023) ran preregistered replication attempts totaling N=1,405. Support varied by task; liking/quality was partially reproduced, while monetary-value effects failed in two separate-evaluation replications [R2]. | Strong, universal effort effects would be an overclaim. Decision to protect an object was not the dependent variable. |
| E3. Interpretation changes effect | Cho and Schwarz (2008) experimentally found that theories emphasizing talent rather than effort could remove the usual quality inference from more producer effort [R3]. | The causal cue might be a learned interpretation of time, not a durable preference. |
| E4. Sunk-cost decisions | Arkes and Blumer (1985) studied greater willingness to continue an undertaking after one's own past investments [R4]. | Protecting a rock one did not create is not, by itself, the sunk-cost effect. |
| E5. Self-made valuation | Norton, Mochon, and Ariely (2012) found higher valuation of successfully self-assembled products in four studies [R5]. | No self-production or successful personal effort was reported for the rock. |
| E6. Originality | Newman and Bloom (2012) found a special role for creative performance and contact history in valuing originals versus duplicates [R6]. | Historical/authentic value is not identical to duration of formation. |
| E7. Object histories | Pesowski and Friedman (2019), four experiments with 400 children ages 4–7, found that distinctive past histories influenced value judgments of ordinary objects [R7]. | Distinctiveness, not elapsed creation time alone, was manipulated; no protection tradeoff was measured. |
| E8. Naturalness | Rozin (2005) found that an item's processing history strongly influences judgments of naturalness [R8]. A 2026 review summarizes broader natural-is-better preferences [R9]. | Material naturalness and process history could confound time. |
| E9. Goal shielding | Shah, Friedman, and Kruglanski (2002) reported six studies in which activation of a focal goal inhibited accessibility of alternatives [R10]. | Offers a candidate account for the seeds being overlooked, not evidence that formation time activated that goal. |
| E10. Rational irreversibility | Arrow and Fisher (1974) show why uncertainty plus irreversibility can favor preservation in some settings [R11]. Cannon et al. (2022) identify long-regeneration and historical/genetic benefits of ancient trees [R12]. | Some preference for hard-to-regenerate materials is well-justified. Neither source reports the proposed psychological residual. |

**Literature conclusion:** Supported: historical process cues sometimes change valuations, alternative goals may be suppressed, and irreversibility can justify asymmetric protection. **Unknown:** whether passive formation duration has an independent causal effect on *protective action* after plausible value, age, rarity, history, and replacement pathways are distinguished. **Disputed in scope:** generality and size of the effort heuristic, especially for monetary value [R2–R3]. This paper proposes a discriminating investigation rather than renaming the entire literature.

## 5. Decision model: the loss prevented, not the object's age

Let a be a feasible action, Y the vector of outcomes for **all affected objects**, I the information actually available, G an expressly stated objective, and C(a) action costs. A normative comparison can be represented by:

$$
EU(a \mid I,G)
= \sum_{y} P(Y=y \mid a,I)\,U_G(y) - C(a).
$$

This equation does **not** require that every relevant value be assigned a dollar price. If values are noncommensurable, compare actions for Pareto dominance and report any unresolved tradeoff instead of inventing utility weights. Historical identity, option value, biodiversity, welfare, cultural rights, and functional replacement must enter G only when supported by the actual decision context.

A coupled decision permits both favorable and adverse effects. Moving the rock may reduce its hazard while increasing the seeds' hazard. The marginal contribution of any action must therefore evaluate joint outcomes rather than only the object named in the immediate protective goal. If replacing the weight with a safe alternative before moving the rock protects both at feasible cost and no additional relevant harm, it can dominate a rock-only response.

A descriptive model for experimental data is:

$$
\operatorname{logit} P(\mathrm{protect}_i)
= \alpha + \beta_F \log(F_i)
+ \beta_A \log(A_i)
+ \beta_R \log(R_i)
+ \beta_E E_i + \beta_H H_i
+ \beta_V V_i + \beta_S S_i
+ \text{registered interactions}.
$$

The symbols are conceptual; no coefficients have been estimated. Correlations among natural F, A, R, and H would make observational coefficients difficult to identify. **Randomizing credible formation-duration information**, while jointly manipulating replacement delay, history and effort, is the principal route to causal inference. Post-treatment perceived rarity or value are candidate *mediators*, not covariates that may be casually controlled away to declare a direct effect.

A positive experimental beta_F demonstrates a **formation-duration choice effect**, not automatically an error. The stronger bias claim requires a specified G under which the choice is demonstrably worse, plus evidence that the duration manipulation did not accidentally alter a legitimate attribute.

## 6. Competing explanations and their discriminators

### H1. Independent formation-duration preference

**Hypothesis:** When object type, condition, origin, age, unique history, availability, hazard, labor and stated prospective value are controlled, merely learning that an object took longer to form raises its chance of protection. This would establish the proposed descriptive phenomenon in that population and task. A trial with objectively inferior outcomes tests the narrower **goal-incongruent** version.

**Falsifier:** Under credible, adequately powered matched conditions, the remaining effect is absent or smaller than a preregistered smallest effect of interest; or the effect vanishes upon separating F from A and R.

### H2. Rational inference about replacement

**Alternative:** Slow formation predicts long replacement delay or genuine loss of unique historical information. Behavior could be adaptive even when market price is low.

**Discriminator:** Vary F and R independently and compare functional replacement to restoration of the identical original. Explicitly provide future availability and measure comprehension. An effect confined to high R or valuable H undermines an independent bias label.

### H3. Effort, sunk cost, or creator identification

**Alternative:** A long process is interpreted as someone's toil, an investment to avoid wasting, or a personal creation worth defending.

**Discriminator:** Contrast passive mineral growth with deliberate craft, equal physical results, and owned versus unowned specimens. Independently manipulate actual worker-hours and elapsed process duration. An effect limited to a maker's investment belongs to those existing literatures.

### H4. History, naturalness, rarity, or age

**Alternative:** The rock is selected because it is an ancient, original, scarce, or natural object, not because its formation took more time.

**Discriminator:** Cross A and F where physically credible; hold replicas and inventories constant; manipulate provenance authenticity, geological versus synthetic origin, and disclosed quantity. Pretest scenarios for perceived credibility, uniqueness and naturalness without using post-treatment failures as a selective exclusion rule.

### H5. Focal-goal shielding and coupled-resource neglect

**Alternative:** Once "keep the rock dry" became a goal, the unmentioned seed-loss hazard was not represented. Formation duration may be unrelated to the omission.

**Discriminator:** In interactive scenarios, vary which object is presented first, whether it has a long formation history, whether actions have hidden effects on a second object, and whether a non-leading prompt asks for *all consequences*. Measure first action, spontaneous mention of the second object, probability estimates, and corrected action. If omission occurs irrespective of F but responds to a joint-risk check, goal shielding is a better explanation.

### H6. Time as a finite moral or historical resource

**Alternative interpretation, presently philosophical:** Past duration may be treated as time "stored" in a material or as a special custodial obligation. This might be an explicit aesthetic, cultural, moral or historical value rather than an unconscious judgment error. Research must elicit these commitments, not define them away as irrational. No neural, evolutionary or mathematical mechanism is asserted from the analogy.

## 7. Proposed empirical program

### 7.1 Preliminary item validation

Construct pairs of credible objects with material descriptions and provenance that can be varied without material contradiction. Distinguish **elapsed processing duration** from **age since completion**: for example, a rapidly crystallized sample completed long ago and a slowly crystallized sample completed recently. Use independently pretested laboratory-grown crystalline or other naturally self-organizing samples so passive formation does not imply worker-hours. Explain replacement inventory separately; a specimen might take years to grow but an equivalent specimen could already be in stock for next-day delivery. In secondary field-transfer items use rock/seed or old-tree/sapling pairs, but do not treat these as matched laboratory objects.

Pretest participants should report what they understood as formation duration, age, human labor, scarcity, replacement latency, nature, cultural history and objective worth. Revise implausible items **before** preregistering the confirmatory study. The original incident's actual mineral is not needed as an experimental prop.

### 7.2 Study 1: Does duration itself alter protection?

**Design:** Randomized, counterbalanced vignette decisions. Manipulate short versus long F, shorter versus longer A, and fast versus delayed R orthogonally in coherent item sets. Specify common condition, hazard probabilities, acquisition costs and decision objective. Add separate, adequately sized blocks for passive formation versus human manufacture, unique-versus-replicable history, and high-versus-low outcome evaluability. The primary task is a costly choice of which item to protect; liking and selling-price estimates are secondary rather than proxy endpoints.

**Primary estimand:** Marginal difference in probability of protecting the designated item between long-F and short-F assignment, averaged over prespecified matched item sets at fixed G and R. Random assignment enables this intent-to-treat contrast; item and participant random effects handle repeated choices. Prespecify the contrast in risk difference units along with logistic-model estimates and confidence intervals.

**Critical negative control:** State that two alternatives are otherwise functionally interchangeable, equally available, and provide equal explicitly assessed services; swap the time labels while retaining the same choice set and order counterbalancing. A duration effect conditional on belief that one is rarer remains inconclusive until that interpretation is separated.

### 7.3 Study 2: Do protective actions neglect secondary hazards?

**Design:** Build interactive, ethically harmless simulated environments. In the focal analogue, a weight protects drying seeds while the weight itself is water-sensitive. Options include leaving the system unchanged, moving the weight without a replacement, or securing the plate with a replacement before moving the specimen. Randomize formation-history information for the weight, whether information is available about the seeds' future benefits, which hazard is announced first, and the presence of a short neutral check: "List the objects whose risk this action changes."

**Primary outcome:** Selection of an action that increases avoidable loss under the stipulated objective when a feasible dominating action is available. **Secondary outcomes:** unprompted mention of seed risk, sequence of considered options, decision time, stated confidence, and response to a delayed all-object evaluation. Code verbal reasons blind to condition; code "not mentioned" as an omission, not evidence of an unconscious mechanism.

**Identification:** A duration manipulation that increases neglect is evidence of a total effect of the cue on omission in this task. If the check reduces omissions irrespective of duration, it supports a practical joint-consequence safeguard but does not prove a particular neuronal cause. A protection-both option should never be treated as free unless the vignette expressly establishes its feasibility and costs.

### 7.4 Study 3: External validity and normative boundaries

Test in materially varied domains: specimen archives, seeds and crops, cultural artifacts, ecological restoration, infrastructure, and digital records. Include decisions in which long formation genuinely implies delayed replacement and distinct decisions in which it does not. Record participant values and objectives prospectively. Include direct comparisons between ages, formation durations and restoration times and assess within-person consistency across task contexts. No domain should be pooled into a "universal" effect without evidence of measurement invariance.

### 7.5 Preregistration and analytical safeguards

**Not yet preregistered.** Freeze primary outcomes, matched materials, coding manual, exclusions, the smallest practically relevant effect, and sample size based on simulation of the clustered design **before collecting confirmatory data**. Conduct a small feasibility pilot separately and label it exploratory. Use an intention-to-treat contrast and disclose all factorial cells. Model participant and stimulus heterogeneity, check learning/order, report all effects including disconfirming estimates, and control the multiple testing family for secondary claims. Consider equivalence tests for a bounded null instead of saying nonsignificance means no effect. Make deidentified materials, code and data available subject to actual consent and privacy constraints. Do not infer general population prevalence from a convenience sample.

An experimenter-specified objective can establish **goal-incongruent** choice relative to that objective; it cannot prove that every personally meaningful preference for the object is irrational. Supplement with a within-person test in which the participant explicitly ranks outcomes *before* formation details are provided, then observe whether the cue causes a choice that contradicts their stated ranking. This design still requires controls for preference updating when new information is genuinely valuable.

Ethics: participants give informed consent; no real mineral, seed collection, cultural property, ecosystem or livelihood is endangered to generate a trial. Respect differences in cultural obligations and provenance and avoid treating living species as interchangeable objects. A field replication would require a separate protocol and appropriate custodial consent.

## 8. Predictions whose failure would change the theory

- **P1 (duration residual):** In matched passive-formation choices, long F increases protection relative to short F, at the registered practical effect threshold. A well-powered near-zero or reversed result falsifies general FDPP for that task.
- **P2 (age-duration separation):** Any F effect survives orthogonal age manipulation; otherwise the claim narrows to an age or history cue.
- **P3 (replacement mediation):** Some, possibly all, F preference is explained by randomized R or by explicitly valued H. Full explanation by these factors prevents claiming an independent error.
- **P4 (omission):** F can increase action-induced neglect of a second object under conditions with coupled risks. A stable F effect on preference but no change in consideration separates valuation from goal shielding.
- **P5 (joint-consequence safeguard):** Asking about all affected objects decreases dominated actions. Failure under realistic choices blocks a claim that a short check debiases decisions.
- **P6 (cross-domain):** Effects need not transfer from craft objects to passive geological formation. Strong context specificity would argue for a family of established heuristics rather than a new general bias.

These are predictions, not observations. If P1 fails but P4 or P5 holds, the practical contribution may be a **secondary-hazard detection protocol** rather than a formation-time theory. The negative result would be scientifically useful and should replace the stronger hypothesis in this source, not be hidden beneath an appended caveat.

## 9. A provisional protective-choice procedure

This is a low-cost reasoning aid justified by the **structure of the decision**, not by demonstrated treatment efficacy.

1. Name the item you are about to protect and the immediate hazard.
2. Identify every other item that currently depends on the first item or on the arrangement you will change.
3. For each candidate action, compare *prospective* avoidable harm to all those items, including genuine restoration delay and distinctive historical value.
4. Look for a feasible action that preserves the first item without sacrificing its supporting function for the second.
5. If an important value cannot be compared in common units, record the conflict instead of silently assigning the oldest item priority.

In the motivating arrangement the obvious candidate to evaluate is **secure the seeds with another suitable weight, then protect the rock**. Whether this was feasible at the actual moment is unknown. The purpose of the check is to reveal coupled resources before their protection is inadvertently withdrawn. It is not advice to disregard old minerals or to privilege seeds in every case.
