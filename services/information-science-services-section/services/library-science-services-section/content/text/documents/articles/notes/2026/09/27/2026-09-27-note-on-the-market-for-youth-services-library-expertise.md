# The market for youth services library expertise

A [youth services librarian](../../../../../../glossary/terms/youth-services-librarianship.md)
is trained to build and weed collections for children and teens, advise young
readers and their adults ([readers' advisory](../../../../../../glossary/terms/readers-advisory.md)),
and design programs. Most of that work is done by library employees. This note
asks who pays for it from outside: which institutions buy it as a service,
which consumers buy products made from it, and what each pays. Evidence was
gathered on the public web on 2026-09-27; every figure carries its source.

## 1. Minnesota: a compliance gap the law opened

- **Every public, school, charter-school, and public college library must now
  have a materials policy administered by a qualified person**, a licensed
  media specialist, an MLS holder, or a trained collection manager, and must
  report each completed challenge to the state (Minn. Stat. 134.51, 2024;
  school collection development plans under 124D.991, 2023). The law is set out
  in the law-science
  [note on Minnesota law for library materials policies](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/law-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/27/2026-09-27-note-on-minnesota-law-for-library-materials-policies.md).
- **Many of those libraries have no one qualified.** The 2024 Minnesota K-12
  School Library Census: 46 percent of schools without a licensed library media
  specialist, a quarter of respondents unable to find a qualified candidate,
  about 61 percent of southwest-Minnesota schools relying on a single
  paraprofessional, nearly half of media centers on budgets under $5,000
  ([Public News Service](https://www.publicnewsservice.org/2025-05-06/education/nearly-half-of-mn-school-libraries-lack-licensed-media-specialist/a96609-1);
  [census report to the Senate](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/archive/minutes/senate/2025/fineduc/20250220/fineduc_20250220_MCMT-MN-School-Library-Report.pdf)).
- **Literacy law adds training demand.** The READ Act (2023) requires K-3
  screeners, evidence-based curricula, staff training, and annual local
  literacy plans ([MDE](https://education.mn.gov/MDE/dse/READ/)). It creates no
  vendor mandate; its demand for outside help is indirect.

The gap is concrete: a written policy, a named administrator, and a
reporting procedure that small boards and districts must have and often
cannot staff. It is paid for from operating budgets, in small amounts, by
institutions a vendor must first know.

## 2. Programming money and how a vendor reaches it

- Minnesota's 12 regional public library systems share a $2.5 million a year
  Legacy (Arts and Cultural Heritage Fund) formula grant, meant in part to
  "promote and share the work of Minnesota authors, including authors from
  diverse backgrounds" ([House Research](https://www.house.mn.gov/hrd/pubs/artculthf.pdf)).
- The Arrowhead Library System (seven northeastern counties, Duluth's region)
  received $231,355 in FY2025 Legacy funding and made 40 mini-grants to member
  libraries in 2025 ([legacy.mn.gov](https://www.legacy.mn.gov/node/10035477);
  [ALS](https://www.alslib.info/blog/2025-als-mini-grants-examples-in-the-als-region-part-1/)).
- The Minnesota Humanities Center runs cultural heritage, festival, and
  emergency grants, the last prioritizing Indigenous and historically
  underrepresented communities ([mnhum.org](https://www.mnhum.org/what-we-do/grants/)).
- Legacy funds intergenerational learning programs, including a digital
  literacy pilot for grandparents raising grandchildren
  ([legacy.mn.gov](https://www.legacy.mn.gov/projects/intergenerational-learning-programs);
  [grandfamilies.org](https://www.grandfamilies.org/Portals/0/State%20Fact%20Sheets/Minnesota%20Fact%20Sheet%202024%20Update.pdf)).

These grants go to institutions, not to companies. A for-profit practitioner
is paid as the vendor the grantee contracts for a program: a flat facilitator
fee written into the library's mini-grant budget.

Duluth's own public library is expanding youth work (a decodable-books
collection in youth services from March 2025, a teen gaming pilot, a youth
careers internship, a foundation campaign for a new downtown youth area:
[City of Duluth](https://duluthmn.gov/web-subscriptions/View-press-release/?prid=7463)).
The Fond du Lac Band runs its own community library and the Ruth A. Myers
Library at its tribal college ([FDLTCC](https://fdltcc.edu/academics/library-services/library-information/)).
No Duluth-area practitioner specializing in children's collections or
programming was found; the local bookstores (Zenith, Fitger's, Chester Creek)
carry general stock.

## 3. Freelance lines and what they pay

| Line | Pay | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Library consulting | median library-consultant pay about $52/hour, middle half $39–69 ([ZipRecruiter](https://www.ziprecruiter.com/Salaries/Library-Consultant-Salary)); ALA's intellectual freedom office charges "from a few hundred dollars for an hour to greater amounts for multi-day training" ([ALA OIF](https://www.ala.org/aboutala/offices/oif/consulting)); Pasadena Public Library budgeted up to $20,000 to review and retrain staff on storytime ([ALA Connect](https://connect.ala.org/core/discussion/advice-on-a-consulting-fee)) | the strongest line; scoped projects priced flat |
| Program facilitation | libraries often run bookbinding and zine programs free; venues charging attendees ask $20–50 for two hours ([The Huntington](https://www.huntington.org/event/bookbinding-workshop)); facilitator fees are set per contract | viable when grant- or budget-funded |
| Trade-journal reviewing | *School Library Journal* reviewers are volunteers; *Booklist* has paid $15 a review and was not taking applicants ([Freedom With Writing](https://www.freedomwithwriting.com/freedom/uncategorized/20-publishers-that-pay-for-book-reviews/)); Kirkus freelance pay is reported, not confirmed, at $50–75 a review | a credential, not an income |
| Sensitivity and authenticity reading | $0.015–0.038 a word or $50–60 an hour (Editorial Freelancers Association 2025 rates, via [Number Analytics](https://www.numberanalytics.com/blog/sensitivity-readers-childrens-literature)) | sold on the reader's own lived identity matching the text's; not a curation skill |
| Children's book subscription boxes | Literati $9.95–11.49 a month; Bookroo $21.95 ([My Subscription Addiction](https://www.mysubscriptionaddiction.com/best-subscription-boxes/best-book-boxes)); OurShelves already sells a diverse-books box for ages 0–8 ([ourshelves.com](https://www.ourshelves.com/)) | crowded, with a niche incumbent; inventory and sales tax |
| Booklists with affiliate links | Bookshop.org pays non-bookstore affiliates 10 percent ([Bookshop](https://support.bookshop.org/en/support/solutions/articles/65000169802-what-is-the-difference-between-a-bookstore-and-non-bookstore-affiliate-)) | a trickle; worth it as the audience channel |
| Teachers Pay Teachers resources | free-tier sellers keep 55 percent; earnings concentrate in curriculum bundles ([SEOTpreneur](https://seotpreneur.com/can-tpt-really-replace-your-teaching-income-in-2025/)) | thin for booklists |
| Paid children's-book newsletters | *Indie Kids Books* reports about 1,800 subscribers and 100+ paid at $7 a month after about three years ([indiekidsbooks.com](https://www.indiekidsbooks.com/p/indie-kids-books-is-a-substack-bestseller)) | slow; years to matter |

## 4. Who is already in the field

- Nonprofit list-makers with large audiences: Social Justice Books (Teaching
  for Change), We Need Diverse Books, The Conscious Kid.
- A solo curator: Here Wee Read (booklists and a Bookshop.org shop), showing a
  single person can hold a list-making audience.
- Debbie Reese's *American Indians in Children's Literature*, a Nambé Pueblo
  scholar's critical practice with a published consulting-fee page. It is the
  precedent for paid Indigenous children's-literature critique, and it rests on
  the critic's own standing in the community.
- Diverse BookFinder (Bates College), a research database rather than a
  vendor.

## 5. What follows

The strongest paid demand for a youth services librarian outside a library job,
in Minnesota now, is **compliance and collection work sold to small public,
school, charter, and tribal libraries**: the 134.51 policy, a named qualified
administrator, and collection audits, priced flat by scope. Second is
**programming delivered as a vendor under institutions' grants**. Consumer
products (boxes, list storefronts, paid newsletters) are real but crowded and
slow, and serve best as the channel that makes a practitioner findable.
