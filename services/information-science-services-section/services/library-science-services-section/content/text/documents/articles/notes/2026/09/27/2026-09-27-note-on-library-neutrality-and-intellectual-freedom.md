# Library neutrality and intellectual freedom

## The argument

At the American Library Association's 2018 Midwinter Meeting in Denver, ALA
President Jim Neal's program staged a debate, "Are Libraries Neutral?"
([*American Libraries*, 2018-06-01](https://americanlibrariesmagazine.org/2018/06/01/are-libraries-neutral/)).

**For neutrality:**

- **James LaRue**, then director of ALA's Office for Intellectual Freedom:
  "Neutrality is about the refusal to deny people access to a shared resource
  just because we don't like the way they think."
- **Em Claire Knowles** (Simmons): neutrality as "the first rung on the ladder
  to advocacy and social justice", demanding deliberate, balanced collection
  work.

**Against:**

- **Chris Bourg** (MIT Libraries): a library "represents a decision about how a
  community spends its resources and those decisions are never neutral—they are
  value-laden."
- **R. David Lankes**: neutrality forecloses the librarian's role as a change
  agent in the community.

**Respondents:**

- **Emily Drabinski** made the material point: if I buy this book, I cannot buy
  that one. Selection under scarcity is never neutral. See "What Is Critical
  About Critical Librarianship?", *Art Libraries Journal* 44(2), 2019
  ([CUNY Academic Works](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/gc_pubs/537/)).
- **Emily Knox** showed how "neutral" non-decisions favor the existing order.

Sam Popowich's *Confronting the Democratic Discourse of Librarianship: A Marxist
Approach* (Library Juice Press, 2019) goes further. It reads the profession's
"democratic" self-understanding as a liberal strand that presents librarianship
as neutral and apolitical while it is tied to the development of capitalism.

Two ALA actions followed:

- **Meeting rooms, 2018–2019.** In June 2018 ALA Council revised the "Meeting
  Rooms" interpretation of the Library Bill of Rights to say a library that
  lends rooms to civic groups "cannot exclude religious, social, civic, partisan
  political, or hate groups." Council rescinded the revision by online vote in
  August 2018, 140 to 4, and a new interpretation was adopted on 2019-01-29
  ([ALA](https://www.ala.org/advocacy/intfreedom/librarybill/interpretations/meetingrooms);
  [*Library Journal*](https://www.libraryjournal.com/story/free-speech-debate-erupts-with-alas-inclusion-of-hate-groups-in-revision-of-bill-of-rights-interpretation)).
- **2021.** ALA Council's "Resolution to Condemn White Supremacy and Fascism as
  Antithetical to Library Work" (CD#43) "acknowledges the role of neutrality
  rhetoric in emboldening and encouraging white supremacy and fascism." It is the
  Council's formal statement on neutrality; no 2018 resolution on neutrality
  exists, although one is sometimes cited.

## Intellectual freedom is a different claim

The two words are often run together. **Intellectual freedom** is a claim
about the reader: a person's right to read, and the library's duty not to
restrict that right for the views a work holds (Library Bill of Rights, Articles
I–III, V). **Neutrality** is a claim about the institution: that its choices
favor no one. A library can hold the first without the second. It can say
openly that its collection serves its whole community, and that it buys with
purpose for readers the market underserves, while refusing to remove anything
for its viewpoint. That is the position of the critics who still defend
intellectual freedom (Drabinski; Knox). It is also what Minnesota's 2024 law
requires: Minn. Stat. 134.51 forbids removal "based solely on its viewpoint"
and leaves selection to professional judgment
([law note](../../../../../../../../../../../social-science-services-section/services/law-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/27/2026-09-27-note-on-minnesota-law-for-library-materials-policies.md)).

The house's critical theory paper on formal neutrality
(`critical-theory-science-services-section`, 2026-07-03, *Formal Neutrality,
Real Dependence*) traces the same mechanism in organizations: a formally neutral
rule built around an unencumbered ideal subject imposes unequal real costs on
those it does not picture. In a library, a "neutral" collection built by
default from reviews, bestseller lists and vendor profiles reproduces the
market's picture of who reads.

## Esther Davis's essay

Esther's 2020 essay "Carnegie and Public Libraries" (republished on
bindinggenerations.net under its original date) argues that the self-education
myth Carnegie built into American public libraries undercuts class solidarity.
It stands with Popowich's historical critique. It argues about what the
institution is for, not about whether it should restrict what people read.

## For a materials policy

A policy written to 134.51 can hold both, and Binding Generations' Read Free
Policy is built this way:

- a selection statement that names the library's purpose, including service to
  readers the market underserves (the institution is not neutral, and says so);
- a reconsideration procedure that judges a challenged work by the selection
  criteria and never by its viewpoint (the reader's freedom holds);
- the Library Bill of Rights and the Freedom to Read Statement incorporated by
  reference.

Each board still decides its own wording.
