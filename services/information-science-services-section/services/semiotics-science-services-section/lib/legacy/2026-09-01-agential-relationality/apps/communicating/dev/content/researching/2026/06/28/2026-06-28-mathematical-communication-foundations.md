# Mathematical communication: accountable closure

Mathematical communication is the discipline by which mathematical artifacts make their required closures inspectable, repairable, and transportable. It studies how definitions frame future use, how proof gaps are licensed or refused, how notation and diagrams compress structure, how proofs support plan reconstruction, and how mathematical identity survives movement across prose, notation, example, diagram, code, and formal proof script.

The central claim is simple and strict: mathematical rigor is not exhausted by formal correctness. Formal correctness audits inference. Mathematical practice also needs representational closure, sequential closure, operational closure, social closure, and register transport. A mathematical artifact is legitimate not merely when its local claims are true, but when its communicative form lets a competent reader reconstruct what kind of work the artifact asks them to perform.

This is not a demotion of proof. Proof remains one of mathematics' strongest instruments for accountable inference. The point is that proof belongs to a larger ecology. Mathematicians do not meet mathematical objects only as finished derivations. They meet them through definitions, examples, notations, diagrams, analogies, computations, seminars, proof sketches, software, formal proof libraries, failed attempts, classifications, counterexamples, benchmarks, institutional review, and narrative bridges. Those are not decoration around mathematics. They are media through which mathematical objects become available for thought.

Good mathematics supports reconstruction. Excellent mathematics makes reconstruction portable across registers.

## Why McCloud belongs here

Scott McCloud's *Understanding Comics* is useful because it gives an operational vocabulary for framed sequential artifacts. Comics create meaning by arranging bounded units in sequence and making readers complete the transitions between them. A panel shows something. A gutter withholds something. Closure is the reader's completion of the withheld transition. Icons compress detail in order to amplify recognition. Sequence makes the closures readable.

The transfer to mathematics is not that mathematics should imitate comics. The transfer is structural. Mathematical artifacts also work through framed units, disciplined omissions, compressive signs, ordered traversal, and reader-completed transitions.

A definition does not contain all of its consequences. A theorem does not contain all of its motivating examples. A proof does not contain every inference. A diagram does not contain every proposition it licenses. A formalization does not contain the whole communicative situation of the informal proof it verifies. A research paper does not contain the notebooks, conversations, failures, and institutional filters through which its object became stable. Mathematics works by framing enough structure that the reader can complete the rest without completing it arbitrarily.

McCloud is not the only source. Will Eisner gives an earlier craft vocabulary for sequential art. Neil Cohn gives a cognitive and linguistic account of sequential images. Thierry Groensteen gives a more formal semiotic account of comics as a system. McCloud's value here is that the terms are unusually operational: panel, gutter, closure, icon, sequence.

The metaphor stops where medium-specific standards begin. Comics page layout, lettering, and drawing style do not transfer whole into mathematics. What transfers is the general mechanism of framed sequential meaning. Mathematical communication then recalibrates the mechanism under mathematical standards: proof obligations, definitions, theorem status, diagrammatic legitimacy, formalization, code invariants, citations, and audience-specific gap norms.

## The core vocabulary

Communicative closure is the reader's disciplined completion of a transition between framed units. It is not a defect. No mathematical artifact can state everything at once. The defect is unaccountable closure: a gap the reader must cross without enough constraints to know whether the crossing is legitimate.

A mathematical panel is a bounded communicative unit with recognizable mathematical status. Definitions, theorems, proofs, lemmas, examples, counterexamples, diagrams, equations, code blocks, proof assistant declarations, benchmarks, conjectures, and narrative bridges are all panels when the artifact frames them as units.

A mathematical gutter is the transition between mathematical panels where closure is required. The gutter between a definition and theorem asks the reader to understand how the definition creates the theorem's object. The gutter between diagram and formula asks which visual features are being formalized. The gutter between code and proof asks which invariant execution preserves. The gutter between proof sketch and proof asks which obligations are being delegated.

A mathematical icon is a compressive sign that removes detail in order to preserve manipulable structure. Notation, commutative diagrams, knot diagrams, string diagrams, type signatures, inference rules, canonical examples, and code interfaces can all act iconically. Good icons compress operative structure. Bad icons compress prestige.

A sequence is the ordered path through panels. Dependency order, proof order, discovery order, pedagogical order, historical order, implementation order, and formalization order support different closures. There is no single correct sequence for every artifact, but every sequence has consequences.

Transport is the preservation of mathematical identity across representational registers. A mathematical idea may move from prose to notation, notation to example, example to diagram, diagram to proof obligation, proof obligation to formal proof script, formal proof script to implementation, and implementation back to use. The movement need not be lossless, but it must have an identity criterion and a loss account.

## Accountable closure

Accountable closure is the normative center of this theory. A mathematical artifact may ask the reader to perform work, but it must make that work classifiable and repairable.

Closure can be accountable in several ways:

- It can be inferentially accountable, as in a proof whose steps are discharged or properly delegated.
- It can be recognitionally accountable, as in an example that trains a reader to recognize a structure.
- It can be representationally accountable, as in a diagram whose constitutive and incidental features are distinguished.
- It can be sequentially accountable, as in a proof plan or pedagogical order that tells the reader why this comes before that.
- It can be operationally accountable, as in code whose types, tests, and invariants show how a structure behaves under computation.
- It can be socially accountable, as in a proof gap whose acceptability is marked relative to a field, venue, or audience.
- It can be transport-accountable, as in a formalization note that states what was preserved, what changed, and what was lost when an informal claim became a formal one.

Unaccountable closure is the failure case. It appears when an artifact forces the reader to supply missing mathematics without enough constraint: a hidden lemma, an unintroduced notation, an overclaimed diagram, a proof script with no conceptual bridge, a benchmark with unclear acceptance criteria, an analogy that silently becomes evidence, or a citation that hides the actual dependency.

The discipline is not "say everything." The discipline is "make the omissions accountable."

## Mathematical rigor as ecology

Mathematical practice is an ecology of registers. Each register has its own way of making closures accountable:

- Informal proof audits inference and plan reconstruction.
- Formal proof script audits machine-checkable inference inside a library.
- Diagram audits visual or relational structure under declared feature discipline.
- Definition prose creates stable objects for future traversal.
- Example trains recognition, boundary sense, and use.
- Counterexample marks failure surfaces and repairs concepts.
- Code audits operational behavior through execution, types, tests, and invariants.
- Benchmark audits computational claims through task, metric, dataset, acceptance criterion, and scope.
- Lecture or tutorial stages closures for a particular audience.
- Metaphor can orient recognition only when its limits are marked.
- Narrative bridge connects panels that would otherwise remain adjacent but untraversed.

Proof has a privileged role in certifying inference, but it does not license every other register to abdicate its own rigor. A proof can be correct and fail to communicate what has been proved, why it matters, how to use the construction, what the notation compresses, or how the result should travel into a different medium. A formal proof assistant script can certify inference and still leave conceptual transport unresolved. A diagram can fail by licensing a false inference, but it can also succeed by making an invariant visible before notation stabilizes.

Medium-specific rigor says that each medium is rigorous when it makes accountable the closures proper to that medium. This is not relativism. A diagram that licenses a false theorem fails. A formalization of the wrong statement fails. Motivational prose passed off as proof fails. Code without an invariant fails. But each fails in a medium-specific way.

## Definitions as infrastructure

Definitions are not mere stipulations or abbreviations. A definition panel creates future gutters. It introduces a sign whose consequences must become traversable.

A good definition positions a reader to recognize examples, detect nonexamples, anticipate theorems, compare neighboring concepts, and transport the concept into other media. A bad definition may still be extensionally correct while failing communicatively: it may compress too much too early, hide the operative invariant, fail to explain why its conditions belong together, or block movement into diagrams, code, examples, or later proof.

Definitions often behave iconically. They remove contingent features so a pattern can be manipulated. They give future panels a handle. That convenience creates an obligation: the definition must be placed in a sequence where its point becomes legible.

Definition placement matters. In proof order, the definition may appear as prerequisite. In discovery order, it may appear as repair after a counterexample. In pedagogy, it may appear after the reader has felt the need for it. In formalization, it may appear where the library requires a type or structure. None of these orders is universally superior. Each supports a different closure.

Definition-writing is mathematical infrastructure.

## Gaps, proofs, and plan reconstruction

Mathematicians intentionally leave gaps in proofs. This is not merely laziness. A published proof cannot expand every routine inference, dependency, or calculation without becoming unreadable. The question is which gaps are acceptable, for whom, under what standards, and with what repair mechanisms.

Routine gutters are crossable by the stated audience using standard manipulation, a known lemma, or a familiar condition. Productive gutters are intentionally left so the reader sees the plan rather than being buried in mechanics. Disciplinary gutters rely on field-specific background and are legitimate only when the intended audience is marked honestly. Unaccountable gutters are neither routine, nor productive, nor properly marked as field-specific.

Proof understanding is not only step checking. It also requires plan reconstruction: the ability to see what the proof is trying to do, why the main moves are chosen, and how the local derivation contributes to the global discharge. A proof may contain every valid inference while still hiding its strategy. Step-accountability does not entail plan-accountability.

Refereeing is one social mechanism for gutter regulation. A referee asks whether a proof's gaps are acceptable for the target community, whether cited results really discharge the delegated work, and whether the reader can reconstruct the proof without silent invention. That does not reduce rigor to social preference. It makes visible that mathematical rigor is socially organized as well as formally constrained.

## Diagrams, icons, and feature discipline

Mathematical diagrams are not merely illustrations. They can be rigorous when their meaningful features and licensed transformations are controlled.

A commutative diagram, for example, has constitutive features: objects, arrows, directions, labels, and the assertion that directed paths between relevant vertices are equal as morphisms. Visual position, distance, angle, and line length are usually incidental. A knot diagram has different constitutive features: strand topology and crossing data, with equivalence controlled by Reidemeister moves. A string diagram has another discipline: connectivity, labels, nodes, regions, ambient isotopy, and the stated axioms of the categorical setting.

Icon discipline asks three questions:

1. What structure is the icon supposed to preserve?
2. Which features are constitutive and which are incidental?
3. Which transformations are licensed?

Notation is also icon work. Variables, operators, indexing, juxtaposition, parentheses, and type signatures compress structure. Good notation amplifies recognition and manipulation. Prestige notation merely looks technical without improving traversal.

## Transport and the formal turn

Formalization is a high-pressure transport site. A formal proof assistant can nearly eliminate unaccountable inference at the level of machine-checkable derivation. It forces hidden obligations into terms, tactics, imports, typeclass instances, library lemmas, and definitions.

But inferential certification is not the same as conceptual transport. A Lean or Coq or Isabelle file certifies that some formal object satisfies some formal statement in some library context. It does not automatically certify that the formal statement is the intended informal claim, that the proof plan is visible, that the chosen definition has the intended generality, or that the reader can move back from the script to mathematical use.

Autoformalization makes this tension visible. When informal mathematical text is translated into a proof assistant, many hidden assumptions must become explicit: ambient structures, type constraints, coercions, equivalence relations, notation, library correspondences, and intended generality. Failures of autoformalization expose gutters in both the original text and the target library. They show where mathematical identity fails to survive register change.

The lesson is not that autoformalization solves mathematical communication. It is that autoformalization is a laboratory for transport.

## Normative criteria

These criteria translate accountable closure into editorial practice:

| Criterion | Failure mode | Repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Frame clarity | The reader cannot tell whether a passage is proof, conjecture, motivation, analogy, computation, example, or implementation. | Mark the panel type explicitly. |
| Closure accountability | The text asks the reader to cross an unlicensed gap. | Add a lemma, example, diagram, citation, proof obligation, or warning. |
| Icon adequacy | The notation or diagram compresses prestige rather than structure. | Replace it with a sign that preserves the operative invariant. |
| Sequential fitness | A concept appears before the reader has the examples, motives, or dependencies needed to use it. | Reorder panels or insert a bridge. |
| Medium honesty | Narrative behaves like proof, or formal proof substitutes for explanation. | State what the medium certifies and what it does not. |
| Audience specificity | The artifact pretends to be universal while relying on field-local expertise. | Identify the intended audience and mark specialized gutters. |
| Transportability | The idea changes identity when moved across prose, notation, diagram, code, or formal proof. | Add translation notes, examples, formalization comments, or comparison panels. |
| Repairability | A reader who fails to close a gap cannot locate the missing resource. | Provide dependencies, references, glossary entries, import paths, glossary entries, or explicit proof obligations. |

## Relation to relational mathematics

This theory belongs inside a broader relational mathematics program because communication is one of the ways relational structure becomes recognizable.

A mathematical artifact is not just a container of propositions. It is a finite arrangement of relational acts that asks the reader to complete, preserve, or transform structure. A mathematical panel is a local framing of recognition. A mathematical gutter is a demanded act of transport. A mathematical icon is a compression that preserves manipulable relation. A sequence is a path through which recognitions become stable. Communicative closure is the reader's act of completing a relation under constraints.

Poor communication is therefore not merely aesthetic failure. When an artifact hides its gutters, mislabels its panels, uses prestige icons, or refuses transport, it damages the relational availability of the mathematics. It may still contain true claims. It does not adequately support the recognitions by which those claims become usable knowledge.
