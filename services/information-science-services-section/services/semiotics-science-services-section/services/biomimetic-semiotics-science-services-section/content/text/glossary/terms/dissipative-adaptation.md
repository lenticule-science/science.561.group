Dissipative adaptation is a research program initiated by Jeremy England,
beginning with "Statistical Physics of Self-Replication" (2013, *Journal of
Chemical Physics*). For a specified class of externally driven stochastic
systems coupled to a thermal bath, England derives a rigorous lower bound
relating the heat dissipated during self-replication to the replicator's
growth rate, its internal entropy, and its durability: faster, more durable
replication is thermodynamically required to dissipate more. Perunov,
Marsland & England (2016, *Physical Review X*, "Statistical Physics of
Adaptation") extend the framework, and it has been used to argue that
non-living, driven systems can self-organize toward configurations that
absorb and dissipate energy more efficiently — offered as a candidate
physical mechanism for why adaptation-like, life-like organization should be
expected to emerge from undirected physics, given a sustained energy source.

The theorem itself is real, peer-reviewed, and derived within a clearly
specified model class. Its popular reception went further, treating it as an
explanation of "why life exists" in general. That stronger reading has drawn
serious and continuing technical pushback: work by Artemy Kolchinsky and
others has challenged the bound's claimed generality, and a 2024 preprint
("Thermodynamic Dissipation Does Not Bound Replicator Growth and Decay
Rates," arXiv:2404.01130) argues directly that no such bound can consistently
apply to both the growth and the decay of a thermodynamically consistent
replicator, contradicting an interpretation England's framework had been
given.

Calibrated status: a valid theorem for a specified model class, under active
technical correction as of the mid-2020s — not a settled universal law that
"life" or "growth" in general equals maximal dissipation. It is a legitimate,
citable source for the qualitative family of claims around Schrödinger's
negentropy and Prigogine's dissipative structures (sustained order requires
continuous free-energy throughput), but should not be cited as though it
proves a specific quantitative dissipation-growth relationship holds outside
the model class in which it was derived.
