Competitive intelligence (CI) is the discipline of systematically collecting, analyzing, and acting on information about competitors, markets, and market actors — gathered exclusively from legal, open, publicly accessible sources. It answers a decision-oriented question ("what should we do, given what our competitors are doing and capable of"), not merely a descriptive one, and it is defined as much by its boundary as by its method: everything in scope is something a competitor published, disclosed, or made observable themselves.

## Origins and lineage

The modern discipline traces to the 1980s, when Leonard M. Fuld began systematizing techniques for extracting competitive information from print, government-filing, and (later) database sources, publishing *Competitor Intelligence: How to Get It — How to Use It* (Wiley, 1985) and *The New Competitor Intelligence* (Wiley, first edition November 1994) — books credited with codifying "open-source research" as a repeatable business methodology rather than an ad hoc executive habit. Fuld was among the first four people named a Fellow of the Society of Competitive Intelligence Professionals and received SCIP's Meritorious Award in 1998, per biographical text that recurs consistently across Fuld & Company's own materials and multiple independent bio pages, though no independent SCIP-published record of the award was directly located; Fast Company called him "the undisputed dean of competitive intelligence," a quote confirmed directly on Fuld's Penguin Random House author page.

**SCIP** — the Society of Competitive Intelligence Professionals — was founded in the Washington, D.C. area in 1986 as the field's first dedicated professional body, providing education, ethics codes, and networking for practitioners. It was renamed Strategic and Competitive Intelligence Professionals in July 2010 and, in April 2023, renamed again to the Strategic Consortium of Intelligence Professionals to reflect an expanded scope covering competitive, strategic, economic, and social intelligence; it describes itself as the world's largest global intelligence association, with membership figures reported (depending on source and date) as spanning 100–120+ countries. SCIP's own definition of the field is durable and load-bearing for this section: "the legal and ethical collection and analysis of information regarding the capabilities, vulnerabilities, and intentions of business competitors."

## Distinction from espionage

The discipline's defining boundary is legal, not merely ethical-in-spirit: the U.S. **Economic Espionage Act of 1996** (Public Law 104-294) criminalizes theft or misappropriation of trade secrets in two forms — Section 1831 (espionage to benefit a foreign government or agent) and Section 1832 (commercial trade-secret theft regardless of beneficiary) — with penalties up to 15 years imprisonment and substantial fines. Trade secrets are defined broadly to cover financial, business, scientific, technical, economic, or engineering information that derives value from not being generally known and not being readily ascertainable through proper means. That last clause is the discipline's whole territory: competitive intelligence operates entirely within "proper means" and "readily ascertainable" — public filings, app-store listings, published pricing, social media, press coverage, patents, job postings, conference talks, customer reviews — and stops exactly where a competitor's information stops being public. Legal-defense commentary on Economic Espionage Act charges identifies "this was competitive intelligence, not theft" as a recognized defense strategy — distinguishing lawful open-source gathering from criminal misappropriation — underscoring that the line is treated as real and enforceable, not rhetorical, even though this entry did not locate a specific named case turning on that exact framing.

## Method, in outline

Classical CI method (Fuld's legacy, and SCIP's professional-practice consensus) runs collection → analysis → dissemination: gather from primary (the competitor's own disclosures — filings, listings, statements) and secondary (press, analyst, forum, review) sources; analyze for patterns, gaps, and strategic implication rather than simply compiling facts; and deliver findings in a form a decision-maker can act on. What counts as evidence has expanded with the sources available to gather it from — 1980s CI worked from print indexes and government filings; contemporary CI adds app-store listings and review aggregators, public roadmap/feedback boards, social-media bios and captions, podcast and press appearances, and privacy-policy self-disclosures as equally legitimate open sources, provided they remain things the subject made public.
