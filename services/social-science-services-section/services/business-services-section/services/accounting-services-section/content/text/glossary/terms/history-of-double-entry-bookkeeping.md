# History of Double-Entry Bookkeeping

## What the evidence can establish

Bookkeeping practices changed gradually, and historians do not all apply the
same criteria when deciding whether an early record is “full” double-entry.
Surviving ledgers prove that a practice existed in that record; they do not prove
the moment or person of invention. Claims about “the first” should therefore
name both the surviving evidence and the criterion being used.

Geoffrey A. Lee's study of the Giovanni Farolfi ledger describes surviving
accounts from 1299–1300 as a developed double-entry system. The article and its
bibliographic record are retained by the
[Accounting Historians Journal](https://egrove.olemiss.edu/aah_journal/vol4/iss2/6/).
An [ICAEW historical timeline](https://www.icaew.com/library/historical-resources/timeline)
also identifies double-entry practice in northern Italian merchant and banking
records during the 1300s, including Genoese communal accounts from 1340. These
sources support medieval use; they do not identify a single inventor.

## Pacioli and the printed description

In Venice in 1494, Luca Pacioli published *Summa de arithmetica, geometria,
proportioni et proportionalita*. The
[Library of Congress copy](https://www.loc.gov/item/49036374/) supplies direct
bibliographic evidence for the work, date, printer, and place. Its bookkeeping
section gave the first printed description of the double-entry system used by
Venetian merchants, according to the
[ICAEW collection history](https://www.icaew.com/library/library-collection/historical-accounting-literature/pacioli).

Pacioli documented existing practice rather than claiming to invent it. His
description related three working records:

- a memorandum or daybook recording events in narrative order;
- a [journal](./journal.md) translating events into debit-and-credit entries;
  and
- a [ledger](./ledger.md) organizing those entries by account.

The distinction still matters. Source evidence describes an event; the journal
expresses an accounting judgment; the ledger accumulates the consequences by
account. None can substitute for the others.

## Transmission and adaptation

Printed manuals allowed the method to travel and be adapted. ICAEW's
[survey of early bookkeeping books](https://www.icaew.com/library/library-collection/historical-accounting-literature/earliest-books-on-bookkeeping)
traces works following Pacioli through continental and English-language
manuals. That history is not a story of an unchanged artifact: account forms,
currencies, organizations, cost systems, reporting requirements, and machines
changed while the debit-credit constraint persisted.

Modern [GAAP](./gaap.md), [IFRS](./ifrs.md), tax rules, and managerial policies
are not consequences of Pacioli's text. They determine entity boundaries,
recognition, measurement, presentation, and disclosure. Double-entry is the
recording structure through which many of those conclusions are carried.

## What the internal check proves

[Double-entry bookkeeping](./double-entry-bookkeeping.md) requires equal debits
and credits for a settled entry. A mismatch proves an arithmetic or posting
problem. Equality proves only that the recorded entry closes under that rule. A
balanced ledger can still contain an omitted event, fictitious transaction,
wrong account, wrong period, unsupported valuation, concealed related party, or
misstated quantity.

That boundary explains why modern books still need source provenance,
reconciliation, cutoff controls, physical or custodial observations, valuation
evidence, and review. Double-entry makes one important class of contradiction
visible; it does not make the ledger self-authenticating.
