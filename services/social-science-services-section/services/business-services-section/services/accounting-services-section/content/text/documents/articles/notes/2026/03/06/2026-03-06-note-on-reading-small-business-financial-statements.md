## What this lesson covers

How to read the [income statement](../../../../../../glossary/terms/income-statement.md),
[cash flow statement](../../../../../../glossary/terms/cash-flow-statement.md),
and [balance sheet](../../../../../../glossary/terms/balance-sheet.md) as
entity-relative reports under a stated accounting basis. Identify the entity,
period, currency, consolidation scope, policies, and assurance before treating
their amounts as comparable.

---

## The income statement (P&L)

The [income statement](../../../../../../glossary/terms/income-statement.md)
presents recognized income and expense over a period. “Spent” and “paid” are
cash concepts and need not occur in the same period as expense recognition.

### Structure

The statement works by subtraction, peeling away layers of cost:

| Line | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Revenue** (top line) | Consideration recognized from satisfied revenue-generating activity under the governing policy, not simply cash customers paid |
| − Cost of goods sold (COGS) | Direct costs of producing what was sold (ingredients, materials, direct [labor](../../../../../../../../../../../sociology-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/labor.md)) |
| = **Gross profit** | What remains after production costs |
| − Operating expenses | Indirect costs of running the business (rent, utilities, salaries, marketing, insurance) |
| = **Operating income** | Profit from core business operations |
| − Interest and taxes | Financing costs and government obligations |
| = **Net income** (bottom line) | Final profit or loss |

### What to look for

**Gross margin** (gross profit ÷ revenue) states the recognized gross profit per
revenue unit under the entity's COGS classification. Price, volume, product mix,
costing policy, waste, capacity use, labor classification, and
[supply chain](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../operations-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/supply-chain.md)
conditions can all affect it; the ratio does not isolate efficiency.

**Operating margin** (operating income ÷ revenue) relates the presented
operating result to revenue. Sustainability also depends on cash conversion,
capital replacement, volatility, financing, commitments, and whether the
reported period and classifications are representative.

**Net income** is a final subtotal, not a complete assessment. Strong operating
income with high financing cost may reflect leverage or refinancing exposure;
weak operating income with a disposal gain may produce positive net income that
does not describe recurring operations.

### Worked example

A food truck operates for one month:

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | \$24,000 |
| − COGS (food, packaging) | \$8,400 |
| = Gross profit | \$15,600 |
| − Operating expenses (truck lease, fuel, insurance, wages, permits) | \$11,200 |
| = Operating income | \$4,400 |
| − Interest on equipment loan | \$350 |
| − Estimated taxes | \$1,000 |
| = Net income | \$3,050 |

Gross margin is 65% and operating margin 18.3% for the stated month and
classifications. One month does not establish a durable margin; weather,
repairs, price, volume, mix, omitted accruals, and seasonality warrant scenario
and multi-period analysis.

## The cash flow statement

The [cash flow statement](../../../../../../glossary/terms/cash-flow-statement.md) answers a different question: when did money actually enter and leave the business? This matters because profit and cash are not the same thing. You can be profitable on your income statement and still run out of money.

### Structure

The statement has three sections:

**Operating cash flow**: money generated or consumed by daily business activities. Starts with net income and adjusts for timing differences — revenue recognized but not yet collected (accounts receivable), expenses incurred but not yet paid (accounts payable), and non-cash charges like depreciation.

**Investing cash flow**: money spent on or received from long-term assets. Buying a \$15,000 oven is an investment outflow. Selling a used vehicle is an investment inflow.

**Financing cash flow**: money from or to investors and lenders. A \$50,000 investor contribution is a financing inflow. A loan repayment is a financing outflow.

### What to look for

**Operating cash flow versus profit**: A persistent divergence warrants
explanation, but it does not identify one error. Growth, collection timing,
inventory, prepayments, supplier terms, noncash charges, classification, and
omitted or duplicated activity can all contribute.

**Cash runway scenario**: Dividing available unrestricted cash by an assumed
constant net cash outflow gives a simple runway estimate. It is not a forecast
when receipts, spending, financing, restrictions, or burn rate can change.

**Capital expenditure timing**: Large equipment purchases create spikes in investing outflows. These are normal but must be planned — an unplanned \$10,000 repair in a month with low sales can create a cash crisis even in a profitable business.

### Worked example

Same food truck, same month — but cash flow tells a different story:

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Net income (from P&L) | \$3,050 |
| + Depreciation (non-cash expense) | \$400 |
| − Increase in accounts receivable (catering invoice unpaid) | −\$2,800 |
| = **Operating cash flow** | **\$650** |
| − Equipment purchase (new generator) | −\$3,500 |
| = **Investing cash flow** | **−\$3,500** |
| + Owner contribution | \$2,000 |
| = **Financing cash flow** | **\$2,000** |
| **Net change in cash** | **−\$850** |

The business is profitable (\$3,050 net income) but cash decreased by \$850. The catering invoice hasn't been paid yet, and the generator purchase consumed more cash than operations generated. Without the owner's \$2,000 contribution, cash would have dropped \$2,850.

## The balance sheet

The [balance sheet](../../../../../../glossary/terms/balance-sheet.md) presents
recognized assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time. Book equity is
not automatically liquidation proceeds or an amount available to owners.

### Structure

The fundamental equation: **Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Current assets** | Cash, inventory, accounts receivable |
| **Non-current assets** | Equipment, vehicles, [property](../../../../../../../../../../../sociology-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/property.md) |
| **Current liabilities** | Bills due within a year, short-term loans, accounts payable |
| **Non-current liabilities** | Long-term loans, mortgages |
| **Equity** | Owner's investment + retained earnings − distributions |

### What to look for

**Current ratio** (current assets ÷ current liabilities) compares two recognized
populations at one date. No universal threshold establishes comfortable
liquidity; conversion timing, restrictions, composition, seasonality,
facilities, and obligation maturities matter.

**Liabilities-to-equity ratio** compares recognized claims with the residual
interest. Its implications depend on maturity, rate, security, covenants,
currency, cash-flow resilience, and measurement bases.

**Working capital** (current assets − current liabilities) is an accounting
residual, not cash available after paying bills. Negative working capital can
indicate pressure or an operating model with rapid collection and later payment;
composition and timing decide its meaning.

### Worked example

The food truck's balance sheet at month-end:

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| **Assets** | |
| Cash | \$4,150 |
| Accounts receivable | \$2,800 |
| Inventory (food supplies) | \$1,200 |
| Equipment (truck, generator, etc.) | \$28,000 |
| **Total assets** | **\$36,150** |
| **Liabilities** | |
| Accounts payable (supplier invoices) | \$1,800 |
| Equipment loan | \$14,000 |
| **Total liabilities** | **\$15,800** |
| **Equity** | |
| Owner's investment | \$17,300 |
| Retained earnings | \$3,050 |
| **Total equity** | **\$20,350** |
| **Total liabilities + equity** | **\$36,150** |

Current ratio is (\$4,150 + \$2,800 + \$1,200) ÷ \$1,800 = 4.5, and
liabilities-to-equity is \$15,800 ÷ \$20,350 = 0.78. The labels “healthy” and
“moderate” require the additional timing, composition, terms, and comparative
evidence described above.

## How the three statements connect

The three statements are not independent documents. They are three views of the same underlying reality:

1. The **income statement** produces net income, which flows into retained earnings on the **balance sheet**.
2. The **cash flow statement** starts with net income from the income statement and reconciles it to actual cash movement, producing the ending cash balance that appears on the **balance sheet**.
3. The closing balance sheet becomes the next period's opening position. The
   next income statement measures new-period performance rather than beginning
   from a balance-sheet amount.

When reading [financial projections](../../../../../../../../../../../market-services-section/services/finance-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/financial-projections.md),
check the full roll-forward. Projected profit should connect to equity after
owner transactions and other recognized components; it need not create an equal
cash increase because working capital, investing, financing, and noncash effects
intervene.

## Guidance

- Find a public company's annual report (10-K filing) and read the three financial statements. Trace net income from the income statement through the cash flow statement to the balance sheet.
- For a small business you know — or one you're planning — draft rough versions of all three statements for a single month. Notice what you don't know and what assumptions you have to make.
- Ask: what does the business look like to someone who can only see these three documents? What would they miss?
