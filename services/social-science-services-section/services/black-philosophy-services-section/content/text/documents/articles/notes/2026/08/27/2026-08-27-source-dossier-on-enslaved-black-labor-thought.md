# Source Dossier on Enslaved Black Labor Thought

## Purpose and evidence rule

This dossier supports the [note on enslaved Black theories of labor](./2026-08-27-note-on-enslaved-black-theories-of-labor.md). It prevents three different kinds of evidence from collapsing into one:

- **Explicit proposition**: a witness states a judgment about right, ownership, obligation, value, work, or freedom.
- **Reported practice**: a witness describes conduct or an arrangement from which a limited proposition may be inferred, but does not state that proposition in the same terms.
- **Later analytic reconstruction**: a historian supplies a concept that organizes primary evidence. The concept may be useful without having been the historical actors' vocabulary.

An explicit proposition is not automatically representative. A reported practice does not transparently reveal every participant's reason. A later analytic term must not be placed into a historical speaker's mouth.

| Frederick Douglass | Maryland Eastern Shore and Baltimore; plantation labor, skilled shipyard labor, and self-hire | *My Bondage and My Freedom* (1855), retrospective autobiography published in an abolitionist setting | Douglass says that he contracted, worked, earned, and collected his wages; Hugh Auld's seizure rested on force, not right. He calls compelled payment for legal freedom a ransom. | Explicit proposition | [Rightful earnings](../../../../../../glossary/terms/rightful-earnings.md); coercive power does not create title; [freedom ransom](../../../../../../glossary/terms/freedom-ransom.md) | Douglass's unusually systematic account cannot stand in for every region, gender, or labor regime. |
| Harriet Jacobs | Edenton, North Carolina; domestic labor, sexual coercion, urban household economy | *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl* (1861), retrospective autobiography edited for abolitionist publication | Jacobs's grandmother baked and sold crackers at night to clothe herself and her children; Jacobs organizes freedom around protection of children as well as escape from an enslaver's sexual power. | Reported practice joined to explicit familial and bodily claims | Labor may properly serve kin; own-time production can oppose and subsidize slavery at once; labor analysis must include reproductive and care relations. | The grandmother's arrangement was conditional permission after compelled household labor, not independent enterprise in a free market. |
| Charles Ball | Maryland and the Lower South; field labor, hiring, plantation patches, and Sunday work | *Slavery in the United States* (1837), narrated autobiography prepared for publication by another writer | Ball describes Sunday wage work and family patches that plantation custom treated as belonging to particular enslaved families. | Reported practice | [Own time](../../../../../../glossary/terms/own-time.md); family claims on products; an [enslaved economy](../../../../../../glossary/terms/enslaved-economy.md) within domination | Customary possession was insecure and could lower enslavers' provisioning costs. Editorial mediation requires caution about wording. |
| William Grimes | Virginia and Georgia slavery, followed by fugitive life and wage labor in Connecticut | *Life of William Grimes* (1825), self-published narrative without the standard sponsorship of later abolitionist narratives | After eight years of labor outside slavery, Grimes says he was stripped of roughly one thousand dollars in hard earnings and compelled to purchase freedom, leaving his family without property. | Explicit judgment about earnings and coercion, attached to narrated experience | Earnings remain vulnerable while a slave claim persists; “self-purchase” can repeat extraction rather than ratify rightful ownership. | Grimes wrote after escape and legal purchase. His northern fugitive labor reveals the interstate reach of southern slave property rather than plantation labor alone. |
| Henry Bibb | Kentucky and repeated hiring-out, escape, recapture, and sale through the Lower South | *Narrative of the Life and Adventures of Henry Bibb* (1849), autobiography introduced and authenticated by abolitionist associates | Bibb states rights to wages, wife and children, liberty and happiness, and conscience. He later distinguishes taking necessities produced by one's own uncompensated labor from stealing a neighbor's property as a free person. | Explicit propositions | Wage rights, family rights, liberty, and conscience form an integrated account of personhood; [labor jurisdiction](../../../../../../glossary/terms/labor-jurisdiction.md) is not reducible to pay; subsistence recovery is bounded by relation and need. | Bibb's published formulation draws partly on revolutionary rights language. That does not make it less Black thought, but it identifies one intellectual idiom rather than a universal one. |
| Solomon Northup, reporting Patsey and other workers | Bayou Boeuf, Louisiana; cotton gang labor with individualized quotas | *Twelve Years a Slave* (1853), Northup's account as a free-born Black man kidnapped and enslaved, prepared for publication with David Wilson | A worker's first-day maximum became the continuing quota. Patsey's exceptional speed exposed her to punishment unless she produced roughly twice the ordinary minimum. Exceeding a quota could raise the next demand. | Reported regime and named example | [Productivity ratchet](../../../../../../glossary/terms/productivity-ratchet.md); ability does not create a moral debt to produce at the maximum; skill can intensify extraction. | Northup reports Patsey's conditions but not her own stated interpretation. The evidence establishes the imposed rule, not a transcript of Patsey's theory. |
| Lunsford Lane | Raleigh, North Carolina; urban self-hire, tobacco manufacture, and trade | *The Narrative of Lunsford Lane* (1842), self-published autobiography | Lane says state law prohibited an enslaved person from commanding his own time. He paid annual hire, operated a tobacco business, and reports another man repeatedly seized and hired for public benefit for the same offense. | Explicit legal and practical analysis | [Own time](../../../../../../glossary/terms/own-time.md) was a jurisdictional capacity whose accumulation could trigger state repression. | Lane had a rare urban privilege and later published as an abolitionist lecturer. His arrangement cannot represent plantation time. |
| Moses Grandy | North Carolina; field labor, Great Dismal Swamp canal and boat work, self-hire, and later free wage labor | *Narrative of the Life of Moses Grandy* (1843), dictated to British antislavery activist George Thompson | Grandy describes paying for freedom repeatedly, losing prior payments, buying his wife and son, and directing publication proceeds toward remaining relatives. He reports daughters Catherine and Charlotte working together to purchase Charlotte after Catherine's release. | Explicit retrospective account with named kin practices | [Freedom ransom](../../../../../../glossary/terms/freedom-ransom.md) could recur; [kin-directed freedom work](../../../../../../glossary/terms/kin-directed-freedom-work.md) made individual release a capacity for collective action. | Dictation and antislavery publication mediate wording. Purchase was an exceptional route unavailable to most enslaved people. |
| Elizabeth Keckley | Virginia, North Carolina, and St. Louis; domestic labor, coerced sexual and reproductive conditions, and skilled dressmaking | *Behind the Scenes* (1868), postwar autobiography | Keckley says the Garland household depended on her work and earnings so heavily that she could not accumulate the price demanded for herself and her son; customers later assembled gifts and loans. | Explicit account of constrained accumulation and family purpose | Skill can support an enslaver household and a freedom network simultaneously; freedom work may be directed toward parent and child together. | Published after emancipation and shaped as a life narrative extending into the White House years. Her successful dressmaking was exceptional. |

## Later analytic matrix

| Scholar | Analytic contribution | Use here | Limit on use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Philip D. Morgan, “Work and Culture” (1982) | Distinguishes task labor from gang labor and analyzes the customary interval between assigned task and the worker's remaining time in the Lowcountry. | Establishes that the practical possibility of [own time](../../../../../../glossary/terms/own-time.md) varied by labor regime. | “Own time” remained conditioned by coercion; the task system was not freedom. |
| Dylan C. Penningroth, *The Claims of Kinfolk* (2003) | Reconstructs extensive property relations among enslaved people and their grounding in kin and community. | Guards against treating independent production as merely individual accumulation. | Informal property claims did not receive secure protection against enslavers. |
| Stephanie M. H. Camp, *Closer to Freedom* (2004) | Analyzes containment of enslaved people's bodies, time, and movement and the “rival geographies” produced through everyday resistance. | Adds spatial and bodily authority to a theory that might otherwise center wages and products. | “Rival geography” is Camp's analytic language, not a period term attributed to every participant. |
| Daina Ramey Berry, “Soul Values and American Slavery” (2021) | Identifies enslaved people's assertion of spiritual and intrinsic worth beyond prices assigned to Black bodies. | Prevents a labor theory from reducing Black personhood to productive contribution, even in a critical account. | Soul value is a historian's synthesis across evidence, not proof of a single shared doctrine. |
| Juliana Rose Walker, “‘This Slavery Business Is a Horrible Thing’” (2023) | Reads WPA testimony for economic negotiation, wages, time purchase, and independent production while foregrounding mediation. | Broadens the economic archive and identifies named testimony for a later research pass. | WPA interviews are retrospective, interviewer-shaped, and unevenly transcribed; they should not silently confirm earlier narratives. |

## Cross-source findings

### 1. The central dispute is jurisdiction, not a simple opposition between work and idleness

Across the sources, the recurring conflict concerns authority over a person's capacities: who chooses the task, fixes its pace, directs movement, receives the product, and determines whether labor serves an enslaver, the worker, or the worker's kin. [Labor jurisdiction](../../../../../../glossary/terms/labor-jurisdiction.md) is the modern umbrella used for this relation. Bibb's explicit list most clearly joins wages, family, liberty, and conscience. Douglass supplies the most developed accounting of time and earnings. Jacobs prevents that accounting from becoming male and wage-centered.

### 2. Labor-regime differences are theoretically substantive

Urban self-hire and Lowcountry task work could leave a contested remainder called own time. Northup's Louisiana cotton account describes almost the opposite temporal structure: daylight and sometimes moonlight field labor, post-field chores, preparation for the next day's subsistence, and individualized output quotas. The lack of own time in a gang-and-quota regime is not missing evidence of the same practice. It is evidence that the material opening through which workers could exercise jurisdiction differed sharply.

### 3. Skill has no single political effect

Douglass's caulking skill widened his movement, contracting knowledge, earnings, and escape capacity. Patsey's cotton-picking skill enlarged the output demanded under threat of whipping. The contrast rules out both “skill liberates” and “skill only increases exploitation.” Skill changes the stakes of the jurisdictional conflict; the labor regime determines who can capture the gain.

### 4. Freedom purchase can preserve the distinction between practical payment and rightful title

Douglass expressly called the payment for his legal freedom ransom. Grimes's former enslaver reached into his post-escape life and converted years of free labor and household property into the price of avoiding re-enslavement. Both cases distinguish the prudence of paying under threat from recognition that the recipient possesses a legitimate saleable interest in the person.

### 5. Worth exceeds labor

The sources support a theory of labor, but they do not support making labor the ground of Black humanity. Bibb includes conscience; Jacobs centers bodily and familial claims; Berry reconstructs soul value. A person's right not to be enslaved does not depend on productivity, skill, wage earning, family status, or capacity to labor.

### 6. After-hours labor is not one category

Northup and the Pye account describe commanded second shifts. Jacobs and Avery describe household production that both served kin and lowered enslavers' costs. Lane describes legally prohibited command over one's time; Avery describes clandestine sale and communal quilting. [Compulsory self-provisioning](../../../../../../glossary/terms/compulsory-self-provisioning.md) must therefore be separated from own time. The relevant tests are whether the principal shift ended, who directed the activity, and who controlled its product or experience.

### 7. Freedom work could be recursive and collective

Grandy's repeated payments show that ransom could absorb earnings without securing release. Keckley's earnings maintained the household that claimed her while she sought freedom for herself and her son. Grandy's daughters worked together for one wage to free a sister. These are not merely stories of individual accumulation. They support [kin-directed freedom work](../../../../../../glossary/terms/kin-directed-freedom-work.md) as a distinct destination of labor.

## Evidence still needed

- Named testimony from women doing field labor in gang and task systems, especially statements about pace, pregnancy, nursing, disability, and care.
- Testimony from children, elders, and disabled people that directly contests plantation measures of usefulness.
- A controlled sample of WPA interviews, retaining interviewer identity, location, transcription conditions, and the difference between recollection and reported family memory.
- Black religious speech, songs, and folktales only where provenance is strong enough to avoid turning expressive culture into an unmediated philosophical transcript.
- Evidence from distinct African and African-descended legal and ethical traditions tied to particular communities, rather than a generic claim of “African retention.”
