Coerced-labor refusal is the withdrawal, obstruction, redirection, or escape of labor claimed through coercion. Under antebellum slavery it could include absence, slowdown, negotiation over tasks, feigned illness, tool breaking, flight, or collective withdrawal.

The term distinguishes refusal of an illegitimate command from refusal of productive activity. Enslaved people who obstructed work for an enslaver also cultivated food, raised animals, practiced crafts, cared for kin, earned wages, worshiped, celebrated, and built community during contested time. The plantation category of laziness erased the difference because it measured willingness only by output delivered to the enslaver.

Not every low output or absence can be interpreted as deliberate political refusal. Illness, exhaustion, disability, inadequate food, weather, skill, and many other conditions affected work. The term applies where evidence supports withdrawal or redirection as an answer to coercive control; it does not turn every performance difference into hidden resistance.

W. E. B. Du Bois's later account of a Civil War “general strike” synthesized wartime mass flight and labor withdrawal at collective scale. Coerced-labor refusal also includes smaller antebellum actions, but the modern term must not be silently attributed to the people whose practices it describes.
