# Black Epistemology

Black epistemology studies knowing and not knowing under racialized relations of power. Its questions include who is recognized as a knower, which experiences become evidence, how ignorance is socially produced, when speakers are silenced or compelled to withhold speech, how communities establish credibility, and how archives and disciplines organize what can become intelligible.

Patricia Hill Collins connects Black women's standpoint and self-definition to collective intellectual work rather than automatic identity privilege. Charles W. Mills analyzes white ignorance as a patterned social cognition tied to racial domination, not merely missing information in individual minds. Kristie Dotson analyzes epistemic violence as failures of audiences to meet speakers' vulnerabilities, including testimonial quieting and smothering. These projects show that epistemic harm can occur through institutions, interpretive resources, habitual perception, and the terms of inquiry itself.

This topic governs the method of the enslaved-labor inquiry. Enslavers produced the majority of contemporaneous records, while enslaved people's statements were restricted, edited, published through abolitionist institutions, or collected retrospectively by WPA interviewers. Recovering a theory from that archive requires separating explicit propositions, reported practices, and later reconstruction. Archive scarcity is not neutral absence; neither does recognizing structural silencing license the researcher to invent speech.

## Boundary

Black epistemology is broader than a theory that oppressed people always know better. Social position can create access, pressure, distortion, divided awareness, or strategic silence. Epistemic advantage, where defended, requires an account of the practice and critical reflection through which a standpoint is achieved. The topic also studies Black ignorance, disagreement, error, and exclusion within Black communities rather than assigning epistemic innocence by identity.
