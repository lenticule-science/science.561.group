A safe harbor, in US internet-intermediary law, is a statutory provision that shields a qualifying service provider from a defined category of liability for content or conduct originating with a third party, conditioned on the provider meeting the statute's own requirements; the two operative hosting-provider safe harbors are 47 U.S.C. §230, which bars treating an interactive computer service as the "publisher or speaker" of third-party content for most civil claims, and 17 U.S.C. §512, which limits copyright liability for a provider that stores user content and expeditiously removes it on proper notice.

Section 230 is broader in subject matter, covering most civil claims such as defamation, but carries its own carve-outs for federal criminal law, intellectual property, and sex-trafficking claims; Section 512 is narrower, covering copyright liability only, but affirmatively requires the notice-and-takedown compliance that Section 230 does not.

Neither safe harbor reaches sanctions law: a provider's compliance with §230 and §512 governs its exposure to civil claims arising from user content and provides no defense to a blocked-property or prohibited-transaction violation under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act and 31 C.F.R. Part 594 once a user or the provider itself is a designated party, because content-intermediary liability and sanctions compliance are legally independent regimes.
