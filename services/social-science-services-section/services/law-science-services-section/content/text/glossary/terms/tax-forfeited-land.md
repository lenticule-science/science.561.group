Tax-forfeited land is real property whose owner failed to pay the property taxes owed on it through the statutory redemption period, so that title passed to the state in trust for the taxing districts.
In Minnesota the county auditor administers it under chapter 282.

The Supreme Court's 2023 decision in *Tyler v. Hennepin County* held that a county keeping the surplus from such a sale takes the owner's property.
Minnesota's 2024 rewrite (effective 2024-07-01, Minn. Stat. 282.005) makes three rules: before the sale any interested party may repurchase by paying the full tax debt; the county must sell every forfeited parcel at public auction, online included, within six months, at a minimum bid equal to the tax debt; and any amount above that is [surplus proceeds](./surplus-proceeds.md) the former owner and other interested parties may claim.
A county may also sell on installments, a [terms sale](./terms-sale.md) of at least ten percent down and up to ten years (Minn. Stat. 282.01, subd. 4), and may sell to a city below market value when the discount "will lead to the development of affordable housing" (subd. 1a(d)), which is how Duluth's free lots reach builders and residents.
County auction lists are therefore a public, recurring supply of parcels, and in counties with many forfeitures a large one: the statewide parcel layer on 2026-09-26 shows St. Louis County administering about 14,000 forfeited parcels with no building on them.
