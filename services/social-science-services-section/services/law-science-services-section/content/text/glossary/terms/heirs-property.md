Heirs' property is land held by multiple co-owners as tenants in common, with title never formally settled through probate after the death of the person who last held clear title.
It is the standing legal condition that results when a landowner dies [intestate](./intestate-succession.md) and the land passes by default inheritance law to all descendants at once, rather than to a single named heir through a will and a closed estate.
Each co-owner holds an undivided fractional interest in the whole parcel rather than a bounded piece of it, and every co-owner's consent is formally required for major decisions about the land — a sale, a mortgage, enrollment in a USDA farm program — even though in practice many co-owners are unlocated, deceased without their own estates settled, or unaware they hold an interest at all.

Heirs' property is concentrated on land historically owned by Black families in the U.S. South, a pattern with a documented cause: intestacy was, for generations, effectively the default outcome for Black landowners who lacked reliable access to estate-planning attorneys, and each unresolved generation compounds the number of co-owners — the condition named [fractionated heirship](./fractionated-heirship.md) once the co-owner count grows large (search synthesis; see the [source record](../../../../../../../../data/records/2026-08-27-mission-aligned-service-markets/service-market-survey.md) for figures and program citations).
The same condition recurs, under a different trust-law regime, in Bureau of Indian Affairs trust-land probate — allotment-era parcels fractionating across generations of intestate succession is the identical structural problem applied to a different body of law.

Heirs' property is legally and financially precarious in ways titled property is not: individual co-owners are historically vulnerable to forced-sale [partition actions](./partition-action.md) brought by a single fractional-interest holder (including outside speculators who acquire a small interest specifically to force a sale), and the land is frequently ineligible for USDA loans, disaster assistance, and other federal farm programs that require a clear, singly-titled owner of record to apply.
Clearing heirs' property — reconstructing the deed, probate, marriage, death, and census records needed to establish a full [chain of title](./chain-of-title.md) and a complete heir tree — is the specific service this section's [note on mission-aligned service markets](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/27/2026-08-27-note-on-mission-aligned-service-markets-in-land-tenure-and-probate-records.md) treats as its spine finding: an existing, priced commercial category with real federal buyer-side funding behind it.
