A finder's fee, in the unclaimed-property context, is the percentage of recovered property value a finder (also called a locator, tracer, or heir-finder) may lawfully charge an owner in exchange for identifying that a specific unclaimed asset exists in the owner's name and assisting with the claim.
Every U.S. state that permits the finder business caps this fee by statute, and the cap is the crux legal fact of the entire practice: a finder who charges above the cap has an unenforceable contract, to the extent of the excess, in every state checked.

A 2026-08-28 research pass (search synthesis via a secondary aggregator, cross-checked directly against primary state-legislature text for four states) found the modal cap nationally is **10%** of recovered value, held by roughly thirty states, with a second cluster at **15%** (including New York, read directly from Abandoned Property Law §1416) and outliers as low as Washington's reported 5% and as high as Arizona's reported 30%; see the [2026-08-28 claims recovery mechanism record](../../../../../../../../data/records/2026-08-28-claims-recovery-mechanism/claims-recovery-survey.md) for the full state table and its attribution grading.
Florida's cap is tiered rather than flat — 15% if the state has held the property 24 months or less, 25% if longer, with total fees and costs capped at 30% regardless (read directly, Florida Statute §717.135) — which illustrates that "the cap" is not always a single number and the governing dormancy period must be checked per state.
The fee cap is typically paired with a **waiting period** before a finder may contact an owner at all (ranging from roughly 90 days to 24 months across the states checked) and, in a minority of states — Texas is the clearest example found, requiring a Class A/C Investigations Company License from the state's Department of Public Safety — an occupational licensing requirement layered on top of the percentage cap.
