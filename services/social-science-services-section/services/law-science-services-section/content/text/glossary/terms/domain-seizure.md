Domain seizure is the legal mechanism by which a government obtains custody or control of a domain name through either of two distinct channels: registry or registrar compulsion, in which a court order or administrative directive requires the registry operator or a registrar to suspend, redirect, or transfer a domain, or civil in rem forfeiture, in which the domain name itself is treated as property subject to seizure under a forfeiture statute such as 18 U.S.C. §981, independent of any criminal proceeding against the registrant.

Registry or registrar compulsion does not require a forfeiture judgment, or necessarily even a court order addressed to the registrant, and can proceed through a directive to the registry operator alone; civil in rem forfeiture under §981 is instead a judicial civil action naming the domain-as-property as the defendant, the mechanism U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement's Homeland Security Investigations used at scale from 2010 in "Operation In Our Sites," posting seizure banners citing "18 U.S.C. Sections 981 and 2323."

Because registry-level compulsion skips the forfeiture process's judicial findings and the registrant's pre-seizure opportunity to contest ownership, it can remove a domain from resolution faster and with less due-process exposure than an in rem forfeiture action, at the cost of the registry or registrar rather than a court making the operative determination. A reported `serverHold` status stopping a sanctioned hosting collective's domain from resolving, without any forfeiture complaint on record, illustrates the registry-compulsion channel; see [a research note on sanctions, designation, and hosting-provider law](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/08/2026-09-08-note-on-sanctions-designation-and-hosting-provider-law.md) [@uscode981ed2026; @iceinoursites2010].
