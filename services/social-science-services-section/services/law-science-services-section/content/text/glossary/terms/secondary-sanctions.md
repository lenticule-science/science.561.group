Secondary sanctions are sanctions measures that expose a third party who is not the original sanctions target — typically a foreign financial institution or company with no independent nexus to the underlying conduct — to penalties, including loss of access to the sanctioning state's financial system or its own designation, for continuing to transact with a primary sanctions target.

Secondary sanctions are distinguished from ordinary primary-sanctions compliance: a primary sanction prohibits a sanctioning state's own persons from dealing with a blocked party, while a secondary sanction (or the functionally equivalent exposure a blocked-party designation creates through financial-system access) reaches non-nationals who have no independent obligation to the sanctioning state's law, by threatening their own access to that state's financial system if they continue the relationship.

The Financial Action Task Force's account of de-risking documents the resulting third-party behavior: banks facing this exposure respond by "terminating or restricting business relationships with clients or categories of clients to avoid, rather than manage, risk," so a designation with only formal primary-sanctions force can still produce comprehensive de facto financial exclusion once third-party institutions book the secondary-sanctions exposure of the relationship themselves. See [a research note on sanctions, designation, and hosting-provider law](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/08/2026-09-08-note-on-sanctions-designation-and-hosting-provider-law.md) for FATF's definition of de-risking and the case in which an Italian bank suspended a designated hosting collective's account days after a US designation, without the bank itself being subject to US primary sanctions [@fatfderisking2021].
