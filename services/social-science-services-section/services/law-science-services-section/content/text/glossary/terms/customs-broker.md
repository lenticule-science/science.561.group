A customs broker is a person or firm licensed by US Customs and Border Protection under 19 U.S.C. 1641 and 19 CFR part 111 to transact customs business on behalf of others. Customs business is the preparation and filing of entries, the classification and valuation of goods, the payment of duties and fees, and the related documents and correspondence with CBP. An individual earns the license by passing the Customs Broker License Examination and a background check; a company holds a license through a licensed officer, and operates under a national permit.

The license's edge is who the business is done for. An importer transacting customs business solely for its own account, and its own employees and officers acting for it, needs no license (19 CFR 111.2(a)(2)(i)) ([eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111)). A company that buys goods abroad and imports them itself is its own [importer of record](../../../../../economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/importer-of-record.md) and files its own entries if it wants to. A company that files entries for a foreign seller, for a fee or not, is doing customs business for another and needs a license, or must hand the filing to a licensed broker. Brokers charged about $90–450 an entry in 2026 by search synthesis.

Since 2026 the licensed broker also carries weight for foreign importers: Executive Order 14411 (2026-06-03) requires a foreign importer on formal entries to be CTPAT-validated itself or to file only through a CTPAT-validated broker, and postal informal entries may be filed only by owners, purchasers or licensed brokers ([the 2026-09-26 law note](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/26/2026-09-26-note-on-us-law-for-doing-business-with-and-for-chinese-firms.md)).
