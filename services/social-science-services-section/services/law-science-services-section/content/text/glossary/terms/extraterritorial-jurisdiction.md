Extraterritorial jurisdiction is a state's exercise of legal authority over persons, conduct, or property located outside its territorial borders, grounded in a connecting basis its own law deems sufficient — nationality, protective interest, effects within the state, or, for financial regulation, access to the state's own financial system.

This differs from ordinary [jurisdiction](./jurisdiction.md), whose connecting link is typically physical presence or conduct within a state's territory, in that the connecting basis for financial extraterritoriality is a foreign actor's own dependence on the regulating state's financial infrastructure — a foreign bank clearing that state's currency, or a registry operator relying on that state's accredited infrastructure — rather than any activity the foreign actor conducts within the regulating state's territory.

This financial-system nexus is what allows a sanctions designation to reach an entity with no operations in the designating state at all: the designation blocks the designating state's currency-denominated and correspondent-banking-routed transactions of any third party that continues to serve the designated entity, giving the designation practical extraterritorial reach through those intermediaries' own dependence on the designating state's financial-system access, rather than through any direct jurisdiction over the designated entity itself. See [a research note on sanctions, designation, and hosting-provider law](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/08/2026-09-08-note-on-sanctions-designation-and-hosting-provider-law.md) for the Autistici/Inventati case, an Italian collective with no US operations reached through this mechanism.
