The Section 102 waiver is the mechanism by which the executive branch builds border wall without complying with the laws that would otherwise govern building anything else on federal or federally regulated land. Its core sentence, unchanged in substance since 1996 and broadened in reach by the REAL ID Act in 2005, reads: the Secretary of Homeland Security "shall have the authority to waive, and shall waive, all legal requirements" the Secretary "in [their] sole discretion, determines necessary to ensure expeditious construction" of border barriers and roads [realidact2005]. "Legal requirements" is not a term of art narrowed by context — it has been read to reach the Endangered Species Act, the National Environmental Policy Act, the National Historic Preservation Act, the Clean Water Act, the Clean Air Act, the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act, the Antiquities Act, and dozens more, environmental and cultural-resource statutes overwhelmingly among them. Dinah Bear, a former general counsel to the Council on Environmental Quality, called the original 2005 invocation "the broadest waiver of law in American history" [bear2009]; two decades of subsequent use have not required a narrower description.

The waiver is exercised per project segment, and the per-instance count is itself a record of how routine the power has become. DHS Secretary Alejandro Mayorkas's October 2023 notice for roughly seventeen miles of wall in Starr County, Texas — issued by a Democratic administration that had campaigned against wall construction — waived 26 federal laws, citing "an acute and immediate need to construct physical barriers and roads... in order to prevent unlawful entries" [washingtontimes2023]. DHS Secretary Kristi Noem's 2025 notice for the San Rafael Valley segment waived 34 — a wider net covering more recent statutory additions and a broader construction footprint (roads, lighting, detection technology, concrete batch plants) than the 2023 notice contemplated [kjzz2025]. The waiver's discretion is unconditioned by party: both administrations reached for the identical clause, and neither needed to argue that the specific laws waived were inapplicable, burdensome, or in conflict with border security — only that the Secretary, alone, found expeditious construction to require setting them aside.

The clause is built to resist challenge as much as it is built to permit construction. It forecloses ordinary judicial review of the waiver decision itself, leaving only a constitutional claim, heard on an expedited track that funnels through the D.C. Circuit and, from there, only by direct application to the Supreme Court — a channel that has produced no successful challenge in nearly two decades of litigation, including the Fifth Circuit's rejection of a nondelegation challenge in *County of El Paso v. Chertoff* (2008), which the Supreme Court declined to review. The Center for Biological Diversity's suit against the Noem waiver for the San Rafael segment ran the same course: a nondelegation theory — that Congress cannot hand an executive officer discretion this unbounded and call it lawmaking still performed by Congress — argued on summary judgment and rejected by Judge Angela Martinez around March 27, 2026 [tucsonsentinel2026]. Construction at Lochiel proceeded on the strength of that ruling with no injunction pending appeal.

Courts decline review here for a structural reason as much as a doctrinal one. The nondelegation doctrine, revived rhetorically in recent Supreme Court terms but rarely applied to actually strike a delegation, asks only whether Congress supplied an "intelligible principle" to guide the agency's discretion — a bar the courts have consistently found satisfied by "expeditious construction of barriers... to prevent unlawful entries," however capacious that principle turns out to be in practice. The clause's own text does the rest of the work: by naming judicial review's scope precisely, Congress pre-empted the argument that unreviewable discretion is itself the constitutional defect, since a court can point to the review channel that exists — however narrow — as evidence that the delegation was not truly unbounded. The result is a power that is nominally checked and practically absolute: reviewable in theory, undefeated in fact.

Section 102 is the clearest working example in this corpus of a legal instrument that operates as [exclusion-powers](../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/glossary/terms/san-rafael-de-la-zanja-grant.md) rather than as reasoned adjudication — a single signature converting dozens of statutes, each the product of its own legislative and evidentiary process, into inapplicable text for a specific stretch of ground. It is [settlerism-as-filing-system](../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-lochiel-history.md) made literal: wall construction is filed as infrastructure, and the laws that would have required treating the grove, the watershed, and the tribal boundary as anything other than an obstacle are filed out of relevance by the same stroke. Compare [due process](./due-process.md), which the note's own line of argument holds this clause structurally against — due process assumes notice, an opportunity to be heard, and a neutral decision-maker; the §102 waiver substitutes a single official's unreviewable discretion, exercised in Federal Register notices that name the laws suspended but owe no one an accounting for why.
