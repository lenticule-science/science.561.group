The Roosevelt Reservation is a strip of federal land sixty feet wide, running the length of the U.S.-Mexico border through California, Arizona, and New Mexico, withdrawn from private entry by Theodore Roosevelt's Proclamation No. 758 on May 27, 1907 [proclamation758]. Texas is absent from the list because Texas, uniquely among border states, retained its own public lands at annexation and had already sold most of the relevant strip into private hands; the federal government had no vacant land there left to reserve. The proclamation's stated purpose was narrow and of its era: to keep the border "free from obstruction as a protection against the smuggling of goods between the United States and Mexico" — a customs-enforcement rationale, not an immigration-enforcement or wall-building one, since no wall existed to build and the automobile smuggling the order anticipated was still a novelty.

Legally, the reservation is a withdrawal, not a grant of new power: it removes federal land within the strip from the mining, homestead, and other public-land statutes that would otherwise let it pass into private title, and it places that strip under direct federal administrative control. For most of the twentieth century this made it an obscure title-and-survey matter, relevant mainly to determining who owned or administered the sixty feet nearest the line. Its late-life second career, cited across three wall-building administrations, is different: because the reservation already places that strip under exclusive federal control, government lawyers argue it forecloses landowners' — and, more consequentially, tribal nations' — objections that a wall built on it exceeds a landowner's consent or intrudes on land the objecting party controls. The 1907 order becomes, retroactively, wall-construction authority a century before Congress or any president meant it as one.

That argument prevailed at Lochiel's parallel front. On August 13–14, 2026, Judge Richard Leon of the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia denied the Tohono O'odham Nation's request to enjoin construction of 62 miles of wall along its reservation's southern boundary. The Nation argued that building on land running down to the international line — land it had held, used, and crossed for generations before the line existed — without its consent amounted to an unconsented taking that would, in practice, shrink its reservation absent congressional action. Judge Leon read the case the other way: because the 1907 proclamation had already reserved the outermost sixty feet to exclusive federal control, the government did not need the Nation's consent to build within it, and the government's border-security interest "outweigh[ed] any surviving irreparable harms" the Nation could show [usnews2026]. Tohono O'odham Chairman Verlon Jose responded that "too many critical issues were not adequately addressed," and the Nation said it would "consider all possible options," including appeal [azcapitoltimes2026]; none had been filed as of the most recent verified reporting.

The ruling's textual foundation is narrower than its practical reach. A proclamation reserving sixty feet is being read to authorize whatever construction footprint the government determines the wall requires — and at San Rafael, reporting places the actual cleared construction swath at roughly 150 feet, two and a half times the reservation's own stated width, encompassing the double bollard wall, the patrol corridor between its two lines, and the staging area the equipment needs. Nothing in the 1907 text anticipates or licenses that gap; it is filled by the same discretionary logic that runs through the [Section 102 waiver](./real-id-act-section-102-waiver.md) — a document drafted for one narrow purpose, read a century later as blanket authorization for whatever the executive branch's current project requires, with the gap between the document's terms and the construction it licenses treated as immaterial rather than as the thing a court might be expected to test.

The Roosevelt Reservation belongs beside the [Section 102 waiver](./real-id-act-section-102-waiver.md) as a second instance of the same argumentative move: a categorical, century-old federal claim to a strip of land does the work that individualized [due process](./due-process.md) — notice, a hearing, a case-by-case weighing of whose land this actually is and what they are owed for its use — would otherwise require. It is [exclusion-powers](../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/glossary/terms/san-rafael-de-la-zanja-grant.md) in its most literal form: a strip of ground the federal government need only point to, not argue for, and [settlerism-as-filing-system](../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-lochiel-history.md) in its clearest instance — a 1907 customs order filed and refiled, at Lochiel and along the Tohono O'odham Nation's boundary alike, as border-security infrastructure whenever the current filing needs one.
