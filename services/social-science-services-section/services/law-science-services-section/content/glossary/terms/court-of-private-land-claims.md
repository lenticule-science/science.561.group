The Court of Private Land Claims was created by Congress in 1891 to do what three decades of surveyors-general and territorial litigation had failed to finish: adjudicate, once and for all, which of the land grants Spain and Mexico had issued across the territory ceded to the United States by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) and the Gadsden Purchase (1853) were valid, and how much land each covered. Five justices, appointed for terms Congress kept extending past their original 1895 expiration until the court finally closed in 1904, heard claims from New Mexico, Arizona, Colorado, Nevada, Utah, and Wyoming and adjudicated title to more than 35 million acres [fjc]. Its founding premise was procedural neutrality: the treaty had obligated the United States to honor property rights it inherited, and the court existed to determine, case by case, what those rights actually were.

In practice the court ran a narrowing machine. Spanish and Mexican land administration had never produced the kind of title documentation American courts were built to recognize — deeds recorded at a central registry, unambiguous metes and bounds, chains of title unbroken by generations of informal transfer and communal use. Grants were often approved by local or provincial officials without surviving paperwork, described by landmarks rather than surveyed coordinates, and held collectively by communities of parcioneros rather than by individual titleholders whose claim a court could cleanly confirm or deny. Every gap and ambiguity the American evidentiary standard could locate became a ground for rejecting or shrinking a claim. In New Mexico alone the court considered 282 claims and confirmed 82 of them — a 24 percent confirmation rate — while roughly 1.3 million claimed acres across seventeen grants were rejected outright on grounds ranging from insufficient documentation to disputed authority to make the grant in the first place [gao2004]. Arizona and California fared differently by degree, not by kind: the pattern of confirmation well below the acreage claimed, and communal grants faring worse than individual ones, held across the court's jurisdiction.

The San Rafael de la Zanja grant is the worked example this corpus returns to, because its numbers are unusually well documented and its outcome is unusually representative. The 1825 grant to Ramón Romero and roughly eighteen to thirty fellow parcioneros of the Santa Cruz presidio covered four square leagues — about 17,400 acres — held communally and conditioned on continuous occupation, exactly the kind of title the court's evidentiary regime was least equipped to credit on its own terms. The court confirmed those four leagues in 1899; the Supreme Court affirmed the confirmation the following year against the Cameron syndicate's argument that the grant should be read as sixteen leagues, roughly nine times the confirmed acreage, which the Camerons had been ranching as if it were already theirs. Justice White's holding was flat: "No duty rests on this government to recognize the validity of a grant to any area of greater extent than was recognized by the government of Mexico" [hadleysheridan1995]. The court did its narrowing job correctly by its own lights — the Camerons' claim to sixteen leagues was never legitimate under Mexican law either — but the case still shows the machine's general operation: a grant that had been a commons, defined by occupation and shared obligation rather than survey lines, run through a tribunal built to output a single defensible number, with everything the number could not capture treated as if it had never existed.

That is the court's durable legacy, independent of any single case's correctness. Confirmation converted a grant into a title an American court, an American buyer, and an American bank could recognize and transact — which is precisely what let the confirmed San Rafael de la Zanja acreage pass, within a generation, from a communal grant no single parciónero could sell to a $500-a-share syndicate, and from the syndicate to a mining baron, and from the mining baron's heirs to a conservation nonprofit and the state of Arizona. Confirmation was the hinge; everything downstream — ranch, enclosure, cattle empire, state natural area, and now border wall segment — depends on the title the court manufactured out of a claim it first cut down. The court is [legal-formatting](./legal-formatting.md) operating at the scale of a whole territory: not a single ruling reshaping institutional behavior, as [adjudication](./adjudication.md) more narrowly does, but a thirteen-year tribunal converting an entire prior land regime — Spanish and Mexican, communal and occupation-based — into the individuated, transactable titles that [property-as-disposition](../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/glossary/terms/san-rafael-de-la-zanja-grant.md) requires as its starting material. The grove at Lochiel stands on ground the court touched twice over: once when it shrank the Camerons' claim to four leagues instead of sixteen, and once, more quietly, when it decided a commons could only be recognized as property once it had first been converted into something a single line on a survey map could confirm.
