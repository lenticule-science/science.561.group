Influencer marketing — brands' use of social-media influencers' self-branded audiences to promote products — has its anchor peer-reviewed systematic review in Demetris Vrontis, Anna Makrides, Michael Christofi, and Alkis Thrassou's ["Social Media Influencer Marketing: A Systematic Review, Integrative Framework and Future Research Agenda"](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijcs.12647) (*International Journal of Consumer Studies* 45(4), 2021), which synthesizes the fragmented literature into an integrative multidimensional framework organized around antecedents, mediators, moderators, outcomes, and contextual factors of influencer-marketing effects.

**Disclosure effects** are studied separately.
Sophie C. Boerman, Eva A. van Reijmersdal, and Peter C. Neijens's ["Sponsorship Disclosure: Effects of Duration on Persuasion Knowledge and Brand Responses"](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.2012.01677.x) (*Journal of Communication* 62(6), 2012, pp. 1047–1064) found experimentally that longer disclosure duration increases viewers' activation of persuasion knowledge (recognizing content as advertising), which in turn produces more negative brand attitudes and lower purchase intentions.
Xia Liu and Hong Zhao's ["A meta-analysis of the effects of sponsorship disclosure in influencer marketing"](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11002-024-09757-z) (*Marketing Letters* 36(3), 2024, pp. 519–532) pools this experimental literature (288 effect sizes from 37 studies, 12,721 participants) and finds, per its public abstract, that disclosure significantly increases persuasion-knowledge activation while its pooled effects on brand attitude and conative responses are statistically insignificant — disclosure reliably makes audiences recognize sponsored content as sponsored, without reliably harming brand attitudes at the pooled level.
**[UNVERIFIED]**: this section could not access the full text to confirm specific pooled effect-size figures (an earlier draft cited Hedges' g ≈ 0.60 for recognition and g ≈ −0.04 for brand attitude; those numbers are removed pending primary-source confirmation), and how many platforms, jurisdictions, and disclosure-format types the pooled studies span was not independently confirmed by this section; cross-platform/cross-jurisdiction generalization of this finding should be treated as open pending direct inspection of that meta-analysis's included-study table.

The primary regulatory source is the US Federal Trade Commission's Endorsement Guides, codified at [16 CFR Part 255, "Guides Concerning Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising"](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-255), revised in 2023 with specific social-media examples: an influencer paid to post about a product must disclose that connection "clearly and conspicuously" rather than only in a bio or "more" section viewers could easily miss, and disclosure is required even when the endorser received free product rather than cash payment.

Influencer marketing draws its self-branding theory from [personal branding](personal-branding.md) — specifically Khamis, Ang & Welling's (2017) extension of self-branding to micro-celebrity — and its media platform mechanics from **social-media influencer/creator studies**, owned by the media-science forest at [`social-media-science-services-section`](/home/emsenn/info/lib/emsenn/services/561-group/services/science-services-section/services/information-science-services-section/services/communication-science-services-section/services/media-science-services-section/services/social-media-science-services-section): this section owns influencer *marketing* — effectiveness, disclosure, and endorsement — while that section owns influencer/creator *media* science, and the two cross-link at that boundary.
