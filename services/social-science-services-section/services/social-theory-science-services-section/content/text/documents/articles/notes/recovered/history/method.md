# method

A method is the system of conventions, specifications, skills, and policies that governs how work is conducted. Method describes how things *should* be done; practice is the actual doing. If practice is a performance, method is the score — the codified, prescriptive form against which the performance is measured and to which it is, ideally, answerable. Generated by: **practice** — method is practice's codified and revisable side, the abstraction of a way of working into an explicit, governing form.

Several properties together make something a method rather than a mere habit or a loose collection of rules. It is *codified*: documented as specifications, policies, or recorded skills, treated as governing rather than merely describing. Uncodified habit is not method; a team that "just knows how to do it" has practice but not method, and method begins when the practice is written down and the writing is treated as authoritative. It is *coherent*: its parts relate as a system rather than as an ad-hoc pile of independently written rules that never reconcile. It is *prescriptive*: it takes a position on the right way to work, and so it can be wrong, unlike a neutral description of how a team happens to behave. And it is *revisable*: it can be changed when it no longer serves the work's purposes; a method that cannot be amended has hardened into ritual.

The relation between method and practice is one of social theory's recurring themes, and it is genuinely two-directional. Method constrains practice, but practice continually tests method, and the gap between the two — the place where the written way and the lived doing diverge — is where learning happens. A method that no one follows is shelfware; a practice that follows no method is improvisation, effective in expert hands but unreproducible and unaccountable. Organized work lives in the productive tension between them: codified method makes a practice transmissible and answerable, while ongoing practice reveals where the method is wrong and forces its revision.

This is also where method connects to the critical analysis of work, and the connection is not innocent. The codification of a way of working is never politically neutral. Scientific management is precisely the *imposition* of method from above — the conversion of workers' own tacit ways of working into an explicit procedure designed by management and handed back as binding instruction. The separation of conception from execution that Braverman analyzed as deskilling is, in this vocabulary, the appropriation of method: capturing the worker's practical knowledge, formalizing it as method, and lodging that method in the planning department, the machine, or the procedure, so that the worker is left only to execute. Method, then, is a site of contest: whose practice gets codified, who controls the resulting method, and whether the codification serves the practitioners or subordinates them.

The tradition of practice theory illuminates the same hinge from another angle. Foucault traced how practices, and the methods that govern them, change and migrate — the prison's timetable from the monastery, its drill from the military — showing methods as historically contingent artifacts carrying their origins with them. Dewey's pragmatism treats practice itself as a form of inquiry whose results feed back into the methods that govern it. As a node, method is the abstract, governing counterpart to concrete practice and the immediate container for skill (compiled units of method an agent can invoke). A methodology is a particular named package of method (a Scrum, a Kanban); a method is the actual system a given endeavor assembles for itself from whatever sources serve the work.
