# office

An office is a normative position that holds authority and persists independently of any particular
holder — the impersonal slot to which power attaches, distinct from whoever happens to occupy it at
a given moment. Generated by: office is what authority becomes when it is vested in a role rather than
a person. It is the hallmark of Weber's rational-legal authority, the form in which a presidency, a
judgeship, or a bishopric exists as a structure of duties and powers that an incumbent steps into and
later vacates, while the structure itself goes on. An office differs from a role in general: every
office is a role, but only those roles that carry authority — the power to bind others — are offices.

The deepest articulation of this idea comes from Roman law and the medieval canonists who built on
it. Roman public service distinguished *munus*, *honor*, and *dignitas*. A *munus* is a public burden
borne without title of dignity — the obligation to fund a public work, say, without thereby holding
office. An *honor* adds recognized standing: administration carried out "with a grade of dignity."
And *dignitas* names the standing that persists beyond any holder — the famous maxim *dignitas non
moritur*, "the dignity does not die." The dignity does not die because the dignity *is* the office's
normative structure, not the mortal individual who currently bears it. This is why succession can be
instantaneous: when a king dies the office passes at once to the successor, since the authority was
never lodged in the dead man's body but in the enduring form he occupied.

Ernst Kantorowicz gave this its most influential modern statement in *The King's Two Bodies* (1957),
reconstructing the Tudor legal theory that the king has two bodies — a body natural, which is born,
ages, and dies, and a body politic, which "cannot be seen or handled," is "utterly void of Infancy,
and old Age, and other natural Defects," and persists across reigns. The body politic is the office;
the body natural is the officeholder. John Searle (*The Construction of Social Reality*, 1995)
supplies the mechanism by which someone enters an office: a *status-function declaration*, a
collective act of the form "this person counts as the holder of this office," which installs the
incumbent by collective acceptance rather than by any physical change in them. Installation, like any
such performative, has felicity conditions — the right authority, the right procedure — and an attempt
by someone lacking the power simply fails, however correct the words.

The separation of office from holder does political work beyond mere persistence. James Madison's
*Federalist No. 51* turns it into the architecture of constitutional government: "Ambition must be
made to counteract ambition. The interest of the man must be connected with the constitutional rights
of the place." Separation of powers is the structural insulation of offices from one another, so that
no holder of one can unilaterally settle the questions reserved to another, and each branch defends
its own prerogatives. The office thus becomes a device for checking the very power it confers.

In this cluster, the office is the immediate child of authority — the role-slot to which the right to
command is fixed — and the building block of bureaucracy, which is nothing but offices systematized
into a hierarchy of fixed jurisdictions and written files. The distinction between office and holder
is also what makes corruption, usurpation, and abuse of office legible as *wrongs*: they are failures
to keep the mortal occupant subordinate to the impersonal structure, treating a position held in
trust as a personal possession to be owned and exploited.
