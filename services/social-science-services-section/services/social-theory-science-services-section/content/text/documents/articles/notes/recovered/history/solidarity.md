# solidarity

Solidarity is the practice of standing with others against shared conditions of domination — not
charity handed down from a position of safety, but mutual commitment among people who recognize
their fates as bound together. Its plainest statement is the one the labour movement gave it: an
injury to one is an injury to all. Generated by: **reciprocating** — solidarity is among the
purest of the reciprocal relations, a bond of equals who pool their capacity to act rather than
one party extracting from another.

The word entered sociology through Durkheim, who used it for the very thing that holds a society
together, and distinguished two kinds. *Mechanical* solidarity binds people through likeness — a
small community of people who share beliefs, work, and ways of life, held together because they
are alike. *Organic* solidarity binds them through difference — a complex society of specialists
who depend on one another precisely because no one is self-sufficient, held together by the
division of labour. Durkheim's question was how social cohesion survives modernity's relentless
differentiation; his answer, that interdependence itself can be a bond, is the ancestor of every
later account of why societies do not simply fly apart.

The political tradition gives the word a sharper, more demanding sense: solidarity is not a
feeling but a practice, and it is defined against charity. Charity flows downward and leaves the
hierarchy between giver and receiver intact — the donor remains a donor, the recipient a
recipient, and the relation that produced the need goes unquestioned. Solidarity refuses that
position. It acts *with* rather than *for*, treats the other as a co-equal participant in a shared
struggle, and aims at the conditions that produce the harm rather than at relief from its
symptoms. The strike, the picket line, the mutual-aid network, the sanctuary all express it. Its
moral core is the recognition that liberation is collective or it is nothing — "none of us are
free until all of us are free."

The hardest and most important form is solidarity across difference: a bond chosen between people
who do *not* share the same position, the same oppression, or the same interests in any obvious
way. This is the solidarity the Combahee River Collective and Audre Lorde theorized — coalition
not as the discovery of a pre-existing sameness but as the deliberate, often uncomfortable work of
binding distinct struggles together without dissolving their differences. It is fragile, because
it cannot rely on identity to hold it; it must be continually remade. But it is the only kind
adequate to a world in which domination is organized along many axes at once, and it is the
reciprocal counterpart to that whole apparatus of domination: where domination divides in order to
rule, solidarity is the work of refusing to be divided.
