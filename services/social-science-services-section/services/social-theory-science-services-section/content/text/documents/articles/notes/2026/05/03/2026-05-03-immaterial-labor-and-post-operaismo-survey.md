*Post-operaismo* (post-workerism) — also called *autonomist Marxism* in its Anglophone reception — is the Italian Marxist tradition that developed from the 1960s *operaismo* (workerism) of Mario Tronti and Raniero Panzieri through the 1970s *autonomia* movement to the late-twentieth and early-twenty-first century theoretical work of Maurizio Lazzarato, Antonio Negri, Paolo Virno, Franco "Bifo" Berardi, Christian Marazzi, and the cognitive-capitalism program of Carlo Vercellone and others. Its central contribution to work sociology is the analysis of *immaterial labor* and the broader claim that contemporary capitalism's value-extraction logic has shifted from the factory to the *social factory* — to the production of subjectivity, communication, knowledge, and affect across the whole of social life.

## Operaismo's foundations

The original *operaismo* of the 1960s — centered on journals *Quaderni Rossi* (1961–1965) and *Classe Operaia* (1964–1967) — developed in confrontation with both orthodox Italian Communist Party Marxism and academic sociology. Raniero Panzieri, founder and editor of *Quaderni Rossi*, supplied the project's animating method in his 1961 essay on the capitalist use of machinery [panzieri-1961]: against an objectivist reading of Marx in which technology develops along its own neutral trajectory, Panzieri argued that the technical organization of production is itself an instrument of capitalist control, with concrete forms shaped by the antagonism between capital and labor at the point of production. The methodological corollary — *conricerca* (co-research) with workers as the proper means of investigating the labor process — became the operaismo tradition's distinctive empirical-political method.

Mario Tronti's *Operai e Capitale* [tronti-1966] supplied the foundational theoretical move: the *Copernican inversion* of orthodox Marxism. Where orthodox Marxism analyzed capital's development and treated the working class as its product, Tronti argued that the working class is the *potential subject* whose struggles drive capital's development; capital's restructurings are *responses to* worker insurgency, not autonomous logics.

The implication: the working class's revolutionary potential lies not in waiting for capitalism to mature but in *refusing* its own role as labor — what came to be called the *refusal of work* — and in continuously inventing forms of struggle that capital must respond to and that progressively transform the terrain.

The 1970s *autonomia* movement carried this politics into mass struggle in Italy until its violent state repression in 1979 (the *7 April* arrests, in which Negri and many others were imprisoned). The theoretical tradition continued in exile and underground, gradually developing the post-operaismo synthesis through the 1980s and 1990s.

## The general intellect

Marx's *Grundrisse* [marx-grundrisse] — the 1857–1858 manuscripts that prepared *Capital*, unpublished until 1939 and unavailable in Italian until the 1960s — became central to the operaismo tradition through one fragment: the *Fragment on Machines* (Notebook VII), in which Marx considers a possible future in which production has become so automated that the *general intellect* of socialized humanity — knowledge, science, communication — becomes "directly a force of production." In such a system, value can no longer be measured in labor time; the law of value enters crisis; new forms of social organization become both possible and necessary.

The post-operaismo tradition reads the late-twentieth-century transformations — automation, computerization, knowledge work, the service economy — as the gradual realization of the *Fragment on Machines* scenario. The central category becomes the *general intellect*: the socialized intellectual capacity that contemporary production increasingly mobilizes and that increasingly exceeds the wage relation's capacity to capture it.

Carlo Vercellone [vercellone-2007] supplied the most systematic theoretical articulation of the cognitive-capitalism thesis, distinguishing three historical capitalist regimes: *mercantile* capitalism (formal subsumption of pre-capitalist production), *industrial* capitalism (real subsumption through the factory), and *cognitive* capitalism (the becoming-productive of socialized knowledge, with the wage relation in protracted crisis). The cognitive-capitalism program has been taken up across French, Italian, and Anglophone heterodox economics.

## Lazzarato and immaterial labor

Maurizio Lazzarato's 1996 essay *Immaterial Labor* [lazzarato-1996] supplied the field's defining concept. *Immaterial labor* is "the labor that produces the informational and cultural content of the commodity" — divisible into:

1. **Informational content** — the cybernetic and computer-control transformations of industrial labor, in which the worker's relevant activity is increasingly the manipulation of symbols and information.
2. **Cultural content** — the production of activities that determine and define cultural and artistic standards, fashions, tastes, consumer norms, and public opinion.

Both kinds of labor:

- *Produce subjectivity* (the worker, the consumer, the public) as their characteristic output, alongside or instead of any material product.
- *Mobilize communicative and affective capacities* that workers develop largely outside the wage relation, in the broader social-cultural environment.
- *Cannot be measured in labor time* in the way industrial labor could; their value is indeterminate.
- *Spill across the boundary* between work and non-work; the worker's whole social life becomes potentially productive.

The concept generated substantial subsequent literature, both in elaboration (the affect-economy, the attention-economy, the experience-economy literatures) and in critique (charges that the concept overestimates novelty, neglects continuing material labor, and exaggerates the obsolescence of older Marxist categories).

## Hardt and Negri: Empire and the multitude

Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri's tetralogy — *Empire* [hardt-negri-2000], *Multitude* [hardt-negri-2004], *Commonwealth* [hardt-negri-2009], and *Assembly* [hardt-negri-2017] — supplied the most ambitious post-operaismo synthesis and the tradition's most influential Anglophone reception.

*Empire* developed the macro-political-economic frame: the contemporary world order is no longer organized around national imperial powers but around a *de-territorialized network sovereignty* — *Empire* — that operates through international institutions (UN, WTO, IMF), transnational corporations, NGO networks, and the global media-information apparatus. There is no longer an outside to capital; the entire planet is its terrain.

*Multitude* developed the corresponding subject of struggle. Not the *people* (a sovereign-political construct) and not the *working class* (the industrial-Fordist subject), but the *multitude* — the open, plural network of singularities whose communicative, affective, and cognitive labor constitutes the productive substrate Empire depends on. The multitude is *constitutive* (not derivative): it produces social life directly through cooperation, with capital's organizing role increasingly redundant or parasitic.

*Commonwealth* and *Assembly* developed the strategic implications: a politics of *the common* (commoning practices, commons-based production, alternative economies) and a politics of *assembly* (horizontal organizational forms drawing on the alterglobalization, Occupy, and squares movements).

The tetralogy generated enormous reception (and substantial critique). The framework has been taken up across political theory, social-movement studies, and contemporary work-and-organization research, even by scholars who reject the full tetralogy's claims.

## Virno: grammar and ambivalence

Paolo Virno's *A Grammar of the Multitude* [virno-2004] supplied the philosophical-anthropological complement. Virno's central thesis: the same human capacities — language, communication, sociality, intellectual cooperation — that capital now mobilizes for production are also the basis on which capital can be exceeded. The general intellect is *ambivalent*: it is both capital's productive substrate and the substrate from which an alternative could be constructed.

Virno develops the analysis through Aristotle (*phronesis* and the *bios politikos*), Marx (the general intellect), Heidegger (*Stimmung* and the publicness of language), and the Italian operaismo tradition. The concept of the multitude in Virno is closer to its Spinozan origin than in Hardt-Negri, and its political implications are more cautious: the multitude's *constitutive ambivalence* means it can be mobilized for fascism as readily as for emancipation, with the political question being which mobilization wins out under contemporary conditions.

## Berardi: the soul at work

Franco "Bifo" Berardi's *The Soul at Work* [berardi-2009] developed the *psycho-pathological* analysis of cognitive capitalism. His argument: when capital's value-extraction logic moves from the worker's body to the worker's *soul* — to attention, affect, communication, intellect — it begins to consume the very subjective capacities that produce sociality. The contemporary epidemics of burnout, depression, anxiety, ADHD, and suicide among knowledge workers and platform workers are not individual pathologies but the *systemic effects* of a value-extraction logic that exceeds the soul's metabolic capacity.

Berardi's analysis connects the post-operaismo framework to the contemporary mental-health crisis and the attention-economy literature, and has substantial influence on contemporary critical theory of work.

## Marazzi: capital and language

Christian Marazzi's *Capital and Language* [marazzi-2008] developed the analysis of *language as productive force*. His argument: the late-twentieth-century financial revolution — the centrality of expectations, communications, and narratives in financial-market dynamics — is not merely a financial-sector phenomenon; it is the financial appearance of a deeper transformation in which *linguistic activity itself* has become directly productive of value. The blurring between economic activity and linguistic-communicative activity is the cognitive-capitalism phenomenon viewed from its financial side.

## Critiques and limits

The post-operaismo tradition has been subjected to substantial critique. Major lines:

- **Empirical overreach**: the immaterial-labor thesis arguably overestimates novelty, neglects the continuing material-labor base of contemporary economies (mining, manufacturing, agriculture, logistics, care, sex work), and treats the knowledge-worker experience as paradigmatic when it is empirically minoritarian.
- **Northern-Atlantic provincialism**: the framework's empirical referents are the post-Fordist economies of Western Europe and North America; its applicability to the global South, where industrial and pre-industrial forms remain dominant, is contested.
- **Class-analytic dilution**: the substitution of "the multitude" for "the working class" has been criticized as theoretically undisciplined and politically demobilizing, evacuating the analytical specificity that class analysis requires.
- **Optimism about communicative cooperation**: critics argue the framework underestimates the violent, coercive, and disciplinary dimensions of contemporary work and overestimates the latent emancipatory potential of contemporary cooperative forms.

Nicholas Thoburn's [thoburn-2003] *Deleuze, Marx and Politics* supplied a sympathetic-critical synthesis, situating post-operaismo in the broader Deleuzian-Marxist current and identifying both its productive insights and its theoretical liabilities.

## Key conceptual distinctions

Three distinctions structure the field:

1. **Material vs. immaterial labor.** The labor that produces material commodities vs. the labor that produces information, communication, affect, and subjectivity; the post-operaismo claim is that the second has become hegemonic.
2. **The factory vs. the social factory.** Value production confined to the wage-relation workplace vs. value production diffused across the whole of social life; the *social factory* is the post-operaismo's central spatial-analytical category.
3. **The working class vs. the multitude.** The Fordist-industrial subject of struggle vs. the open-plural network of singular cooperators that post-operaismo proposes as the contemporary subject.

## What the tradition supplies

Three contributions:

1. **An analytical vocabulary for cognitive and affective work.** Where orthodox labor process theory developed its categories for industrial labor, post-operaismo developed categories — immaterial labor, cognitive capitalism, the general intellect, the social factory — for the work that increasingly characterizes contemporary economies.
2. **A theoretical frame for the value problem in cognitive capitalism.** The classical labor theory of value, formulated for material commodities under measurable labor time, faces structural difficulties when applied to information, communication, and affect; post-operaismo supplies the most developed Marxist framework for thinking through these difficulties.
3. **A bridge between work theory and politics.** The tradition's continuous concern with *politics* — refusal of work, autonomy, commoning, assembly — keeps work-theoretic analysis connected to questions of organization, struggle, and alternatives that more academic work theories tend to bracket.
