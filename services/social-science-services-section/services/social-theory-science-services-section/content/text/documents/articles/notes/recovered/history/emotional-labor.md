# emotional-labor

Emotional labor is Arlie Russell Hochschild's term, introduced in *The Managed Heart: Commercialization
of Human Feeling* (1983), for the work of producing or suppressing one's own feeling in order to meet
the affective expectations of an employer, institution, role, or audience. Where physical labor acts on
materials and mental labor on information, emotional labor acts on the worker's own emotional state:
the flight attendant who must summon warmth toward a hostile passenger, the bill collector who must
manufacture intimidation, the nurse who must hold steady reassurance through fear. What is being worked
on, and made into a commodity, is feeling itself. Generated by: **feeling-rules** — emotional labor is
the work of bringing one's own feeling into conformity with the rules an institution prescribes.

The concept builds on Hochschild's prior idea of *feeling rules*: the social prescriptions that dictate
which emotions count as appropriate responses to a given situation, and how intensely, for how long, and
toward whom they should be felt. A wedding prescribes joy, a funeral grief, a service encounter cheerful
deference. Feeling rules carry normative force; they tell people not only how to act but what to feel.
Emotional labor is what a person does to close the gap between the feeling they actually have and the
feeling the rule demands. When that labor is performed privately, among intimates, Hochschild calls it
*emotion work*. When it is sold — when an employer purchases the worker's capacity to produce the right
feeling in customers as a condition of the job — it becomes emotional labor, a managed and commercialized
form of the same activity.

Hochschild's central analytic distinction is between *surface acting* and *deep acting*. In surface
acting, the worker manufactures an outward display they do not feel — the pasted-on smile, the simulated
patience — while remaining inwardly detached; the feeling is faked at the level of expression. In deep
acting, the worker actually induces the required feeling in themselves, summoning real warmth or real
calm by reworking their interior state, as a stage actor does using emotional memory. Deep acting is, in
one sense, the more successful — the customer receives sincere feeling — but it is also the more costly,
because it reaches further into the self. Hochschild's worry, drawn from her fieldwork with Delta
Airlines flight attendants, is that when feeling is managed this thoroughly for pay, the worker can lose
access to their own spontaneous emotional signals — a kind of estrangement from one's own feelings that
she frames as the emotional analogue of Marxist alienation. The heart, managed long enough on someone
else's terms, ceases to be reliably one's own.

Emotional labor is, in Hochschild's account, deeply gendered and systematically unrecognized. The jobs
that demand it most heavily — flight attendant, nurse, receptionist, caregiver, service worker — are
disproportionately held by women, and the skill they require is treated not as labor but as a natural
feminine disposition, which both naturalizes the demand and removes it from the wage. This makes
emotional labor a paradigm case of invisible work: indispensable to the operation, exhausting to perform,
and largely uncompensated because it is not seen as work at all. The concept has since been extended,
sometimes loosely, to the unpaid relational and "office-housework" feeling-management that falls
disproportionately on women in households and workplaces — an extension Hochschild herself has cautioned
can blur her original, more specific claim about commercially purchased feeling.

For a graduate student, emotional labor matters as the point where affect and political economy meet. It
shows that feeling is not simply private and interior but can be extracted, like physical capacity, as a
condition of employment — and that this extraction operates on the worker's own emotional state rather
than on anything outside it. Within the broader apparatus analyzed in this branch, emotional labor is the
concrete, wage-level form of rule-by-feeling: feeling rules become demands, the demands become work, and
the work is performed inside the self. In platform-mediated conditions, the same dynamic recurs at new
scale, as engagement metrics enforce affective expectations and route emotional self-management through
the feed.
