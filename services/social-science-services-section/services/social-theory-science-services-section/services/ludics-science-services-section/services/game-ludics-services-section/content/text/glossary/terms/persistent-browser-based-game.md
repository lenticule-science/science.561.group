A persistent browser-based game, abbreviated PBBG, is defined in a practitioner-level article from Kanoplay by what its server does between sessions: it "store[s] the state of every object and character on the server," so that "if you log out and then log back in later, your game world will still be exactly how it was when you logged out." The definition is minimal and mechanical, and the sources gathered for this section give the category no fuller scholarly treatment than that: a general Wikipedia article on browser games as a whole, spanning from Earth 2025 (1995) through the Flash era and the .io era, is, in this section's own words, "notably silent on PBBGs/tick-based games as a category," which the sweep behind this glossary reads as "a gap in even the general encyclopedic record."

Two titles in this section's sources give the definition documented substance. Urban Dead (Kevan Davis, launched July 2005, closed March 2025) ran for nearly twenty years on an action-point economy — covered under that term in this glossary — that throttled play by the tick rather than by session length, and its persistence produced, according to a Dead Entertainment retrospective, a world whose "lore and backstory has been created by its players over the years" rather than by its developer, because Davis, in the retrospective's account, "set up the basic framework of the game and then turn[ed] it over to the playerbase." Kingdom of Loathing (Zack "Jick" Johnson, 2003) is described by a JayIsGames review as "composed entirely in DHTML and compatible with most browsers," with persistent multiplayer chat rooms and a daily-turn allowance that similarly stores state across sessions but on a different rhythm, banking unused turns against a cap rather than regenerating them continuously. The 2000–2005 wave of tick-based strategy PBBGs named in this section's sources — Planetarion, OGame, Travian, Utopia — could be sourced only at Wikipedia-article grade, and their absence from substantial journalism or scholarship is itself flagged as a finding about the literature, not merely a search failure.

The PBBG's defining property — a world that keeps its own state and keeps advancing that state on its own schedule — is the same property the idle game shares, and the tick is the specific mechanism by which many PBBGs realize it, though PBBGs are not restricted to tick-based designs and typically host many simultaneous players sharing one persisting world rather than a single player's private instance. Neopets (1999, Donna Williams and Adam Powell) is documented in the sources as a related case that pairs persistent-world storage — a pet that "get[s] sad" if neglected across sessions — with a Neopoints economy, though this section flags a genuine reading gap here: the one scholarly source found treating Neopets critically, Sara M. Grimes and Leslie Regan Shade's 2005 article on its "immersive advertising," was not read directly and is cited only at second hand.
