Play community, as used in this literature, is defined by Celia Pearce, citing Bernie DeKoven, as a group "whose commitment to playing together transcends any specific game or its rules." Pearce's own case study is the concept's proof of concept: The Gathering, a roughly 350-member group formed within *Uru: Ages Beyond Myst*, kept playing together across four subsequent virtual worlds after Uru itself ceased to exist, which is the behavior the definition is built to capture — the commitment outlives the particular game. This entry relays Pearce's citation of DeKoven's definition rather than DeKoven's own text, which was not itself among the sources read for this glossary.

Pearce's ethnography treats the play community, not the individual player or the individual world, as the unit that actually persists. When Uru's servers closed on February 9, 2004, roughly 10,000 players were displaced, and many experienced genuine, reported grief; but for The Gathering specifically, that shared trauma "served as a catalyst for fortifying the group identity" — the community's persistence and the world's persistence turned out to be independent variables, and losing the second did not force losing the first. Pearce further reports that The Gathering eventually took over creative stewardship of Uru-inspired content after the official game ended, an act she reads as "vindicating the game's narrative," which had originally cast its players as restorers of a lost civilization.

Play community sits directly beneath fictive-ethnicity, Pearce's name for a play community's group identity once it has been forged and hardened by shared history and loss, and beside intersubjective-flow, her proposed mechanism for how such a community sustains its members' engagement in each other. Together the three terms make a claim that cuts against a purely operator-centered reading of persistence: even though Koster's laws describe persistence as an obligation the operator can never discharge (see persistent-world), Pearce's material shows that a play community can, in effect, out-persist the operator's own world entirely.
