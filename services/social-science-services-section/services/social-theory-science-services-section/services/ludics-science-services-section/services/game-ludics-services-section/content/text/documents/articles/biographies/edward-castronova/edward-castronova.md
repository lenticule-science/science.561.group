description: "The economist who first treated a virtual world's economy as a real one to be measured, and whose definitional and boundary work anchors three of the section's programs."
date-created: 2026-08-22
---

Edward Castronova is an economist, currently Professor of Media at Indiana University Bloomington. He holds a PhD in economics from the University of Wisconsin–Madison (1991) and a BS in international affairs from Georgetown University. He held faculty positions at the University of Rochester and California State University, Fullerton before joining Indiana University in 2004, where he has taught in telecommunications and cognitive science and directed graduate studies. In 2003 he co-founded Terra Nova, an early and influential weblog on virtual worlds. His two books most relevant to this portfolio are *Synthetic Worlds: The Business and Culture of Online Games* (2005) and, with Vili Lehdonvirta, *Virtual Economies: Design and Analysis* (MIT Press, 2014).

Castronova's load-bearing document for the section is "Virtual Worlds: A First-Hand Account of Market and Society on the Cyberian Frontier" (CESifo Working Paper No. 618, Dec. 2001), a paper that is as much first-person ethnography as economic report — it opens, "Journal entry, 18 April. I have called my avatar 'Alaniel.'" Working from a 3,619-respondent survey of EverQuest's Norrath plus hundreds of scraped currency and avatar auctions, Castronova computed the world as if it were a nation: a currency, the platinum piece, trading at $0.01072 — higher than the yen or the lira, and falling 25% in one quarter; a GNP per capita of $2,266, making Norrath "the 77th richest country in the world, roughly equal to Russia," richer than China or India; an average hourly wage of $3.42; chronic 29%-per-year deflation because items never decayed while new players kept arriving; and wealth inequality worse than "post-industrial societies on Earth." His methodological wager was explicitly economic: "economists believe that it is the practical actions of people, and not abstract arguments, that determine the social value of things." Castronova also supplied the section's cleanest working definition of a persistent world, built on three features — interactivity, physicality (scarcity), and persistence, the last of which he stated independently of any game-studies vocabulary: "the program continues to run whether anyone is using it or not; it remembers the location of people and things, as well as the ownership of objects." A separate strand of his work, the "almost-magic circle" chapter of *Synthetic Worlds*, argues the play-boundary is "definitely there but also definitely porous, and this is by choice of the users" — a membrane that lets players tune their own immersion while real-money markets make virtual goods "just as real as the goods outside of it." *Virtual Economies*, co-written with Lehdonvirta, insists games' currencies are fiat money in the full technical sense ("Both the US Dollar and World of Warcraft coins are fiat money") and that virtual economies are designed for engagement and revenue rather than efficiency — the section holds this claim through a secondary academic review and block quotations reproduced in later scholarship, not a primary read of the book itself, and treats it accordingly.

Castronova's position in the field is foundational rather than partisan: he is credited as the first economist to treat virtual worlds as subjects of serious economic measurement, and his 2001 paper became one of the most downloaded working papers in its archive, independent of the game-studies debates that followed. Three of the section's six programs hold him. Obsmetrology of Play holds the bulk of his work — the Norrath accounts and the *Virtual Economies* synthesis are load-bearing sources for the program's central claim that a live persistent world is a continuously instrumented observation system. Boundary and Membrane holds the "almost-magic circle" material as the paper's economic mechanism for boundary porosity. Persistent World Foundations holds his interactivity/physicality/persistence triple as one of four independent practitioner and scholarly formulations of persistence that the program treats as convergent.
