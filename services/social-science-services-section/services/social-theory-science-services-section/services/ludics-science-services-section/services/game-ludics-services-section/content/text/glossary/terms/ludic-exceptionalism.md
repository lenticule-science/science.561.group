Ludic exceptionalism is one of two failure modes Thomas Malaby diagnoses in games scholarship in "Beyond Play" (2007), the practice of treating games as categorically set apart from the rest of human experience — either dismissed as trivial because they are "just a game," or romanticized as a separate, protected, utopian space precisely because they are set apart. Its companion diagnosis is formalism: the treatment of a game's rules as fixed, ahistorical structures, a habit of mind Malaby traces to the von Neumann and Morgenstern tradition of treating games as closed formal systems defined completely by their rule sets. Malaby presents the two as sharing a single root cause rather than as independent errors — both amount to forgetting that games are processual, "grounded in (and constituted by) human practice and are therefore always in the process of becoming," and not reducible to their rules at any given moment.

Malaby's evidence for the processual claim, and so against both formalism and exceptionalism at once, is that games visibly change from the inside, through play itself, in ways a purely formal or purely bounded reading cannot accommodate. His examples are the National Football League's "tuck rule" controversy, in which a contested rule interpretation itself became a site of dispute and change, and the introduction of the shot clock in basketball after players learned to exploit stalling tactics the original rules had not anticipated — in both cases, practice inside the game generated a change to the game, which formalism's static rule-set picture cannot represent and exceptionalism's sealed-off picture cannot explain the stakes of. This is the same processual argument that grounds Malaby's redefinition of a game as a domain of contrived contingency rather than as a bounded instance of play, and the two diagnoses are best read as the negative half of that positive redefinition: formalism and exceptionalism are what scholarship falls into when it treats a domain of contrived contingency as if it were instead a closed, separate, static system.

The stakes Malaby assigns to exceptionalism specifically are ethical rather than merely definitional. He writes that "the events within the border are given special social meanings" even though "all human activities are equally real," and warns in a footnote that virtual worlds are where the exceptionalist fallacy causes the most concrete harm, since treating an online world as "a place where nothing happens" licenses designers and observers alike to set aside ethical scrutiny of what happens inside it. This glossary reads that warning as directly continuous with the material treated under boundary-work and playbour, in both of which real consequence — grief, labor, exploitation — is documented crossing a boundary that exceptionalist framing would predict should have kept it out.
