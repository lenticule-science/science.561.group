Persona is Richard Bartle's name for the deepest of the three levels of identification he distinguishes in *Designing Virtual Worlds*, above avatar (a doll) and character (a simulacrum treated more like a friend). At the persona level, in Bartle's words, "you're not assuming an identity, you are that identity... If you're killed in a fight, you don't feel that your character has died, you feel that you have died."

Bartle treats this depth of identification as the point of the whole design activity, not as a curiosity: "The celebration of identity is the fundamental, critical, absolutely core point of virtual worlds... It's why achievers achieve, explorers explore, socializers socialize, and killers... batter you with a stick" — tying persona-level investment directly to the four player-types. He also treats anonymity as the condition that makes persona-level experimentation survivable at all: "contact with reality collapses virtual identity like observation does a quantum wave."

Persistence complicates the protection anonymity is supposed to offer. Bartle observes that the longer a player inhabits a persona, the more costly it becomes to abandon it even when nothing but the player's own attachment holds them there: "the longer you play a character, the more attached to it you become... you soon reach the stage where you've invested so much in that character that if you do screw up you would be very reluctant to throw it all away. Instead, you ride out the storm." A world with no reset is, on this reading, a world where persona-level investment can only accumulate, never be cleanly discharged — part of what this section's persistent-world-foundations program reads as the underlying mechanism behind Julian Dibbell's account of real emotional harm registering through a persistent character (see wizard), and behind Celia Pearce's account of a play-community's grief at a persistent world's shutdown (see fictive-ethnicity, intersubjective-flow).
