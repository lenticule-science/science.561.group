Fictive ethnicity is Celia Pearce's term, introduced in her ethnography "Communities of Play: The Social Construction of Identity in Persistent Online Game Worlds," for the durable group identity a play community can develop out of shared history within, and shared attachment to, a persistent world — an identity strong enough to outlast the particular world in which it formed. Pearce coined the term to describe The Gathering, a roughly 350-member group she followed for eighteen months after the game that had brought them together, *Uru: Ages Beyond Myst*, shut its servers down on February 9, 2004, displacing an estimated 10,000 players.

Pearce's account is explicit that trauma, not mere continuity, is what forged the identity: members "reported weeping as the clock struck midnight and the avatars on the screen froze in place," and reported symptoms of post-traumatic stress at the shutdown — yet, in Pearce's words, this "served as a catalyst for fortifying the group identity, which evolved into a sort of fictive ethnicity. This shared group identity created both the necessity and the substrate for migrating their individual avatar identities into... other virtual worlds." The Gathering went on to carry that identity across four different virtual worlds over the study period, treating the persistence of the group as separable from the persistence of any single world.

The term is closely tied to two others in this glossary: play-community, the more general category, via Bernie DeKoven, of a group "whose commitment to playing together transcends any specific game or its rules," of which a fictive ethnicity is Pearce's account of one especially cohesive instance; and intersubjective-flow, the mechanism Pearce proposes by which such a group identity is actually produced and sustained moment to moment. The Uru diaspora is, in this literature, the sharpest documented case of a world's ending and a community's ending coming apart entirely — the arena was deleted, but the fictive ethnicity it had produced simply relocated.
