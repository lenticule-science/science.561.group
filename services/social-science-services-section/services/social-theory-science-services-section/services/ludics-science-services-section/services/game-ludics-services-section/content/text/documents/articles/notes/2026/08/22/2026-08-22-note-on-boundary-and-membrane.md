## Scope

Maintain the section's account of the play-boundary — what it is, how porous
it is, who tunes the porosity, and what crosses it — with persistent online
worlds as the forcing case, since there the leak is the norm and the boundary
never closes.

## Motivating questions

1. What survives of the magic circle after the 2005–2014 critique wave, and
   in what decomposition? (Working answer: Stenros's triple — psychological
   bubble, negotiated social contract, persisting cultural arena — joined by
   Goffman's transformation-membrane.)
2. Is boundary-leakage in persistent worlds an exception or the standing
   condition? What replaces the circle: membrane, frame, contract, or
   contingency-domain?
3. What does the play/work distinction dissolve into when a world sustains
   wage labor (gold farming), unwaged value-production (playbour), and
   self-reported play-as-chore (the labor of fun) simultaneously?
4. What do breach events look like when nothing can close them — and what
   discipline studies them when they escape game studies entirely
   (epidemiology for Corrupted Blood; law and ritual studies for the
   Serenity Now funeral raid)?

## Objects of study

The magic-circle debate corpus; Goffman's interaction membrane as applied to
play; playbour, modding EULAs, and free labor; gold farming and real-money
trading as economic sectors; documented breach events (Corrupted Blood,
Serenity Now, EVE's tolerated scams); player-level boundary-tuning.

## State of knowledge (2026-08-22 sweep)

- **The circle's textual history** (read, via Stenros): in Huizinga the
  phrase is nearly incidental — one item beside the arena, the card-table,
  the temple, the court of justice; Salen and Zimmerman built the formal
  boundary-concept, as Zimmerman admitted ("we more or less invented the
  concept").
- **The critique** (Consalvo key passage verbatim; Malaby read in full):
  Consalvo — ordinary rules "apply, but in addition to, in competition with,
  other rules"; Malaby — separable/safe/pleasurable are "cultural
  accomplishments," and a game is "a semibounded and socially legitimate
  domain of contrived contingency that generates interpretable outcomes,"
  processual, "always in the process of becoming."
- **The defenses** (Juul, Stenros read in full): Juul — "the boundary that
  players negotiate," with the puzzle piece as the better image; Stenros —
  the bubble/contract/arena decomposition, and Goffman's membrane: the border
  *transforms* what crosses, and "it is possible for play to be collapsed by
  external events."
- **The economic mechanism** (Castronova quote cross-confirmed, chapter not
  directly read; Heeks, Dibbell, Yee, Kücklich read in full): the
  "almost-magic circle," porous "by choice of the users"; Heeks's conclusion
  "there is just 'economics'"; Kücklich's playbour ("voluntarily given and
  unwaged, enjoyed and exploited"), with the industry's material interest in
  keeping the play-frame's classification intact; Yee's players describing
  play verbatim as work; Dibbell's Nanjing farmers as the administrative
  erasure of the distinction, with its involuntary remainder ("You want to
  play").
- **Breach case studies** (Serenity Now paper read; Lancet piece reported):
  the funeral-raid controversy unresolved for seven years along three
  simultaneous axes — ethical, ontological, aesthetic; Corrupted Blood as a
  game bug made legible to epidemiology because the world didn't reset.

## Obsmetrological bridge

The corpus reads the magic circle as the admission codec and deep play as
codec breach. This note's findings recommend two refinements, both graded
**rhyme**: (1) split the single codec the way Stenros splits the circle —
the persisting arena (the corpus's event structure), the negotiated contract
(the codec proper), and a per-player protective frame the corpus does not
currently model; (2) replace the codec-as-characteristic-function picture
with codec-as-transformation (Goffman): porosity is graded,
direction-dependent, and player-tuned, so breach is a matter of degree and
channel, not a binary. Malaby's four contingency sources
(stochastic/social/performative/semiotic) invite a four-regime comparison;
only stochastic ≈ alea aligns cleanly, and the mapping is explicitly *not*
asserted. The Serenity Now case is held as the concrete picture of a single
event carrying unresolved charge along several independent axes at once —
adjacent to the corpus's H¹ reading of Sutton-Smith, and filed as **rhyme**.

## Open problems

- Whether porosity admits measurement: can the direction and rate of
  value/affect transfer across a world's membrane be instrumented the way its
  economy already is? (Joint problem with Obsmetrology of Play.)
- The consent problem under persistence: Suits's lusory attitude is consent
  at admission; nothing in the literature or the corpus yet models consent to
  an arena that is rewritten after admission.
- Whether EVE's absorb-the-breach strategy (legislating betrayal into the
  ruleset) and LambdaMOO's adjudicate-the-breach strategy exhaust the
  operator's options, or are two points in a describable space.

## Reading debts

Consalvo's full 2009 article and her *Cheating* (2007); Castronova's
"Almost-Magic Circle" chapter in primary text; Lofgren and Fefferman's
Lancet piece in primary text; Carter's *Treacherous Play* (open access,

(2009) and the racialization-of-labor flank of gold-farming scholarship;

games.

## Annotated sources

Annotated sources on the magic-circle debate, the play/work economic pipeline, and documented breach events in persistent worlds.


### Sources

## Richard A. Bartle, 1996, "Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs," *Journal of MUD Research* 1(1)

URL: https://mud.co.uk/richard/hcds.htm

**Status:** read in full

The founding player-type taxonomy — Achievers, Explorers, Socialisers,
Killers, plotted on a Players-vs-World / Acting-vs-Interacting graph —
but its real content is dynamical rather than classificatory: the four
populations sit in standing feedback loops (Achievers "breed" Killers
"in a typically Malthusian fashion," Socialisers attract both more of
themselves and, fatally, more Killers) that only exist because a MUD's
population never resets between sessions. Bartle identifies three stable
configurations a MUD can settle into, with the balanced "Type 3" rare
because it demands sustained population management most administrators
lack. For this note the paper matters as the earliest evidence that
persistence turns player composition into a governance problem rather
than a level-design one.

## Richard A. Bartle, 2003, *Designing Virtual Worlds*, New Riders

URL: https://mud.co.uk/richard/DesigningVirtualWorlds.pdf

**Status:** read in substantial part

Read closely for its opening definitional chapter, its change/persistence
grid, and its chapter on "The Celebration of Identity." Bartle names
persistence as one of five co-equal necessary conditions for a virtual
world — the environment "continues to exist and develop internally...
even when there are no people interacting with it" — and his
change/persistence grid cross-tabulates how much players can build
against how much of it survives a reboot, using the result to answer a
single design question he treats as fundamental: "Whose world is it?"
The identity chapter argues players graduate from avatar to character to
persona, and that sustained investment in a persistent character makes
it too costly to abandon even when anonymity would technically permit
it. Note: the phrase "world, not a game" belongs to Koster, not to
Bartle; no source in this note locates that literal contrastive
framing anywhere in Bartle's own text, and this distinction should not
be blurred.

## Mark W. Bell, 2008, "Toward a Definition of 'Virtual Worlds,'" *Journal of Virtual Worlds Research* 1(1)

URL: https://jvwr-ojs-utexas.tdl.org/jvwr/article/view/283/237

**Status:** read in full

The field's standard synthesis definition, built explicitly by combining
and critiquing Bartle's, Koster's, and Castronova's informal definitions:
"a synchronous, persistent network of people, represented as avatars,
facilitated by networked computers." Bell's gloss on persistence is the
cleanest statement available in this note of why it changes the
phenomenology of play rather than merely its infrastructure — "no longer
is one participant the center of the world but a member of a dynamic
community and evolving economy." The paper is needed here as the
definitional anchor the practitioner tradition converges on, independent
of the theoretical magic-circle debate.

## Marcus Carter, 2022, *Treacherous Play*, MIT Press

URL: https://direct.mit.edu/books/oa-monograph/5255/Treacherous-Play

**Status:** reported at second hand

Examines EVE Online, DayZ, and *Survivor* as games where deception and
betrayal are structurally rewarded rather than policed, treating EVE's
institutionalized tolerance of scamming — CCP Games' own position that
it is "despicable, but allowed" under the EULA — as the mechanism by
which EVE manufactures its own high-stakes legitimacy. The monograph is
nominally open access but was bot-walled during the sweep for this
note, so its account here rests on search-level summaries only. It is
the note's sole source on EVE's absorb-the-breach strategy and is
consequently the single most valuable unread item for closing the
question of what an operator's real options are when a world cannot
close its own breaches.

## Edward Castronova, 2005, *Synthetic Worlds: The Business and Culture of Online Games*, University of Chicago Press, ch. 6, "The Almost-Magic Circle," pp. 147–160

URL: https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.7208/9780226096315-008/html

**Status:** key passages verbatim; not read in full

Argues the boundary of a synthetic world is real but structurally
porous, "by choice of the users," coining "almost-magic circle" for a
membrane that lets players regulate their own immersion while real-money
markets make virtual goods economically indistinguishable from real
ones. The chapter itself is paywalled; the verbatim passage used
throughout this note's sources — "the membrane between synthetic
worlds and daily life is definitely there but also definitely porous"
— was retrieved via secondary citation and cross-checked across two
independent citing sources, but the chapter has not been read directly.
It is needed as the origin point of "membrane" as the field's operative
term for the boundary, arrived at independently of Stenros's later
Goffman-derived usage.

## Mia Consalvo, 2009, "There Is No Magic Circle," *Games and Culture* 4(4), 408–417

URL: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1555412009343575

**Status:** key passages verbatim; not read in full

Argues against treating games as sealed spaces where "ordinary rules of
life do not apply," using cheating as the central case: cheating is
legible only because real-world norms of fairness, legal frameworks, and
social standing already operate inside gameplay, not outside some
circle. The article itself is paywalled; the verbatim passage used here
— "we cannot say that games are magic circles... Of course they apply,
but in addition to, in competition with, other rules" — was retrieved
via search-engine indexing rather than the full text. The paper is
needed as the sharpest, most concretely cased version of the
critique-wave argument, and its companion book, *Cheating* (2007), which
would extend the case with the "hard rules/soft rules" and "gaming
capital" framework, remains an unread reading debt for this note.

## Julian Dibbell, 1993, "A Rape in Cyberspace," *The Village Voice*, December 21, 1993 (reprinted 1998 in *My Tiny Life*)

URL: http://gunkelweb.com/coms465/texts/rape_cyberspace.pdf

**Status:** read in full

The foundational essay on the Mr. Bungle incident on LambdaMOO, in which
a player used a scripted "voodoo doll" object to narrate other players'
characters into sexual violence. The essay follows the community's
real-time invention of justice — an inconclusive thirty-person open
meeting, a wizard's unilateral deletion of the offender's account, and
the archwizard's subsequent construction of a permanent petition-and-
ballot democracy — and argues the harm was real despite being made of
nothing but "digital code and snippets of creative writing," evidenced
by a victim's later account of writing her public response through
genuine tears. It matters to this note as the founding case of a
breach a persistent world had no prior machinery to handle, and as a
demonstration that no punishment in a world with no outside is more than
symbolic: the offender simply re-registered under a new name and
reoffended.

## Julian Dibbell, 2007, "The Life of the Chinese Gold Farmer," *New York Times Magazine*, June 17, 2007

URL: https://web.archive.org/web/2015/http://www.juliandibbell.com/texts/goldfarmers.html

**Status:** read in full

Long-form reportage from inside a Nanjing gold-farming operation:
twelve-hour, seven-day shifts, a wage chain running from roughly $1.25
per 100 gold at the worker's end to as much as $20 at the Western
retail end, and the emotional cost of converting play into repetitive,
surveilled labor. Its most useful passage documents a forty-person
in-house raiding guild assembled to farm end-game loot industrially, and
the essay closes on worker Min, who spends a year training himself to
feel nothing about the game that is his job, only to be caught by an
old, involuntary thrill mid-flight from a monster: "It's instinctual —
you can't help it... You want to play." The note needs it as the
ground-level face of the economic mechanism Heeks describes abstractly.

## Martin Gibbs, Marcus Carter, Michael Arnold, and Bjorn Nansen, 2013, "Serenity Now Bombs a World of Warcraft Funeral: Negotiating the Morality, Reality and Taste of Online Gaming Practices," *Selected Papers of Internet Research* 3 (AoIR 14.0)

URL: https://spir.aoir.org/ojs/index.php/spir/article/download/8845/7050/57859

**Status:** read in full

Analyzes the 2006 incident in which the guild Serenity Now attacked
mourners gathered in-game for a deceased player's real memorial, framing
the seven-years-unresolved controversy as Gieryn-style "boundary-work"
running along three entwined axes: ethical (breach of decency versus
"playing the game how it should be played"), ontological (whether an
avatar's grief is real), and aesthetic (funny versus reprehensible). The
paper's central finding — that the dispute "still remain[s] largely
unresolved and are perhaps irreconcilable" — is the note's clearest
concrete instance of a breach event that a persistent world simply
cannot close, since nothing forces resolution the way a match's end
forces one.

## Richard Heeks, 2010, "Understanding 'Gold Farming' and Real-Money Trading as the Intersection of Real and Virtual Economies," *Journal of Virtual Worlds Research* 2(4)

URL: https://jvwr-ojs-utexas.tdl.org/jvwr/article/view/868/633

**Status:** read in full

Traces gold farming as a replication of the full arc of real-world
capitalist development — subsistence, barter, monetization, wage labor,
offshoring, automation — inside virtual economies, estimating roughly
400,000 people employed in gold farming worldwide circa 2008–2009 and
documenting an average 85% devaluation of MMOG currencies against the
dollar between 2005 and 2009. Its conclusion is the note's clearest
statement of the economic thesis: "there is no 'real economics' to
counterpose against a 'virtual economics;' there is just 'economics.'"
The paper is load-bearing for the note's claim that the play/work
distinction does not survive contact with a persistent world's actual
labor market.

## Kim-Phuong Huynh, Sun-Wei Lim, and Marko M. Skoric, 2013, "Stepping out of the Magic Circle: Regulation of Play/Life Boundary in MMO-Mediated Romantic Relationship," *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication* 18(3), 251–264

URL: https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/18/3/251/4067486

**Status:** read in full

An interview study showing MMO players individually and variably
regulate how porous the game/life boundary is around online romance,
using Castronova's "porous membrane" and a three-frame model of primary,
game, and character framework as scaffolding. Its typology — Splitters,
who hold a rigid boundary; Migrators, who treat an online relationship as
a stepping stone offline; Blenders, who reject the boundary outright —
is the note's clearest empirical evidence that porosity is tuned
player by player rather than fixed by the game itself, which is exactly
the object the note's motivating questions ask about under the
heading "player-level boundary-tuning."

## Jesper Juul, 2008, "The Magic Circle and the Puzzle Piece," *Philosophy of Computer Games 2008* (Potsdam), 56–67

URL: https://jesperjuul.net/text/magiccirclepuzzlepiece.pdf

**Status:** read in full

Defends the magic circle against what Juul reads as an over-corrected
critical consensus, arguing that critics including Taylor, Copier,
Malaby, and Pargman and Jakobsson attack a "strong-boundary" position
that neither Huizinga nor Salen and Zimmerman actually held. His own
resolution — "the magic circle is best understood as *the boundary that
players negotiate*" — is paired with a three-frame model of any game
action (goal-orientation, experience, social context) and the puzzle-
piece metaphor, which replaces the question of whether a game is
separate with the question of how it fits a given context. The paper is
the note's key defense-side text, positioned between the critique
wave and Stenros's later, fuller reconciliation.

## Raph Koster, 1998 (maintained), "The Laws of Online World Design," and 2000, "Declaring the Rights of Avatars"

URL: https://www.raphkoster.com/games/laws-of-online-world-design/the-laws-of-online-world-design/

**Status:** read in full

Practitioner essays from a lead designer of Ultima Online. "The Laws"
states flatly that persistent worlds are categorically not games — "It's
a SERVICE. Not a game. It's a WORLD. Not a game. It's a COMMUNITY. Not a
game" — and pairs this with the operational cost of persistence: "Once
you open your online world, expect to keep your team on it indefinitely.
Some of these games have never closed." "Declaring the Rights of
Avatars" is a nineteen-article Bill-of-Rights thought experiment modeled
on 1789, arguing that because the player/administrator relationship
never terminates the way a boxed game's does, administrators accrue
something like sovereign obligations to their communities. This is the
note's strongest and plainest statement that persistence removes the
exit, and the source of the "world, not game" claim this note is
careful not to misattribute to Bartle.

## Julian Kücklich, 2005, "Precarious Playbour: Modders and the Digital Games Industry," *Fibreculture Journal* 5, FCJ-025

URL: https://five.fibreculturejournal.org/fcj-025-precarious-playbour-modders-and-the-digital-games-industry/

**Status:** read in full

Coins "playbour" for modding as free labor generating real value for
publishers — branding, extended shelf-life, customer loyalty, an R&D and
recruitment pool — while remaining classified as leisure. Its load-
bearing claim is that this classification is not neutral: "the
perception of modding as play is the basis of the exploitative
relationship between modders and the games industry," and the industry
has direct material interest in preserving the perception that
everything in a game is "just play." The note needs it as the
vocabulary that ties playbour, gold farming, and Yee's labor-of-fun
testimony into a single classification problem rather than three
separate phenomena.

## Eric T. Lofgren and Nina H. Fefferman, 2007, "The Untapped Potential of Virtual Game Worlds to Shed Light on Real World Epidemics," *The Lancet Infectious Diseases* 7(9), 625–629

URL: https://www.thelancet.com/journals/laninf/article/PIIS1473-3099(07)70212-8/fulltext

**Status:** reported at second hand

Argues the 2005 World of Warcraft "Corrupted Blood" incident — a boss
debuff that leaked out of its intended raid-instance containment via
hunter pets and spread player-to-player across the persistent world —
could serve virtual worlds as a bridge between uncontrolled real-world
epidemiological observation and mathematical models lacking real human
behavior, citing deliberate curiosity-driven exposure-seeking as a
behavior existing models did not capture. The article is paywalled and
was not fetched directly for this note; everything reported here
comes from search-indexed abstracts and press coverage and should be
treated as lower-confidence. It is needed as the note's primary
example of a game bug becoming legible to an entirely unrelated
discipline because the world in which it occurred did not reset.

## Thomas M. Malaby, 2007, "Beyond Play: A New Approach to Games," *Games and Culture* 2(2), 95–113

URL: https://voidnetwork.gr/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/Beyond-Play-A-New-Approach-to-Gamesby-T.-M.-Malaby.pdf

**Status:** read in full

Rejects "play" as a meta-category for games, arguing its three inherited
features — separable, safe, pleasurable — are culturally specific
"accomplishments" rather than intrinsic properties, and redefines a game
as "a semibounded and socially legitimate domain of contrived
contingency that generates interpretable outcomes." Diagnoses games
scholarship's twin failure modes as formalism and exceptionalism, and
breaks contingency into four generative sources — stochastic, social,
performative, semiotic — using his own ethnography of Cretan dice
gambling as the empirical case for games as processual, "always in the
process of becoming." The note needs it as the theoretical spine of
the critique wave and as the source of the four-contingency-source
framework the note's obsmetrological bridge holds against the
corpus's four regimes, with only the stochastic source aligning cleanly.

## Celia Pearce, 2009 (companion essay), "Communities of Play: The Social Construction of Identity in Persistent Online Game Worlds," *Electronic Book Review*

URL: https://electronicbookreview.com/publications/communities-of-play-the-social-construction-of-identity-in-persistent-online-game-worlds/

**Status:** read in full

An eighteen-month ethnography of "The Gathering," a roughly 350-member
group from *Uru: Ages Beyond Myst* that survived the February 2004
shutdown of Uru's servers by migrating, as an intact social unit, across
four subsequent virtual worlds. Members reported weeping and
post-traumatic symptoms at the shutdown, yet Pearce argues the shared
trauma "served as a catalyst for fortifying the group identity" rather
than dissolving it, concluding that the magic circle is "more porous
than previously thought." The note needs it as a concrete case of an
arena being deleted outright while a community's identity and practice
persist and re-embed elsewhere — ending decoupled from closure.

## Jaakko Stenros, 2012, "In Defence of a Magic Circle: The Social and Mental Boundaries of Play," *Proceedings of DiGRA Nordic 2012*

URL: https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/605/605/602

**Status:** read in full

An earlier, shorter version of Stenros's reconciliation project, tracing
the magic-circle concept from Huizinga through Salen and Zimmerman to
its critics — T. L. Taylor, Malaby, Copier, Lammes, Juul, Consalvo — and
resolving the debate into a psychological bubble, a negotiated social
"magic circle," and a persistent cultural arena. Reports Castronova's own
preferred term for the boundary — "membrane would be a better term for
the barrier that separates synthetic... worlds from the Earth" —
independently of the later ToDiGRA paper's Goffman-derived usage. Useful
alongside the 2014 version for genealogical detail on individual critics
that the fuller paper compresses.

## Jaakko Stenros, 2014, "In Defence of a Magic Circle: The Social, Mental and Cultural Boundaries of Play," *ToDiGRA* 1(2), 147–185

URL: https://todigra.org/index.php/todigra/article/download/1701/1701

**Status:** read in full

The fullest version of Stenros's reconciliation, separating what earlier
writers had conflated into three distinct boundaries: the psychological
bubble (Apter's "protective frame," present even in solitary play), the
magic circle of play proper (a social contract created through
negotiation, which other social systems such as law or economics can
come to recognize), and the arena (a culturally recognized site that
persists as residue of play even when empty). Its central move for this
note is importing Goffman's interaction membrane — "the border around
play is permeable... it is also possible for play to be collapsed by
external events" — which replaces a binary open/shut boundary with a
graded, transforming one. The note's own proposed refinements to the
corpus's admission codec are built directly on this triple and this
membrane concept, both graded rhyme rather than established.

## Nick Yee, 2006, "The Labor of Fun: How Video Games Blur the Boundaries of Work and Play," *Games and Culture* 1(1), 68–71

URL: https://nickyee.com/daedalus/archives/pdf/Yee_labor_of_fun.pdf

**Status:** read in full

Uses survey data (players averaging 20–22 hours a week) and player
testimony to argue MMORPGs function as literal work platforms,
behaviorally engineered to "train us to become better game workers."
Player quotes describe play sliding into "obligation, tedium, and more
like a second job than entertainment," and Yee closes by noting that for
employees of gold-selling firms such as IGE, "playing and working have
become the same thing." The note needs it as the player-side
testimony that closes the loop on Kücklich's playbour argument and
Heeks's economic mechanism: the blur is not only structural but
subjectively experienced by the people inside it.

## Reading debts

Several primary texts this note's account leans on have not been read
directly and remain open debts. Johan Huizinga's *Homo Ludens* has not
been consulted anywhere in this note's source material; every claim
attributed to him arrives through Stenros's secondary citation, a thin
foundation given how load-bearing the magic-circle mapping is for the
corpus. Mia Consalvo's book-length *Cheating* (2007) has not been
consulted, and her 2009 article itself is known only through a retrieved
verbatim fragment rather than the full text. Edward Castronova's
*Synthetic Worlds* is known only through one cross-confirmed sentence
from its "Almost-Magic Circle" chapter, never the chapter itself. Eric
Lofgren and Nina Fefferman's Lancet piece and Marcus Carter's
*Treacherous Play* are both reported here at second hand only; Carter is
the note's sole source on EVE's absorb-the-breach strategy and would
most repay a direct read, since the monograph is nominally open access.
T. L. Taylor's *Play Between Worlds*, invoked repeatedly across this
note's narrative as a major critique voice, has not been read
directly in any pass; automated fetch attempts against the publisher's
open-access pages, a mirror, and an archive.org lending copy all failed,
so everything attributed to her here comes through Stenros's secondary
citation.

On the economic side, Lisa Nakamura's "Don't Hate the Player, Hate the
Game: The Racialization of Labor in World of Warcraft Gold Farming"
(2009) and the racialization-of-labor flank of gold-farming scholarship
more broadly have not been touched by this note, leaving its economic
account without engagement on how gold-farming labor is racialized in
industry practice and player discourse. Julian Dibbell's book-length
sequel, *Play Money* (2006), a natural extension of the reportage already
read here, remains unread. Stenros's own discussion of brink play and
pervasive games (2014, pp. 15–26) was not fully captured in either
reading pass and would likely sharpen the arena concept for cases where
play deliberately courts boundary collapse rather than avoiding it.

Two further debts bear on the note's obsmetrological bridge
specifically rather than on its own internal argument. Bernard Suits's
*The Grasshopper*, source of the lusory attitude the note's consent
problem is framed against, has been used only at second hand through a
secondary quotation, never read in its own formulation. Brian
Sutton-Smith's *The Ambiguity of Play*, which the corpus's H¹ reading is
built against, has likewise never been read directly anywhere in this
note's material; the "irreducible disagreement" mapping the note
holds against the Serenity Now case rests entirely on secondary
paraphrase, and should not be treated as more settled than that until
Sutton-Smith's own seven-rhetorics framework has been read.

No dedicated academic scholarship on EVE's Guiding Hand Social Club heist
was located during either sweep for this note; it remains an
unaddressed gap in the breach-case-study record alongside the Carter
monograph above.
