## Scope

Study the live persistent world as a world under continuous instrumented
observation by its operator — what is measured, how the measurements steer
the arena, what the steering optimizes for, and what the field's own
conscience (the dark-patterns critique and its counter-critique) says about
the loop. This is obsmetrologia's namesake note in the games portfolio:
the mathematics of observation meeting the industry that observes for a
living.

## Motivating questions

1. What does a world's operator actually observe, and with what apparatus?
   (Telemetry pipelines; employed economists; survey instruments;
   unsupervised behavioral clustering.)
2. What does the observation steer, and toward what? (Faucet/drain patches;
   NPC price policy; monetization tuning; content cadence.)
3. Does the loop have a fixed point — and is reaching one desirable? (The
   note's standing conjecture, inherited from the obsmetrological note of
   2026-08-22: *boredom is premature closure* — UO's hoarding death was the
   economy settling, and the operator's interventions are anti-closure
   operations.)
4. When is the observational apparatus itself the distortion — which
   player-models are artifacts of the clustering method rather than sections
   of the real behavior space?
5. What are the ethics of steering measured players — and does "dark" name a
   property of a mechanism, or a claim adjudicated between communities and
   frameworks?

## Objects of study

Virtual-economy management (faucets, drains, sinks; closed vs. open loops);
economic measurement of worlds (Castronova's Norrath accounts); the employed
world-economist (EVE's QEN apparatus); behavioral telemetry and player
modeling; live-service steering (GTA Online's operated economy); the
dark-patterns literature and its critique.

## State of knowledge (2026-08-22 sweep)

- **Simpson's UO postmortem** (read in full — the founding document): the
  closed-loop economy failed through *hoarding*, not inflation — "the
  resources always flowed into inventory and just sat there... the world
  became boring and the players began to complain loudly"; the faucet/drain
  vocabulary; sink-design as political ("feel too much like real world
  'work'"); the simulated NPC economy going broke ("central planning doesn't
  work"); and a closing proposal for exactly a standing telemetry apparatus.
- **Castronova's Norrath accounts** (read in full): a currency above the yen;
  hourly wage $3.42; GNP per capita "roughly equal to Russia"; chronic
  deflation from non-decaying items; inequality worse than any
  post-industrial society — a world measured as an economy, 2001.
- **Lehdonvirta and Castronova** (reported — primary text not obtained):
  "Both the US Dollar and World of Warcraft coins are fiat money"; virtual
  economies are designed for engagement and revenue, not efficiency; the
  engineered scarcities are the product.
- **EVE's economist** (press release and interview read): Guðmundsson's
  eleven-person unit; quarterly economic reports as infrastructure; the same
  apparatus doubling as anomaly detection (bots at 2% of players consuming
  30% of server CPU); the reporting cadence itself later throttled — the
  cost of standing observation is a real operator variable.
- **Player modeling** (Drachen et al. 2014 read; the 2009 founding paper
  reported through it): telemetry defined ("any action the player takes...
  can potentially be recorded and stored"); F2P business models *requiring*
  "constant monitoring and evaluation of the player population behavior";
  and the methodological caveat — k-means and Archetypal Analysis yield
  interpretable player types while PCA/NMF bases can correspond to
  game-impossible behaviors.
- **GTA Online** (trade press read; economics thesis read in part):
  Rockstar's own account of organizational unreadiness ("GTA Online was
  slowly failing... right up until the middle of 2014"); the monopoly
  economy (publisher as sole retailer, currency sold directly); the Umeå
  thesis's whale finding (largest Shark Card tier associated with *worse*
  measured in-game productivity). The circulating "4% of players fund ~74%
  of revenue" statistic is **unverified** and is not to be cited from
  section documents.
- **Dark patterns** (Karlsen and the Deterding critique read; Zagal et al.
  reconstructed from directly-quoting secondary sources): the founding
  definition; the three families (time, money, social network); Karlsen's
  loyalty complication; and Deterding, Stenros and Montola's three-count
  demolition — ontological incoherence, unstated ethics, historical bias
  toward canonizing AAA design as neutral.

## Obsmetrological bridge

The note's namesake claim is definitional, not graded: a live persistent
world is a continuously instrumented observation system with an actuation
loop, run by its operator. Within it: the **conjecture** *boredom is
premature closure* (the operator as a standing settlement organ run in
reverse — observing continuously in order to keep the settling from
completing, because a fully settled world is a finished game, and a finished
game is a number, and nobody lives in a number); the clustering caveat as a
**rhyme** with the section's locality discipline (an ill-chosen local method
yields sections of nothing); and the tempting reading of the Deterding
critique as an argument for four-valued treatment of "dark" — explicitly
**not in the source**, filed as the section's own apparatus applied
externally.

## Open problems

- Test the boredom-as-closure conjecture against a real economy's telemetry:
  does a measurable settling indicator (trade-velocity collapse, wealth
  concentration, activity-entropy decline) precede player-reported boredom
  and churn?
- The observation-cost frontier: EVE throttled its own reporting cadence —
  is there a describable optimum for how much standing observation an
  operator sustains, and what does it trade against?
- Whether the whale finding (highest spend, worst measured productivity)
  replicates outside one survey — and what it says about what is actually
  being sold (time, or standing?).
- Instrumenting the membrane (joint with Boundary and Membrane): can
  cross-boundary value flow (RMT volume, playbour output) be measured with
  the same discipline as in-world flow?

## Annotated sources

Annotated sources on live-world telemetry, player modeling, and managed virtual economies, with access status and reading debts for this note.


### Sources

## Castronova, Edward. "Virtual Worlds: A First-Hand Account of Market and Society on the Cyberian Frontier." CESifo Working Paper No. 618, December 2001.

URL: https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo_wp618.pdf

**Status:** read in full (38 of approximately 41 pages).

Castronova treats EverQuest's Norrath as a real economy and computes its national accounts: an exchange rate of the platinum piece against the dollar (higher than the yen or lira, and falling 25% in one quarter), a GNP per capita of $2,266 — "the 77th richest country in the world, roughly equal to Russia" — an hourly wage of $3.42, chronic 29%-per-year price deflation from non-decaying items, and wealth inequality worse than any real post-industrial society. The data are drawn from a 3,619-respondent survey and hundreds of scraped currency and avatar auctions. He defines a virtual world by interactivity, physicality, and persistence — "the note continues to run whether anyone is using it or not" — and defends the whole exercise on explicitly economic-methodological grounds: "economists believe that it is the practical actions of people, and not abstract arguments, that determine the social value of things." The note needs this paper as the first rigorous demonstration that a game world can be measured with the full apparatus of a national economy, and as the earliest clear articulation of persistence as the property that makes standing observation necessary rather than optional.

## CCP Games / EVE Online. "EVE Online Appoints In-World Economist." Official press release, 2007.

URL: https://community.eveonline.com/news/news-channels/press-releases/eve-online-appoints-in-world-economist-1/

**Status:** read in full.

CCP's own announcement of hiring Dr. Eyjólfur Guðmundsson, the first such economist hire by an MMO operator. CEO Hilmar Pétursson frames the hire in explicitly observe-and-steer terms: "Just as entrepreneurs and executives rely on real-world economic indicators, EVE Online players need timely information and analysis of the in-game economy." Guðmundsson states the transparency rationale directly: "By ensuring everyone has access to the same data, we will enhance the player experience and facilitate economic stability in EVE." The release specifies the role's scope: quarterly economic reports on inflation, growth, and price trends; data support for player strategic decisions; influence on future development; and coordination with academic researchers. The note needs this release as the founding institutional document of standing observation as a formal job description rather than an ad hoc practice.

## Deterding, Christoph Sebastian, Jaakko Stenros, and Markus Montola. "Against 'Dark Game Design Patterns.'" DiGRA'20 Abstract Proceedings (accepted version via White Rose Research Online).

URL: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/156460/1/DiGRA_2020_paper_189.pdf

**Status:** read in full (4 pages).

This paper argues that "dark game design patterns," as defined by Zagal, Björk and Lewis (2013), is an unstable concept on three counts: it is ontologically incoherent, making players' and designers' subjective states a necessary condition for "darkness" while also naming specific material features dark in themselves; it never specifies the ethical framework doing its evaluative work, hinting at a deontological or contractualist reading of "the interaction with a system as a contract" without saying so and thereby hiding consequentialist, virtue-based, or care-based alternatives; and its chosen examples reveal, in the authors' words, "a bias against a particular historical formation of digital games, namely then-ascendant freemium casual and social network games," treating 1990s–2000s AAA design as an unmarked neutral baseline. The authors do credit the original paper's "sensitizing questions" with surfacing real values (transparency) and real player experiences (regret) worth tracking empirically. The note needs this paper as the sharpest available critique of the field's own diagnostic vocabulary, and it is the source of this note's own (explicitly external) reading of the critique as an argument for four-valued rather than two-valued treatment of design-pattern ethics — a reading the paper itself does not make.

## Drachen, Anders, Christian Thurau, Rafet Sifa, and Christian Bauckhage. "A Comparison of Methods for Player Clustering via Behavioral Telemetry." arXiv:1407.3950 (also published in IEEE Transactions on Computational Intelligence and AI in Games).

URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1407.3950

**Status:** read in full (8 pages).

The paper compares four unsupervised clustering methods — k-means, NMF, PCA, and Archetypal Analysis — applied to six years of World of Warcraft playtime and leveling telemetry from 70,014 players, and finds that k-means and Archetypal Analysis produce human-interpretable player archetypes ("casual player," "hardcore player") while PCA and NMF produce statistically valid but sometimes uninterpretable factorizations, occasionally corresponding to behaviors the game does not allow, such as a player losing levels. It also reports the founding 2009 result it extends: Drachen, Canossa and Yannakakis's Tomb Raider: Underworld study, which "classified the behavior of 1365 players... using Self-Organizing Networks and k-means clustering, locating four classes of user behavior that encompassed over 90 percent of the users in the dataset," translated directly into design vocabulary for the game's developers. It defines telemetry plainly: "Any action the player (user) takes while playing can potentially be recorded and stored." The note needs this paper both for its methodological caution — that the choice of observational method determines whether a player model is legible as a claim about real behavior — and as the best available secondhand account of the 2009 founding study, which this note has not read directly.

## Francis, Bryant. "How GTA Online painfully pulled Rockstar into the 'live games' biz." Game Developer (formerly Gamasutra).

URL: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-i-gta-online-i-painfully-pulled-rockstar-into-the-live-games-biz

**Status:** read in full.

Trade journalism, built from direct interview quotes with Rockstar design director Imran Sarwar, documenting the studio's own admitted unreadiness to run GTA Online as a continuously operated live service: "As a company we did not realize how challenging it was going to be to have a team that essentially ran a content service, not make normal games." Sarwar states plainly that "GTA Online was slowly failing — and quickly losing players — right up until the middle of 2014," attributing the turnaround to Rockstar building organizational capacity for sustained content updates and live operations it had not planned for at launch. The note needs this piece as direct evidence that the observe-and-steer capacity central to this inquiry is not automatic even for a well-resourced studio — it has to be institutionally built, and the building itself is observable history.

## Ivanik, Ivan. "Monetary Value of a Player in GTA Online: Role of Inflation." MSc Degree Project in Economics, Umeå University, Spring 2023.

URL: https://umu.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1807228/FULLTEXT01.pdf

**Status:** read in substantial part (pages 1–10: title, abstract, introduction, and start of literature review).

Ivanik applies Lehdonvirta and Castronova's virtual-economy framework directly to GTA Online, surveying 171 respondents from r/gtaonline and computing a per-player monetary value as GTA$-earned-per-hour multiplied by an indirect black-market exchange rate to the euro, regressed against playstyle and Shark Card purchase behavior. The headline results: a "grinder" playstyle raised measured monetary value by €8.1 per hour; purchasing a Megalodon Shark Card correlated with +€38.7 in monetary value; purchasing the largest, Whale Shark Card correlated with −€59.1 — the biggest real-money purchases associated with the worst measured in-game productivity. The thesis frames GTA Online explicitly as a monopoly virtual economy, "the publisher is the only retailer of virtual goods and gets to dictate prices," and reproduces several load-bearing quotations from Lehdonvirta and Castronova's textbook directly. The note needs this thesis as its primary quantitative case study of the "whale" phenomenon and as a contemporary (2023) instance of the operator continuing to tune a live economy years after launch.

## Karlsen, Faltin. "Dark game design patterns in online games: exploitation, loyalty and engagement." Proceedings of the 1st Joint DiGRA/FDG Conference, 2016.

URL: https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/889/889/886

**Status:** read in full (2-page extended abstract).

Karlsen applies Zagal, Björk and Lewis's dark-patterns taxonomy empirically to World of Warcraft and FarmVille 2, confirming the taxonomy's three categories — designs that increase time spent, money spent, and designs that exploit a player's social network — and using self-determination theory and marketing loyalty-note research to complicate the framework. He finds WoW offers "play-by-appointment goals ranging from an hourly basis (garrison activities) to a yearly basis (seasonal events like Feast of Winter Veil)," while FarmVille 2 concentrates on hourly and daily activities, a difference he ties to "the different economical logics of the games, e.g. subscription versus micro transactions." He argues players may experience patterns designed to retain them as loyalty-building satisfaction rather than exploitation. The note needs this paper both as an empirical confirmation of the dark-patterns taxonomy's content, and as a source that complicates the taxonomy from within, before Deterding et al. complicate it from without.

## Lehdonvirta, Vili, and Edward Castronova. Virtual Economies: Design and Analysis. MIT Press, 2014.

URL: https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/lsereviewofbooks/2014/09/19/book-review-virtual-economies-design-and-analysis-by-vili-lehdonvirta-and-edward-castronova/

**Status:** reported at second hand (not read in primary form; reconstructed from a review by Clare Chambers-Jones in LSE Review of Books, September 2014, and from block quotations reproduced in Ivanik 2023).

The book is the field's textbook synthesis, applying core monetary-policy apparatus to game economies while insisting they are designed differently from real economies. Its most quoted claim, recovered directly: "Both the US Dollar and World of Warcraft coins are fiat money" (p.191). Via the Ivanik thesis, further direct quotations were recovered: "Virtual economies aim to create products that attract users, satisfy them and make the publisher of the virtual economy money... They do not care about efficiency," and, on subsidies and inflation, "this additional source of money flowing into the economy needs to be offset with a corresponding sink; otherwise it can cause inflation. Inflation is a subtle tax on everyone who owns money." The note needs this book as the field's synthesizing text, but its content here rests entirely on secondhand quotation whose selection by the intermediary sources cannot be independently verified — it is a genuine reading debt, not a settled citation.

## Simpson, Zachary Booth. "The In-game Economics of Ultima Online." Origin Research Fellow report, 7 April 1999 (presented at the Computer Game Developer's Conference, San Jose, March 2000).

URL: http://www.mine-control.com/zack/uoecon/uoecon.html

**Status:** read in full (24 pages).

Simpson's first-person postmortem, written from inside Origin as the team was still fixing its own mistakes, is the founding document of this entire literature. UO's original closed-loop economy failed not through inflation but through hoarding: "the resources always flowed into inventory and just sat there... the result: the world became boring and the players began to complain loudly." The fix introduced the field's standard vocabulary — faucet, drain, sink — and Simpson is explicit that the underlying problem was political rather than technical, since effective drains "feel too much like real world 'work' and are therefore politically unacceptable." He documents a second failure, a simulated NPC supply-and-demand economy that collapsed because players had a "non-demand incentive to produce," concluding "central planning doesn't work." His closing proposal — instrumented tracking of trades, vendor stock, inflow, outflow, churn, and housing occupancy as ongoing infrastructure — is the first appearance in this literature of the operator as a standing observer. The note needs this report as its founding text and the source of its core vocabulary and its central conjecture.

## Ten Ton Hammer. "EVE Online Economics: A Q&A with Dr. Gudmundsson" (interview with Dr. Eyjólfur Guðmundsson).

URL: https://www.tentonhammer.com/articles/eve-online-economics-a-q-a-with-dr-gudmundsson

**Status:** read in full.

A direct interview with CCP Games' in-world economist detailing the practical operation of his eleven-person unit — divided across in-game economics, subscriber trends, Council of Stellar Management liaison, and internal-affairs monitoring of CCP's own staff — across roughly 300,000 players. Guðmundsson describes recommending the removal of NPC-seeded goods to push the economy toward being more player-driven ("anything that makes EVE more player driven is something that I really like"), frames the PLEX time-for-currency system as embodying exchange "without government-like banking oversight," and reports that the same economic-monitoring apparatus used for market analysis also discovered that 2% of players — bots — were generating 30% of server CPU load. The note needs this interview as the clearest documented case of a single observational apparatus serving both an economic and a security function inside one operator's toolkit.

## Zagal, José P., Staffan Björk, and Chris Lewis. "Dark Patterns in the Design of Games." Proceedings of the 8th International Conference on the Foundations of Digital Games (FDG 2013), pp.39–46.

URL: http://www.fdg2013.org/program/papers/paper06_zagal_etal.pdf

**Status:** key passages verbatim; not read in full (primary PDF not directly obtainable — four independent fetch attempts across different mirrors each failed with 404, 403, or certificate errors; content reconstructed via two secondary sources that quote it directly: Karlsen 2016 and Deterding, Stenros and Montola 2020).

This is the founding paper of the dark-patterns critique, defining dark game design patterns as design "used intentionally by a game creator to cause negative experiences for players which are against their best interests and likely to happen without their consent," and taxonomizing them into three families — patterns that increase time spent, money spent, and patterns that exploit a player's social network. The paper reportedly frames player-system interaction "as a contract," implying an unstated deontological or contractualist ethical grounding, per Deterding et al.'s critique. The note needs this paper as the origin point of the field's ethical self-scrutiny, but its individual pattern-by-pattern catalogue — the full write-ups of grinding, pay-to-skip, social pyramid schemes, and any patterns not named in the secondary sources — has never actually been read and remains this note's most significant unresolved reading debt.

## Reading debts

Zagal, Björk and Lewis's 2013 paper is listed above with its own status line but bears repeating as a debt: only its verbatim definition and top-level taxonomy have been recovered, never its full pattern-by-pattern catalogue, despite four independent attempts to fetch the primary PDF.

Drachen, Canossa and Yannakakis, "Player Modeling using Self-Organization in Tomb Raider: Underworld" (IEEE CIG 2009) — the founding player-modeling paper this note's own telemetry thread traces back to — has never been read directly; everything known about it comes through its 2014 successor paper's summary of the same result.

Seif El-Nasr, Drachen, and Canossa, eds., Game Analytics: Maximizing the Value of Player Data (Springer, 2013) — confirmed to exist, with over thirty chapters and fifty contributors, and confirmed as a citation anchor across this literature, but no chapter content has been read; what the book itself argues about telemetry categories or "metrics-driven design" is unverified.

Lehdonvirta and Castronova, Virtual Economies: Design and Analysis (MIT Press, 2014) — the field's textbook synthesis, known to this note only through a review and secondhand block quotations reproduced in Ivanik's 2023 thesis; the primary text has not been obtained.

An actual EVE Online Quarterly Economic Newsletter — a URL for the newsletter series was located but no individual PDF was fetched or read in this note; what a QEN's internal structure, charts, or a specific quarter's findings actually look like remains unknown firsthand.

The loot-box empirical literature, particularly David Zendle and Paul Cairns's studies on loot-box spending and problem gambling — closely adjacent to the dark-patterns material this note does cover, appearing in the same venues (PLOS ONE, DiGRA/FDG), but not consulted in this sweep at all.

Primary financial disclosures from Take-Two Interactive — needed specifically to replace the unverified "4% of players fund roughly 74% of revenue" Shark Card statistic, which originates in an aggregated web-search summary never traced to a fetchable source and must not be cited as fact anywhere in this note's writing until it is either confirmed against a primary disclosure or dropped.

Beyond this note's own sources, the critic's report additionally names two adjacent debts worth carrying: Julian Dibbell's Play Money (2006), a book-length account of real-money trading and gold-farming inside Ultima Online that would extend this note's UO coverage from the design side to the labor side; and the broader gold-farming and real-money-trade labor scholarship, including Lisa Nakamura's work on the racialization of World of Warcraft gold-farming labor — a whole political-economy dimension of managed virtual economies that this note's sources do not touch.
