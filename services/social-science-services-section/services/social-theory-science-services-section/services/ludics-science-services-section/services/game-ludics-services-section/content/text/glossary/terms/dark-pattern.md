Dark pattern names a design element a game's creator uses intentionally to produce outcomes against players' own best interests, typically without their meaningful consent. The concept's founding text is Zagal, Björk and Lewis's "Dark Patterns in the Design of Games" (FDG 2013); this entry's grounding in that paper is secondhand, since its primary text could not be obtained directly despite repeated attempts, and what is reported here is reconstructed from two secondary sources that quote it verbatim. Its recovered definition, via Deterding, Stenros and Montola (2020): dark patterns are design "used intentionally by a game creator to cause negative experiences for players which are against their best interests and likely to happen without their consent." Faltin Karlsen's 2016 follow-up confirms a three-part taxonomy: designs that increase time spent, designs that increase money spent, and designs that exploit the player's social network.

Karlsen's own empirical study contrasts World of Warcraft's "play-by-appointment" goals — ranging from hourly garrison activities to yearly seasonal events — against FarmVille 2's tighter hourly and daily cadence, tying the difference explicitly to "the different economical logics of the games, e.g. subscription versus micro transactions." He also complicates the concept's implied harm directly: players "may find satisfaction (intrinsic rewards) through patient earning rather than skills... and may in the process embrace design that has as its main purpose to stimulate loyalty" — a pattern engineered to retain players is not necessarily experienced by them as exploitative.

The sharpest treatment found for this entry is a rebuttal rather than an extension: Deterding, Stenros and Montola's "Against 'Dark Game Design Patterns'" (DiGRA 2020), read in full. Their three central objections, stated directly: the concept is ontologically incoherent, since it makes players' and designers' subjective states — intent, harm, absence of consent — a necessary condition for "darkness," while the original paper simultaneously declares specific material design features, such as loot boxes, grinding, appointment play, and pay-to-skip, dark per se; the ethical framework is never made explicit, gesturing at "the interaction with a system as a contract" without ever naming the deontological or contractualist commitments that phrase implies, and eliding consequentialist, virtue-ethical, or care-ethical alternatives that might evaluate the same patterns differently; and, most damningly, the specific patterns flagged as dark reveal "a bias against a particular historical formation of digital games, namely then-ascendant freemium casual and social network games" — quietly treating 1990s-2000s AAA console and PC design conventions as a neutral baseline. Deterding, Stenros and Montola do credit the original paper's "sensitizing questions" with surfacing genuine values, like transparency, and genuine player experiences, like regret, worth tracking empirically even where the binary dark-or-not judgment does not itself hold up.

Dark patterns are this literature's name for what happens when an operator's continuous observe-and-steer capacity (see game-telemetry) is turned toward retention or spending rather than player interest, and its own internal critique is best read as an argument that this alignment question cannot be answered from a design pattern's material features alone. Monetization-family dark patterns are named in this literature as the mechanism by which high-spending players are specifically targeted — though the GTA Online findings this section documents under whale complicate any simple story about who those patterns actually work on.
