The labor of fun is Nick Yee's finding and the title of his 2006 paper, which uses survey data and player testimony to argue that MMORPGs are, in a precise sense, work platforms rather than simply entertainment products. Yee reports that players in his sample spent an average of twenty to twenty-two hours a week in online games, and that a substantial number described their own play, unprompted, in the vocabulary of labor rather than leisure: as obligation, as tedium, as "more like work then fun," or as what one respondent called a "digital treadmill." Yee's own gloss on this pattern is direct: "Users spend on average 20 hours a week in online games, and many of them describe their game play as obligation, tedium, and more like a second job than entertainment."

The paper's player quotations are its strongest evidence and are worth preserving in the terms Yee gives them. One respondent, a twenty-year-old man, wrote, "It became a chore to play... I wanted to get away from real life... and social etiquette followed me in!" Another, twenty-two, wrote, "Was more like work then fun. One day got burnt out trying to get exp for level 55 and quit." A third, twenty-one, made the mechanism itself explicit: "The problem is that it goes from enjoyable to just work so gradually that unless you step back for a while and evaluate you do not even realize you're working." Yee's argument is that this drift is not incidental to MMO design but a consequence of it — the games are built on behavioral-conditioning principles that, in his words, "train us to become better game workers," meaning the design that keeps unpaid players engaged is architecturally the same design that would train an employee into a productive routine.

Yee closes the loop between voluntary drudgery and actual employment by pointing to gold-farming firms such as IGE and TopGameSeller, for whose paid staff, he writes, "playing and working have become the same thing" — the same behaviorally engineered activity that an ordinary subscriber experiences as an unpaid second job is, for a gold farm's employees, their literal first one. Read alongside playbour and gold farming, the labor of fun supplies the third leg of this glossary's account of how work and play come apart inside a persistent world's membrane: playbour names unpaid production the industry has an interest in calling leisure, gold farming names paid production disguised as ordinary play, and the labor of fun names unpaid, ordinary players experiencing that same collapse from the inside, with no wage and no industry to blame it on but the game's own design. Yee's paper closes by asking the question this convergence forces: "what does 'fun' really mean?"
