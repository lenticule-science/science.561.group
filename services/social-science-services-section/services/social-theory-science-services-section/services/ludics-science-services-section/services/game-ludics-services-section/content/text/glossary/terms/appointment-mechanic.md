This entry must open with a flag: the phrase "appointment mechanic" does not appear, attributed to a named theorist, anywhere in the section's sources. It is the section's own working label — used in the Web Temporality program charter to name one of a small set of "distinct temporal regimes" the web hosts, alongside the continuous tick, the banked daily allowance, the shared daily instance, and pure idle accrual — applied to a phenomenon that is, in fact, well documented in the primary material even though none of that material calls it by this name.

The clearest concrete instance is Ian Bogost's Cow Clicker (2010), triangulated for this section through Bogost's own site, Wikipedia, and its treatment in both the Spiel et al. and Fizek papers: the game required clicking a cow sprite every six hours, with friends' cows in a shared pasture generating additional clicks, and a premium currency ("Mooney") available to buy back the wait. Bogost identified this scheduled-return structure as one of four "disturbing, dangerous aspects" of social games he built Cow Clicker to distill and expose, naming it "Destroyed Time": social games, in his account, "create compulsive obligation even during the player's absence," disrespecting the player's time while imposing endless upkeep demands on the developer as well. Mia Consalvo's content analysis of seventy Facebook-era social games in "Using your friends: Social mechanics in social games" (2011) documents the same underlying structure at genre scale in its account of "visiting/tending" mechanics — crops, fish tanks, and other personalized spaces that decay or stall unless a player, or a friend visiting on the player's behalf, returns to tend them on the game's schedule rather than the player's own.

Josh Wardle's Wordle is read across the section's sources as this mechanic's deliberate inversion rather than its absence: Wordle still asks for one scheduled daily return, but strips out the extraction the appointment mechanic ordinarily serves. Wardle, in a TechCrunch interview, states this as an explicit design choice made against the genre's standard playbook: "I kind of deliberately did what you're not meant to do if growth is your goal," rejecting push notifications ("Why don't you just forget about Wordle for a little bit?") and monetization alike. The angle document that grounds this glossary names this contrast directly — Wordle as "a direct, self-conscious counter-design to the appointment/compulsion mechanics Bogost satirized: same daily-return structure, opposite intent." The appointment mechanic is thus best read as one point, alongside tick, action point, and shared daily instance, in the family of return-scheduling structures the sources document without yet supplying a comparative theory of; the section's own reading connects it to the corpus's account of the game as a receptive structure the player's absence and return synchronize with, offered here as a proposed reading rather than an established one.
