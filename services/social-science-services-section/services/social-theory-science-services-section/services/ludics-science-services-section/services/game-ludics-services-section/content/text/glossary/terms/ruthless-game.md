A ruthless game, in Marcus Carter's coinage as quoted by Ian G. Brooks's "Is Betrayal in EVE Online Unethical?" (Journal of Virtual Worlds Research 10:3, 2017), is one where "players are lawfully afforded the opportunity to act in ways that have great, negative consequences on other players." This entry's grounding is secondhand at its center: Carter's own full-length treatment of the concept, Treacherous Play (MIT Press, 2022), was not obtainable for the research behind this entry, so everything reported here about his argument is mediated entirely through Brooks's citations of it, and should be treated accordingly.

EVE Online is this literature's paradigm ruthless game because CCP Games states its non-intervention policy in its own words rather than leaving it to inference. Brooks quotes CCP developer "CCP Phantom" (2016) directly: "scamming and unethical behavior...is not only allowed, it is encouraged and rewarded by the game mechanics." Carter's claim, again as relayed by Brooks, is that within a ruthless game's explicit rules and culture, betrayal "bears no immoral, subversive, transgressive, or anti-social qualities" — the harm was, in effect, consented to at admission to the game, so it is not a wrong in need of a remedy.

Brooks does not simply report this position; he argues against it philosophically, drawing on Huizinga's magic circle and, via Ashley John Craft (2007), the observation that roleplayers and non-roleplayers within EVE effectively disagree about where the game's boundary is drawn — meaning uniform consent to "just part of the game" cannot be assumed across the whole player base. Independent of that philosophical argument, the institutional fact Brooks documents is that EVE University maintains a volunteer "Personnel Department" doing out-of-game counterintelligence against infiltrators (see guild) — a private institution players built specifically because EVE's ruthless-game design leaves betrayal formally unpoliced by the operator.

A ruthless game is this section's clearest documented case of an operator resolving griefing's community-relative definitional problem not by adjudicating individual acts after the fact, but by declaring an entire category of harmful acts legal in advance — legislating the breach into the ruleset rather than settling it case by case, the way LambdaMOO's petition-and-ballot system does. EVE's council-of-stellar-management has its advisory mandate drawn explicitly around this non-intervention stance, consistent with the CSM White Paper's own statement that "under no circumstances will the legislator interfere" in these disputes.
