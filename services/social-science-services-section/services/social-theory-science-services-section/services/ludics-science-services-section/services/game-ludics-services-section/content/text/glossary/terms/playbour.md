Playbour is Julian Kücklich's coinage, from "Precarious Playbour: Modders and the Digital Games Industry" (2005), for the hybrid status of modding: work that produces real value for a publisher while being classified, and experienced, as leisure. Kücklich borrows the underlying formula from Tiziana Terranova's earlier account of free digital labor, quoting Terranova's description of such labor as "'[s]imultaneously voluntarily given and unwaged, enjoyed and exploited,'" and argues this is precisely what renders modding "unclassifiable in traditional terms of work and leisure" — it does not fit the categories of wage labor, of freelance contracting, or of pure recreation, because it partakes of features from all three without settling into any one of them.

The precariousness in Kücklich's title is not incidental. He documents the concrete legal mechanism by which the ambiguity is maintained: the Half-Life software development kit granted modders the rights needed to build content while retaining the resulting intellectual property for Valve and shifting legal liability onto the modder, a structure Kücklich reads as depending directly on the perception that modding is play rather than work — "the perception of modding as play is the basis of the exploitative relationship between modders and the games industry." He catalogues four benefits this arrangement delivers to the industry: branding, extended shelf-life for a product, customer loyalty, and a functioning pipeline of unpaid research, development, and recruitment. The classification of modding as leisure is, on his account, not a neutral description but the load-bearing fiction that licenses uncompensated extraction, and the industry accordingly has "every incentive to maintain the *perception* that everything in a game is 'just play.'"

Kücklich's playbour sits at one end of a labor-of-fun spectrum this glossary traces across several entries. Where playbour describes unpaid production preserved under the sign of leisure, gold farming and real-money trading describe the same boundary crossed in the opposite direction, with waged labor producing goods sold back into the game as commodities; Nick Yee's labor of fun describes players who are paid nothing and yet report experiencing their play as job-like drudgery regardless. All three, in this glossary's reading, document the same underlying phenomenon Kücklich names directly: that the boundary the almost-magic circle and interaction membrane describe as porous to value and consequence is, in the specific case of labor, porous to something the industry benefits from calling by only one of its two true names.
