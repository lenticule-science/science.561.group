# A Guide to Persistent World Foundations

## 1. The story of the field

The literature this note draws on did not set out to answer one question. It arrived at one anyway. Three traditions grew up more or less independently — practitioner design writing from the people who actually ran MUDs and MMOs (Richard Bartle, Raph Koster), ethnographic and critical game studies watching what players did inside those worlds (Julian Dibbell, Celia Pearce, T. L. Taylor at second hand here), and definitional scholarship trying to pin down what a "virtual world" even is (Mark Bell, working from Bartle, Koster, and Edward Castronova) — and by the time you read them side by side, they are visibly circling the same claim from different directions: persistence is not a feature bolted onto a game. It is the thing that turns a game into a world, and it does this by removing the exit. No reset, no final position, no dissolution of the play-space, no clean boundary that harm or attachment can be safely confined behind.

The story starts with taxonomy that turns out to be dynamics. Bartle's 1996 "Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades" is remembered as the paper that named Achievers, Explorers, Socialisers, and Killers, but its real claim is that these four populations are locked in feedback loops that only a world with no session boundary could produce: "Increasing the number of achievers will, over time, increase the number of killers in a typically Malthusian fashion." A balanced world, in this telling, is not something a designer builds once — it is something an operator has to keep managing, indefinitely, because the population never goes back to zero. Bartle's 2003 book, *Designing Virtual Worlds*, promotes this from taxonomy to definition: a virtual world needs automated physics, a persistent character, real-time shared interaction, and — treated as a co-equal necessary condition rather than an afterthought — the property that "the environment continues to exist and develop internally... even when there are no people interacting with it." From there Bartle asks the question this whole tradition keeps returning to in different words: whose world is it? His change/persistence grid cross-tabulates how much a codebase lets players build against how much of that building survives a reboot, and the answer ranges from worlds that belong entirely to their designers, "like a movie belongs to its director," to worlds like LambdaMOO that belong to the players, because what the players make actually stays made.

Mark Bell's 2008 synthesis, built explicitly by combining Bartle, Koster, and Castronova, gives the clearest single sentence for why this changes the experience of play rather than merely its infrastructure: a participant "has a sense the systems in the space... exist with or without a participant's presence." That is the moment, stated as plainly as the literature ever states it, that a game — built around a player — becomes a world, which merely includes one. Raph Koster's practitioner essays push the same point to its bluntest form and its practical consequence at once: "It's a SERVICE. Not a game. It's a WORLD. Not a game... Anyone who says, 'it's just a game' is missing the point" — paired immediately with the operational cost of that claim: "Once you open your online world, expect to keep your team on it indefinitely. Some of these games have never closed."

Two case studies then show what removing the exit actually does when it happens to real people. Julian Dibbell's 1993 "A Rape in Cyberspace" is the founding account of a world's internal harms refusing to stay internal. A player used a scripted object to force sexual violence on other players' characters on LambdaMOO; one victim, exu, wrote publicly that "Mr. Bungle was being a vicious, vile fuckhead," and told Dibbell months later that the post had been written through "posttraumatic tears... a real-life fact that should suffice to prove that the words' emotional content was no mere fiction." What makes the essay a persistence story and not just an online-harm story is its second half: LambdaMOO's wizards had four months earlier abdicated social governance entirely, so this permanent, never-resetting population had to build its first constitution live, under crisis, because there was no session boundary after which the problem would simply go away. A chaotic open meeting produced no consensus; one wizard unilaterally deleted the offender's account; the archwizard then built a standing petition-and-ballot democracy specifically because ad hoc intervention could not be sustained in a world with no closing date — and even then, the offender simply re-registered under a new name, which Dibbell reads not as a loophole but as proof that "his punishment... had been no more or less symbolic than his crime." There is no permanent expulsion possible from a world that has no outside.

Celia Pearce's account of the Uru diaspora inverts the question: what happens when the world ends but the community does not? When Uru's servers closed in 2004, roughly 10,000 players were displaced; Pearce followed one 350-member group across four different worlds over eighteen months and found genuine grief — "reported weeping as the clock struck midnight and the avatars on the screen froze in place" — that nonetheless "served as a catalyst for fortifying the group identity" rather than dissolving it. Her claim is that avatar identity is not an individual psychological fact but an "intersubjective accomplishment," and that the magic circle is "more porous than previously thought" — porous enough for a community to walk through the wall of one world's ending and reconstitute itself inside another's rules entirely, a possibility the single-session model of "a game" does not predict.

## 2. The load-bearing concepts

**Persistence as the removal of the exit.** Every other concept in this literature is downstream of one structural fact: a persistent world keeps running, keeps changing, and keeps mattering to the people in it whether or not any particular person is logged in. A single-session game has a boundary you can walk away from cleanly; a persistent world does not offer that boundary, and the literature treats this absence — not graphics, not scale, not genre — as the thing that actually separates a world from a game.

**The definitional core.** Bartle's five conditions (automated physics, a persistent character, real-time interaction, shared access, and persistence itself) and Bell's synthesis ("a synchronous, persistent network of people, represented as avatars, facilitated by networked computers") are not competing definitions so much as the same claim assembled from different angles. What matters for a reader is which clause is load-bearing. Remove persistence and you get a first-person shooter; remove synchrony and you get a play-by-mail game; remove avatars and you get Facebook. Persistence is the one condition that, once present, retroactively changes what all the other conditions mean — a "shared" space is a different thing to inhabit when it does not forget you.

**The ownership grid.** Bartle's cross-tabulation of change (how much players can build) against persistence (how much of it survives a reboot) answers a question this literature treats as close to its deepest: whose world is it? A world where nothing players do outlasts the session belongs to its designer the way a film belongs to its director. A world where everything players build survives — LambdaMOO is the canonical case — belongs, in a meaningful sense, to the players. The grid matters because it turns "ownership" from a legal or rhetorical claim into something measurable directly off the architecture.

**The identity gradient.** Bartle distinguishes avatar (a doll you control), character (a simulacrum you identify with), and persona (an identity you simply are — "if you're killed in a fight, you don't feel that your character has died, you feel that you have died"). Persistence is what drives investment up this gradient: a character you have poured months of uninterrupted history into becomes too costly to abandon even when nothing forces you to keep it, which is why Bartle observes that players "ride out the storm" rather than start over. Anonymity is what makes the early, cheaper end of this gradient survivable at all; persistence is what makes the expensive end sticky.

**The population ecology.** Because nothing resets, a persistent world's player-type balance is not something a designer sets once but something that has to be actively managed forever, against real feedback loops: achievers breed killers, killers drive off achievers and then starve, socialisers attract both more socialisers and more killers. Bartle treats a stable, balanced world as rare precisely because it requires "time and player management skills that aren't usually available to MUD administrators" — a governance problem native to persistence, not a level-design problem that a good initial build could solve.

**Ending without closure.** Pearce's Uru material forces apart two things ordinary single-session games never separate: the world ending and the community ending. A community can survive its arena's literal deletion and reconstitute itself somewhere else, carrying its history and its social structure with it. This is the sharpest available challenge to any account that identifies a play-community with the world it happens to be playing in at the time.

**The stretched magic circle.** Huizinga's old image of play as bounded off from ordinary life gets pressure-tested hardest by exactly this literature, because a world with no exit cannot offer a clean boundary to step back across. Jaakko Stenros's resolution — splitting "the magic circle" into a personal psychological bubble, a negotiable social contract between participants, and a persisting cultural arena that stays recognizable as a play-structure even when empty — is the most careful attempt in this material to say what survives of the boundary once persistence has worn most of it away, and Castronova's rival image of a "porous membrane," crossed constantly in both directions, is the plainer version of the same concession.

## 3. How to read this literature

Start with Bartle's 1996 "Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades." It is short, it is the paper everything else in this note eventually cites, and reading it first lets you see the population-ecology argument in its original, uncluttered form before it gets folded into the 2003 book's larger apparatus.

Read Bartle's *Designing Virtual Worlds* next, but only the sections this note has actually worked through: the opening definitional chapter, the change/persistence grid, and "The Celebration of Identity." The book runs past 400 pages and the rest — economy design in depth, community-management mechanics, the history of MUD1 — has not been read for this note and should not be treated as covered.

Read Bell's 2008 synthesis third. It is short, it is explicitly built from Bartle, Koster, and Castronova, and it gives you the field's standard definition in one place before you meet the practitioner voices that definition was assembled from.

Read Koster's "Laws of Online World Design" and "Declaring the Rights of Avatars" fourth. They are where the "world, not game" claim actually lives — plainly, repeatedly, and in Koster's own words, not Bartle's — and where the definitional claim turns into an operational one about what running a persistent world actually costs.

Read Dibbell's "A Rape in Cyberspace" fifth. By this point you have the definitional and design vocabulary to see why the essay's central move — a permanent population forced to become a polity because there was no session boundary after which the problem would resolve itself — is a persistence story specifically, not just a story about online harm.

Read Pearce's Uru essay sixth, as the case that inverts everything read so far: instead of a world that never ends, a world that does, and a community that turns out not to need it to.

Read Stenros's "In Defence of a Magic Circle" last. It is the best map of the theoretical fight the rest of this literature is having without naming it, and it is where you meet — at second hand only — the two authors this note most needs and has not yet read directly: T. L. Taylor's critique of the game/life boundary, relayed through Stenros's citation of *Play Between Worlds*, and Castronova's "porous membrane" image, relayed through a single sentence quoted from *Synthetic Worlds*. Huynh, Lim, and Skoric's empirical study of MMO romantic relationships can be read alongside Stenros as a worked example of players individually tuning that porosity rather than the game fixing it for them.

## 4. Where this touches the corpus

The note's obsmetrological note treats this material as bearing on the *unclosed arena* — an arena with growing event support and no maximal configuration — and grades the connections carefully rather than uniformly. The four independent practitioner and definitional formulations of persistence (Bartle's, Koster's, Bell's, and the change/persistence grid together) are graded **established** as convergent: four people writing from operations and definition-writing, not from game theory, arrived at compatible statements of the same architectural fact. That the convergence itself instantiates the corpus's unclosed-settling premise is graded only a **rhyme** — the note is explicit that this is "consistency, not corroboration," since all four are describing the same server architecture rather than independently verifying a structural claim about settlement.

Pearce's Uru diaspora is graded a **rhyme** as well, and the note calls it "the sharpest known challenge to any identification of a play-community with its arena" — here an arena is literally deleted while the strategy, the community's shared history and ongoing play, persists and re-embeds somewhere else. This is worth reading as a rhyme rather than a formal result: nothing in Pearce's account settles into anything the corpus would recognize as a closed number, which is precisely what makes the case sharp rather than merely illustrative.

Two further readings drawn from the source material are weaker and should be held that way. Bartle's change/persistence grid — its diagonal finding that more builder access tends to co-occur with, but does not require, more persistence — is proposed as loosely analogous to a closure-ordinal ordering of regimes, but the source material itself cautions that "the analogy should be held loosely," since Bartle's axes are about authorship and ownership, not about the settlement of contested charges. Stenros's three-way split of the magic circle into psychological bubble, social contract, and arena is proposed as a possible sharpening of the corpus's own codec/arena distinction — his arena mapping onto the corpus's event-structure arenas, his negotiable social contract mapping onto the codec itself — but this is offered as a suggestion for how the corpus's own apparatus might be split, not as a result Stenros himself draws, and should be graded no higher than **conjecture**.

Two more connections in this family are conjectural for a different reason: they lean on primary texts this note has not read. The reading of Dibbell's essay as a near-literal case study of Geertz's "deep play" was drawn without anyone opening Geertz's own essay to check the mapping; the porousness debate's suggestion that the corpus's codec should distinguish graded, direction-dependent porosity (as Huynh et al.'s Splitter/Migrator/Blender typology shows players doing individually) leans on Castronova's and Taylor's positions at second hand, through Stenros, without either author's primary text in hand. Both should stand as **conjecture** until those primary sources are read.

## 5. What we don't know yet

The clearest open question the note states plainly: whether "world" admits a structural definition independent of persistence at all. Bartle's own aside — that a world is "an environment that its inhabitants regard as being self-contained" — gestures at a phenomenological alternative that nothing in this literature develops fully, and it is worth noting explicitly that the crisp, repeated claim "It's a WORLD. Not a game" belongs to Koster, not Bartle; no sweep of Bartle's book has located that phrasing there, and this note should not let it drift onto Bartle's name.

Two further questions are genuinely unresolved rather than merely unread. What, in a formal treatment, actually distinguishes a player *having* a strategy from a player *being* one — the persona level of Bartle's identity gradient has never been given anything like a rigorous account of what that transition consists of. And Bartle's type-ecology, for all its influence, has apparently never been fit quantitatively against a real world's population telemetry; the claim that achievers breed killers "in a typically Malthusian fashion" remains a design intuition dressed in demographic language, not a measured result.

The reading debts are concrete. T. L. Taylor's *Play Between Worlds* has been read by this note only through Stenros's secondary citations, never in primary text, despite Taylor's critique of the game/life boundary being central to exactly the question this note exists to answer. Castronova's *Synthetic Worlds* is known only through one sentence — "porous membrane," relayed twice over, through Stenros and through Huynh et al. — and its chapter titled, promisingly, "The Almost-Magic Circle" has not been opened. Huizinga's *Homo Ludens*, the actual source of the magic-circle image that Stenros and Huynh both build on, has not been read directly by this note either; both authors' treatment of it should be taken as relayed, not verified. The remaining chapters of Bartle's *Designing Virtual Worlds* — on economy, on community management, on the mechanics of world design proper — went unread past the definitional, ownership, and identity material this note has actually used. And three further ethnographies the note has flagged but not touched — Turkle's *Life on the Screen*, Boellstorff's *Coming of Age in Second Life*, and Nardi's *My Life as a Night Elf Priest* — sit adjacent to Dibbell and Pearce as obvious next reading, along with Nick Yee's Daedalus Project survey work and *The Proteus Paradox* as the empirical successor this note needs to test Bartle's typology against actual player populations rather than design intuition alone.
