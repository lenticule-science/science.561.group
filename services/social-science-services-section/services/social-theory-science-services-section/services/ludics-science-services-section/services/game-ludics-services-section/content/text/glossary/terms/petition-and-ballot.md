The petition-and-ballot system is LambdaMOO's in-world legislature, the first sustained mechanism in this literature by which players could change the rules of a running, never-resetting game from inside it. It grew out of Pavel Curtis's ("Haakon") December 9, 1992 announcement, "LambdaMOO Takes a New Direction" (LTAND), that the wizards would withdraw from "the discipline/manners/arbitration business" and become pure technicians — and it was forced into concrete existence by the crisis that followed, the March 1993 toading of Mr. Bungle. Unable to reach consensus on that case through any existing process, the wizards unilaterally imposed balloting from May 1, 1993: any matter could be decided by ballot, and any proposition drawing roughly twice as many votes for as against became binding.

The mechanics as documented directly in LTAND and its 1996 sequel: any player could author a petition, have it vetted by the wizards against a published rubric, and collect signatures within a window to advance to a ballot vote. This entry flags a limit in its own grounding: specific numeric thresholds circulated in popular retellings of the system — a thirty-day membership requirement for eligibility, a roughly two-thirds approval margin — could not be independently verified against a primary rules document beyond LTAND and its sequel, which establish that the system existed and was later amended, but not its full original mechanical specification collected in one place. Those figures should be treated as plausible but unconfirmed.

The system's own history includes its partial reversal. "LambdaMOO Takes Another Direction" (LTAND II, May 16, 1996) reintroduced wizardly fiat with explicit carve-outs: a reserved right to "re-interpret and/or explicitly veto any clause of any passed ballot," a reserved veto over Arbitrator decisions that would "significantly impair the ability of the wizards to do their jobs," and, as a release valve, a standing shutdown-petition mechanism allowing any eligible voter to trigger a binding vote on shutting the MOO down entirely. The community's own verdict on this reversal survives as a primary artifact: the ballot "LTAD-Legality" closed 321 in favor, 111 against, 272 abstaining — the population formally ratifying the wizards' reclamation of the very power the ballot system had originally curtailed.

This section reads the arc as a case of settlement authority handed outward and then partly reclaimed: the wizards ceding closure over disputed matters to an open community process in 1992, that process failing to converge cleanly on the live Bungle case in 1993, and a privileged fixed point — wizardly veto — reasserted from outside the normal ballot mechanism in 1996. That reading is this section's own synthesis, not a framing any single source states. Lawrence Lessig's parallel account treats the system as the clearest real-world instance of code-is-law: LambdaMOO is his case where code literally implements the outcome of a vote, quoting Jennifer Mnookin's phrase "politics implemented through technology." F. Gregory Lastowka and Dan Hunter sharpen the same point institutionally, noting that the 1992 abdication "was not as absolute as it may have seemed," because the wizards retained the power to implement ballot outcomes as coded law — "LambdaMOO society fully understood that the wizards, no matter what they claimed, were still wizards." The petition-and-ballot system is, in this sense, LambdaMOO's own partial answer to the god problem, and its 1996 amendment is the clearest documented case in this literature of an in-world legislature being walked back by its own sovereign.
