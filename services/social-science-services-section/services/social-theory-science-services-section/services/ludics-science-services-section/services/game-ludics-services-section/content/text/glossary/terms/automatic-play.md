Automatic-play is Stefano De Paoli's term, from "Automatic-Play and Player Deskilling in MMORPGs" (Game Studies, 2013), for "the use of game bots, macros and other software that allow a total or partial automation of gameplay and in particular avatar levelling." His analysis is grounded in gold-farming bots and levelling macros in persistent MMORPGs, which he decomposes into a fixed cycle of five automatable tasks — move, target/kill, loot the corpse, loot the items, and heal — each of which existing software already handles independently, with features such as "Auto-attack," "Auto-loot," and "Auto-Heal" (the last configurable with a player-set threshold, De Paoli's example being a heal triggered below 245 hit points). The effect is that a character's progress through the game's levelling curve continues to advance while the software, rather than the player, performs the underlying moment-to-moment decisions.

De Paoli's central claim is that this automation is not neutral with respect to skill: it produces what he calls player deskilling, treated as its own entry in this glossary, paired with a converse he terms the enskilling of machines, drawing on Bruno Latour's actor-network theory — the bots come to embody the timing, risk-assessment, and resource-management judgment that were previously exclusively human competencies. He frames the whole process through Karl Marx's account of industrial automation, in which workers become "mere appendices of machines," a citation this section carries at second hand, not having read Marx's text directly for this entry, and connects it to Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew McAfee's Race Against the Machine on labor obsolescence, cited by De Paoli and likewise not independently read here. He records a player's own summary of the practice in terms that anticipate the interpassivity literature on idle games without using that vocabulary: "we're letting a program play it for us."

Automatic-play and idle games' ambience acts are, in this section's reading, structurally cousins rather than the same phenomenon: both are cases where a persistent world's state keeps advancing through delegated, non-human agency while the nominal player is elsewhere, but automatic-play is documented in the sources as covert automation of an ordinarily manual MMORPG grind — often against the game's own terms of service — where the idle game's self-play is a designed, sanctioned feature of the genre from the start. The section's web-temporality program treats De Paoli's bots as posing an open formal question that the literature itself does not answer: when a receptive strategy is executed by a delegated automaton rather than a human player, what, if anything, distinguishes the player's strategy from the player's simple absence — a question the sources treat as one of labor and agency, not yet as a formal one.
