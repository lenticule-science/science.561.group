Griefing, in the definition James Grimmelmann uses in "Virtual Worlds as Comparative Law" (2004) — quoting Hunter and Lastowka's draft "Virtual Crimes" — is "any activity that genuinely bothers most players of the game in question." Grimmelmann observes that this definition, however serviceable in practice, "renders futile the task of developing any general doctrine of virtual criminal law," because the same acts — violence, harassment — are simultaneously the wrongs a community wants deterred and the only sanctions available to deter them, and no in-world community holds the real-world state's monopoly on disproportionate punishment. As he puts it: "the set of unpleasant and wrongful acts players might wish to deter is identical to the set of unpleasant and effective sanctions available as deterrence." The only real limit on punishment is the target's own tolerance for staying: "It is impossible to punish a criminal beyond the value she places on continuing to remain in the game because she can always respond by quitting."

The literature's best-known positive taxonomy is Foo and Koivisto's "Defining Grief Play in MMORPGs" (ACM ACE 2004), whose primary text could not be obtained directly for this entry; the four-category taxonomy reported here is reconstructed from cross-checked secondary sources rather than verified against the paper's own wording or methodology, and should be read with that qualification attached. The reported categories are harassment (impeding another player's activity), power imposition (exploiting a mechanical or positional weakness to assert dominance), scamming (cheating others of money, items, or information), and greed play (acting purely for personal benefit at others' expense, notably including unintentional grief, where harm to others is a side effect of self-interest rather than its point).

The paradigm case grounding this term in this literature is the March 1993 Mr. Bungle incident at LambdaMOO (see toading): behavior nearly every observer agreed was intolerable, but that no existing rule of the MOO reached, which produced first a unilateral wizard toading and then LambdaMOO's own legislative apparatus, the petition-and-ballot system. Grimmelmann's own answer to who actually enforces norms against griefing, given that no formal doctrine can settle the question in advance, is guild: player organizations the game software barely formalizes but that become, in his words, "the locus of effective decision-making power."

Griefing's community-relative definition sits in explicit contrast with EVE Online's institutional response to comparably harmful conduct. Rather than adjudicating scamming and betrayal as griefing case by case, CCP Games declares such acts out of jurisdiction in advance and lets the community absorb them — the condition this section elsewhere names a ruthless-game, overseen at arm's length by the council-of-stellar-management. This section reads LambdaMOO and EVE as opposite strategies for the same underlying problem — one adjudicating the breach after the fact through community process, the other legislating the breach into the ruleset before it occurs — a comparison this section proposes as a synthesis across the two case studies, not a claim any single source in this literature makes directly.
