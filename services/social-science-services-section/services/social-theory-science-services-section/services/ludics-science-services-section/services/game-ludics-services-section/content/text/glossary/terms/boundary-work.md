Boundary work is Thomas Gieryn's sociological concept, originally developed to describe how scientists demarcate legitimate science from pseudoscience; it is not itself the subject of this dossier's reading, and is known here only in its applied form, through Martin Gibbs, Marcus Carter, Michael Arnold and Bjorn Nansen's 2013 paper analyzing a single documented incident. In September 2006, players from the guild Serenity Now raided and killed the mourners at a real in-game memorial gathering held for a deceased *World of Warcraft* player. The controversy that followed, rather than settling into an agreed verdict, split along three simultaneously contested lines that Gibbs and colleagues describe as "entwined filaments" of boundary work.

The three filaments are ethical, ontological, and aesthetic, and the paper quotes both poles of each debate directly from the forum arguments that constituted it: ethically, breach of common decency against a claim to have been simply "playing the game how it should be played"; ontologically, the claim that "this is an MMO, your character is quite literally an extension of yourself" against the deflationary "it's only a game"; aesthetically, funny against reprehensible, argued out through invective rather than resolved by it. The paper's central finding is that none of these three axes reached closure. Writing seven years after the event, the authors note that the controversy "has been ongoing since 2006 and has still not reached closure," which they read as "a strong indication that these still remain largely unresolved and are perhaps irreconcilable." The incident went on to attract attention from moral philosophy, legal-liability scholarship, and ritual studies as an object in its own right, evidence that the boundary work it provoked did not stay contained within game studies either.

Read against this glossary's other entries, the Serenity Now case is a concrete instance of what happens when a breach crosses the interaction membrane and the community on the far side has no settlement mechanism ready to hand: unlike the LambdaMOO "Mr. Bungle" affair, where an ungoverned community was forced to construct a permanent adjudication system under crisis, no comparable institution formed here, and the dispute simply persisted, unsettled, alongside everything else the world's persistence carries forward. The case is also notable for failing to resolve along three independent axes at once rather than one, a structural feature this glossary's boundary-and-membrane program reads as a striking instance — offered here as a proposed reading, not an established equivalence — of a single event carrying unresolved charge along more than one register simultaneously.
