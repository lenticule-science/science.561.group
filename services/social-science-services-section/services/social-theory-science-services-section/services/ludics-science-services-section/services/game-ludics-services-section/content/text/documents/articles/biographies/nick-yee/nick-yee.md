title: "Nick Yee"
description: "The psychologist whose Daedalus Project survey research showed MMORPG players describing their own play as work, closing the loop on the section's play/labor literature."
date-created: 2026-08-22
---

Nick Yee earned his PhD in Communication from Stanford University in 2007, completing his doctoral research at Stanford's Virtual Human Interaction Lab under Jeremy Bailenson. His long-running Daedalus Project, begun while he was a doctoral researcher, surveyed the psychology and sociology of MMORPG players and eventually collected data from more than 40,000 respondents before entering hibernation in 2009. He later worked at the Palo Alto Research Center applying social network analysis and predictive analytics to large-scale World of Warcraft data, and subsequently as a senior research scientist at Ubisoft, where he co-founded the Gamer Behavior Research group. In 2015 he co-founded Quantic Foundry with Nicolas Ducheneaut, a firm applying survey-based motivation research to game design and marketing. His paper on the motivations of online gamers is highly cited.

Yee's load-bearing document for this portfolio is "The Labor of Fun: How Video Games Blur the Boundaries of Work and Play" (Games and Culture 1(1), 2006), drawn from Daedalus Project survey data. Its headline finding is that MMORPG players average roughly twenty hours a week in-world, and describe that play, unprompted, in the vocabulary of a job rather than leisure: "It became a chore to play... I wanted to get away from real life... and social etiquette followed me in!"; "Was more like work then fun. One day got burnt out trying to get exp for level 55 and quit."; "The problem is that it goes from enjoyable to just work so gradually that unless you step back for a while and evaluate you do not even realize you're working." Yee's argument is that this is not incidental but designed-in: MMORPGs are built on behavioral-conditioning principles that function as work platforms, training players to become, in his phrase, better game workers. He extends the argument to the gold-farming industry, where firms such as TopGameSeller assign staff to level characters for paying customers — "For these employees of IGE and TopGameSeller, playing and working have become the same thing" — the point at which the blurred boundary he documents in ordinary players becomes a literal wage relationship for others. He closes by asking what the finding does to the concept it is nominally about: "the blurring of work and play begs the question — what does 'fun' really mean?"

Yee's position in the field is as the player-side counterpart to the industry-side gold-farming literature: where Richard Heeks and Julian Dibbell document the wage-labor economics and lived experience of gold farmers, Yee's survey data shows that even unpaid, ordinary subscribers report experiencing their play in the same terms — obligation, tedium, burnout — closing the loop between playbour as an industry-imposed classification and playbour as a felt condition among players who are nominally just having fun. One program of the section holds him: Boundary and Membrane, where "The Labor of Fun" sits alongside Julian Kücklich's "playbour" and Heeks's and Dibbell's gold-farming work as the literature documenting what the play/work distinction dissolves into once a persistent world sustains wage labor, unwaged value production, and self-reported play-as-chore simultaneously. His later Daedalus Project corpus and *The Proteus Paradox* (2014) are recorded elsewhere in the section as an unread reading debt for the Persistent World Foundations program, which regards them as the likely empirical successor to Bartle's player-type typology, but that connection is not yet established in the section's holdings and is not asserted here as settled.
