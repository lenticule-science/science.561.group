Jesper Juul is a Danish game theorist and designer, and the first person to receive a PhD in game studies, awarded by the IT University of Copenhagen (his dissertation became the book *Half-Real: Video Games between Real Rules and Fictional Worlds*, 2005). He was founding faculty at the NYU Game Center from 2009, where he held a position as visiting assistant arts professor, and has also held positions at MIT's Comparative Media Studies program and the IT University of Copenhagen's Center for Computer Games Research; he has since taught at the Danish Design School in Copenhagen. Beyond *Half-Real*, his books include *The Art of Failure: An Essay on the Pain of Playing Video Games* (2013) and *Handmade Pixels: Independent Video Games and the Quest for Authenticity* (2019).

His load-bearing text for this portfolio is "The Magic Circle and the Puzzle Piece" (*Philosophy of Computer Games* 2008, Potsdam, read in full), written to rescue the magic circle from what he treats as an over-corrected critical consensus. Juul surveys the critics — T. L. Taylor, who reads the circle as an oppressive structure players push against; Marinka Copier, for whom the play-space is "constructed in negotiation," not given; Malaby; Pargman and Jakobsson — and identifies an irony: most of them claim to be countering Huizinga and Salen and Zimmerman by insisting on the social construction of the boundary, which is exactly what Huizinga and Salen and Zimmerman already said. His own resolution: "the magic circle is best understood as *the boundary that players negotiate*. To deny the magic circle is to deny that players negotiate this boundary." He offers a three-frame model of any game action — goal-orientation, experience, and social context, nested inside one another with no fixed priority — under which a self-handicapping player, or a parent deliberately losing to a child, does not disprove the circle but simply shows its imperfect, negotiated nature. His proposed replacement metaphor is the puzzle piece, which drops the assumption of a uniform interface: a game may or may not fit a given context of platform, time, player count, or prior knowledge, and the interesting question becomes how it fits rather than whether it is separate. His formulation inverts the usual causality: "a game must be integrated into a context in order to be experienced as separate from that context."

Juul's position in the field's boundary debate is that of the concept's principal defender against the 2005–2014 critique wave, arguing not that the critics are wrong about porosity but that they are attacking a strong-boundary reading no one actually held. Two of the section's six programs hold him. Boundary and Membrane holds "The Magic Circle and the Puzzle Piece" as one of the debate's defenses, alongside Stenros's more systematic reconciliation.
