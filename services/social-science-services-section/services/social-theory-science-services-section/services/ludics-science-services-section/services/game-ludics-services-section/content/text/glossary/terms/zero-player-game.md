A zero-player game is a game in whose running the player performs no action whatsoever once it is set going — the boundary case at which "playing" a game and merely watching it become the same act. In the material gathered for this glossary, the term is Sonia Fizek's, used in "Interpassivity and the Joy of Delegated Play in Idle Games" to place John Conway's Game of Life (1970) as the genre's "ur-example," and to link the 2017 idle game Rouguathia back to it as a direct descendant. Conway's cellular automaton needs only an initial configuration; after that, its entire subsequent history unfolds by fixed rule with no further input, which is exactly what distinguishes it, in Fizek's account, from idle games proper such as Progress Quest, where the player still performs one act — "rolling" a character — before the game proceeds unattended.

Fizek anchors the boundary this term marks against a stricter definition of play she quotes from Björk and Juul (2012), not independently read for this section and so reported here at second hand: "unless players have some agency to affect the outcome of a game and can intentionally exercise it, they are not really … playing a game." Measured against that criterion, a zero-player game is a limit case that arguably falls outside "playing" altogether, which is precisely what makes it useful to Fizek as the far end of a spectrum whose near end is ordinary, fully interactive play. Idle games occupy the middle of that spectrum: they retain a nominal player role and a moment or two of initiating action, but hand the great majority of subsequent state-change to the system, which is why Fizek treats Conway's Life, rather than any idle game, as the case where interpassivity — the delegation of the pleasure or the doing of play to the machine, discussed in this glossary under that term — reaches its purest, most literal form.

The zero-player game is thus best read alongside idle game, incremental game, and interpassivity: it is not itself a genre with a design discourse of its own in the sources gathered here, but a theoretical fixed point that those genres are defined by their distance from. No source consulted for this entry treats the zero-player game as a commercially or culturally significant category in its own right — its work in the literature is conceptual, supplying the endpoint against which the idle game's residual, minimal player agency can be measured.
