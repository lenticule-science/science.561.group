title: "Thomas Malaby"
description: "The anthropologist who rejected play as games' meta-category and redefined games as processual domains of contrived contingency."
date-created: 2026-08-22
---

Thomas M. Malaby is Professor and Chair of Anthropology at the University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee, where he received his PhD from Harvard in 1998. His research concerns the relationships among institutions, unpredictability, and technology, pursued largely through games and game-like processes. His first book, *Gambling Life: Dealing in Contingency in a Greek City* (University of Illinois Press, 2003), is an ethnography of backgammon cafés, card clubs, and dice gambling in the city of Chania, Crete — the first ethnography to take gambling itself as its core subject. His second, *Making Virtual Worlds: Linden Lab and Second Life* (Cornell University Press, 2009), is an ethnographic study of the company behind Second Life. In 2018 he co-founded the Digital Cultures Collaboratory at UWM's Center for 21st Century Studies.

His load-bearing text for this portfolio is "Beyond Play: A New Approach to Games" (*Games and Culture* 2(2), 2007, read in full), published two years ahead of Consalvo's essay in the same journal and making a structurally similar but more theoretically ambitious argument. Malaby rejects "play" itself as games' supercategory, arguing that its three inherited features — separable, safe, pleasurable — are not intrinsic but "cultural accomplishments," historically specific to Western modernity and traceable to modernist amateurism and the class politics of nineteenth-century sport. His replacement definition: "a game is a semibounded and socially legitimate domain of contrived contingency that generates interpretable outcomes." He diagnoses two errors in games scholarship — formalism (treating rules as fixed, ahistorical structures, in the manner of von Neumann and Morgenstern) and exceptionalism (treating games as categorically set apart, whether dismissed as trivial or romanticized as utopian) — and traces both to a single forgetting: that games are processual, "grounded in (and constituted by) human practice and therefore always in the process of becoming," illustrated with the NFL's "tuck rule" controversy and the shot clock's introduction after players learned to stall. He further breaks "contingency" into four generative sources — stochastic (dice, lag, weather), social (not knowing others' intentions), performative (an action that can succeed or fail), and semiotic (the instability of what an outcome means, which he illustrates from his own experience winning at backgammon in Greece as a foreigner and thereby altering what the win signified) — arguing that games are best understood as domains that calibrate these forms of contingency rather than eliminate them, making games "ontologically on a par" with the rest of experience. His *Gambling Life* fieldwork on *zaria*, Cretan dice gambling, supplies the ethnographic wedge: an "existential engagement with the game" involving inheritance, status, and standing with the state, and explicitly not reducible to "fun" or "leisure."

Malaby's position in the field's boundary debate is foundational rather than reconciliatory: alongside Consalvo, he is one of the critics whom Jesper Juul's and Jaakko Stenros's later defenses of the magic circle explicitly answer, and his footnote warning that treating a game world as "a place where nothing happens" forecloses ethical scrutiny of what its design actually does anticipates the section's material on gold farming and playbour. One of the section's six programs holds him: Boundary and Membrane, where "Beyond Play" stands alongside Consalvo's essay as the founding critique, and where his four-way division of contingency is read as an open invitation to a four-regime comparison — a mapping the program explicitly declines to assert as established.
