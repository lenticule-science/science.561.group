Richard Allan Bartle (born 1960) is a British writer and game researcher who, as an undergraduate at the University of Essex, co-created MUD1 with Roy Trubshaw in 1978 — the first multi-user dungeon and the direct ancestor of every persistent online world the section studies. He took a first-class degree in computer science at Essex in 1981 and a PhD in artificial intelligence there in 1988, becoming the university's youngest lecturer at the time; he lectured until 1987, when he left to work on MUD full-time, and later returned to Essex as a part-time professor and principal teaching fellow in the Department of Computing and Electronic Systems, supervising its computer game design courses until his retirement in April 2025, after which he became Emeritus Professor.

Bartle's load-bearing work for this portfolio is twofold. His 1996 paper "Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs" is usually read as a taxonomy — Achievers, Explorers, Socialisers, and Killers plotted on a Players-vs-World, Acting-vs-Interacting graph — but its real claim is dynamical: the four types sit in standing feedback loops that only a population which never resets between sessions can produce. Achievers breed Killers "in a typically Malthusian fashion"; Socialisers attract both more Socialisers and, fatally, more Killers, risking "a downward spiral in numbers"; and a balanced world is rare because it demands sustained population management that "most MUD administrators" lack. His 2003 textbook, Designing Virtual Worlds, turns this into an explicit definition built from five co-equal necessary conditions — automated "physics," a single persistent character per player, real-time interaction, a shared environment, and persistence itself, stated as "the environment continues to exist and develop internally... even when there are no people interacting with it." The book's change/persistence grid, cross-tabulating how much a world lets players build against how much of what they build survives a reboot, answers what Bartle treats as the field's deepest design question — "Whose world is it?" — ranging from worlds that belong entirely to their designers to worlds, like LambdaMOO, that belong to their players. Its chapter on "The Celebration of Identity" distinguishes avatar, character, and persona as successive depths of self-investment, and argues that persistence ratchets that investment upward: "the longer you play a character, the more attached to it you become... you would be very reluctant to throw it all away. Instead, you ride out the storm."

Bartle sits inside the field's central debate about what separates a game from a world without originating its sharpest slogan: the frequently repeated line "It's a WORLD. Not a game" belongs to Raph Koster, not to Bartle, whose own text makes the same point more obliquely, through the ownership logic of the change/persistence grid and the escalating stakes of the identity chapter. Within this section his work is held by the Persistent World Foundations program, which treats his five-condition definition, his player-type ecology, and his identity gradient as among its load-bearing, independently-arrived-at results; his change/persistence grid is also read there as a loose, deliberately non-formal analogue to questions the section pursues about how worlds settle over time.
