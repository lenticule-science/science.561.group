Julian Dibbell is an American journalist and author. Writing as a freelance contributor to The Village Voice in the early 1990s, he began covering internet subcultures including LambdaMOO, and went on to publish My Tiny Life: Crime and Passion in a Virtual World (1998), an extended account of LambdaMOO's social life and politics, and Play Money: How I Quit My Day Job and Made Millions Trading Virtual Loot (2006), which followed his own experience trading virtual items and traced the rise of real-money trading and gold-farming economies.

Dibbell's load-bearing work for this portfolio is "A Rape in Cyberspace," published in The Village Voice on December 21, 1993 and reprinted in My Tiny Life. The essay tracks, in granular, verbatim detail, how the LambdaMOO community discovered — in real time, with no prior mechanism in place — that it needed to become a polity to answer the question of what to do about a player who used a scripted "voodoo doll" object to narrate other players' characters into sexual violence. Its central formal move is naming a split explicit in every MOO event: a "second, complicating set: the 'real-life' facts," where "a certain tension invariably buzzes in the gap between the hard, prosaic RL facts and their more fluid, dreamy VR counterparts" — a gap the Bungle incident made "striking" rather than routine. One victim, exu, posted publicly at the time: "Mostly voodoo dolls are amusing... But I also think that Mr. Bungle was being a vicious, vile fuckhead"; months later she told Dibbell the words had been written through "posttraumatic tears... a real-life fact that should suffice to prove that the words' emotional content was no mere fiction." The essay's second half documents the community's improvised response: a thirty-person open meeting that reached no consensus, a wizard's unilateral deletion of Bungle's account, and archwizard Pavel Curtis's subsequent construction of a permanent petition-and-ballot system. Dibbell also records the punishment's limit: Bungle simply re-registered under a new name and reoffended, which the essay reads not as a loophole but as confirmation that in a world with no outside, "his punishment, ultimately, had been no more or less symbolic than his crime."

Dibbell's work sits at the intersection of several of the section's debates and is held by three of its programs. The Persistent World Foundations program reads the Bungle essay as the founding case of in-world harm registering as real harm, and of a permanent population forced to invent governance from nothing; the Ludocratic Institutions program reads the same essay, alongside Lessig's and Lastowka and Hunter's later legal accounts of it, as the origin case for the question of what restrains an operator-sovereign and how in-world rule-change actually happens. The Boundary and Membrane program separately draws on Dibbell's later reporting — his account, in Play Money, of gold farmers in Nanjing — as a documented instance of the play/work distinction being administratively erased for a paid laborer, with an involuntary remainder the section frames, following Dibbell's own reported phrasing, as "you want to play."
