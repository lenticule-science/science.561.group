A virtual world is a computer-implemented environment that is synchronous (many participants act within it at once), persistent (it continues to run and change whether or not any given participant is present), and populated by avatars through which participants act. No single source read for this glossary coins the phrase outright; the working definition the section relies on is assembled from two convergent formalizations: Richard Bartle's 2003 *Designing Virtual Worlds*, which sets out five co-equal necessary conditions, and Mark W. Bell's 2008 "Toward a Definition of 'Virtual Worlds,'" written explicitly to combine and adjudicate between Bartle's, Raph Koster's, and Edward Castronova's informal definitions into one operational statement.

Bartle's five conditions are that the world implements automated "physics" (rules governing what can and cannot happen), that each player acts through a single persistent character, that interaction runs in real time, that the world is shared among its participants, and that it is persistent — "the environment continues to exist and develop internally... even when there are no people interacting with it." Bell's synthesis compresses this into one sentence: "A synchronous, persistent network of people, represented as avatars, facilitated by networked computers." Bell treats the definition as a filter rather than a slogan: it excludes Mario Kart (synchronous but not persistent) and excludes Facebook (persistent but without avatars or navigable space), which is what makes it do classificatory work rather than merely gesture at the concept. Bell also reports Raph Koster's own 2004 phrasing at second hand — "a virtual world is a spatially based depiction of a persistent virtual environment, which can be experienced by numerous participants at once, who are represented within the space by avatars" — a formulation this entry has not verified against Koster's original text directly.

The line most often associated with this literature's strongest claim about virtual worlds — "It's a WORLD. Not a game." — belongs to Raph Koster's "The Laws of Online World Design," not to Bartle, notwithstanding the title of Bartle's own book; the two practitioners converge on the same conclusion, but the line itself is Koster's, and this is a documented misattribution hazard worth naming explicitly. Bartle's own argument that a virtual world is not simply a game runs instead through his change/persistence grid and his account of persona-level identity investment. This entry's own necessary condition of persistence is treated at greater length under persistent-world; the codebase families that historically instantiate the definition are treated under mud and moo; and the identity levels the definition's "represented as avatars" clause presupposes are treated under avatar and persona.
