## Scope

Establish, from the primary literature, what persistence does to play — and
maintain the section's working answer to the question the literature keeps
circling: what makes a world a world rather than a game?

## Motivating questions

1. What is the minimal definition of a persistent world, and which of its
   clauses does the load-bearing work? (Bartle's five conditions; Bell's
   synthesis; Castronova's interactivity/physicality/persistence triple.)
2. What does the removal of the exit — no reset, no final position, no
   dissolution of the play-space — do to stakes, attachment, and identity?
3. What population dynamics exist only because nothing resets? (Bartle's
   player-type ecology as the founding model.)
4. Who does a world belong to, and how is that determined by what players can
   change and how much of it survives a reboot? (Bartle's change/persistence
   grid.)
5. What is an ending, once world-death and community-closure can come apart?
   (The Uru diaspora as the type case.)

## Objects of study

MUDs and MOOs; graphical MMOs; the definitional literature on "virtual world";
player typologies and their dynamics; avatar/character/persona identity
depth; play communities as entities separable from the worlds they play in;
the operator's standing obligations ("it's a SERVICE").

## State of knowledge (2026-08-22 sweep)

The literature is three traditions grown together — practitioner design
writing (Bartle, Koster), ethnographic game studies (Dibbell, Pearce, Taylor),
and definitional scholarship (Bell, Castronova) — converging on one claim in
different vocabularies: persistence is not a feature bolted onto a game but
the thing that turns a game into a world, specifically by removing the exit.
Load-bearing results now held by the section:

- **Bartle's definition** (read): five co-equal necessary conditions, with
  persistence stated as "the environment continues to exist and develop
  internally... even when there are no people interacting with it."
- **Bartle's player-type ecology** (read): achievers breed killers "in a
  typically Malthusian fashion"; the balanced world is a population-management
  achievement, not a design artifact.
- **Bartle's identity gradient** (read): avatar → character → persona, with
  persistence ratcheting investment ("you ride out the storm").
- **Bell's synthesis definition** (read): "a synchronous, persistent network
  of people, represented as avatars, facilitated by networked computers,"
  with the decisive gloss that the participant senses the world's systems
  "exist with or without a participant's presence."
- **Koster's operational laws** (read): "Once you open your online world,
  expect to keep your team on it indefinitely"; the categorical "It's a
  WORLD. Not a game" is Koster's claim — a standing misattribution hazard,
  never Bartle's.
- **Dibbell's Bungle essay** (read): the founding case of in-world harm
  registering as real harm, and of a permanent population forced to become a
  polity — shared jurisdiction with the Ludocratic Institutions note.
- **Pearce's Uru diaspora** (read): world-death decoupled from
  community-closure; identity as an "intersubjective accomplishment."

## Obsmetrological bridge

The note's phenomena instantiate the *unclosed arena* the obsmetrological
note of 2026-08-22 files as an open formalization obligation: an arena with
growing event support and no maximal configuration. Graded per the corpus
discipline: the four independent practitioner formulations of persistence are
**established** as convergent; that the convergence instantiates the
unclosed-settling premise is a **rhyme** (all four describe the same server
architecture — consistency, not corroboration). The Uru case — arena deleted,
strategy persisting and re-embedding — is a **rhyme** with no current formal
counterpart at all, and is the sharpest known challenge to any
identification of a play-community with its arena.

## Open problems

- Whether "world" admits a structural definition independent of persistence
  (Bartle's "an environment that its inhabitants regard as being
  self-contained" suggests a phenomenological alternative).
- The formal status of the persona level of identity-investment: what, in an
  event-structure treatment, distinguishes a player *having* a strategy from
  a player *being* one?
- Whether Bartle's type-ecology has ever been fit quantitatively to a real
  world's population telemetry (joint problem with Obsmetrology of Play).

## Reading debts

Turkle's *Life on the Screen* (1995); Boellstorff's *Coming of Age in Second
Life* (2008); Nardi's *My Life as a Night Elf Priest* (2010); Yee's Daedalus
Project corpus and *The Proteus Paradox* (2014) as the empirical successor to
Bartle's typology; T.L. Taylor's *Play Between Worlds* in primary text (all
current Taylor material is relayed through Stenros's citations).

## Annotated sources

Annotated bibliography of the foundational primary texts on persistent worlds, what persistence does to play, and the game/world boundary.


### Sources

**Bartle, Richard, "Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs," *Journal of MUD Research* 1(1), 1996.**
URL: https://mud.co.uk/richard/hcds.htm
Status: read in full.
The founding player-type taxonomy — Achievers, Explorers, Socialisers, Killers, plotted on a Players-vs-World / Acting-vs-Interacting graph — but its substantive claim is dynamical rather than descriptive: the four types sit in standing feedback loops that only a population which never resets between sessions can produce. Achievers "breed" Killers "in a typically Malthusian fashion"; Socialisers show both positive and negative feedback, risking "a downward spiral in numbers"; a balanced "Type 3" world is "extraordinarily difficult to achieve and maintain" because it demands sustained population management, not a one-time design decision. The note needs this as the founding statement of persistence-driven population dynamics — the claim that a world's demographic balance is a standing governance problem specifically because nothing resets it.

**Bartle, Richard A., *Designing Virtual Worlds*, New Riders, 2003.**
URL: https://mud.co.uk/richard/DesigningVirtualWorlds.pdf
Status: read in substantial part (the opening definitional chapter, the change/persistence categorization, and "The Celebration of Identity"; the book's remaining 400-plus pages — economy design, community-management mechanics, MUD1 history — were not read for this note).
Bartle's textbook promotes the 1996 taxonomy into an explicit five-condition definition of a virtual world, treating persistence — "the environment continues to exist and develop internally... even when there are no people interacting with it" — as co-equal with automated physics, a single persistent character, real-time interaction, and shared access. Its most load-bearing move for this note is the change/persistence grid, which cross-tabulates how much players can build against how much of it survives a reboot and uses the result to answer "Whose world is it?", ranging from designer-owned worlds ("like a movie entirely belongs to its director") to player-owned ones like LambdaMOO. The identity chapter supplies the avatar/character/persona gradient central to the note's account of investment and stakes: "You're not assuming an identity, you are that identity... if you're killed in a fight, you don't feel that your character has died, you feel that you have died." No verified instance of the phrase "world, not a game" was located inside this text; that framing belongs to Koster, and this note should not let it migrate onto Bartle's name.

**Bell, Mark W., "Toward a Definition of 'Virtual Worlds'," *Journal of Virtual Worlds Research* 1(1), July 2008.**
URL: https://jvwr-ojs-utexas.tdl.org/jvwr/article/view/283/237
Status: read in full.
The field's standard synthesis definition, built explicitly by combining and critiquing Bartle's, Koster's, and Castronova's informal definitions: "a synchronous, persistent network of people, represented as avatars, facilitated by networked computers." Its gloss on persistence is the clearest single statement in the literature of why the property changes the phenomenology of play rather than merely its infrastructure — a participant "has a sense the systems in the space... exist with or without a participant's presence." The paper also applies the definition diagnostically, excluding Mario Kart (synchronous but not persistent) and Facebook (persistent but without avatars or navigable space), which sharpens exactly which clause is doing the definitional work. The note needs this as the reference-standard definition against which Bartle's and Koster's more informal statements can be checked.

**Dibbell, Julian, "A Rape in Cyberspace," *The Village Voice*, December 21, 1993 (reprinted 1998 in *My Tiny Life*).**
URL: http://gunkelweb.com/coms465/texts/rape_cyberspace.pdf
Status: read in full.
The founding case study of the "Mr. Bungle" incident on LambdaMOO, in which a scripted "voodoo doll" object was used to narrate other players' characters into sexual violence, and the essay's granular, real-time account of the community's ad hoc invention of justice in response — a chaotic open meeting, a wizard's unilateral deletion of the offender's account, and the archwizard's subsequent construction of a permanent petition-and-ballot democracy. Its central formal move is naming the split between "the hard, prosaic RL facts and their more fluid, dreamy VR counterparts," and showing that in this case the gap became "striking" rather than routine; one victim's own account of writing through "posttraumatic tears" is offered as proof the harm was real despite being made of nothing but "digital code and snippets of creative writing." The note needs this as the type case of a persistent, permanent population being forced to become a polity because no session boundary existed after which the problem would simply end — confirmed by the essay's coda, in which the offender re-registers and reoffends, so that "his punishment... had been no more or less symbolic than his crime."

**Huynh, Kim-Phuong, Sun-Wei Lim & Marko M. Skoric, "Stepping out of the Magic Circle: Regulation of Play/Life Boundary in MMO-Mediated Romantic Relationship," *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication* 18(3), 2013, 251–264.**
URL: https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/18/3/251/4067486
Status: read in full.
An empirical interview study showing that MMO players individually and variably regulate how porous the game/life boundary is around online romance, using Castronova's "porous membrane" image and Fine's primary/game/character frame model as scaffolding. It identifies three player postures — "Splitters," who maintain a rigid boundary ("You can't really love someone you haven't met"); "Migrators," who treat an online relationship as a "stepping stone" to offline commitment; and "Blenders," who reject the boundary and treat online meeting as "exactly the same" as offline — along with stigma-management strategies such as "mainstreaming" and "concealment." The note needs this as the concrete empirical counterpart to the theoretical porous-boundary debate: evidence that porosity is something individual players tune, not a property the game architecture fixes uniformly for everyone.

**Koster, Raph, "The Laws of Online World Design" (MUD-Dev mailing list, 1998; maintained at raphkoster.com) and "Declaring the Rights of Avatars," August 27, 2000.**
URL: https://www.raphkoster.com/games/laws-of-online-world-design/the-laws-of-online-world-design/
Status: read in full.
Practitioner essays from a lead designer of Ultima Online. The Laws state the strongest and plainest version of the "world, not game" claim in the entire literature — "It's a SERVICE. Not a game. It's a WORLD. Not a game. It's a COMMUNITY. Not a game" — paired with its operational cost: "Once you open your online world, expect to keep your team on it indefinitely. Some of these games have never closed." The Rights essay, a Bill-of-Rights thought experiment modeled on 1789, argues from the same premise that because the player/administrator relationship does not terminate the way a boxed game's does, administrators accrue something like sovereign obligations toward their communities. The note needs this as the source of the categorical claim other authors are read against — and as the standing misattribution hazard: this claim is Koster's, never Bartle's.

**Pearce, Celia, "Communities of Play: The Social Construction of Identity in Persistent Online Game Worlds," *Electronic Book Review* (companion essay to *Communities of Play: Emergent Cultures in Multiplayer Games and Virtual Worlds*, MIT Press, 2009).**
URL: https://electronicbookreview.com/publications/communities-of-play-the-social-construction-of-identity-in-persistent-online-game-worlds/
Status: read in full.
An eighteen-month ethnography of "The Gathering," a roughly 350-member Uru: Ages Beyond Myst player group that survived the February 2004 shutdown of Uru's servers by migrating, as an intact social unit, across four different virtual worlds. Members "reported weeping as the clock struck midnight and the avatars on the screen froze in place," yet the shared trauma "served as a catalyst for fortifying the group identity, which evolved into a sort of fictive ethnicity." Its theoretical claim — that "individual avatar identity is an intersubjective accomplishment," produced by group-level "intersubjective flow" — is the note's type case for decoupling a world's death from a community's closure, and for its claim that the magic circle is "more porous than previously thought."

**Stenros, Jaakko, "In Defence of a Magic Circle: The Social and Mental Boundaries of Play," *Proceedings of DiGRA Nordic 2012: Local and Global — Games in Culture and Society*.**
URL: https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/605/605/602
Status: read in full.
A genealogy of the magic-circle concept from Huizinga through Salen and Zimmerman to its critics — T. L. Taylor, Thomas Malaby, and others — resolving the debate by splitting the single metaphor into three distinct objects: a personal "psychological bubble," a negotiated social "magic circle" that can be renegotiated or can collapse, and a persisting cultural "arena," recognized as a structure for play even when empty. It transmits, at second hand, Taylor's critique that "the division between game and life needs [not] be as strong" as the standard reading assumes, Malaby's claim that "any game can have important consequences not only materially but also socially and culturally," and Castronova's rival image of a "porous membrane" separating synthetic worlds from the rest of life. The note needs this as its best available map of the theoretical fight the rest of the literature is having without naming it directly — with the caveat that Taylor's and Castronova's own primary texts have not themselves been read by this note, only Stenros's citations of them.

## Reading debts

T. L. Taylor's *Play Between Worlds* (2006) has been read by this note only through Stenros's secondary citation, with page numbers but no primary text — direct attempts to obtain the open-access chapter pages and a library mirror both failed, and the archive.org lending copy has no searchable OCR for this item. Given that Taylor's critique of the game/life boundary sits at the center of this note's founding question, the primary text should be obtained before any further claim is built on the Stenros relay.

Edward Castronova's *Synthetic Worlds* (2005) is known to this note only through a single sentence — "porous membrane... a better term for the barrier that separates synthetic (i.e. online) worlds from the Earth" — quoted twice over, first by Stenros and again by Huynh et al. A promotional book-review PDF was located but is not the book itself. Chapter 6, titled "The Almost-Magic Circle," is exactly on-topic and has not been opened.

Johan Huizinga's *Homo Ludens* (1938; 1949 Beacon Press translation) is the actual source of the magic-circle image that both Stenros and Huynh et al. build on, and it has not been read directly by this note. Both authors' use of Huizinga should be treated as relayed rather than verified until the primary chapter is read.

The remaining chapters of Bartle's *Designing Virtual Worlds* — on economy design, community management, and the history of MUD1 — went unread past the definitional, ownership, and identity material this note has drawn on, and may hold further persistence-relevant argument.

Sherry Turkle's *Life on the Screen: Identity in the Age of the Internet* (1995), Tom Boellstorff's *Coming of Age in Second Life* (2008), and Bonnie Nardi's *My Life as a Night Elf Priest* (2010) are canonical ethnographies of identity in persistent worlds, contemporaneous with or extending Bartle and Dibbell, and none has been engaged by any sweep feeding this note.

Nick Yee's Daedalus Project survey corpus and *The Proteus Paradox* (2014) are the major empirical successor to Bartle's player-type typology — a large-N test of the achiever/explorer/socialiser/killer model against actual player populations — and remain unread; this is also the most direct route to answering whether Bartle's type-ecology has ever been fit quantitatively to real telemetry, an open problem the note has not yet resolved.
