The interaction membrane is Erving Goffman's term for the border around a bout of play, taken up by Jaakko Stenros as the load-bearing concept in his reconciliation of the magic-circle debate. Goffman's own text was not read directly for this dossier; the concept is known here entirely through Stenros's citation and quotation of it. As Stenros relays it, the border "around play is permeable; the wider world passes through more than just application of the transformation rules... it is also possible for the external elements to endanger the transformation rules and thus the encounter itself. It is possible for play to be collapsed by external events." The decisive move in this formulation is treating the boundary as a membrane rather than a valve: it does not simply decide what is let in and what is kept out, it applies "rules of transformation" to whatever crosses it, converting outside material into a form the play can incorporate — and it can, if the pressure from either side of it is great enough, fail outright, ending the play rather than merely admitting an intrusion into it.

This is the concept Stenros treats as doing the real work that the phrase "magic circle" is loosely used to cover, and it underlies his broader argument that persistent worlds specifically make membrane failure visible in ways that shorter, session-bounded games do not. Two documented cases illustrate collapse under pressure from opposite directions. The 2005 Corrupted Blood incident in *World of Warcraft* — a contained boss debuff that leaked into the general population and spread player to player across the persistent world — is a case of the transformation rules being overwhelmed from inside the game's own systems, one legible enough to real epidemiology that Eric Lofgren and Nina Fefferman argued in *The Lancet Infectious Diseases* that virtual worlds could serve as a new kind of epidemiological laboratory precisely because the world did not reset. The 2006 "Serenity Now bombs a World of Warcraft funeral" incident, by contrast, is a case of a real bereavement passing into a game space and a game raid passing back out into real grief and controversy; this glossary treats that episode under the entry on boundary-work.

Independently of Goffman, Edward Castronova reaches for the same image in describing the boundary of a synthetic world, calling it, in language reported through Stenros's own citation of Castronova, a "porous membrane" crossed constantly in both directions, carrying real attitudes and consequences with it rather than filtering them out — the economic register of the same phenomenon this glossary treats under the almost-magic circle. That two authors working from sociology of interaction and from economics respectively converge on "membrane" as the operative word is treated in the section's own reading, not asserted by either source, as evidence that the image names something real about how persistent worlds' boundaries actually behave, rather than being a coincidence of vocabulary.
