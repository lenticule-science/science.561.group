## Scope

Document and theorize how persistent worlds govern themselves — the
legislatures, property regimes, courts, and enforcement institutions that a
game without an ending is forced to grow, and the standing constitutional
defect (the operator-sovereign) under which all of them sit.

## Motivating questions

1. What happens when the settlement of disputes cannot be deferred to the
   game's end, because there isn't one? What institutions accrete, in what
   order, and under what pressure?
2. The god problem: what actually restrains an operator no one can depose?
   (Grimmelmann's catalogue: exit, the investor analogy, constitutional
   self-binding.)
3. Can players legislate the arena they are playing in — and what are the
   documented outcomes when they try? (LambdaMOO's full arc; A Tale in the
   Desert's walked-back lawmaking; EVE's advisory-only CSM.)
4. What is property in a world one party can rewrite by patch? (Fairfield's
   rivalrous/persistent/interconnected triple; the missing case law.)
5. What is crime in a world where the wrongs and the sanctions are the same
   set of acts, and expulsion is impossible? (Griefing's community-relative
   definition; Bungle's return as Dr. Jest.)

## Objects of study

LambdaMOO's primary constitutional documents; the virtual-law literature
(Lessig, Lastowka and Hunter, Fairfield, Grimmelmann); EVE Online's CSM and
tolerated-scam jurisprudence; griefing taxonomies and their enforcement;
guilds as the effective locus of norm-enforcement; Ostrom-framework empirics
across game communities.

## State of knowledge (2026-08-22 sweep)

- **The LambdaMOO arc, from primary documents** (read verbatim, still hosted
  in the MOO's own help system): LTAND (Dec 1992) — wizards withdraw from
  "the discipline/manners/arbitration business"; the Bungle crisis (Mar 1993)
  forces the vacuum closed — no consensus, one wizard's unilateral toading,
  then the petition-and-binding-ballot system; LTAND II (May 1996) — "The
  line between 'technical' and 'social' is not a clear one, and never can
  be," with an explicit reserved veto over any passed ballot and a standing
  shutdown-petition release valve; and the ratifying ballot, 321 for, 111
  against, 272 abstaining — a legislature voting on the legitimacy of its own
  executive's self-coup, count preserved.
- **Lessig** (chapter read): "From a space self-regulated to a space
  regulated by self"; code as one of four regulators; the MOO as the case
  where code implements the outcome of a vote.
- **Lastowka and Hunter** (read): the abdication "was not as absolute as it
  may have seemed" — implementing ballots as code kept the wizards wizards;
  Habitat's 1986 recoding of theft and murder as the earliest
  legislation-by-patch.
- **Fairfield** (read): virtual property as rivalrous, persistent,
  interconnected code; courts over legislatures, on public-choice grounds.
- **Grimmelmann** (read): the god problem and its three functioning
  restraints; griefing's definitional futility; guilds as the real
  enforcement institution; A Tale in the Desert's lawmaking walked back by
  its sovereign (secondhand via his footnote).
- **EVE** (CSM white paper and Brooks read): the sovereign's own admission
  that accountability "cannot be emulated"; crime declared out of
  jurisdiction ("under no circumstances will the legislator interfere");
  the tolerated-scam culture and the private counterintelligence institutions
  that grow in the gap; Carter's "ruthless game" via Brooks.
- **The empirical regularity** (Frey et al. read): across ~80,000 communities
  on three platforms, institutional formalization rises with age, size, and
  engagement, converging on weak norms over strong requirements; Ostrom and
  Basurto's seven-default "state of nature" as the ungoverned baseline.

## Obsmetrological bridge

All graded **rhyme**, with the critic's flags carried: the LambdaMOO arc is
settlement-authority case law — an operator handing the closure operator to
an open community process, the process failing to converge on a live breach,
and a privileged fixed point being reasserted from outside the normal
closure, itself then settled by the community's machinery. The god problem
translated as the operator sitting genuinely outside the arena with the power
to rewrite its event structure mid-play — breaking the Player/Opponent
symmetry the dualizing involution presumes — is this section's translation,
not Grimmelmann's claim (he frames it as sovereignty and rule of law).
Griefing's community-relative definition reads as a charge resolvable only
through the community's own settling, never computable from the move alone.
The Ostrom "state of nature" as the bare, uncodec'd arena is flagged for
re-verification against Ostrom's primary text.

## Open problems

- A formal account of *amendment*: what operation on an arena is "the players
  changed the rules from inside," and how does it differ from the operator's
  patch? (The petition/ballot system is the concrete instance to model.)
- Whether the enforcement degeneracy (sanctions = wrongs; no expulsion) is a
  theorem about persistent worlds or a contingency of the studied cases.
- The gap between advisory and binding player institutions: every studied
  legislature is either advisory (CSM), revocable (LambdaMOO), or walked back
  (A Tale in the Desert) — is a binding one possible under the god problem
  at all, short of player-hosted worlds?

## Reading debts

Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* in primary text; Foo and Koivisto's griefing
paper in primary text (taxonomy currently secondhand); Carter's *Treacherous
Play*; Bragg v. Linden Research (E.D. Pa. 2007) — the missing adjudicated
case law; A Tale in the Desert's own lawmaking documents; Consalvo's

scam rulings if one exists.

## Annotated sources

Annotated sources on governance, property, and enforcement in persistent worlds, from LambdaMOO's constitutional arc to EVE Online's advisory council and the cross-platform empirics of institutional growth.


### Sources

## Brooks, Ian G.

**Brooks, Ian G. (2017). "Is Betrayal in EVE Online Unethical?" *Journal of Virtual Worlds Research* 10:3 (special EVE Online issue, guest eds. Kelly Bergstrom & Marcus Carter).**
URL: https://jvwr-ojs-utexas.tdl.org/jvwr/article/view/7259/6412
Status: read in full.
Brooks argues, against the game's own stated culture, that betraying a genuine in-game friendship in EVE Online is unethical even though CCP's rules and public statements permit and reward it, engaging Huizinga's magic circle and Marcus Carter's "ruthless game" and "treacherous play" concepts along the way. Its load-bearing content for this note is institutional rather than ethical: Brooks quotes a CCP developer stating plainly that "scamming and unethical behavior...is not only allowed, it is encouraged and rewarded by the game mechanics," and documents EVE University's volunteer "Personnel Department," an out-of-game counterintelligence institution built entirely by players to vet applicants against infiltration. That institution is the note's clearest documented case of a private, emergent governance body growing inside a still-running game to manage a harm the operator has explicitly declared out of its jurisdiction. Brooks's discussion of roleplayers and non-roleplayers disagreeing about where the game's boundary is drawn — via Ashley John Craft's observation that the two groups are, in an important sense, "not playing the same game" — is a sourced instance of boundary ambiguity worth checking against the note's other codec-boundary material rather than accepted outright, since Brooks frames it as a question of moral responsibility, not formal obstruction.

## Carter, Marcus

**Carter, Marcus (2022). *Treacherous Play*. MIT Press.**
URL: not resolved in the source material (nominally open access; blocked by a Cloudflare bot-challenge before it could be fetched).
Status: reported at second hand.
Carter's book develops the "ruthless game" and "treacherous play" concepts used throughout this note's EVE material — the idea that some games lawfully afford players the opportunity to inflict serious, real consequences on one another, and that in EVE specifically betrayal "bears no immoral, subversive, transgressive, or anti-social qualities" given the game's explicit rules and culture. Everything attributed to Carter here is mediated through Ian Brooks's citations of him in "Is Betrayal in EVE Online Unethical?" and a secondhand Sydney Business Insights interview summary; the book's own text has not been read. Because Carter's framing does much of the argumentative work behind the note's tolerated-scam material, this is one of the more consequential reading debts in the bibliography, and no claim sourced only to Carter should be treated as more than provisionally established until the primary text is available.

## Council of Stellar Management White Paper (CCP Games)

**CCP Games (periodically updated). *Council of Stellar Management White Paper*.**
URL: https://cdn1.eveonline.com/community/csm/CSM-WHITEPAPER.pdf
Status: read in full.
This is CCP's own constitutional and institutional document for the Council of Stellar Management, EVE Online's player advisory body: its mission, the rights EVE citizenship is said to confer, the mechanics of its Single Transferable Vote elections, and CCP's official institutional history from a 2001 idea through a hand-picked 2003 council to the elected body running today. Its load-bearing content is a direct admission of the god problem from the sovereign's own mouth: "Because EVE is a virtual society that relies on the technical support of CCP, this model cannot be emulated," referring to term-limited or recallable democratic government. The same document states that CCP treats in-world "crimes" — theft, fraud, "murder" — as entirely outside its jurisdiction so long as the EULA's meta-law is respected, which is the constitutional basis for EVE's tolerated-scam culture documented elsewhere in this bibliography. The note needs this document as its primary evidence that an operator can state the god problem explicitly, in its own official voice, rather than have it inferred by outside scholars.

## Curtis, Pavel ("Haakon")

**Curtis, Pavel, writing as "Haakon" (1992, 1996). "LambdaMOO Takes a New Direction" (LTAND, Dec. 9, 1992); "LambdaMOO Takes Another Direction" (LTAND II, May 16, 1996); the ratifying ballot "LTAD-Legality" (May 1996). Preserved verbatim in LambdaMOO's in-world help system.**
URL: https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/classes/AY2001/cs6470_fall/LTAND.html
Status: read in full.
These are the note's primary constitutional documents: Curtis's 1992 announcement that LambdaMOO's wizards would withdraw from social governance and become pure technicians, on the ground that the society had "left the nest" and no longer needed "wizard-mothers"; the 1996 reversal reintroducing wizardly fiat, on the ground that "the line between 'technical' and 'social' is not a clear one, and never can be"; and the community's own ratifying vote on that reversal, closing 321 in favor, 111 against, 272 abstaining. The 1996 reversal is not a simple return to autocracy — it reserves an explicit veto over any passed ballot and any Arbitrator decision that impairs the wizards' work, and it installs a standing shutdown-petition mechanism as a release valve. The note needs these documents as ground truth: every other source in this bibliography narrates or interprets this arc, and having read the documents directly means each later paraphrase can be checked against Curtis's own words.

## Fairfield, Joshua A. T.

**Fairfield, Joshua A. T. (2005). "Virtual Property." *Boston University Law Review* 85: 1047–1102.**
URL: https://www.bu.edu/law/journals-archive/bulr/volume85n4/FAIRFIELD.pdf
Status: read in full.
Fairfield argues that virtual objects with three properties — rivalrous, persistent, and interconnected — should be governed by common-law property doctrine rather than left to contract law, defining the class precisely: "If one person owns and controls them, others do not... Unlike the software on your computer, they do not go away when you turn your computer off... Other people can interact with them." His institutional argument is the note's load-bearing content: courts, not legislatures, should develop this doctrine, because concentrated intellectual-property interests reliably defeat a diffuse population of players in any legislative arena, and because contract law only allocates costs between the contracting parties while property law manages externalities imposed on third parties. The note needs Fairfield as the sharpest available statement of the courts-versus-legislatures institutional question, a question that becomes considerably more pointed once read against how fragile and revocable every documented player legislature in this bibliography has actually turned out to be.

## Foo, Choon Yang & Koivisto, Elina M. I.

**Foo, Choon Yang & Koivisto, Elina M. I. (2004). "Defining Grief Play in MMORPGs." Proceedings of ACM Advances in Computer Entertainment (ACE) 2004.**
URL: not resolved in the source material (behind an ACM Digital Library / Cloudflare paywall the note could not pass).
Status: reported at second hand.
This paper is credited in secondary literature with the field's foundational four-category taxonomy of griefing: harassment (impeding another's activity), power imposition (exploiting a weakness to assert dominance), scamming (cheating others of money, items, or information), and greed play (acting purely for personal benefit at others' expense, including unintentional harm). The taxonomy as reported here comes from cross-checked secondary sources rather than the paper's own text, and has not been verified against its original wording or methodology. The note needs this taxonomy as the standard reference point against which Grimmelmann's later, deliberately anti-taxonomic definition of griefing ("any activity that genuinely bothers most players") is implicitly arguing, but any claim about Foo and Koivisto's specific framing beyond the four category names should be treated as unconfirmed.

## Frey, Seth, Zhong, Qiankun, Bulat, Beril, Weisman, William D., Liu, Caitlyn, Fujimoto, Stephen, Wang, Hannah M., & Schweik, Charles M.

**Frey, Seth, Zhong, Qiankun, Bulat, Beril, Weisman, William D., Liu, Caitlyn, Fujimoto, Stephen, Wang, Hannah M., & Schweik, Charles M. (2022). "Governing Online Goods: Maturity and Formalization in Minecraft, Reddit, and World of Warcraft Communities." *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction* (CSCW). arXiv:2202.01317.**
URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.01317
Status: read in full.
This paper applies Elinor Ostrom's Institutional Analysis and Development framework quantitatively across roughly 80,000 online communities on three unrelated platforms — 9,800 Minecraft servers, 67,000 subreddits, and 45 private World of Warcraft servers — using Ostrom and Basurto's formal typology of regulatory versus constitutive, and strategy/norm/requirement, institutional statements. Its two headline findings are that institutional formalization rises with a community's age, population, and engagement across all three platforms, and that communities independently converge on weak norms over strong requirements. The paper also reproduces Ostrom and Basurto's seven-item "state of nature" table describing the ungoverned baseline any community starts from before growing institutions. The note needs this paper as its only cross-platform, quantitative evidence that institutional accretion is a general pattern rather than an artifact particular to LambdaMOO or EVE, though its use of Ostrom is itself secondhand relative to her own primary text — see Reading debts below.

## Grimmelmann, James

**Grimmelmann, James (2004). "Virtual Worlds as Comparative Law." *New York Law School Law Review* 49:1, 147–184.**
URL: https://james.grimmelmann.net/files/articles/virtual-comparative.pdf
Status: read in full.
Grimmelmann treats virtual worlds as genuine legal systems worth comparative study in their own right, covering virtual property, the near-absence of virtual contract, and — most load-bearing for this note — "The God Problem," his account of what restrains a designer-sovereign no one can vote out: market exit, an international-law investor analogy, and voluntary constitutional self-binding through published intervention policies. He gives the field's sharpest treatment of griefing as an institutional rather than definitional problem, quoting Hunter and Lastowka's unpublished "Virtual Crimes" definition — "any activity that genuinely bothers most players of the game in question" — and arguing this renders any general doctrine of virtual criminal law futile, because the acts to be deterred and the only available sanctions are drawn from the identical set. His claim that "whoever understands the nature of guilds will understand the nature of community and community norm-enforcement in modern virtual worlds" identifies the field's real locus of enforcement power. A footnote on *A Tale in the Desert*'s referendum-based player lawmaking, later walked back by its own designers, is the note's only current source on that episode and has not been independently verified against the game's own primary rulebook.

## Lastowka, F. Gregory & Hunter, Dan

**Lastowka, F. Gregory & Hunter, Dan (2004). "The Laws of the Virtual Worlds." *California Law Review* 92:1, 1–73, esp. Part III, "The Rights of Wizards and Cyborgs."**
URL: https://lawcat.berkeley.edu/record/1119331?v=pdf
Status: read in full.
Lastowka and Hunter argue that virtual worlds are best understood by asking what real-world legal categories — property, crime, rights, sovereignty — actually map onto in-world practice, rather than dismissing virtual harms as "just pixels." Part III narrates LambdaMOO's abdication as "a reverse coup d'état," and makes the qualifying point the note treats as load-bearing: the abdication "was not as absolute as it may have seemed," because the wizards retained the power to implement ballot outcomes as coded law, so "no matter what they claimed, [they] were still wizards." They also discuss Habitat's 1986 recoding of theft and murder in response to player complaints, an earlier and structurally different instance of in-world rule-change than LambdaMOO's ballot-based legislation — legislation by patch rather than by vote. The note needs this article as the source of its working vocabulary for designer power and its incompleteness, and as the earliest documented case of legislation-by-patch to set against LambdaMOO's contrasting mechanism.

## Lessig, Lawrence

**Lessig, Lawrence (2006). *Code: Version 2.0*. Basic Books. Chapter "cyberspaces," section "LambdaMOO," pp. 97–114.**
URL: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fd/Code_v2.pdf
Status: read in substantial part (the cited chapter, pp. 97–114, was read in full; the rest of the book was not consulted for this note).
This chapter is Lessig's canonical "code is law" treatment of LambdaMOO, narrating the Mr. Bungle incident, the community's split between vigilante and democratic responses, wizard TomTraceback's unilateral toading of Bungle, and the wizards' subsequent unilateral imposition of ballot-based democracy from May 1, 1993. Its load-bearing line is Lessig's own gloss on that democracy: "LambdaMOO's move to self-government... was not just an achievement. It was also a failure... From a space self-regulated to a space regulated by self." The chapter situates LambdaMOO within Lessig's broader four-regulator argument (law, norms, market, and code, or architecture), reading the MOO as the case where code literally implements the outcome of a political vote — quoting Jennifer Mnookin's phrase "politics implemented through technology." The note needs this chapter as the connective narrative between LambdaMOO's bare primary documents and the legal scholarship that followed, and as the source of the field's most-quoted single sentence about the episode.

## Reading debts

Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for
Collective Action* (1990) has never been read in its own primary text by
this note; everything currently known about her Institutional Analysis
and Development framework and its "state of nature" table comes filtered
through Frey et al.'s 2022 application of it, and the note's reading of
that table as a formalization of a bare, uncodec'd arena has been flagged as
needing re-verification against Ostrom's own text before it is leaned on
further.

Foo and Koivisto's "Defining Grief Play in MMORPGs" (2004) sits behind an
ACM Digital Library paywall the note could not pass; its four-category
griefing taxonomy is reported here only through cross-checked secondary
sources.

Marcus Carter's *Treacherous Play* (MIT Press, 2022) is nominally open
access but was blocked by a Cloudflare bot-challenge; everything attributed
to it in this bibliography is mediated through Ian Brooks's citations and a
secondhand interview summary, not the book's own text.

Bragg v. Linden Research, Inc. (E.D. Pa. 2007) — the landmark United States
case on virtual-property seizure and arbitration clauses in Second Life —
has not been consulted at all. This is a significant gap: it is the one
piece of actually adjudicated case law bearing directly on the
courts-versus-legislatures argument Fairfield and Lastowka and Hunter make
on purely theoretical grounds.

*A Tale in the Desert*'s own lawmaking and referendum documents have not
been fetched or read; the note's only knowledge of that game's
walked-back player legislature comes from a single footnote in Grimmelmann.

Mia Consalvo's *Cheating: Gaining Advantage in Videogames* (MIT Press, 2007)
has not been consulted, despite being a natural companion to this note's
griefing-as-community-adjudicated-charge material.

A dedicated jurisprudential history of EVE Online's specific scam rulings —
a documented sequence of CCP decisions distinguishing tolerated scams from
banned ones, if one has been written — was not located and remains an open
search.
