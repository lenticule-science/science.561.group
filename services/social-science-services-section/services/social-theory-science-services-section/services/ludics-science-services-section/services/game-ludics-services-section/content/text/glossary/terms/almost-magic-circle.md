The almost-magic circle is Edward Castronova's term, from the chapter of that name in *Synthetic Worlds* (2005), for the boundary of a persistent online world understood as real but deliberately incomplete. Where the classical magic circle is imagined as a sealed enclosure, Castronova argues the membrane around a synthetic world is "definitely there but also definitely porous, and this is by choice of the users" — its apparent purpose being to retain "all that is good about the fantasy atmosphere of the synthetic world, while giving users the maximum amount of freedom to manipulate their involvement with them." This entry rests on a single verbatim passage retrieved through secondary citation and cross-checked across independent sources rather than on a direct reading of the chapter, which was not accessible to the underlying research; the phrase and its content should be treated with that lower confidence in mind, though the passage itself is consistently and identically quoted wherever it appears.

The mechanism Castronova identifies for the boundary's porousness is economic. Real-money markets for virtual goods — originally improvised through eBay, later formalized into dedicated exchanges — mean that "every good inside the membrane [is] just as real as the goods outside of it," so that a world's fantasy content and a player's ordinary income become convertible into one another at a market rate. Richard Heeks's later economic study of gold farming (2010) takes this argument to what it treats as its logical conclusion, tracing gold farming as the reproduction of an entire capitalist production chain across the membrane — wage labor, offshoring, automation, brokerage, and currency devaluation — and concluding that "there is no 'real economics' to counterpose against a 'virtual economics;' there is just 'economics.'" The almost-magic circle is accordingly the conceptual hinge between this glossary's entry on the magic circle generally and its entries on gold farming and real-money trading specifically: it is the porous economic boundary that those two practices are documented as crossing continuously in both directions.

Castronova's image sits alongside, and independently arrives at similar language to, Jaakko Stenros's report that Castronova elsewhere preferred the word "membrane" outright for the barrier separating synthetic worlds from the everyday one, and to Goffman's interaction membrane as taken up by Stenros for the magic circle's transformation-rather-than-exclusion behavior. The three formulations were arrived at from different starting points — economics, ethnography of play, and sociology of interaction respectively — which is some evidence that the porousness they each describe is a real property of persistent worlds rather than an artifact of any one author's vocabulary, though this reading is this glossary's own synthesis rather than a claim made by any single source.
