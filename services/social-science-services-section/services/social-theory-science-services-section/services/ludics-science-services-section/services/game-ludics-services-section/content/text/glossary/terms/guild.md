A guild, in the sense this literature uses the term, is a player-formed organization that a persistent world's own software grants little or no formal power, but that becomes the practical seat of governance anyway. James Grimmelmann's "Virtual Worlds as Comparative Law" (2004) states the claim directly: "whoever understands the nature of guilds will understand the nature of community and community norm-enforcement in modern virtual worlds." He offers guilds as his account of who actually enforces norms in worlds where formal governance is thin, advisory, or absent — the condition his own god problem names — and where griefing's community-relative definition means there is no formal doctrine to appeal to instead.

EVE Online supplies a documented instance of the pattern beyond Grimmelmann's own examples. Ian G. Brooks's "Is Betrayal in EVE Online Unethical?" (2017) reports that EVE University, a large player-run training corporation, maintains a volunteer "Personnel Department" doing out-of-game counterintelligence work — vetting applicants against infiltration by hostile organizations. This is exactly the pattern Grimmelmann describes: a private, emergent, sub-state institution, built by players entirely inside the still-running game, managing a category of harm — betrayal and scamming — that CCP Games has explicitly declared outside its own jurisdiction (see ruthless-game).

Guilds sit at the informal end of a spectrum this literature also documents at its formal end. EVE's council-of-stellar-management is an elected, CCP-recognized advisory body with published election mechanics; EVE University's Personnel Department has no charter from the operator at all — it exists purely because players built it in the gap CCP's non-intervention leaves. Both are responses to the same underlying condition, the god problem: an operator no citizen can vote out, and a citizenry left to build its own institutions inside whatever space that leaves open.

Grimmelmann's discussion places guilds directly downstream of griefing's own definitional futility: because no general doctrine can identify an act as griefing independent of the affected community's own judgment, guild-level informal enforcement — reputation, blacklisting, vouching — ends up doing the adjudicating work that no formal rule, whether wizardly fiat or a petition-and-ballot vote, can settle in advance.
