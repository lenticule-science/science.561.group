A MUD, in the sense fixed by this literature, is Richard Bartle's own closing definition in "Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades": "a (text-based) multi-user virtual world" in which players act, in real time, on and against each other and the world itself. Bartle's 1996 paper treats this as a strict definition rather than a loose label — losing any one element (multiple simultaneous users, a shared persistent world rather than an inert simulation, players acting rather than merely interacting, or the world itself rather than a bare chatline) collapses the form into something else entirely.

Julian Dibbell's 1993 "A Rape in Cyberspace" gives the field's most-quoted description of what a MUD is from the inside, describing LambdaMOO as "a kind of database especially designed to give users the vivid impression of moving through a physical space that in reality exists only as words." The description matters because it names the split at the center of Dibbell's whole essay: a MUD's objects and locations are, mechanically, database entries and text, but the experience of moving through them is treated by their inhabitants as an experience of place — which is why an assault narrated through such an object could register as a real-life fact rather than as a fiction with no purchase on anyone.

MUDs are the ancestral case for several other entries in this glossary. Bartle's four player-types were derived from, and named for, MUD populations specifically; his change-persistence-grid classifies MUD and post-MUD codebases, from low-change, low-persistence AberMUDs through to the high-change, high-persistence MOOs, of which LambdaMOO is his paradigm example (see moo); and Dibbell's Bungle case, which grounds the wizard entry, is set on that same MOO-family MUD. This entry does not attempt to reconstruct the history of MUD1 or the coinage of the term itself: the section's own reading notes flag Bartle's biographical and design-history material in *Designing Virtual Worlds* as substantially unread beyond its definitional and identity chapters, so any claim about the term's origin as a named form would exceed what has actually been verified here.
