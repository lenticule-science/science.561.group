Toading is LambdaMOO community slang for a wizard's deletion of a player's character — recoding the account into the game's placeholder object-class for a removed player, colloquially a "toad." It names the rawest exercise of administrative power available on the MOO: not a sanction voted on or adjudicated, but a single wizard's keystroke, exercised outside any authorized process.

The case that fixes the term's meaning in this literature is the March 1993 Mr. Bungle "virtual rape" incident, narrated at length in Lawrence Lessig's Code: Version 2.0 (2006), which quotes Julian Dibbell's account of the episode. A roughly three-hour community meeting split between a vigilante faction — "LambdaMOO did not need a state; it needed a few good vigilantes" — and a democracy faction proposing government-by-ballot with no constitutional limit on what such balloting could decide. Neither position won outright. Into that impasse, wizard TomTraceback acted alone: he toaded Bungle's account, unilaterally, without waiting for either faction's proposed process to resolve.

The consequence is what makes the case load-bearing for this section rather than merely notorious. Having watched a citizen act alone rather than through any collective mechanism, the wizards responded not by formalizing vigilante toading as policy but by unilaterally imposing balloting themselves, effective May 1, 1993: any matter could thereafter be decided by ballot, any proposition drawing twice as many votes for as against became binding — see petition-and-ballot. Lessig's own gloss on the sequence is the load-bearing line of his whole LambdaMOO discussion: the resulting move to democracy "was not just an achievement. It was also a failure. The space had failed... to engender values in its population sufficient to avoid just the sort of evil Bungle had perpetrated... From a space self-regulated to a space regulated by self."

Toading sits at the origin point of two neighboring terms this section documents. It is the concrete, pre-legislative instance of code-is-law: an act of pure administrative fiat, executed in code, prior to any ballot mechanism existing to authorize or constrain it. And Bungle's underlying conduct is this literature's paradigm griefing case — the act that exposed how far short existing norms fell of resolving what "genuinely bother[ed] most players of the game in question," in James Grimmelmann's later phrase, forcing the community to grow an institution rather than simply appeal to one that already existed.
