The god problem is James Grimmelmann's name, coined in Part V of "Virtual Worlds as Comparative Law" (New York Law School Law Review 49, 2004), for the fact that a persistent world's designer-operator cannot be removed by any process available to the people who play there. Where a Lockean political theory of legitimate government presumes the possibility of revolution or a vote of no confidence, Grimmelmann writes plainly that "there is no way to depose the designers of a game." Whatever checks exist on that power have to be voluntary, partial, or indirect, because none of them can be a ballot cast against the operator itself.

Grimmelmann catalogues three restraints that function anyway, none of them constitutional in the ordinary sense. The first is market exit: players quitting, which operates as a continuous signal rather than a binary referendum, and which happens entirely outside the game's own interface, since, as he puts it, "quit" is not really a command in the game interface. The second is an international-law analogy: players resemble foreign investors who cannot repatriate their capital, and can only find a buyer to take over their position — a formalization of Julian Dibbell's observation about secondary markets in avatars and accounts. The third is a due-process or constitutional analogy: designers voluntarily bind their own discretion by publishing rules about what conduct they will and will not intervene on — offensive speech, commercialization, griefing — as a legitimating technique, echoing administrative-law guidance documents rather than enacted law.

EVE Online's own constitutional document supplies the sharpest confirmation of the diagnosis, offered from the sovereign's own mouth rather than a critic's. CCP Games' Council of Stellar Management White Paper states outright: "in most democratic models, government legislators can either be replaced by popular vote or are limited by finite term durations. Because EVE is a virtual society that relies on the technical support of CCP, this model cannot be emulated." The resulting body, the council-of-stellar-management, is elected and consulted but explicitly advisory — CCP's own institutional answer to a problem it names in almost exactly Grimmelmann's terms, thirteen years and one industry apart.

This section reads the god problem as a case where the game's Player and Opponent roles, ordinarily treated as symmetric inside one shared event structure, come apart: in a persistent world the operator sits genuinely outside the arena, holding the power to rewrite the event structure itself mid-play, rather than occupying a dual position within it. That translation is this section's own reading, not a claim Grimmelmann makes; he frames the problem in the vocabulary of sovereignty and rule of law, not of game-theoretic symmetry. The literature's other documented answers to the god problem sit alongside his three restraints: LambdaMOO's petition-and-ballot system is one attempt to grow a legislature inside the arena itself, and Grimmelmann's own answer to who actually enforces norms day to day, absent any of this, is guild — player organizations the software barely formalizes but that become the practical locus of decision-making power.
