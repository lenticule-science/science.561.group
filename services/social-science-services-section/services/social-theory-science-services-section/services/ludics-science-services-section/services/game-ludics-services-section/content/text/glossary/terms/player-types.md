Player types is the standard name for the taxonomy Richard Bartle introduced in his 1996 "Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs": four kinds of MUD player — Achievers, Explorers, Socialisers, and Killers — distinguished by where they sit on two axes, one running from acting on the world to interacting with other players, the other running from a focus on the world itself to a focus on other players. Achievers, in Bartle's description, "regard points-gathering and rising in levels as their main goal, and all is ultimately subservient to this"; Killers require a real emotional reaction from a real opponent to mean anything, since "the more massive the distress caused, the greater the killer's joy at having caused it" — mere arcade-style scoring does not satisfy them.

The taxonomy is often read as a static classification, but Bartle's own argument is dynamical: the four populations are locked into feedback loops that only a persistent, continuously running population can produce. "Increasing the number of achievers will, over time, increase the number of killers in a typically Malthusian fashion." Socialisers show both a positive loop (more attract more) and a negative one (they also attract killers, risking "a downward spiral in numbers"). Explorers are comparatively stable — "only huge numbers of killers will reduce" them — and Bartle observes that deliberately increasing the explorer population is "the only way to reduce the number of killers without also reducing the player numbers in other groups." From these dynamics Bartle identifies three stable MUD configurations: a killer/achiever-dominated "Type 1," a pure-socialiser "Type 2," and a balanced "Type 3" that is "extraordinarily difficult to achieve and maintain," since sustaining it requires ongoing population management that "most MUD administrators" lack.

Bartle's later work in *Designing Virtual Worlds* folds the taxonomy into his account of identity, arguing that all four types are ultimately in service of identity-construction at different levels of investment: "It's why achievers achieve, explorers explore, socializers socialize, and killers... batter you with a stick" (see avatar, persona). The type ecology is, in this literature, one of the clearest instances of a population dynamic that exists only because a world is persistent — none of the described feedback loops, breeding, driving-off, or starving-out, makes sense for a population that resets between sessions, which is why this glossary treats player types as belonging as much to persistent-world as to any narrower theory of play motivation.
