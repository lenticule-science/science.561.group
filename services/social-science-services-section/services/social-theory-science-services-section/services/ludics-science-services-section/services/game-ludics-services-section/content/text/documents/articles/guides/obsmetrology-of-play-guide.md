# A Guide to the Measurement of Play

## 1. The story of the field

A persistent game world does not get built, shipped, and left alone. Someone keeps watching it, and keeps adjusting it in response to what the watching finds. This note's literature is five conversations converging on that fact from different directions — economists treating a game as a country, a studio watching its own broken economy in real time, a company hiring an economist to watch a game like a nation, data scientists watching player behavior at population scale, and critics asking whether the arrangement is fair to the people being watched.

The earliest and most concrete conversation begins with Zachary Booth Simpson's 1999 report on Ultima Online's economy. Simpson was not an outside analyst but an Origin employee writing candidly about his own team's mistakes while they were still being fixed. UO's original economy was a *closed loop* — resources flowed from an abstract pool into circulation and, in theory, back out again, on the assumption that a fixed quantity of stuff would prevent inflation. It failed, but not through inflation. Resources simply stopped moving: players hoarded everything, the pool at the top dried up, and, in Simpson's words, "the resources always flowed into inventory and just sat there... the result: the world became boring and the players began to complain loudly." That sentence is the seed of the note's standing conjecture — that this boredom was not a content problem but a *settling* problem, an economy reaching a stable configuration nobody wanted.

Simpson's fix gave the field its vocabulary. A *faucet* creates resources from nothing; a *drain* destroys them rather than recirculating them; a *sink* is a drain engineered on purpose to absorb a specific surplus. He insists the hoarding problem was political, not technical: designers knew how to fix it — taxes, decay, maintenance costs — but "many of these drains feel too much like real world 'work' and are therefore politically unacceptable." A second failure compounds this: UO's NPC shopkeepers ran a genuine simulated supply-and-demand algorithm, and it collapsed too, because players had a "non-demand incentive to produce" — skills improved through use regardless of demand, so overproduction crashed prices. Verdict: "central planning doesn't work." His closing move matters most here: rather than another one-time fix, he proposes turning the operator into a *standing observer* — tracking trades, vendor stock, inflow, outflow, churn, even housing occupancy, continuously.

Two years later, Edward Castronova applied the same move — treat a game world as an economy worth measuring — to EverQuest's Norrath, writing it up as first-person ethnography ("Journal entry, 18 April. I have called my avatar 'Alaniel.'..."). His paper computes *national accounts* for a fictional country: Norrath's exchange rate beat the yen and the lira; its GNP per capita, at $2,266, made it "the 77th richest country in the world, roughly equal to Russia," richer than China or India; its wealth inequality exceeded any real post-industrial society. His justification is methodological: "economists believe that it is the practical actions of people, and not abstract arguments, that determine the social value of things." He defines a virtual world by interactivity, physicality (scarcity), and — the hinge this note turns on — persistence: "the note continues to run whether anyone is using it or not." A world that keeps running without you is a world somebody has to watch continuously, because nobody experiences the whole of it at once.

Vili Lehdonvirta and Edward Castronova's later textbook, *Virtual Economies: Design and Analysis*, generalizes this into a field — though this note has it only secondhand, through a review and quotations reproduced in a later thesis. Its most quoted line: "Both the US Dollar and World of Warcraft coins are fiat money," licensing real monetary-policy tools on game economies. But it also names the disanalogy that keeps game economies from being small countries: a game economy is designed to "attract users, satisfy them and make the publisher... money," and engineered scarcity — travel time, cooldowns, drop rates — is not friction to optimize away. It is the product.

Where Simpson and Castronova measure a world after the fact, EVE Online's 2007 hire of Dr. Eyjólfur Guðmundsson shows a studio committing to measurement as permanent infrastructure. CCP's press release frames this in exactly observe-and-steer terms: CEO Hilmar Pétursson said, "Just as entrepreneurs and executives rely on real-world economic indicators, EVE Online players need timely information and analysis of the in-game economy," and Guðmundsson put the transparency case plainly: "By ensuring everyone has access to the same data, we will enhance the player experience and facilitate economic stability in EVE." His eleven-person unit published Quarterly Economic Newsletters running to fifty pages, covering roughly 300,000 players. The same apparatus doubled as security instrumentation: tools tracking price trends also caught that bots — 2% of players — were generating 30% of server CPU load. The newsletter's own cadence was later throttled, quarterly to monthly: standing observation has a cost the operator keeps deciding whether to keep paying.

The fourth conversation shifts the object of observation from market to player. Anders Drachen's work on behavioral telemetry asks what *kinds of players* the flow reveals. The founding 2009 study — Drachen, Canossa and Yannakakis on Tomb Raider: Underworld — was not read directly for this note, only reconstructed through a 2014 follow-up, so treat its details as secondhand. That follow-up reports the 2009 paper "classified the behavior of 1365 players... using Self-Organizing Networks and k-means clustering, locating four classes of user behavior that encompassed over 90 percent of the users," classes "translated into design terminology for use by the game's developers." The field's self-definition, from the paper read in full: "Any action the player (user) takes while playing can potentially be recorded and stored." Two growth drivers are named: telemetry now reaches entire populations rather than lab samples, and F2P models *require* "constant monitoring and evaluation of the player population behavior in order to drive revenue." Standing observation stopped being optional once the business model stopped being a one-time sale.

The same paper contributes a methodological caution worth treating as load-bearing: comparing k-means, NMF, PCA, and Archetypal Analysis on six years of WoW telemetry, it finds k-means and Archetypal Analysis produce human-legible player types ("casual player," "hardcore player"), while PCA and NMF produce statistically valid factorizations sometimes uninterpretable or corresponding to game-impossible behaviors, like a player losing levels. The lesson: the choice of observational apparatus determines whether what comes out is a claim about the world at all, independent of the math's validity.

The fifth conversation is the field's own conscience. José Zagal, Staffan Björk and Chris Lewis's 2013 paper coined "dark game design patterns" — designs "used intentionally by a game creator to cause negative experiences for players which are against their best interests and likely to happen without their consent." This note never obtained that paper's primary text; its definition and taxonomy — three families, patterns increasing time spent, money spent, and exploiting social networks — come reconstructed through two secondary sources quoting it verbatim. Faltin Karlsen's 2016 follow-up, read in full, applies the taxonomy to WoW and FarmVille 2, tying their different cadences to "the different economical logics of the games, e.g. subscription versus micro transactions" — noting players may experience these patterns as loyalty-building rather than exploitative.

The sharpest turn is Deterding, Stenros and Montola's 2020 "Against 'Dark Game Design Patterns,'" read in full, which takes the concept apart on three fronts. It is ontologically incoherent: it makes subjective states — intent, harm, lack of consent — necessary for "darkness," yet keeps naming material features (loot boxes, grinding, pay-to-skip) dark *in themselves*. Its ethical framework is never named: Zagal et al. gesture at "the interaction with a system as a contract," implying something deontological, without saying so, hiding consequentialist, virtue-based, and care-based alternatives that might sort the same patterns differently. And the patterns singled out reveal, in Deterding et al.'s words, "a bias against a particular historical formation of digital games, namely then-ascendant freemium casual and social network games" — treating 1990s–2000s AAA design as the neutral baseline. They still credit the original's "sensitizing questions" with surfacing genuine values like transparency and experiences like regret, even where the dark/not-dark binary doesn't hold up.

Grand Theft Auto Online is the note's contemporary case, stitched from a thesis and one trade piece. Rockstar's design director Imran Sarwar told Game Developer: "As a company we did not realize how challenging it was going to be to have a team that essentially ran a content service, not make normal games," and on the rocky 2013 launch, "GTA Online was slowly failing — and quickly losing players — right up until the middle of 2014." That is the observe-and-steer loop having to be *built* by a studio that hadn't planned to run one. Ivan Ivanik's 2023 Umeå thesis applies Lehdonvirta and Castronova's framework directly to GTA Online, surveying 171 players and finding "grinder" playstyle raised measured monetary value by €8.1/hour, a Megalodon Shark Card purchase correlated with +€38.7, and — the finding worth remembering — a Whale Shark Card purchase correlated with *−€59.1*: the biggest real-money purchases went with the worst measured productivity. One circulating number — 4% of players generating 74% of Shark Card revenue — is not part of this evidentiary trail; it came from an aggregated search summary never traced to a source, and must not be repeated as fact.

Together, these five conversations describe one structure from five vantage points: an operator does not design an economy once and depart, but builds a standing observational apparatus and keeps re-steering the arena with what it reports. None of these systems reach a solved state — UO's drains are still adjusted, EVE throttled its own cadence, GTA Online's Shark Cards were still being tuned in 2023. The dark-patterns literature is the field auditing its own motive, worrying that steering toward engagement and revenue is not the same as steering toward the player's interest.

## 2. The load-bearing concepts

**Faucet, drain, sink.** Coined in Simpson's UO report, these are the field's basic unit of analysis. A faucet introduces value from outside player-to-player exchange; a drain permanently removes value from circulation; a sink is a drain engineered on purpose for a specific surplus. The vocabulary reframes "the economy" into adjustable valves — and Simpson's history shows that closing the loop entirely, so faucets and drains balance to zero, is exactly what produces hoarding, since a truly closed system has nowhere for surplus to go.

**The observe-and-steer loop.** The structural claim under every source: an operator watches — telemetry, surveys, employed economists, clustering — and adjusts in response, continuously, with no announced terminal state. EVE's newsletters kept publishing; UO's drains kept getting redesigned; GTA Online's economy kept getting retuned years after launch. "The economy," in these sources, names something under permanent surveillance and revision, never a fixed object designed once.

**Hoarding as premature closure.** Simpson found UO's economy failed not through inflation but through resources accumulating in inventory and stopping there, until "the world became boring." This note elevates that into a standing conjecture: boredom may be a symptom of a system that settled before anyone wanted it to, and continuous re-steering exists to keep forcing it back open. Whether this generalizes past UO is an open question, not a proven law.

**Telemetry and its methodological trap.** Telemetry, per Drachen and colleagues, is any recordable player action — sounding neutral. But the 2014 clustering finding complicates that: which method turns raw telemetry into "player types" determines whether the result is interpretable as a claim about real behavior at all. An observational apparatus is never a passive window; the processing method can manufacture the shape of what gets reported back, independent of whether the data was collected honestly.

**The engineered-scarcity thesis.** Lehdonvirta and Castronova's claim that virtual economies are "designed to attract users, satisfy them and make the publisher... money" rather than be efficient explains why game economists behave differently from national ones. Ivanik's thesis restates this vividly: an economically "efficient" GTA Online, where travel and acquisition were instant, would be efficient and "not fun to be a part of." Scarcity is a design choice, and Shark Cards sell a shortcut past a scarcity the operator built on purpose.

**Dark patterns, and the fight over what "dark" means.** Zagal, Björk and Lewis's coinage names design intentionally harming players' interests without their consent, and Karlsen's follow-up shows it doing real analytic work. But Deterding, Stenros and Montola's critique is equally load-bearing here: applying "dark" as an objective property while its own definition depends on unestablished subjective intent, harm, and consent means the label can smuggle in one community's aesthetic preferences — historically, AAA design — as a neutral baseline. Any use of "dark pattern" in this note's writing should carry that instability rather than treat the term as settled.

## 3. How to read this literature

Begin with Simpson's UO report, the founding document, written from inside a real failure as it happened. Read it for the hoarding story and the closing proposal to turn the operator into a standing telemetry observer — the first appearance of the note's central structural claim.

Read Castronova's Norrath paper next. It makes Simpson's implicit "the economy is real" stance explicit and quantitative, and defines a virtual world by persistence in language worth extracting on its own — and shows how early (2001) this kind of measurement started.

Read the Lehdonvirta and Castronova textbook material third, with the caveat that this note has it only *at second hand*. Extract the fiat-money equivalence and the design-for-engagement thesis, held loosely until the primary book is read.

Read the EVE press release and the Ten Ton Hammer interview as a pair: the release gives the institutional framing, the interview the day-to-day texture, including the bot-detection finding — the clearest example of one observational apparatus serving two purposes at once.

Read Drachen et al. 2014 for the player-modeling thread, understanding its account of the 2009 founding paper is itself *secondhand*. Extract the telemetry definition, the F2P-requires-monitoring claim, and the clustering-method caution.

Read Karlsen 2016 for the taxonomy applied empirically, then Zagal, Björk and Lewis's founding definition — remembering it too is reconstructed *at second hand*, through Karlsen and through Deterding et al.'s direct quotations. Read Deterding, Stenros and Montola last, since their paper answers a claim you now understand; extract all three objections, and note their critique does not itself propose a four-valued treatment of "dark" — that reading belongs to this note.

Finish with the GTA Online material — Ivanik's thesis and the Francis trade piece — as the case study where every earlier concept operates at once: faucets and drains, an operator learning to run a live service under pressure, engineered scarcity monetized directly, and a whale-spending pattern inverting the intuitive relationship between real-money purchase and measured productivity.

## 4. Where this touches the corpus

The source material draws a small number of explicit connections to obsmetrologia's own apparatus, each carrying its own evidentiary weight — none more settled than the grade attached to it.

Simpson's UO history is offered as a **rhyme**, not an identity: hoarding is read as resources accumulating in a state with no path back into circulation, freezing the system in an unsettled configuration the operator must force open with a new drain. The source material frames this as "the paradigm case of an arena requiring perpetual operator intervention because it structurally cannot self-settle" — an interpretive reading, not something Simpson argues in those terms himself.

Castronova's persistence definition — "the note continues to run whether anyone is using it or not" — is noted as sitting close to a working definition of an arena outliving any single session, arrived at independently by an economist in 2001: a convergence of language, not a confirmed shared theory.

EVE's employed-economist model is read as literalizing continuous instrumented observation, turning a metaphor into a job description; the QEN's throttled cadence is offered as a genuine data point about how much standing observation an operator judges worth sustaining, and as a live question about how fast an operator needs to re-settle its model of the arena's state — not a result already established.

Deterding, Stenros and Montola's incoherence critique is explicitly marked as a **conjecture applied externally**: their objection that "dark" is treated as an objective binary despite depending on subjective, community-relative conditions is read as an argument *for* a four-valued rather than two-valued treatment — evidence-for, evidence-against, no-evidence, contradictory-evidence-depending-on-community. The source material is unambiguous this is the note's own apparatus brought to the paper, not present in it, and should never be written up as though Deterding et al. endorsed it.

The GTA Online monopoly structure — publisher as sole retailer, scarcity imposed through time rather than material limits — is read as a **reported** instance of an admission-boundary idea: the operator continuously re-prices what lies on the other side of the boundary it admits players through, and Shark Cards are described as "a literal cash-for-boundary-crossing-speed exchange."

Drachen et al.'s clustering-method finding is flagged in the source material itself as bearing on the corpus's locality apparatus only **loosely**: a caution that a local observational method's output is not automatically a faithful section of the underlying behavior space, offered explicitly as a caution worth carrying over, not a literal application of the corpus's formal machinery.

## 5. What we don't know yet

The note's central conjecture — boredom as premature closure — has not been tested against a real economy's telemetry. The open question is whether a measurable settling indicator (trade-velocity collapse, wealth concentration, declining activity entropy) actually precedes player-reported boredom and churn, or whether UO is a one-off that fits a compelling narrative.

The observation-cost frontier is unresolved: EVE visibly throttled its own reporting cadence, so standing observation has a real cost weighed against something, but this note has no account yet of what that trade-off looks like, or whether a describable optimum exists.

The GTA Online whale finding comes from a single 171-respondent survey in one thesis; it is not known whether it replicates elsewhere, or what it implies about what is actually being sold to the highest-spending players — time, or standing.

Whether cross-boundary value flows — real-money trading volume, gold-farming labor output — can be measured with the same discipline as in-world flow is untested and, per the critic's report, entirely unaddressed here: the sweep covers UO, EVE, and GTA Online from the design and operator side only, with no engagement at all with the labor side — no Dibbell's *Play Money*, no Nakamura on the racialization of gold-farming labor. This is a real gap, not folded quietly into "future work."

Several sources this note leans on remain genuine reading debts, not settled citations: Zagal, Björk and Lewis's founding dark-patterns paper, obtained only through secondary quotation after four fetch attempts failed; the 2009 Drachen, Canossa and Yannakakis paper, known only through a later paper's account; Seif El-Nasr, Drachen and Canossa's *Game Analytics*, confirmed to exist but with no chapter content read; Lehdonvirta and Castronova's *Virtual Economies*, known only through a review and secondhand quotations; an actual EVE Quarterly Economic Newsletter PDF, never fetched despite a findable URL; and the loot-box empirical literature (Zendle and Cairns), never touched despite sitting adjacent to the dark-patterns material this sweep does cover. The "4% of players / 74% of revenue" GTA Online statistic must not be treated as fact anywhere in this note's writing until traced to an actual Take-Two disclosure or an independently fetched piece of journalism — right now it is an aggregated search summary, nothing more.
