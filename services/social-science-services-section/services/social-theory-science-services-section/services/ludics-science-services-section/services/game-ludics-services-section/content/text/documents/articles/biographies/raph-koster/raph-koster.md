Raphael "Raph" Koster (born 1971) is an American game designer and entrepreneur. He entered the field as an implementor on LegendMUD in 1992 and, in 1995, joined Origin Systems as lead designer of Ultima Online, one of the earliest and most influential graphical persistent worlds; he went on to lead design across Ultima Online's early expansions before joining Verant Interactive in 2000 as creative director of Star Wars Galaxies, later becoming chief creative officer at Sony Online Entertainment. He is also the author of A Theory of Fun for Game Design (2004).

Koster's load-bearing work for this portfolio is a pair of practitioner essays that state the "world, not game" argument more plainly than anyone else in the literature. "The Laws of Online World Design," circulated on the MUD-Dev mailing list from 1998 and maintained since at raphkoster.com, asserts flatly: "It's a SERVICE. Not a game. It's a WORLD. Not a game. It's a COMMUNITY. Not a game. Anyone who says, 'it's just a game' is missing the point." He pairs this with an operational claim about what persistence costs an operator in practice: "Once you open your online world, expect to keep your team on it indefinitely. Some of these games have never closed. And closing one prematurely may result in losing the faith of your customers, damaging the prospects for other games in the same genre." His 2000 essay "Declaring the Rights of Avatars" (sometimes titled "Declaring the Rights of Players") extends the same premise into a governance thought experiment: modeled explicitly on the 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man, it proposes nineteen articles — no punishment without a pre-existing code of conduct, a presumption of innocence, compensation for deprivation of virtual property, freedom of assembly bounded by preserving world viability — arguing that because the relationship between players and administrators does not terminate the way a boxed game's does, administrators accrue something like sovereign obligations toward the communities they run. Koster later reworded the same substance without "rights" language, as "Advice to Virtual World Admins," having found it more palatable to industry audiences.

Koster's claim is the field's most frequently cited formulation of the game/world distinction, and it is a common misattribution hazard: it belongs to him, not to Bartle, whose own definitional writing makes a structurally similar point without ever using that phrasing. Within this section, Koster's Laws are held by the Persistent World Foundations program as one of several independently-arrived-at practitioner statements of what persistence does to a game's ontology; "Declaring the Rights of Avatars" is held by the Ludocratic Institutions program as an early, explicitly constitutional proposal for restraining an operator-sovereign, sitting alongside Grimmelmann's later academic treatment of the same "god problem" — what actually restrains a designer no player can vote out.
