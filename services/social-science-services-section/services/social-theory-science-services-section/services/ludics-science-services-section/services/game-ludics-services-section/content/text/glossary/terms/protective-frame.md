The protective frame is Michael Apter's term for the personal, phenomenological sense of safety that accompanies a playful mindset — the felt experience, independent of any social agreement, that one is protected from the ordinary consequences of what one is doing. Apter's own text was not read directly for this dossier; the concept is known here only as relayed and quoted by Jaakko Stenros, who reports it in these words: "In the play-state you experience a protective frame which stands between you and the 'real' world and its problems, creating an enchanted zone in which, in the end, you are confident that no harm can come." The defining feature Stenros draws from this is that the protective frame is present even in solitary play — it requires no other participant, no negotiation, and no social contract, which distinguishes it sharply from the second of Stenros's three boundaries, the magic circle of play proper, a social contract created through negotiation and metacommunication between participants.

Stenros isolates the protective frame as the first of three things that discussions of "the magic circle" had, in his account, been conflating under a single name. He calls it the "psychological bubble": personal rather than social, a matter of an individual's felt state rather than of an agreement between players, and therefore not the kind of thing that can be publicly negotiated, violated by an opponent, or recognized by an external social system such as law or economics — properties Stenros reserves for the second boundary, the magic circle proper, and for the third, the persisting cultural site this glossary treats as the ludic arena. Because it is purely phenomenological, the protective frame is also the boundary most vulnerable to being pierced from the inside — by the player's own attention or anxiety — rather than only from an external event breaching a shared agreement, which is the register in which Goffman's interaction membrane, also taken up by Stenros, describes collapse. Stenros's three-part decomposition is offered as this glossary's most direct source for treating the protective frame, the negotiated magic circle, and the ludic arena as related but non-identical concepts, each doing separable work that a single undifferentiated "magic circle" cannot do on its own.
