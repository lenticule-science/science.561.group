# A Guide to the Play-Boundary Under Persistence

## 1. The story of the field

The literature this guide introduces answers one question in three
overlapping registers — theoretical, economic, forensic — and the
question is simple to state and hard to close: when a game never resets,
what, if anything, still separates it from the rest of life?

The textual starting point is thinner than the field built on it. In
*Homo Ludens*, Johan Huizinga's "magic circle" is nearly an aside — one
item in a list that also includes "the arena, the card-table, the magic
circle, the temple, the stage, the screen, the tennis court, the court of
justice," all named as "temporary worlds within the ordinary world,
dedicated to the performance of an act apart." It was Katie Salen and
Eric Zimmerman's *Rules of Play* (2004) that turned this incidental
phrase into game studies' central boundary-concept — closed,
self-sufficient, its rules absolute within — and Zimmerman later
admitted the scale of the invention: "we more or less invented the
concept... cobbling together ideas from Huizinga and Caillois." It is
against this formalized reading, more than Huizinga's looser original,
that the critique wave of 2005–2014 breaks.

Thomas Malaby's "Beyond Play" (2007) is the most theoretically ambitious
opening move. He rejects "play" itself as a supercategory, arguing that
its three supposedly intrinsic features — separable, safe, pleasurable —
are instead "cultural accomplishments," historically specific to Western
modernity. His replacement definition is exact: "a game is a
semibounded and socially legitimate domain of contrived contingency that
generates interpretable outcomes." He diagnoses two linked errors in
games scholarship, formalism and exceptionalism, both rooted in a
failure to see that games are processual — "always in the process of
becoming," able to generate practices that transform the game itself, as
when players' stalling tactics forced basketball to invent the shot
clock. His fieldwork among Cretan dice gamblers, for whom *zaria* is "an
existential engagement" bound up with inheritance and standing rather
than fun, supplies the empirical wedge, and his fourfold breakdown of
contingency — stochastic, social, performative, semiotic — becomes a
tool later writers keep reaching for. Consalvo's "There Is No Magic
Circle" (2009) makes the same point through a narrower case: cheating is
legible only because the outside world's judgments of fairness and
honesty are already operating inside supposedly sealed play — "we cannot
say that games are magic circles, where the ordinary rules of life do
not apply. Of course they apply, but in addition to, in competition
with, other rules."

Jesper Juul's "The Magic Circle and the Puzzle Piece" (2008) answers the
critique wave by arguing it has over-corrected: most critics, he notes,
claim to counter Huizinga and Salen/Zimmerman by insisting the boundary
is socially constructed — which is what those authors already said. His
own resolution: "the magic circle is best understood as *the boundary
that players negotiate*." His puzzle-piece image drops the assumption of
a uniform interface, so that "a game must be integrated into a context
in order to be experienced as separate from that context," inverting the
usual causality between game and world.

Jaakko Stenros's "In Defence of a Magic Circle," published first at
DiGRA Nordic in 2012 and in fuller form in ToDiGRA in 2014, is the
field's most systematic reconciliation. Reading every major critic in
sequence, Stenros separates three things earlier writers had treated as
one: the psychological bubble (Apter's "protective frame," a personal
sense of safety present even in solitary play), the magic circle of play
proper (a social contract "created through implicit or explicit social
negotiation and metacommunication," which law or economics can come to
recognize), and the arena (the culturally recognized site — a tennis
court, an empty chessboard — that persists as residue of play even when
no one is playing). The load-bearing move for persistent worlds is his
use of Goffman's interaction membrane: "the border around play is
permeable... it is also possible for play to be collapsed by external
events." A membrane transforms what crosses it rather than simply
admitting or refusing it, and under enough pressure it can fail
outright.

Edward Castronova's *Synthetic Worlds* (2005) supplies the economic
mechanism generating that pressure. His chapter "The Almost-Magic
Circle" — known here only through a verbatim passage cross-confirmed via
secondary citation — argues the boundary is "definitely there but also
definitely porous, and this is by choice of the users," and that
real-money markets make virtual goods "just as real as the goods outside
the membrane." Richard Heeks's study of gold farming (2010) takes the
claim to its conclusion: gold farming reproduces the entire capitalist
production chain across the membrane — wage labor, offshoring, bots,
brokers, an 85% average devaluation of virtual currencies between 2005
and 2009 — and states flatly that "there is no 'real economics' to
counterpose against a 'virtual economics;' there is just 'economics.'"
Julian Dibbell's reporting from inside a Nanjing gold-farming operation
(2007) gives the abstraction a face: twelve-hour shifts, a forty-man
"raid team" run as a labor crew, and a worker named Min who, having
trained himself for a year to feel nothing about the game that is his
job, is caught mid-flight from a monster by an old, involuntary thrill:
"It's instinctual — you can't help it... You want to play." Nick Yee's
"The Labor of Fun" (2006) closes the loop from the player's side —
subjects logging twenty-plus hours a week describe their play as "a
chore," "more like work then fun," evidence Yee reads as the games
functioning as literal "work platforms" that "train us to become better
game workers." Julian Kücklich's "Precarious Playbour" (2005) supplies
the vocabulary tying the thread together: modding is "simultaneously
voluntarily given and unwaged, enjoyed and exploited," a status the
industry has every material incentive to preserve, since the perception
that a game is "just play" licenses uncompensated extraction.

Two documented breach events show what persistence does to the boundary
under real pressure, and a third supplies the founding case for a
persistent world with no prior machinery to handle one. Dibbell's "A
Rape in Cyberspace" (1993) is that founding case: on LambdaMOO, a player
used a scripted object to narrate other players' characters into sexual
violence, and a community that had, four months earlier, formally
abdicated social governance was forced to invent a polity in real time.
An inconclusive open meeting gave way to a wizard unilaterally deleting
the offender's account, and the archwizard then built a permanent
petition-and-ballot system precisely because ad hoc intervention could
not be sustained in a world with no closing date. The offender simply
re-registered and reoffended — confirmation, in Dibbell's reading, that
"his punishment, ultimately, had been no more or less symbolic than his
crime," since no permanent expulsion is possible from a world with no
outside. The 2005 Corrupted Blood incident — a boss debuff that leaked
out of its intended containment in World of Warcraft and spread
player-to-player like a real pathogen — drew epidemiologists Eric
Lofgren and Nina Fefferman to argue, in a piece known only at second
hand here, that persistent worlds could bridge epidemiological
observation and modeling, not least because players' curiosity-driven
exposure-seeking was a behavior no existing model captured. The 2006
"Serenity Now" funeral raid, in which a guild attacked mourners gathered
in-game for a deceased player's real memorial, is analyzed by Gibbs,
Carter, Arnold, and Nansen (2013) as Gieryn-style "boundary-work" along
three simultaneous, unresolved axes — ethical, ontological, aesthetic —
"still... largely unresolved and perhaps irreconcilable" seven years on.
EVE Online institutionalizes the opposite response to the same pressure:
rather than adjudicating betrayal after the fact, CCP Games absorbs it
into the ruleset, treating scamming as "despicable, but allowed."
Marcus Carter's *Treacherous Play*, known only at second hand, reads
this as the mechanism by which EVE manufactures its own high-stakes
legitimacy.

A separate, practitioner-rooted tradition arrives at nearly the same
place by a different route. Bartle's 1996 taxonomy of MUD player types is
usually read as a classification scheme, but its real content is
dynamical: Achievers, Explorers, Socialisers, and Killers sit in feedback
loops — Achievers "breed" Killers "in a typically Malthusian fashion" —
that only exist because a MUD's population never resets between
sessions. His 2003 book *Designing Virtual Worlds* turns this into a
definitional claim: an environment "continues to exist and develop
internally... even when there are no people interacting with it." Bell's
2008 synthesis definition glosses persistence plainly: "no longer is one
participant the center of the world but a member of a dynamic community
and evolving economy" — the moment a game built around a player becomes a
world that includes one. It is Raph Koster, not Bartle — a
distinction worth holding carefully, since the misattribution is easy to
make — who states the claim in its strongest form: "It's a SERVICE. Not
a game. It's a WORLD. Not a game," paired with the operational cost:
"Once you open your online world, expect to keep your team on it
indefinitely. Some of these games have never closed." Pearce's
ethnography of the "Uru diaspora" inverts the usual failure mode: when
Uru's servers closed in 2004, one 350-member group she followed for
eighteen months carried its identity intact across four subsequent
worlds, evidence, in her reading, that the boundary is "more porous than
previously thought." Huynh, Lim, and Skoric's interview research on
MMO-based romance (2013) shows the same porosity tuned individually:
respondents sort into "Splitters," a rigid boundary, "Migrators," a
stepping-stone offline, and "Blenders," who reject the boundary
outright. Koster's and Bell's formulations arrived independently of the
theoretical debate, from operations rather than game theory — suggestive,
but not strong enough to lean on as deeper corroboration than industry
knowledge restated in two registers.

## 2. The load-bearing concepts

Six ideas do most of the work in this literature, and a reader who holds
them will be able to place any new source correctly.

**Stenros's triple, with Goffman's interaction membrane.** Splitting "the
magic circle" into a psychological bubble, a negotiated social contract,
and a persisting arena resolves a debate that had been arguing past
itself: critics attacking the circle as an oppressive fixed structure
were really attacking the arena or the contract-as-imposed, while
defenders were really describing the bubble or the contract-as-
negotiated. The membrane completes the picture by replacing the image of
a wall with a transforming border — what crosses is converted, not
simply admitted or refused, and the conversion can fail outright under
enough pressure.

**Processual, contingency-based games**, Malaby's contribution. A game is
not a fixed structure that either contains an event or doesn't; it
calibrates contingency from four sources — stochastic, social,
performative, semiotic — and remains "always in the process of
becoming." This makes breach intelligible as something other than a
design failure: a game rewriting its own rules in response to play is
doing, in miniature, what a persistent world does continuously.

**Playbour and the material interest in the play/work classification.**
Kücklich's coinage names an activity simultaneously freely given and
economically extracted, unclassifiable by wage labor or leisure. Its
force is structural: the industry benefits from players and modders
believing their activity is "just play," since that belief licenses
uncompensated extraction. Seen this way, playbour, gold farming, and
Yee's labor-of-fun testimony become one classification problem
approached from three angles.

**The almost-magic circle / porous membrane**, Castronova's image, later
generalized by Heeks into the claim that there is no virtual economy to
counterpose against a real one — only economics. This removes the escape
hatch of treating a game's economy as bracketed off from consequence:
once gold has a dollar exchange rate and a wage-earning labor force
producing it, the membrane is porous by definition.

**Breach as failure-to-close**, illustrated by Corrupted Blood, Serenity
Now, and the Mr. Bungle affair. What unites these cases is not that
something bad happened inside a game, but that nothing outside it closed
the question afterward — Serenity Now's three axes stayed "largely
unresolved and perhaps irreconcilable" seven years on, because a
persistent world offers no mechanism that forces resolution the way a
match's final whistle does.

**Persistence as the thing that removes the exit**, the practitioner
contribution. Bartle's change/persistence grid asks a single design
question — "whose world is it?" — by cross-tabulating how much players
can build against how much survives a reboot; Koster's "some of these
games have never closed" and Bell's "exist with or without a
participant's presence" restate, from operations rather than theory, the
fact the whole literature circles: nothing in a persistent world resets,
so nothing the boundary contains gets a clean discharge.

## 3. How to read this literature

Start with **Stenros (2014, ToDiGRA)**, the field's map: it gives the
bubble/contract/arena vocabulary and the Goffman membrane every later
source can be located against. Read **Malaby (2007)** next for the
processual/contingency framework motivating the critique wave, then
**Consalvo (2009)** for the sharper cheating case, held lightly since
only a verbatim fragment was retrieved, not the full article. Read
**Juul (2008)** third for the pushback against the critique wave, and
**Stenros (2012)** as a companion to the 2014 piece: an earlier, shorter
version useful for genealogical detail the later paper compresses.

Move to the economic thread once the theoretical vocabulary is in hand.
Read **Castronova's chapter** for the "almost-magic circle" coinage
(known only through a cross-confirmed quotation), then **Heeks (2010)**
for the full mechanism, **Dibbell (2007)** for its ground-level face,
**Yee (2006)** for player testimony, and **Kücklich (2005)** for the
vocabulary tying playbour, gold farming, and the labor of fun together.

Read **Dibbell (1993)** next, both as the founding breach case and as a
study of a community inventing governance with no prior machinery, then
**Lofgren and Fefferman** and **Gibbs et al.** as the two breach case
studies proper — the former known only at second hand and to be treated
as reported rather than confirmed, the latter read in full and reliable.
Read **Carter (2022)** for the mirror-image response, EVE's strategy of
absorbing betrayal into the ruleset, at the same caution.

Finish with the practitioner and identity-adjacent cluster — **Bartle**,
**Koster**, **Bell**, **Pearce**, and **Huynh et al. (2013)** — which
comes last because it does not engage the magic-circle debate directly.
It supplies "the boundary never closes" from operations and ethnography,
and Huynh et al.'s typology gives the clearest empirical picture here of
porosity tuned player by player.

## 4. Where this touches the corpus

The note's synthesis proposes two refinements to the corpus's
admission codec, both graded **rhyme** — a resemblance worth noting, not
an established mapping. The first splits the codec the way Stenros
splits the magic circle: a persisting arena (already modeled as event
structure), a negotiated contract (the codec proper), and a per-player
protective frame the corpus does not model. The second replaces
codec-as-characteristic-function with codec-as-transformation, following
Goffman: porosity is graded, direction-dependent, and player-tuned, so
breach becomes a matter of degree and channel rather than a binary
condition.

Malaby's four contingency sources invite comparison to the corpus's four
regimes, and only the stochastic source aligns cleanly with alea; the
note's account is explicit that the broader mapping is *not*
asserted, and this guide keeps that caution. The Serenity Now case is
held, at the same rhyme grade, as a concrete picture of one breach
carrying unresolved charge along several independent axes at once —
adjacent to the corpus's reading of Sutton-Smith, though that reading
itself rests on secondary paraphrase, not a direct read of his book.

Further resemblances in the source material carry no formal grade and
should be held loosely: Bartle's change/persistence grid as loosely
analogous to an ordering of regimes by durability of change, though the
analogy concerns authorship rather than settlement of contested charges;
Pearce's account of an arena deleted while a community's strategy
persists into a new one, as a concrete instance of ending decoupled from
closure; and Huynh et al.'s typology as an empirical instance of porosity
tuned individually, consistent with but not derived from the
codec-as-transformation reading above.

## 5. What we don't know yet

The clearest gap is textual rather than conceptual: Huizinga's own text
has not been read directly anywhere in this note's source material.
Everything attributed to him arrives through Stenros's citation — a
reasonable relay for a genealogy but a thin foundation given how
load-bearing the magic-circle mapping is for the corpus.

Several other primary texts are wanted but unread. Consalvo's full 2009
article survives only as a verbatim fragment, and her book-length
*Cheating* (2007) has not been consulted at all. Castronova's
"Almost-Magic Circle" chapter is known only through one cross-confirmed
sentence. Lofgren and Fefferman's Lancet piece and Carter's *Treacherous
Play* are both reported at second hand only; Carter is worth closing
first, since it is the sole source here on EVE's absorb-the-breach
strategy and the monograph is nominally open access. T. L. Taylor's
*Play Between Worlds*, cited repeatedly as a major critique voice, has
never been read directly — every claim attributed to her arrives through
Stenros's secondary citation.

On the economic side, the racialization-of-labor flank of gold-farming
scholarship — centrally Lisa Nakamura's work — has not been touched,
leaving the economic account without engagement on how that labor is
racialized in practice and discourse. Dibbell's book-length sequel,
*Play Money* (2006), remains unread, as does Stenros's discussion of
brink play and pervasive games (pp. 15–26), which would likely sharpen
the arena concept for play that courts boundary collapse rather than
avoiding it.

Two problems remain genuinely open rather than merely unread. Whether
porosity admits measurement — whether value or affect transfer across a
membrane could be instrumented the way a world's economy already is —
is shared jointly with the corpus's economic work. And the consent
problem under persistence has no answer here: Suits's lusory attitude,
known only at second hand rather than through *The Grasshopper* itself,
models consent at admission, but nothing yet models consent to an arena
rewritten after admission — precisely the condition a persistent world
imposes on every player for as long as it stays open. Finally, whether
EVE's strategy of absorbing betrayal and LambdaMOO's strategy of
adjudicating it exhaust an operator's real options, or are two points in
a larger describable space, remains unanswered and would require reading
Carter directly.
