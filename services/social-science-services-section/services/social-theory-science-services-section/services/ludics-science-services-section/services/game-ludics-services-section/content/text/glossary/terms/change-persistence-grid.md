The change/persistence grid is a classification Richard Bartle introduces in *Designing Virtual Worlds*, cross-tabulating two properties of a virtual-world codebase: how much its ordinary players, as opposed to its designers, are able to build or alter (change), and how much of what gets built survives a reboot of the world (persistence). Plotting real codebases on the two axes, Bartle finds they cluster along a diagonal — greater player-side building capacity tends to co-occur with greater persistence of the results, though the relationship runs only in that direction, never the reverse.

Bartle treats the grid as answering, concretely, a single design question he treats as the field's deepest: "Whose world is it?" At the low-change, low-persistence corner, exemplified by early AberMUDs, "the world entirely belongs to the designers, like a movie entirely belongs to its director." At the high-change, high-persistence corner, exemplified by LambdaMOO, "the world belongs to the players." The grid is thus not merely descriptive of engine architecture; it is Bartle's proposed answer to a question about political and creative ownership, read directly off two measurable properties of a codebase.

The grid connects directly to mud and moo, whose codebase families are what it actually classifies, and to wizard, since LambdaMOO's high position on the grid is what made its post-Bungle petition-and-ballot system — players legislating changes to a world they had substantial building rights in — a live possibility rather than a hypothetical. This section's own obsmetrological reading treats Bartle's diagonal finding as loosely analogous to an ordering of worlds by how far their settling toward a fixed state has progressed, but flags this explicitly as an analogy to be held loosely: Bartle's two axes describe authorship and ownership, not the settlement of contested claims, and this reading does not assert the two are the same measurement.
