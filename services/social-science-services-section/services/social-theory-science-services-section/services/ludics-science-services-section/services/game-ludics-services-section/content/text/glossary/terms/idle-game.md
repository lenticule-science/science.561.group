An idle game is a game whose defining design problem is not what happens while the player is present but what happens while they are gone. The working scholarly definition, quoted by Katta Spiel, Sultan A. Alharthi, Andrew Jian-lan Cen, Jessica Hammer, Lennart E. Nacke, P. O. Toups Dugas and Theresa Jean Tanenbaum in "'It Started as a Joke': On the Design of Idle Games" (CHI PLAY 2019) from the earlier taxonomy paper by Alharthi and colleagues, "Playing to Wait: A Taxonomy of Idle Games" (CHI 2018), holds that idle games "require players to leave the game and return to it at a later, unspecified point, where the game progresses on its own during that time and opens up different options for further play upon return." The taxonomy paper itself could not be obtained directly for this entry; everything held here from it is second-hand, as it survives embedded in Spiel et al.'s quotation, so its categorization axes and the extent of its 66-game corpus remain unverified at first hand. Sonia Fizek's parallel definition, also quoted by Spiel et al., describes idle games as those "in which humans are spectators and that playing is mostly an activity of game itself," with the genre's pleasure derived from "outsourcing gameplay onto the game."

The CHI PLAY 2019 study, built from interviews with six idle-game designers — Ian Bogost (Cow Clicker), Frank Lantz (Universal Paperclips), Jake Hollands (Spaceplan), bloodrizer (Kittens Game), Eric Fredricksen (Progress Quest), and Lucas Mills (Tap Titans) — is the fullest firsthand account of how the genre understands itself. bloodrizer states the decentering premise directly: "You are, essentially, not a center of the world but a spectator, world goes on with or without you" (emphasis in original). Bogost locates the specific temporal structure precisely: "It's not just the asynchrony between players, like, I play a hand or something, but it's asynchrony with the state, with the game itself. Like, you're there and then you're not and you're there again." Hollands describes engineering this deliberately in Spaceplan: "What I'm aiming to do is have it so that the game gets [to a certain point] then waits until you're ready." The paper organizes its findings into five themes, among them a documented ethical unease within the genre's own designers about the compulsion their games can produce — Hollands: "I feel really unhealthy about playing them. The only time I stop playing them is when I sit back for like a day without touching it" — set against a countervailing theme in which some designers, notably Lantz with Universal Paperclips, build a deliberate ending into an otherwise open-ended structure: "I wanted it to have a start, a middle and an end and have a dramatic arc and then at the end, it's done and it lets you go."

The idle game sits at one boundary of a set of neighboring terms this glossary treats separately. It is closely paired with, but not identical to, the incremental game, a label the sources use interchangeably with "idle game" without offering it an independent definition of its own. At its far limit it shades into the zero-player game, the case, exemplified for Fizek by Conway's Game of Life, where no player input is ever required at all; idle games proper retain some minimal player action (Progress Quest requires "rolling" a character at the outset) even as most of their state change proceeds unattended. Fizek's own theoretical account of why idle games work — interpassivity, the delegation of enjoyment to the machine — and her term for the specific moments of unattended self-play — the ambience act, borrowed from Alexander Galloway — both name mechanisms internal to the idle game as defined here. The genre also stands in a family relation to tick-based persistent browser-based games such as Urban Dead and Kingdom of Loathing: both share the property that the world's state advances independent of any player's presence, though PBBGs generally sustain many simultaneous players in one shared world rather than a single player's private instance, and the section's web-temporality program reads this as a shared structural signature — an arena whose causal development on the "Opponent" side is not paused by the player's absence — rather than as an established fact about either genre.
