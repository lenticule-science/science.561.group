Gold farming is the organized production of in-game currency, items, or leveled characters, by paid workers, for sale to other players through real-money trading. Richard Heeks's 2010 economic study, read in full for this dossier, traces its origin to three events converging in 1997: the launch of *Ultima Online*, the launch of eBay, and the Asian financial crisis, which together supplied a game with persistent tradeable value, a marketplace to trade it on, and a labor force with reason to do the trading. Heeks estimates roughly 400,000 people employed in gold farming globally around 2008–2009, some 85 percent of them China-based, in a market he sizes at over a billion dollars, with other estimates cited running considerably higher. He documents an average 85 percent devaluation of major MMOG currencies against the US dollar between 2005 and 2009 across titles including *World of Warcraft*, *EVE Online*, *RuneScape*, and *Lineage II* — a currency-market effect of sustained, large-scale farming activity on the games' own internal economies.

Heeks's larger argument is that gold farming does not merely sit at the border between real and virtual economies but reproduces the entire developmental arc of a capitalist economy across and inside that border: subsistence production, barter, monetization, wage labor, offshoring, and eventually automation via bots. His conclusion is stated flatly: "there is no 'real economics' to counterpose against a 'virtual economics;' there is just 'economics.' There are no 'real economies' to counterpose against 'virtual economies;' there are just 'economies.'" He suggests gold-farming firms are best modeled with the tools of development economics rather than games economics — a game company functioning as a dictatorship-style central bank, farming labor as subsistence production invisible to any GDP accounting, and the trade itself resembling a black market.

Julian Dibbell's 2007 *New York Times Magazine* reportage, also read in full, supplies the human interior of that abstraction from inside a Nanjing gold-farming operation: twelve-hour, seven-day shifts; a wage chain in which a worker earned roughly 10 yuan, about $1.25, per hundred gold, which the boss resold for three dollars or more and a Western retailer resold again for up to twenty; and an episode in which the bosses assembled a forty-person in-house raiding guild to farm end-game loot, industrializing what is elsewhere treated as cooperative high-end play. One worker, Min, describes suppressing the pleasure of that raiding once it became rote labor, and, in the essay's closing scene, is caught mid-flight from a monster feeling an old thrill he cannot suppress: "It's instinctual — you can't help it... You want to play." Gold farming, in this glossary's reading, is the labor side of the pipeline that real-money trading names as a market and that Nick Yee's labor of fun names from the ordinary player's own experience of the same games.
