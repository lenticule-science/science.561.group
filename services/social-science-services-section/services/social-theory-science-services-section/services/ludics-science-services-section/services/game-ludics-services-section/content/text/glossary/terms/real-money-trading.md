Real-money trading, abbreviated RMT in the literature, is the sale of virtual currency, items, characters, or services for real-world money outside a game's own systems. Edward Castronova, coining the almost-magic circle for the porous boundary of a synthetic world, identifies real-money markets as the specific mechanism that makes that boundary porous in an economic sense: because such markets exist, "every good inside the membrane [is] just as real as the goods outside of it." These markets began, in the account this dossier draws on, in an improvised form on general-purpose platforms such as eBay, before consolidating into dedicated virtual-goods exchanges as the practice scaled.

Richard Heeks's 2010 study treats real-money trading and gold farming as two faces of one intersection between real and virtual economies rather than as separate phenomena — gold farming is the labor that produces the tradeable supply, real-money trading is the market that clears it — and his documentation of an average 85 percent devaluation of several major MMOG currencies against the dollar between 2005 and 2009 is, on his reading, direct evidence of that market's scale and its effect on the games' internal economies. Jaakko Stenros's survey of the magic-circle debate reports a further consequence of RMT for legal scholarship, relayed here through Stenros's citation rather than read directly in Lastowka's own text: David Lastowka's argument that virtual worlds are properly games, and that courts should therefore treat disputes arising in them as belonging to a separate jurisdiction — a position Stenros immediately complicates by observing that once a world is also, in practice, a site of continuous real-money exchange, "the situation is more complex" than a clean game/non-game jurisdictional line can capture.

Real-money trading is, in this glossary's structure, the market-side counterpart to gold farming's labor-side account of the same value flow, and both sit downstream of the almost-magic circle's claim that a synthetic world's boundary is porous specifically because its users choose to make it so. Julian Dibbell's reporting on Nanjing gold farming supplies the wage chain that RMT's market prices set in motion — a worker's hundred gold sold at roughly $1.25, resold by a broker for three dollars or more, and resold again to a Western buyer for as much as twenty — which is, concretely, what it looks like for a game's currency to acquire a real exchange rate.
