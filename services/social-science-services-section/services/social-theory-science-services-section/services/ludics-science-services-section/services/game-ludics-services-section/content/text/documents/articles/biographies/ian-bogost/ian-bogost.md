Ian Bogost is an American academic and video game designer. He holds a PhD in comparative literature from UCLA and a BA in philosophy and comparative literature from the University of Southern California. He held a joint professorship at the Georgia Institute of Technology, in the School of Literature, Media, and Communication and in Interactive Computing, where he was the Ivan Allen College of Liberal Arts Distinguished Chair in Media Studies, before moving to the McKelvey School of Engineering at Washington University in St. Louis. He is a founding partner of Persuasive Games, LLC, and has designed videogames addressing subjects including airport security, consumer debt, the petroleum industry, and pandemic flu, alongside authoring eleven books on games and computation.

Bogost's load-bearing work for this portfolio is Cow Clicker (Facebook, 2010), a satirical social game he coded in three days for an NYU seminar after Zynga's FarmVille won "Best New Social/Online Game" at the Game Developers Choice Awards. Mechanically, Cow Clicker asked players to click a cow sprite every six hours, with friends' cows in a shared pasture generating more clicks and a premium currency, "Mooney," letting players skip the wait; the project ended with the deliberate, irreversible "Cowpocalypse" in September 2011, when every cow was permanently replaced with a blank space — a designed ending set against the genre's usual endlessness. Reporting on the game and its reception converges, across multiple independent accounts, on four concerns Bogost articulated for what social games like it distill to their essence: Enframing, in which "friends aren't really friends; they are mere resources" for systems optimizing connection for extraction; Compulsion, psychological manipulation on slot-machine variable-reward logic that exists "solely for that purpose"; Optionalism, in which mechanics are technically skippable via cash payment, producing "games you don't have to play" that assume most people won't exercise the option; and Destroyed Time, the way such games create compulsive obligation even during a player's absence while imposing endless upkeep demands on the developer as well. Bogost's own essay making this case in his own prose was not independently read for this file; what stands here is convergent secondary reporting. Directly read and independently verified, by contrast, is his interview testimony in the 2019 CHI PLAY study "'It Started as a Joke': On the Design of Idle Games," where he describes the genre's characteristic temporal structure: "It's not just the asynchrony between players... but it's asynchrony with the state, with the game itself. Like, you're there and then you're not and you're there again."

Bogost's work is held by the Web Temporality program, which names him directly as one of its interviewed designers and treats Cow Clicker as the type case for the ethics of scheduled returning appearing simultaneously as extraction and as design critique. The program reads his asynchrony-with-the-state language as a close natural-language match for a distinction the section's own arena formalism draws — locating the temporal gap not between two players but between a player and the underlying event structure itself, which keeps unfolding on its own regardless of whether any particular strategy is currently live. That identification is the section's own reading, offered as a proposed correspondence rather than a claim Bogost makes in the source material himself.
