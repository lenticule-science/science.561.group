A wizard, in the MUD and MOO literature, is a player granted administrative and technical control over a persistent world's code and objects — the closest thing such a world has to a designer with standing power to intervene after launch. The fullest documented account of what wizardly power actually does and does not accomplish is Julian Dibbell's 1993 "A Rape in Cyberspace," set on LambdaMOO.

Dibbell's essay records that four months before the incident it centers on, LambdaMOO's wizards had formally abdicated responsibility for the community's social life: in a document called "LambdaMOO Takes a New Direction," they declared they "would make no decisions affecting the social life of the MOO" and would act only as "pure technicians." That abdication left a vacuum that the Bungle affair — a player's use of a scripted "voodoo doll" object to narrate other players' characters into sexual violence — forced closed in real time. A thirty-person open meeting produced no consensus on what to do; a wizard named TomTraceback then broke the deadlock by acting alone, privately deleting the offending account ("Mr. Bungle, it would have said, is not the name of any player"). The archwizard Haakon subsequently built a permanent petition-and-ballot system explicitly because he judged that ad hoc wizardly intervention could not be sustained in a world with no closing date — a system under which any social scheme requiring wizardly power could be put to a binding popular vote.

Dibbell's account also records the limit of wizardly power over a persistent population: the offending player simply re-registered under a new name, "Dr. Jest," and reoffended, which Dibbell reads not as a loophole in the wizards' authority but as confirmation that "his punishment, ultimately, had been no more or less symbolic than his crime" — there is no permanent expulsion available from a world that has no outside. This section's ludocratic-institutions research program, working from LambdaMOO's own subsequent constitutional documents, reads Haakon's petition-and-ballot system as the first instance in this literature of a wizard handing settlement of a live dispute to an open community process rather than deciding it unilaterally, and treats the episode as the founding case for its broader study of what institutions a persistent world's administrators are forced to grow once the option of a clean ending is no longer available. This connects wizard directly to persistent-world (the property that made a one-time ad hoc fix insufficient), moo (the codebase type whose high player-side building rights made a legislative solution imaginable at all — see change-persistence-grid), and player-types (Bartle's Killers, the type this literature's other case studies most often associate with the disputes wizards are called on to adjudicate).
