James Grimmelmann is an American legal scholar. He holds the Tessler Family Professorship of Digital and Information Law at Cornell Tech and Cornell Law School. He holds a JD from Yale Law School and an AB in computer science from Harvard College, worked as a programmer at Microsoft before law school, and clerked for a federal appellate judge afterward; he previously taught at New York Law School, Georgetown, and the University of Maryland. He is the author of the casebook Internet Law: Cases and Problems and of more than seventy articles and essays on generative AI, content moderation, platform regulation, and digital property, and he serves as a board member of Creative Commons and an affiliated fellow of the Yale Information Society Project.

Grimmelmann's load-bearing work for this portfolio is "Virtual Worlds as Comparative Law" (New York Law School Law Review 49, 2004), read in full. Its governing move is to treat virtual worlds as legal systems worth studying comparatively in their own right — "law in virtual worlds," in his framing, rather than "the law of virtual worlds" imposed from outside — and its Part V, "The God Problem," is the section's sharpest available treatment of what restrains a designer with no possibility of being deposed: "there is no way to depose the designers of a game." He catalogues three restraints that actually function: market exit, which he distinguishes from the designer's own interface — "'Quit' is not really a command in the game interface" — and which therefore operates as a continuous signal rather than a single referendum; an international-law analogy in which players resemble foreign investors who cannot repatriate capital but can sell their position to another buyer; and a due-process analogy in which designers voluntarily bind their own discretion by publishing the narrow domains — offensive speech, commercialization, griefing — where they will intervene, legitimating their authority the way an administrative agency's guidance documents do. He gives griefing its most cited definition, quoting Hunter and Lastowka's unpublished "Virtual Crimes": griefing is "any activity that genuinely bothers most players of the game in question," a definition he shows "renders futile the task of developing any general doctrine of virtual criminal law," since the same acts — violence, harassment — are simultaneously the wrongs to be deterred and the only sanctions available, and no in-world authority holds a monopoly on disproportionate punishment. His answer to who actually enforces order under that constraint is guilds: player organizations the software barely formalizes but that become, in his phrase, the real "locus of effective decision-making power."

Grimmelmann is one of the four core authors — with Lessig, Lastowka and Hunter, and Fairfield — the Ludocratic Institutions program draws its virtual-law literature from, and the God Problem and the griefing analysis are the program's two most directly load-bearing borrowings from him: the program records griefing's community-relative definition as a charge that can only be resolved through a community's own settling process, never computed from the isolated act. The section also extends his God Problem past his own claim, reading it as a case where the operator sits genuinely outside the play structure with the power to rewrite it mid-play — a proposed reading, not one Grimmelmann himself makes, since he frames the problem in terms of sovereignty and rule of law rather than in the section's own vocabulary.
