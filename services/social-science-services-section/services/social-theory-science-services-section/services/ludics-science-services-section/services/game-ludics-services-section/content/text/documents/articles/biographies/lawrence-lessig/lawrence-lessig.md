Lawrence Lessig is an American legal scholar. He holds the Roy L. Furman Professorship of Law and Leadership at Harvard Law School, where he has taught for much of his career; he previously taught at Stanford Law School, where he founded the Center for Internet and Society, and at the University of Chicago. He holds a JD from Yale, an MA in philosophy from Cambridge, and undergraduate degrees in economics and management from the University of Pennsylvania, and clerked for Judge Richard Posner on the Seventh Circuit and for Justice Antonin Scalia on the Supreme Court. In 2001 he cofounded Creative Commons, the nonprofit licensing organization, and it is under a Creative Commons license that the free edition of his own work is distributed.

Lessig's load-bearing work for this portfolio is the "LambdaMOO" chapter of Code: Version 2.0 (2006), read in full from the free Creative Commons-licensed edition. The chapter narrates the 1993 Mr. Bungle incident — a player's use of a scripted "voodoo doll" to narrate other characters into sexual violence — as the forcing case for his general argument that a space is regulated by four interacting modalities: law, social norms, the market, and code, with code capable of directly implementing the outcome of any of the other three. He recounts the roughly three-hour community meeting split between a vigilante faction ("LambdaMOO did not need a state; it needed a few good vigilantes") and a democracy faction proposing government by ballot with "no constitution limiting the scope of what democracy could decide," and how neither position resolved the case: wizard TomTraceback instead unilaterally "toaded" Bungle's account outside any authorized process, after which the wizards imposed balloting from May 1, 1993, so that any proposition receiving twice as many votes for as against became law. His own gloss on what followed is the passage the section treats as load-bearing: LambdaMOO's move to democracy "was not just an achievement. It was also a failure. The space had failed... to engender values in its population sufficient to avoid just the sort of evil Bungle had perpetrated... From a space self-regulated to a space regulated by self." He quotes Jennifer Mnookin's description of the result as "politics implemented through technology," and contrasts LambdaMOO's case — code literally enacting a vote's outcome — against AOL (pure architecture, no norms) and Usenet's cc.general (pure norms, no code enforcement).

Lessig's account is one of the section's four principal treatments of the LambdaMOO arc, alongside Lastowka and Hunter's, Fairfield's, and Grimmelmann's, and the Ludocratic Institutions program is the one that holds him: it names Lessig explicitly, together with Lastowka and Hunter, Fairfield, and Grimmelmann, as the core of the section's virtual-law literature, and it reads the arc he narrates — an operator handing settlement authority to an open community process, that process failing to converge on the Bungle case, and a privileged fixed point (wizardly fiat, later formal balloting) being reasserted from outside the normal process — as an instance the section's own settlement mathematics would describe as a closure operator forced to climb from a coarser fixed point to a finer one and then being capped again from outside. That reading is the section's own extension, proposed rather than asserted, and is not a claim Lessig makes in the source text himself.
