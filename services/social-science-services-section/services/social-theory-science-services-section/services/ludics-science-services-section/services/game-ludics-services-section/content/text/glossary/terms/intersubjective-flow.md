Intersubjective flow is a term Celia Pearce coins in "Communities of Play" to name the mechanism by which a play community sustains its members' engagement collectively: "players can keep each other in a state of flow." The term relocates the general psychological idea of flow — sustained absorption in an activity — from a single player's individual experience to a group's capacity to sustain that absorption in each other; this glossary has not traced the term to a source outside Pearce's own essay.

The concept underwrites Pearce's larger theoretical claim about identity in persistent worlds: that "individual avatar identity is an intersubjective accomplishment and is as much a product of social emergence as the group itself." On this account, an avatar's felt reality is not something a lone player generates internally and merely displays to others; it is co-produced, moment to moment, by the group's ongoing responsiveness — which is also why Pearce found that a community like The Gathering could carry its members' individual avatar identities intact across four different worlds after Uru's shutdown (see fictive-ethnicity): the identity had never been purely individual property to begin with.

Pearce connects intersubjective flow directly to her broader claim about the magic circle, arguing it is "more porous than previously thought, especially when seen in the larger context of all online games" — a community sustaining each other's flow can carry that flow straight through the boundary of one game's ending into another game's rules, which is not something a model of a single bounded play-session predicts is possible. This entry's grounding is limited to Pearce's own essay; the term does not appear, so far as the sources read for this glossary establish, in Bartle's, Koster's, or Bell's work, and should be read as specific to Pearce's ethnographic account rather than as a term of art shared across this literature generally.
