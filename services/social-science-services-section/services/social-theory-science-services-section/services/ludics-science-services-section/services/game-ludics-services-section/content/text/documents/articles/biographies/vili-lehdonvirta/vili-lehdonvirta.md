Vili Lehdonvirta is Professor of Economic Sociology and Digital Social Research at the Oxford Internet Institute, University of Oxford, and Professor of Technology Policy at the Department of Computer Science, Aalto University; he is also a senior research fellow of Jesus College, Oxford. He holds a PhD in Economic Sociology from the Turku School of Economics (2009) and an MSc in Information Networks from Helsinki University of Technology (2005), and worked earlier at the London School of Economics, the University of Tokyo, and the Helsinki Institute for Information Technology. Before his academic career he worked as a game programmer, a background that shows in the practitioner-level specificity of his later research on digital marketplaces and platforms.

For this portfolio, Lehdonvirta's load-bearing contribution is *Virtual Economies: Design and Analysis* (MIT Press, 2014), co-written with Edward Castronova, which the section treats as the field's textbook synthesis. The section has not obtained the book's primary text directly; its holdings come from a substantive academic review and from block quotations reproduced in a 2023 master's thesis that uses the book as its literature-review spine, and are held with that provenance flagged. Two claims recur and are treated as load-bearing: the insistence that game currencies are not toy money but the genuine article — "Both the US Dollar and World of Warcraft coins are fiat money" — which licenses applying the full apparatus of monetary policy (money supply, inflation as "a subtle tax on everyone who owns money," a CPI-style representative market basket) to a game economy; and the claim that "virtual economies aim to create products that attract users, satisfy them and make the publisher of the virtual economy money," not to be efficient — which is why engineered scarcity (travel time, cooldowns, drop rates) is the deliberate product of a virtual economy rather than friction to be designed away. This second claim is the book's sharpest methodological departure from ordinary economics: a designer who applied conventional efficiency criteria to a virtual economy would build a world where any item is instantly obtainable, which is not fun and not what publishers build.

Lehdonvirta's position in the field is that of a synthesizer who takes the practitioner-facing insights of writers like Zachary Booth Simpson and Edward Castronova and gives them a systematic, textbook-level economic apparatus, while keeping the field's founding insight — that engagement and revenue, not efficiency, are what a game economy is designed for — at the center rather than treating it as an embarrassment to be reasoned around. One program of the section holds him: Obsmetrology of Play, where *Virtual Economies* stands alongside Simpson's UO postmortem and Castronova's Norrath accounts as the discipline's core textbook statement of what a game operator measures and why.
