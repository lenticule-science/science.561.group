Celia Pearce (born 1961) is an American game designer, scholar, and professor. She began designing interactive attractions and exhibitions in 1983 and has held academic appointments since 1998, directing the Experimental Game Lab and the Emergent Game Group at Georgia Tech before joining Northeastern University's College of Arts, Media and Design in 2014, where she has continued to teach game design and study multiplayer communities and virtual worlds. She received her PhD in 2006 from the SMARTlab Centre, then at Central Saint Martins College of Art and Design, University of the Arts London, and is a co-founder of IndieCade and the Playable Theatre Project.

Pearce's load-bearing work for this portfolio is her ethnography of "the Uru diaspora," published as "Communities of Play: The Social Construction of Identity in Persistent Online Game Worlds" and as a chapter of her book Communities of Play: Emergent Cultures in Multiplayer Games and Virtual Worlds (MIT Press, 2009). When Uru: Ages Beyond Myst's servers closed on February 9, 2004, an estimated 10,000 players were displaced; Pearce followed one 350-member group, "The Gathering," through eighteen months as they migrated intact across four different virtual worlds, carrying their group identity — which she calls a "fictive ethnicity" — with them. Members "reported weeping as the clock struck midnight and the avatars on the screen froze in place," and reported symptoms consistent with genuine grief at the shutdown; but on Pearce's account that trauma "served as a catalyst for fortifying the group identity" rather than dissolving it. Her theoretical claim is that avatar identity is not a private psychological fact but an "intersubjective accomplishment," produced by group feedback loops she terms "intersubjective flow," in which "players can keep each other in a state of flow." She concludes that the magic circle is "more porous than previously thought" — porous enough that a community can walk through the wall of one game's ending and reconstitute itself inside another game's rules entirely, a possibility the single-session, single-arena model of "a game" does not predict.

Pearce's Uru study occupies a distinct position among this portfolio's foundational texts: where Bartle, Koster, and Bell define persistence by what the world does while the player is away, Pearce's case inverts the question by asking what happens to a community when the world, not the player, is the one that ends. It is held by the Persistent World Foundations program as its sharpest documented instance of world-death coming apart from community-closure, and the section reads it, as a proposed rather than established connection, as a concrete case of an "ending" decoupled from any formal notion of settlement — a community carrying forward an open, renegotiated identity into an entirely new arena rather than any part of it settling into a fixed, final state.
