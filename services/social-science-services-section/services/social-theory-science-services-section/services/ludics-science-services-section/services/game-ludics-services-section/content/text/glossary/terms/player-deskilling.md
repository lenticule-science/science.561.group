Player deskilling is Stefano De Paoli's central claim in "Automatic-Play and Player Deskilling in MMORPGs" (Game Studies, 2013): that the rise of automatic-play software in MMORPGs produces "the deskilling of players with the transfer of human skills to automatic-play software." The skills in question are the ones a manual levelling grind actually requires — timing an attack, assessing risk, managing resources such as health and inventory across a repeated combat-loot-heal cycle — and De Paoli's claim is that these do not simply go unused when a bot takes over; they migrate, becoming properties of the software rather than the player. He names the mirror-image process, drawing on Bruno Latour's actor-network theory, the enskilling of machines: the bot comes to embody judgment that was previously exclusively human, not merely to imitate its outward effects.

De Paoli frames this transfer explicitly through Karl Marx's account of industrial-era automation, under which factory workers become "mere appendices of machines" — a citation this entry carries at second hand, De Paoli's own text having been read in full for this section but Marx's original not independently verified — and asks whether MMORPG levelling faces an analogous obsolescence to manual industrial labor, a question he connects to Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew McAfee's Race Against the Machine, likewise cited by De Paoli rather than read directly here. The claim is thus explicitly a labor-theoretic one, treating the automated MMORPG grind as a case of skill migrating from a human worker to a machine under conditions of production, with playing a persistent world's levelling content standing in for waged industrial labor.

Player deskilling names the human-loss side of automatic-play, defined elsewhere in this glossary as the software practice that produces it; the two terms describe one phenomenon from opposite ends. The section's web-temporality program reads De Paoli's argument as adjacent to, but analytically distinct from, the idle game's interpassivity: interpassivity, per Sonia Fizek, is a designed and sanctioned delegation the player consents to as the point of the genre, while the deskilling De Paoli documents is typically an unsanctioned circumvention of a game whose designers intended manual play, with the loss of skill presented as a genuine cost rather than a pleasure. Whether these are, at a deeper level, the same structure wearing different institutional clothing — a question the section poses but does not answer — is filed here as an open bridge rather than an established identification, consistent with how the source literature itself treats the question: as one of labor and agency, with no formal treatment of what changes when the executing agent stops being a human strategy-holder.
