title: "Mia Consalvo"
description: "The scholar whose 2009 polemic gave the magic-circle critique its title, arguing that ordinary rules never actually stop at a game's edge."
date-created: 2026-08-22
---

Mia Consalvo is Professor and Canada Research Chair in Game Studies and Design at Concordia University in Montreal, where she directs the Centre for Technoculture, Art and Games (TAG) and has served as Graduate Program Director of the PhD program in Communication Studies. She served as president of the Digital Games Research Association (DiGRA) from 2012 to 2016. Her books include *Cheating: Gaining Advantage in Videogames* (2007), *Atari to Zelda: Japan's Videogames in Global Contexts* (2016), *Players and their Pets: Gaming Communities from Beta to Sunset* (2017, with Christopher A. Paul), and *Real Games: What's Legitimate and What's Not in Contemporary Videogames* (2022, with Christopher A. Paul).

Her load-bearing text for this portfolio is "There is No Magic Circle" (*Games and Culture* 4(4), 2009), the paper that gives the section's boundary program its titular provocation. The article itself sits behind a paywall for this sweep; the section holds only a verbatim key passage recovered through search-engine indexing, not a direct read of the full text, and that limitation is carried forward here rather than smoothed over. In it, Consalvo writes: "Structures may be necessary to begin gameplay, but we cannot stop at structures as a way of understanding the gameplay experience. Because of that, we cannot say that games are magic circles, where the ordinary rules of life do not apply. Of course they apply, but in addition to, in competition with, other rules and in relation to multiple contexts, across varying cultures, and into different groups, legal situations, and homes." Her proof case is cheating: the category only makes sense if judgments from outside the supposed circle — fairness, honesty, social standing — are already operating inside it. This is continuous with the vocabulary of her earlier book *Cheating*, which distinguishes "hard rules" from "soft rules" and introduces "gaming capital," the social and cultural capital that determines who in a given community gets to say what counts as cheating in the first place. As Jaakko Stenros later summarizes her position, Consalvo argues the magic circle "upholds a structuralist conceptualization of games and that it emphasizes form at the cost of function," losing the context her cheating cases depend on.

Consalvo's position in the field's magic-circle debate is that of its sharpest and most-cited polemicist: her essay is treated across the section's sources as the piece that named the critique, even where later writers (Juul, Stenros) push back against the strength of the claim. One of the section's six programs holds her: Boundary and Membrane, where her cheating-based case against a sealed play-space sits alongside Thomas Malaby's parallel argument as the founding critique the program's later defenses (Juul, Stenros) and economic material (Castronova, Kücklich, Heeks) respond to. A second connection is marked rather than established: the program's reading debts flag her *Cheating* monograph as material that would extend into Ludocratic Institutions' norm-enforcement questions — a debt the section has not yet paid, and so not a holding, only a noted direction.
