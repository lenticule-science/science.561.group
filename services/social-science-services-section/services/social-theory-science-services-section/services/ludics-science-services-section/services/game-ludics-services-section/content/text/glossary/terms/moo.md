MOO names a family of MUD codebases that Richard Bartle's change-persistence-grid places at the high-change, high-persistence corner of its classification: worlds in which ordinary players, not only the world's designers, can build lasting objects and spaces that survive a server reboot. Bartle's own example of this quadrant is LambdaMOO, of which he writes that "the world belongs to the players" — the opposite pole from a low-change, low-persistence codebase, which he compares to a world that "entirely belongs to the designers, like a movie entirely belongs to its director."

The sources read for this glossary do not themselves spell out MOO's derivation from "MUD"; what they establish is functional rather than etymological — a MOO is the codebase type whose defining property is that building is both widely available and durable. Julian Dibbell's 1993 essay refers to LambdaMOO simply as "a MUD" in his own descriptive language, calling it "a kind of database especially designed to give users the vivid impression of moving through a physical space that in reality exists only as words," which is consistent with treating a MOO as a specific, more building-capable subtype within the broader MUD category rather than as a wholly separate form. Nothing in the sources read for this entry should be taken to establish the acronym's expansion; it is used here strictly in the codebase-classification sense Bartle gives it.

LambdaMOO is the load-bearing example connecting moo to several other entries here: it is the setting of the Bungle case that grounds wizard and much of this section's account of governance under persistence; its high position on the change-persistence-grid is the concrete instance behind Bartle's claim that world-ownership tracks a codebase's change/persistence position; and its post-Bungle petition-and-ballot system — built by the archwizard Haakon after an ungoverned population was forced to construct its first constitution live — is the clearest documented case in this literature of players actually exercising the "belongs to the players" condition the grid predicts for a world in its quadrant.
