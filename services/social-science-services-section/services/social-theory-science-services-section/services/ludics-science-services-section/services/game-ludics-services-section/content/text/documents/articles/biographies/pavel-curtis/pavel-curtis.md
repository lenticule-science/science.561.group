Pavel Curtis is an American computer scientist. He was a member of the research staff at Xerox PARC from 1983 to 1996, working on programming language design, programming environments, and online collaboration systems, and in 1990 founded LambdaMOO, developing it from work initiated by Stephen White into one of the most influential early persistent social worlds. Within LambdaMOO Curtis operated under the character name Haakon, its archwizard. He left Xerox in 1997 to co-found PlaceWare, a web-conferencing company later acquired by Microsoft, where he continued as a software architect.

Curtis's load-bearing work for this portfolio survives verbatim, as primary documents still preserved in LambdaMOO's own help system rather than as secondhand description. On December 9, 1992, as Haakon, he posted "LambdaMOO Takes a New Direction" (LTAND), announcing that the wizards would withdraw from "the discipline/manners/arbitration business" and become pure technicians. His framing cast the wizards as "wizard-mothers, guarding the nest" of a society that had "left the nest" and grown past the point where five people's judgment could substitute for its own; he explicitly rejected both electing untrusted players to wizard status and building an unsupervised "wizard machine" to run arbitrary code with no human in the loop. Within months this abdication produced LambdaMOO's petition-and-ballot system, the first sustained legislative apparatus grown from inside a running, never-resetting game. That system was forced into being under crisis: the March 1993 Mr. Bungle incident produced a chaotic community meeting with no consensus, a wizard's unilateral deletion of the offender's account, and then Curtis's construction of a permanent petition-and-binding-ballot mechanism — a response to seeing a citizen act alone rather than through any collective process. Three and a half years later, on May 16, 1996, Curtis posted the reversal, "LambdaMOO Takes Another Direction" (LTAND2), reinstating wizardly fiat with explicit carve-outs: a reserved right to "re-interpret and/or explicitly veto any clause of any passed ballot," a reserved veto over Arbitrator decisions, and, as a release valve, a standing shutdown-petition mechanism. His stated reason for reversing course: "The line between 'technical' and 'social' is not a clear one, and never can be. The harassment that ensues each time we fail to achieve the impossible is more than we are now willing to bear." The community's own verdict survives as a preserved ballot, LTAD-Legality, which closed 321 in favor, 111 against, 272 abstaining — a legislature formally ratifying its own executive's self-coup.

Legal scholarship treats this arc as the founding case study for asking what actually restrains an operator no player can depose. Lastowka and Hunter frame Curtis's 1992 withdrawal as "a reverse coup d'état" while noting it was never absolute, since the wizards retained the power to implement ballot outcomes as coded law and so, in Curtis's own later words, "no matter what they claimed, [they] were still wizards." Lessig's Code 2.0 reads the same history as code implementing the outcome of a vote, and glosses the whole episode as a passage "from a space self-regulated to a space regulated by self." Within this section, Curtis's LambdaMOO documents are held primarily by the Ludocratic Institutions program, which reads the 1992 abdication and 1996 reclamation as a case of a settlement process being handed to an open community, failing to converge on a live crisis, and having a privileged authority reasserted from outside that process — a reading the section frames explicitly as its own translation of the history, not a claim advanced by Curtis or the legal scholars who study him.
