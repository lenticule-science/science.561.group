Joshua Fairfield is an American legal scholar. He is a professor of law at Washington and Lee University in Lexington, Virginia, holding the William D. Bain Family Professorship of Law, and directs W&L's AI Legal Innovation Strategy. He holds a JD from the University of Chicago, clerked for the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, and practiced as an associate at Jones Day before entering teaching; he held a visiting position at Columbia Law School and taught for two years at Indiana University's law school before joining Washington and Lee in 2007. He has been elected to the American Law Institute. His scholarship spans digital property, electronic contracts, and the law of emerging technologies including cryptocurrencies and smart contracts, and his policy writing has appeared in the New York Times, Forbes, and the Financial Times.

Fairfield's load-bearing work for this portfolio is "Virtual Property" (Boston University Law Review 85, 2005), read in full. His central definitional move: virtual objects that are rivalrous, persistent, and interconnected constitute a distinct category he names virtual property — "They are rivalrous. If one person owns and controls them, others do not. They are persistent. Unlike the software on your computer, they do not go away when you turn your computer off. And they are interconnected. Other people can interact with them. This kind of code I term 'virtual property.'" His argument is institutional as much as doctrinal: he contends contract law is structurally inadequate to govern these objects because a contract only allocates costs between the two contracting parties, while property law is built to manage the externalities — search costs, third-party reliance — that a genuinely rivalrous and interconnected asset imposes on people outside the contract. He further argues courts, not legislatures, are the right institution to develop this doctrine, on public-choice grounds: "when an identifiable, small, and wealthy group (like the holders of intellectual property) seeks legislative action against a disorganized, inchoate, and dispersed majority, the former can be confidently expected to prevail in the legislature," so he treats judicial reluctance to recognize virtual property claims as itself a kind of institutional failure — "it would be an abdication for a court to refuse to adjudicate a virtual property claim merely because no statute exists."

Fairfield's argument sits inside the Ludocratic Institutions program, which names him among the core virtual-law authors it draws on — Lessig, Lastowka and Hunter, Fairfield, and Grimmelmann — and specifically as the writer who poses the property question the program tracks as still substantially open: "what is property in a world one party can rewrite by patch?" The program records that the case law Fairfield's argument anticipates and calls for is itself a documented gap in the section's reading — no adjudicated instance comparable to what his article proposes has yet been read in primary text — which keeps his position in the literature a normative argument for courts to act rather than a report of courts having acted.
