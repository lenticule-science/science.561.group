# A Guide to Ludocratic Institutions

A persistent world is a game that never ends, and that creates a problem no
tournament or console title has to solve: it cannot defer its disputes to a
final whistle. In a game with an ending, a bad call or a betrayal can be
adjudicated after the fact, or simply outlived. A world that keeps running
has no such release valve — its disputes must be settled *while play
continues*, by institutions grown inside the arena itself, because there is
no outside the arena can hand the problem to. This guide introduces the
literature this note draws on to study those institutions: where they
came from, what they argue, and what a new reader should take from each.

## 1. The story of the field

Nearly every author here tells the same origin story, because there is
really only one candidate for "first sustained legislature grown inside a
running game": LambdaMOO. Its primary documents survive verbatim, still
hosted in the MOO's own help system, and the note has read them directly
rather than through paraphrase.

On December 9, 1992, the MOO's chief wizard, Pavel Curtis (known in-world as
Haakon), posted "LambdaMOO Takes a New Direction" — LTAND — announcing that
the wizards, who had run the world as a small unaccountable administrative
elite, would withdraw from what Curtis called "the discipline/manners/
arbitration business" and become pure technicians. His own image for the
change was maternal and pointed: the wizards had been "wizard-mothers,
guarding the nest," and the society had grown past needing them. He rejected
two alternatives players had floated — electing untrusted people into
wizard bits, or building an unsupervised "wizard machine" to run arbitrary
code with no human in the loop — as unacceptable, because the MOO ran on
one real machine, under real legal liability, so some centralization was
structurally forced no matter how social authority was distributed. Within
months this abdication produced a petition and ballot system: any
sufficiently tenured player could author a proposal, have it vetted by the
wizards against a published rubric, gather signatures, and pass it into
force by roughly two-to-one vote — the note's first concrete answer to
what institutions accrete when disputes cannot be deferred to an ending:
first a voluntary withdrawal, then a legislative apparatus built to fill
the vacuum it left.

The vacuum did not fill itself peacefully. In March 1993 a player using the
character Mr. Bungle used a scripted object to force other players' avatars
into degrading, violent acts the community described as "virtual rape."
Lawrence Lessig's *Code: Version 2.0* narrates the aftermath at length, and
the note read the complete chapter. A roughly three-hour community
meeting split into a vigilante faction arguing "LambdaMOO did not need a
state; it needed a few good vigilantes," and a democracy faction proposing
government by ballot with no constitution limiting what a majority could
decide. Neither position carried the room. Instead, outside any authorized
process, wizard TomTraceback unilaterally deleted ("toaded") Bungle's
account — and it was that unilateral act that pushed the wizards to impose
balloting themselves from May 1, 1993 onward: any proposition drawing twice
as many votes for as against became binding. Lessig's own gloss on this
sequence is the single line most worth carrying out of the literature:
LambdaMOO's move to democracy "was not just an achievement. It was also a
failure. The space had failed... to engender values in its population
sufficient to avoid just the sort of evil Bungle had perpetrated... From a
space self-regulated to a space regulated by self." His broader argument
treats code as one of four regulators of behavior, alongside law, norms,
and market, and reads LambdaMOO as the case where code literally implements
the outcome of a vote — quoting Jennifer Mnookin's phrase, "politics
implemented through technology."

That democracy did not last as originally built. On May 16, 1996, the
wizards posted "LambdaMOO Takes Another Direction" — LTAND II — formally
reversing three and a half years of self-denial. Their reasoning is the
clearest statement in the literature of why a pure technical/social
separation cannot hold in a persistent world: "The line between 'technical'
and 'social' is not a clear one, and never can be. The harassment that
ensues each time we fail to achieve the impossible is more than we are now
willing to bear." The reversal was not a simple return to autocracy — it
reserved a right to "re-interpret and/or explicitly veto any clause of any
passed ballot," reserved a veto over Arbitrator decisions that impaired the
wizards' work, and, as a release valve, instituted a standing
shutdown-petition mechanism decided by simple majority with an eight-week
grace period. The community's own verdict survives as a primary artifact:
the ratifying ballot "LTAD-Legality" closed 321 in favor, 111 against, 272
abstaining — a legislature voting, and passing, on the legitimacy of its
own executive's self-coup.

Legal scholarship picked this arc up as a founding case for two projects.
Lastowka and Hunter's "The Laws of the Virtual Worlds," read here in full,
calls Curtis's announcement "a reverse coup d'état" and adds the crucial
qualifier: the abdication "was not as absolute as it may have seemed,"
since the wizards retained power to implement ballot outcomes as coded law,
so "no matter what they claimed, [they] were still wizards." Their wider
argument — that virtual harms deserve analysis through real legal
categories, not dismissal as "just pixels" — sits behind Joshua Fairfield's
narrower claim in "Virtual Property," also read in full: virtual objects
that are rivalrous, persistent, and interconnected deserve common-law
property protection rather than contractual EULA governance, with courts,
not legislatures, developing that doctrine, since a concentrated
intellectual-property lobby reliably beats a diffuse population of players
in any legislative arena.

The piece most directly useful for the note's second motivating question
— what actually restrains an operator no one can depose — is James
Grimmelmann's "Virtual Worlds as Comparative Law," read in full. Its Part V,
"The God Problem," opens from the observation that "there is no way to
depose the designers of a game," unlike a Lockean citizenry facing a
government. Grimmelmann catalogues three restraints that do function in
practice: market exit, a continuous rather than binary signal that happens
outside the game's own interface ("'Quit' is not really a command in the
game interface"); an international-law analogy in which players resemble
foreign investors who cannot repatriate capital, only sell their position;
and a due-process analogy, where designers bind their own discretion by
publishing the narrow domains — offensive speech, commercialization,
griefing — where they will actually intervene. He also gives the sharpest
treatment here of griefing as an institutional rather than definitional
problem, and identifies guilds — barely formalized by the software — as the
real locus of enforcement power, a claim this guide returns to below.

EVE Online supplies the note's other major case, unusually well
documented because its operator, CCP Games, publishes its own constitutional
text. The CSM White Paper, read here in full, is CCP's official account of
the Council of Stellar Management, EVE's player advisory body, and states
its own limits with striking candor: "Because EVE is a virtual society that
relies on the technical support of CCP, this model cannot be emulated,"
referring to the recallable legislatures ordinary democracies use. The same
document says CCP does not adjudicate in-world "crimes" — theft, fraud,
"murder" — so long as a player complies with the EULA's "meta-law": "under
no circumstances will the legislator interfere." That non-intervention
produces EVE's celebrated tolerated-scam culture, examined in Ian Brooks's
"Is Betrayal in EVE Online Unethical?", also read in full. Brooks quotes a
CCP developer directly — "scamming and unethical behavior...is not only
allowed, it is encouraged and rewarded by the game mechanics" — and
situates it against Marcus Carter's "ruthless game," where "players are
lawfully afforded the opportunity to act in ways that have great, negative
consequences on other players." The institutional fact Brooks documents
matters more than the ethical debate: EVE University maintains a volunteer
"Personnel Department" doing out-of-game counterintelligence against
infiltrators — an institution players built inside a still-running game to
manage a harm the operator declared out of its jurisdiction.

The literature's one cross-platform, quantitative result comes from Seth
Frey and colleagues' "Governing Online Goods," read here in full, which
applies Elinor Ostrom's Institutional Analysis and Development framework
across roughly 80,000 online communities — Minecraft servers, subreddits,
and private World of Warcraft servers — using Ostrom and Basurto's formal
typology of institutional statements. Two findings recur across all three
platforms: formalization rises with a community's age, population, and
engagement, and communities converge, independently, on weak norms rather
than strong requirements. This is the closest thing the field has to a
general law about how persistent-world institutions accrete, rather than a
single anecdote about one MOO or one spaceship game.

## 2. The load-bearing concepts

A handful of ideas recur across every source here and are worth holding
onto individually, not just as part of the LambdaMOO or EVE narratives.

**The god problem.** Grimmelmann's name for the fact that a persistent
world's operator cannot be deposed the way a government can: ordinary
political theory assumes an aggrieved citizenry can eventually replace its
rulers, but a designer-sovereign authors the very rules by which any
revolution would have to be conducted. The three restraints he identifies —
exit, the investor analogy, and constitutional self-binding — are not
solutions so much as partial, imperfect substitutes for the missing
mechanism of removal, and understanding why each is only partial is most of
the content of "The God Problem."

**Code as a regulator.** Lessig's argument, developed well beyond the
LambdaMOO case, is that behavior in any space is shaped by four forces —
law, social norms, market, and architecture (code) — and that code in
virtual worlds is unusually powerful because it can make an act flatly
impossible rather than merely discouraged. LambdaMOO is his paradigm case
because it shows code used to *implement* the outcome of a political
process, a vote becoming an enforced rule — a stronger thing than code
simply constraining behavior by default.

**Property in rewritable worlds.** Fairfield's triple — rivalrous,
persistent, interconnected — isolates the class of virtual objects that
behave enough like real property to deserve its legal protections, without
sweeping in ordinary consumable items: rivalrous if one person's control
excludes another's, persistent if it survives the owner logging off,
interconnected if other people can act on it. The argument is institutional
as much as definitional: Fairfield wants courts, not legislatures,
developing this law, because a concentrated industry lobby reliably
outmaneuvers a diffuse population of players in any legislature.

**Griefing as a community-relative charge, not a computable property of a
move.** The field's working definition — quoting Hunter and Lastowka's
unpublished "Virtual Crimes" via Grimmelmann — is "any activity that
genuinely bothers most players of the game in question," which Grimmelmann
says "renders futile the task of developing any general doctrine of virtual
criminal law," since it makes wrongfulness depend on a community's
after-the-fact reaction rather than any property inherent in the act. The
corollary this note treats as most load-bearing is an enforcement
degeneracy: "the set of unpleasant and wrongful acts players might wish to
deter is identical to the set of unpleasant and effective sanctions
available as deterrence," because a persistent world's community lacks the
state's monopoly on disproportionate punishment and cannot expel a player
beyond what quitting already does.

**Guilds as the real enforcement institution.** Grimmelmann's claim that
"whoever understands the nature of guilds will understand the nature of
community and community norm-enforcement in modern virtual worlds" points at
something the software barely formalizes: player organizations with almost
no mechanically granted power nonetheless do most of the actual governing,
through reputation, gatekeeping, and organized retaliation. EVE University's
Personnel Department, documented in Brooks, is the clearest instance in the
note's material — an institution built entirely by players, with no
charter from the operator, filling a gap the operator refused to fill.

**Institutional formalization as an empirical regularity, not a design
choice.** Frey et al.'s finding — formalization rises with a community's
age, size, and engagement across three unrelated platforms — reframes
everything above as an instance of a general pattern rather than unrelated
anecdotes: the LambdaMOO arc and EVE's advisory council are not
idiosyncratic outcomes of particular personalities, but predictable
products of communities maturing under a shared pressure.

## 3. How to read this literature

1. **Start with the LambdaMOO primary documents** — short, and the ground
   truth every other source narrates, so paraphrase can be checked against
   Curtis's own words.
2. **Read Lessig's LambdaMOO chapter next**, for the connective narrative —
   the Bungle incident, the vigilante-versus-democracy split, TomTraceback's
   unilateral act — plus the "regulated by self" framing later authors
   borrow or argue against.
3. **Read Lastowka and Hunter for the wider legal frame.** Their "reverse
   coup d'état" language supplies the field's vocabulary for designer
   power, and their Habitat discussion is an earlier instance of
   legislation by patch, worth holding against LambdaMOO's ballots.
4. **Read Grimmelmann's "Virtual Worlds as Comparative Law" as the
   conceptual spine** — "The God Problem," the griefing-futility argument,
   and the guilds claim are stated here more precisely than anywhere else
   that cites them secondhand.
5. **Read Fairfield for the narrower property argument**, once the
   governance vocabulary is in place — his courts-versus-legislatures claim
   only makes sense against how fragile the legislative alternative is.
6. **Read the CSM White Paper for EVE's own constitutional
   self-description**, including its admission that no recallable democracy
   "can be emulated" while the operator keeps technical control.
7. **Read Brooks for what that non-intervention actually produces**: the
   tolerated-scam culture, and the counterintelligence institution that
   grows in the resulting gap.
8. **Read Frey et al. last, as the empirical capstone**, generalizing what
   the LambdaMOO and EVE material shows about two communities into a
   cross-platform regularity, with its Ostrom and Basurto "state of nature"
   table stating the ungoverned baseline every community starts from.
9. **Treat Foo and Koivisto's griefing taxonomy and Carter's *Treacherous
   Play* as second-hand.** Both primary texts were paywalled; what
   circulates here is filtered through secondary sources and Brooks.

## 4. Where this touches the corpus

Every connection this section draws to the wider obsmetrologia apparatus is
graded **rhyme** — a suggestive structural resemblance the note is
proposing, not a fact established in the source literature, and none of it
should be read as a claim the cited authors themselves make.

The note reads the LambdaMOO arc as settlement-authority case law: LTAND
is an operator handing its closure authority to an open community process;
the Bungle crisis is that process failing to converge on a live breach; and
the unilateral toading, followed by LTAND II's reserved veto, is a
privileged fixed point reasserted from outside the normal closure — itself
then settled by the community's own ratifying ballot. Lessig's line, "from
a space self-regulated to a space regulated by self," is read as a
description of that sequence: an informal settling process superseded by a
formal, explicit one because the informal process failed to converge.

Grimmelmann's god problem is read as a translation of the corpus's
Player/Opponent dualizing involution breaking down: in a finite game the
designer and player occupy symmetric roles inside one event structure, but
in a persistent world the operator sits outside the arena, with the power
to rewrite its event structure mid-play. This translation belongs to the
note, not to Grimmelmann — he frames the material as sovereignty and
rule of law, not game semantics — and the critic's review has flagged this
mapping as aggressive, needing a check against Grimmelmann's own text before
it is leaned on further.

Griefing's community-relative definition is read as a charge that only
resolves through a community's own settling process, never computable from
the move alone — a Belnap-Dunn-flavored reading the note itself
proposes, not a claim Grimmelmann or Hunter and Lastowka make explicitly.
And Ostrom and Basurto's seven-item "state of nature" table, encountered
here only through Frey et al.'s use of it, is floated as a candidate
formalization of the bare, uncodec'd arena the corpus's admission-codec
apparatus builds up from — flagged for re-verification against Ostrom's own
text, since Frey et al. may have adapted the framework in a direction that
doesn't cleanly support it.

## 5. What we don't know yet

Three open problems sit at the center of this note, and none has a
settled answer yet. First, there is no formal account of what distinguishes
"the players changed the rules from inside" from an ordinary operator patch
— the petition-and-ballot system is the concrete case waiting to be
modeled. Second, it is unclear whether the enforcement degeneracy
Grimmelmann identifies — sanctions and wrongs drawn from the same set of
acts, with no expulsion beyond voluntary quitting — is a theorem that holds
for persistent worlds generally, or a contingency of the cases studied so
far. Third, every documented player legislature here is either merely
advisory (EVE's CSM), fully revocable (LambdaMOO's ballots, which the
wizards reserved the right to veto), or actively walked back (A Tale in the
Desert's lawmaking, known only through Grimmelmann's footnote) — leaving
open whether a genuinely binding player legislature is possible at all
under the god problem, short of players hosting the world themselves.

The reading debts behind these gaps are specific. Ostrom's *Governing the
Commons* has never been read in its own primary text — everything known
about her framework is filtered through Frey et al.'s application of it, a
real risk given how much weight the "state of nature" reading carries. Foo
and Koivisto's griefing taxonomy sits behind a paywall; the four-category
account reported here is reconstructed from secondary sources, not
verified against the paper's own wording. Marcus Carter's *Treacherous
Play* was blocked by a bot-wall; everything attributed to it is mediated
through Brooks's citations and a secondhand interview summary. A Tale in
the Desert's lawmaking documents have never been fetched, so the note's
only knowledge of its walked-back legislature is Grimmelmann's footnote.
And Bragg v. Linden Research (E.D. Pa. 2007) — the one piece of adjudicated
American case law on virtual property — has not been consulted, a real gap
given how much of Fairfield's and Lastowka and Hunter's argument concerns
what courts should eventually do; Mia Consalvo's *Cheating* would similarly
sharpen the norm-enforcement material. Until these are closed, this
literature's confidence rests on strong engagement with the LambdaMOO and
EVE material, and thinner ground wherever Ostrom, Foo and Koivisto, or
Carter are invoked.
