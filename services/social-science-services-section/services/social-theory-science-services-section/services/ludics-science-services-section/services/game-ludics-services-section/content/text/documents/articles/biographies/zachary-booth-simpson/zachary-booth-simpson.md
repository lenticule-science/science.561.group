Zachary "Zack" Booth Simpson served as Origin Systems' director of technology and a research fellow from 1992 to 1995, and returned as a consultant in 1998–1999 to study Ultima Online's economy. He went on to co-found Titanic Entertainment and later worked as an artist making interactive installations for museums; across his time at Origin he is credited on eleven games. He presented his UO economics research at the Computer Game Developer's Conference in San Jose in March 2000.

Simpson's load-bearing document for this portfolio is "The In-game Economics of Ultima Online" (Origin research report, 7 Apr 1999; presented at CGDC 2000), which the section treats as the founding document of the entire virtual-economy-management literature. It is unusually candid because Simpson was an Origin employee writing about his own team's mistakes in real time. UO's original economy was designed as a closed loop, on the theory that a fixed quantity of resources circulating from abstract to concrete and back would prevent inflation; it failed completely, but through hoarding rather than inflation — resources piled up in player inventories and simply sat there, monsters stopped spawning loot, and, in Simpson's words, "the world became boring and the players began to complain loudly." The fix introduced an explicit faucet (new resources created from nothing at a roughly constant rate) and drain (a terminal sink where items are destroyed) — the faucet/drain/sink vocabulary the field still uses. Simpson is explicit that the underlying problem was political, not technical: designers knew how to fix hoarding through taxes, decay, or maintenance fees, but "many of these drains feel too much like real world 'work' and are therefore politically unacceptable." He documents a second, independent failure: NPC shopkeepers ran "a very complicated artificial intelligence algorithm to simulate supply and demand," but because players had a non-demand incentive to produce — an "improve-by-doing" skill system that rewarded practice regardless of whether a market existed for the result — overproduction crashed prices and the shopkeepers "went broke." His verdict, stated flatly: "central planning doesn't work." The paper closes by proposing exactly the kind of standing telemetry apparatus the section's Obsmetrology of Play program takes as its subject — player-to-player, player-to-NPC, and player-to-vendor trade logs, stock levels, inflow and outflow, and player churn — framing UO as a unique research platform because, unlike a laboratory economy, "the commodities traded are virtual, [but] the resulting economies are not simulations": real preferences, operating in a long-term social environment.

Simpson's position in the field is that of the founder whose vocabulary everyone downstream inherited without necessarily crediting: Castronova's later academic treatment of Norrath, Guðmundsson's institutionalized EVE economist role, and Lehdonvirta and Castronova's textbook synthesis all work within a faucet/drain/sink frame that Simpson's 1999 postmortem established first, from inside a studio rather than from an economics department. One program of the section holds him: Obsmetrology of Play, where his UO postmortem stands as the founding case study of an arena that cannot reach a stable settled state on its own and instead requires the operator to maintain continuous instrumented observation and repeated intervention.
