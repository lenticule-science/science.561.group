Persistent world names both a property — persistence — and the class of worlds that have it. Persistence is the condition that a world's state (its objects, its inhabitants' possessions, the consequences of past action) continues to exist and to develop when no participant is observing or acting on it, and that nothing resets the world to a prior state between sessions. Richard Bartle's 2003 *Designing Virtual Worlds* states the condition directly: "the environment continues to exist and develop internally... even when there are no people interacting with it; this means it is persistent." Bartle treats this as one of five co-equal necessary conditions for a virtual world (see virtual-world), not as an optional feature layered on top of a game.

Raph Koster's "The Laws of Online World Design" gives the operator-facing cost of the same property: "Once you open your online world, expect to keep your team on it indefinitely. Some of these games have never closed." Persistence, on Koster's account, is not a design choice that can be quietly reversed — closing a persistent world "prematurely may result in losing the faith of your customers." Mark W. Bell's 2008 synthesis definition gives the phenomenological correlate: "A virtual world cannot be paused. It continues to exist and function after the participant has left... No longer is one participant the center of the world but a member of a dynamic community and evolving economy. A participant has a sense the systems in the space... exist with or without a participant's presence." This is the sentence this glossary treats as marking the exact place where a game, built around a player, becomes a world, which merely includes one.

Persistence is what removes a game's exit: no reset, no final position, no clean boundary behind which harm or attachment can be safely confined. That removal is the shared premise behind several other entries here — Bartle's player-types exist as standing feedback loops only because a population never resets between sessions; the change-persistence-grid cross-tabulates persistence against how much of a world's content the players themselves can alter; and persona-level identity investment intensifies specifically because nothing ever forces a clean break with a character. The section's own persistent-world-foundations research program reads the convergence of Bartle's, Koster's, and Bell's independent formulations — arrived at from world design, service operation, and definitional scholarship respectively — as evidence that the underlying property is real rather than an artifact of any one author's framing, while treating that convergence itself as consistency among descriptions of the same architecture, not as independent corroboration of any further claim.
