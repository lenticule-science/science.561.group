Avatar, in the vocabulary Richard Bartle sets out in *Designing Virtual Worlds*, names the shallowest of three levels of identification a player can have with the character they act through in a virtual world. At the avatar level, Bartle writes, "avatars are dolls" — the player manipulates the character as an extension of their will without emotionally investing in it as a self; a setback that befalls the avatar is experienced as a setback to a possession, not to the player.

The term is also used more loosely, and more commonly outside Bartle's identity chapter, simply to mean the on-screen figure through which a participant is represented — this is the sense in Mark W. Bell's 2008 synthesis definition of virtual world, "a synchronous, persistent network of people, represented as avatars, facilitated by networked computers," where "avatar" carries no claim about depth of investment at all. The two usages are compatible rather than contradictory: Bell's is the general representational sense that any virtual world requires by definition, while Bartle's is a claim about how much of the player's sense of self, if any, has migrated into that representation.

Bartle sets avatar against two deeper levels: character, where the player experiences the figure as something closer to a friend, and persona, where "you're not assuming an identity, you are that identity... if you're killed in a fight, you don't feel that your character has died, you feel that you have died" (see persona). Persistence is what makes the climb from avatar toward persona possible at all in Bartle's account — a figure that is deleted and remade every session cannot accumulate the history a persona requires, so the depth of identification a world's players reach is itself downstream of how persistent that world is (see persistent-world).
