Agon (agôn) is one of the four fundamental categories of play in Roger
Caillois's *Man, Play and Games* — play in the form of a contest whose
outcome depends on the competitors' own skill, strategy, strength, or
endurance, and in which the stakes are decided under conditions of
artificially equalized opportunity. Caillois's examples span chess,
racing, wrestling, and organized sport generally: activities in which
players are matched, rules and equipment are standardized, and victory
is meant to reflect a genuine, if narrow, superiority rather than an
accident of starting position.

The equalization is the load-bearing feature. An agonistic contest is
meaningful only insofar as the players began comparably situated; an
outcome decided by unequal starting resources is not agon in Caillois's
sense but a foregone conclusion dressed as a contest. This is why agon
generates so much apparatus around fairness — handicaps, weight classes,
standardized equipment, officiating — all aimed at holding constant
everything except the competitors' own capacities, so that what varies
in the outcome is attributable to them.

Caillois pairs agon with alea (chance) as a structural opposite: in
agon, the player is answerable for the outcome; in alea, the outcome is
explicitly surrendered to what the player cannot control. He treats the
two as answers to the same problem — the desire to have one's merit or
fortune recognized — approached from opposite directions: agon proves
merit by removing chance as an excuse; alea removes merit as a factor so
that fortune alone is on display.
