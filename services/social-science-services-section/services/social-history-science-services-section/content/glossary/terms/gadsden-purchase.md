The Gadsden Purchase (signed December 30, 1853; ratifications exchanged June 8, 1854) was the U.S. acquisition of 29,670 square miles of northern Mexico — the land south of the Gila River that is now southern Arizona and southwestern New Mexico — for $10 million. Its rationale was a railroad: the flattest southern route to the Pacific, promoted by negotiator James Gadsden and Secretary of War Jefferson Davis as the artery that would bind a slaveholding empire to the ocean.

The transaction's shape records the sectional conflict directly. Gadsden arrived instructed to offer up to $50 million for Baja California and much of northwestern Mexico; Santa Anna, bankrupt and facing American filibusters, sold only the minimal strip at $15 million; the U.S. Senate then cut both price ($10M) and territory, with northern senators deliberately shrinking the prospective slave domain. The line's price, extent, and course are artifacts of a fight over slavery, not of geography. The treaty also abrogated Article XI of Guadalupe Hidalgo — the U.S. obligation to police Indian raids into Mexico — and its Article VIII secured U.S. transit of persons, goods, mails, and "troops and munitions" across the Isthmus of Tehuantepec.

Nobody consulted the O'odham, whose lands ran from the Gila to the Sea of Cortez; the line bisected them, eventually sorting one people into "American Indians" and "Mexican Indians" without anyone moving. In Mexico the sale was named among the indictments of the Plan de Ayutla (March 1854) and helped end Santa Anna's rule for the last time (1855) — a boundary so illegitimate it finished the career of the man who signed it. The survey that materialized it was itself imprecise: Boundary Monument 127, near Lochiel, Arizona, was placed 4.41 miles from the treaty's intended coordinates in 1855 over the Mexican commissioner's objection, and the 1891–96 resurvey grandfathered the error into the permanent line.

The railroad came a generation late — Southern Pacific connected the southern transcontinental in 1881, built substantially by Chinese workers who were barred from the country by the Exclusion Act of 1882. The line the Purchase drew is now the route of the $46.5 billion border wall program (2025–), which at Lochiel runs through a cottonwood grove older than the Purchase itself.

The corpus's comparative case is the northern line: the [Northern Boundary Survey](./northern-boundary-survey.md) and the [Medicine Line](./medicine-line.md) — the same structure (empire-to-empire treaty, error-ridden survey ratified over its mathematics, Indigenous nations drawn through without consultation) with the opposite exclusion-polarity, sheltering for four years the Lakota the southern structure's descendants now wall.
