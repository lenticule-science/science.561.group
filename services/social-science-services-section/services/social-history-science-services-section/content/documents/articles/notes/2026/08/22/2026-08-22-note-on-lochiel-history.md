# The Gadsden line at Lochiel: history relative to the cottonwood grove

Compiled 2026-08-22 from six parallel research streams (Indigenous history of the valley; the Gadsden family and Purchase; Lochiel/San Rafael local history; riparian ecology; the material history of the line; the 2026 tree-sit and the cottonwood in ceremony), followed by a resolution pass against primary documents. Organizing frame: **the history of the line, told relative to the grove** — the grove as fixed point, the line as the late-arriving visitor. Current events are stated as of the most recent verified reporting (Aug 19, 2026). Genuinely open questions are gathered at the end; everything else is stated as the evidence supports.

Anchor source: **Hadley, Diana & Sheridan, Thomas E. 1995. *Land Use History of the San Rafael Valley, Arizona (1540–1960)*. USDA Forest Service Gen. Tech. Rep. RM-GTR-269** [H&S 1995], full text on archive.org.

## 0. Timeline skeleton

- **c. 1250–1400** — Salado-attributed sites in the valley. The Sobaipuri O'odham are documented on the San Pedro and upper Santa Cruz from the 13th–14th c. (Seymour's chronometric dating, 80+ sites); the San Rafael Valley itself shows no settlement at contact — it was shared hunting/gathering ground between Sobaipuri riverine villages and Apache seasonal mountain use.
- **1539** — Fray Marcos de Niza's expedition. His Cíbola report is doubted (Hallenbeck 1949: "a lying monk"); his route through this valley is a minority reconstruction (Wilson 1987) against the majority San Pedro route (Sauer 1932; [H&S 1995] concur with Sauer).
- **1690s–1711** — Kino mission era on the Santa Cruz (Guevavi, Tumacácori, San Xavier); Kino likely crossed the valley in 1692. Congregación concentrated O'odham communities and epidemic disease followed; the Sobaipuri as a named group dissolved into the Tohono and Akimel O'odham by the early-to-mid 1800s — the people persisted, the category did not.
- **1751–52** — Pima Revolt (Luis Oacpicagigua; surrendered March 1752, died in Spanish custody); Tubac presidio founded in response — and by 1787 its garrison was a company of O'odham soldiers.
- **1821–1825** — La Zanja surveyed (Oct 5, 1821); auction won by **Ramón Romero** for 1,200 pesos; the **San Rafael de la Zanja grant issued May 15, 1825** to Romero *and associated residents* of the Santa Cruz presidio — four square leagues held communally by roughly 18–30 parcioneros, conditioned on continuous occupation. If the age estimates for the Lochiel cottonwoods hold (see §1), the grove germinated in this same decade.
- **c. 1843** — Apache raid at La Boca de la Noria kills as many as 30 rancheros; permanent Mexican occupation of the valley ends.
- **1853–54** — **Gadsden Purchase** (§3). The line eventually splits the settlement at La Noria: the Mexican side keeps the name **La Noria**; the U.S. side becomes **Lochiel**.
- **1855** — Emory survey sets the Gadsden line (fieldwork complete Oct 15, 1855); 52 monuments border-wide, mostly dry-laid stone. **Monument 127, near Lochiel, was placed 4.41 miles off the treaty's intended coordinates.** Mexican commissioner Francisco Jiménez questioned the placement on June 15, 1855; Emory dismissed the concern. The 1891–96 Barlow-Blanco resurvey (monuments increased to 258, later 276) repaired the monument but left the boundary where the error put it — the mistake is grandfathered into the modern line. (Sources: Werne, *The Imaginary Line*; the Barlow-Blanco Commission's own 1898 report, via nomadicborder.com.)
- **1880–1884** — Rollin Richardson buys the grant (1880), sells to the **Cameron syndicate / San Rafael Cattle Company** (1883). Post office: Luttrell (1880) → **Lochiel** (1884).
- **1880s–1900** — The Camerons' twenty-year campaign to hold ~152,899 acres against the confirmed ~17,474; the **U.S. Supreme Court** confirms four leagues only (*United States v. Green*, 185 U.S. 256 (1902); Hadley & Sheridan give 1900 — discrepancy pending the CPLC case file). The 1891–93 drought kills 50–75% of herds territory-wide; arroyo-cutting begins on the Santa Cruz.
- **1902/03** — Cameron sells to **William Cornell Greene** ($1.5M). **June 1, 1906: Cananea strike** — 275 armed American volunteers cross into Mexico under Arizona Ranger Capt. Rynning to help suppress Mexican strikers at Greene's mine; ~20–33 dead; widely read as prefiguring the 1910 Revolution.
- **1909–11** — The first federally funded border fence anywhere is a **USDA cattle-tick quarantine fence** at Otay Mesa. The line hardened first against livestock disease.
- **1913** — The Santa Cruz River loses perennial flow (groundwater depletion, diversions, entrenchment — decades before climate change).
- **1918** — Battle of Ambos Nogales (Aug 27; up to 129 Mexican dead, including the mayor of Nogales, Sonora, shot approaching under a white flag) produces the first permanent fence dividing two border towns: 2 miles, 6 feet, barbed wire, $5,000.
- **1924** — Border Patrol founded.
- **1883 → 1934 → 1978** — The Sun Dance ban and its lifting (§7).
- **1939** — The **Fray Marcos de Niza monument** built at Lochiel by the National Youth Administration; dedicated April 15, 1939, with the governors of Arizona and Sonora attending (§6).
- **1955** — *Oklahoma!* filmed in the valley, chosen after actual Oklahoma proved too visibly modern to play itself.
- **1983** — The Lochiel port of entry closes (Reagan-era; Nogales International retrospective, corroborated by a Sept 1983 photo of customs agent Mike Danielson still staffing the gate). No official closure record surfaced; the "safety reasons" explanation in one USFS-derived summary is uncorroborated.
- **1994** — "Prevention Through Deterrence" formalized (§5).
- **1998–99** — Sharp family (owners since 1903) sell; Nature Conservancy bridge purchase; **San Rafael Ranch State Natural Area** created Jan 27, 1999.
- **July 4, 2025** — One Big Beautiful Bill Act: **$46.5B** for border barrier plus roads, cameras, lighting, and detection.
- **June 2025** — **$309.46M contract to Fisher Sand & Gravel** for the ~27-mile San Rafael Valley double wall (USASpending award 70B01C25F00000328). A separate **$1.495B "Tucson 2" contract** (Dec 2025, award 70B01C26F00000405) covers ~19 miles of primary + secondary wall and 136 miles of detection technology elsewhere in the sector; the "$334M" seen in some coverage is a rounding variant of the $309M award, not a third contract. DHS Secretary Noem's waiver for the San Rafael project set aside **34 federal laws** (NEPA, ESA, NHPA among them) under §102 REAL ID Act authority.
- **~March 27, 2026** — CBD's constitutional challenge to the waiver power (nondelegation theory) ends in summary judgment for the government (Judge Angela Martinez). Construction proceeds with no judicial check.
- **Late June 2026** — After an **Indigenous women youth-led solstice ceremony** at Lochiel, the cottonwoods begin to be called "**grandmothers**" (organizer Kate Scott, Madrean Archipelago Wildlife Center). June 18: CBP letter promises no felling if migratory birds are nesting.
- **July 27–28, 2026** — Fisher crews uproot **three of the four** cottonwoods. A local resident blocks the machinery at the fourth and is arrested (released without charges); volunteer Logan Miller is also arrested while monitoring. CBP says its surveys found no active nests; Sierra Club's Erick Meza says he has video of nesting activity from the day before. The dispute is unresolved; the June 18 promise and the absence of monitors at the felling are documented.
- **July 29, 2026** — Platform installed ~60 ft up the surviving Grandmother tree; the Center for Biological Diversity calls it "**the first tree sit aimed at stopping U.S. border wall construction**."
- **Aug 5–19, 2026** — Solidarity action at Fisher's Phoenix office (Aug 5); camp grows past 100 total participants, predominantly queer- and femme-led, rotating sitters by harness, pulley-bucket resupply, names withheld citing domestic-terrorism-charge fears; monsoon rains help stall construction. **Aug 17–19: the camp goes binational**, with a second sit a few feet south of the line in Sonora; two activists briefly detained by Mexican state police, no charges. Banners documented at different moments: "BORDERS KILL" and "From Minnesota to Mexico, resistance is fertile!" **As of Aug 19 (latest verified reporting): the sit continues in its third week; no removal attempt — felling a tree with a person in the canopy risks killing them — and no negotiated resolution.**
- **Aug 13–14, 2026 (parallel front)** — Judge Richard Leon (D.D.C.) denies the **Tohono O'odham Nation's** injunction against wall construction on its 62-mile boundary, reading the 1907 Roosevelt Reservation strip as permitting construction; "border security" interests "outweigh any surviving irreparable harms." Chairman Verlon Jose: "Too many critical issues were not adequately addressed... The Nation will consider all possible options." An appeal is under consideration; none filed as of Aug 19.
- **Aug 2026 (parallel front)** — **July 2026 was the deadliest month in two years** on the Arizona-Mexico border: 19 bodies recovered, against historically low apprehensions — the deterrence funnel operating independent of crossing volume. Cumulative: 4,500+ documented deaths on this border in 25 years. The Colibrí Center, the DNA-identification infrastructure for the dead, was administratively dissolved Dec 31, 2025.

## 1. The water and the grove

Cottonwoods germinate almost exclusively on fresh flood-recession alluvium — seeds viable one to five weeks, germinating within 48 hours on a suitable seedbed. Every cottonwood is the signature of one specific flood; a grove of mixed ages is an archive of floods. Dams and flow alteration measurably suppress recruitment.

The Lochiel trees are estimated at 200–250 years by Sierra Club and CBD field assessment. That is an estimate, not a ring count, and it sits at the outer edge of the species' documented longevity (typical maxima 130–150 years, though tree-ring work has dated individuals to 130–227 years) — plausible for exceptional trees at a perennial headwaters site. If it holds, germination falls in the 1820s: the grove and the land grant are contemporaries, both roughly thirty years older than the line.

Fremont cottonwood is a foundation riparian species: nest cavities for flickers, Gila and Lewis's woodpeckers, screech-owls, with 40+ secondary cavity users; Neotropical migrant stopover canopy; beaver forage (a nested keystone loop). Southwest riparian forest loss is best stated as Johnson & Haight (1984)'s **70–95% range** — the single "90%" figures in circulation derive from it.

The Santa Cruz River rises in the valley, flows south into Sonora, and loops back north into Arizona — crossing the border twice. It lost perennial flow by 1913; downstream reaches now run partly on treated effluent; climate modeling projects −15% precipitation driving roughly −22% streamflow with compounding recharge loss. The grove is precarious independent of the wall; construction water demands (on-site wells, concrete production) compound it — precedent from the 2019–21 build cycle includes Quitobaquito Springs drawn down to record lows and San Bernardino NWR pumping at DHS's own estimate of 83,000–100,000 gallons/day.

The valley is Southern Arizona's last unwalled major wildlife corridor: Sky Island Alliance cameras recorded 71 species in 90 days, 17 large species using gaps in the old vehicle barriers, and a monthly average of **5 human crossings versus 180 white-tailed deer**; a 30-ft wall with 4-inch bollard gaps is projected to cut all wildlife crossings by 86%. A male jaguar ("Cinco") was documented just north of the valley in June and late July 2026, mid-construction; the first evidence suggestive of a female in the borderlands came in early 2024; the nearest breeding populations of jaguar and ocelot are in Sonora and depend on this connectivity. The 2018 *BioScience* consensus paper (2,556 scientist signatories, 43 countries) found the border bisects the ranges of 1,506 native species, 62 of them IUCN-threatened.

## 2. Indigenous history of the valley and watershed

The Sobaipuri O'odham — Piman-speaking, riverine, canal-irrigating — held the San Pedro and upper/middle Santa Cruz from at least the 13th–14th century. Recent archaeology (Seymour) has substantially revised the older Bolton/Di Peso documentary picture, and restored O'odham agency to the contact record: Sobaipuri attacks are credited with destroying the Spanish townsite San Gerónimo III in 1541. The Hohokam→O'odham continuity question remains genuinely open in the field.

The San Rafael Valley itself was not village territory: Danson's 1941 survey found no Sobaipuri settlement at contact, and the valley reads as shared-use ground — Sobaipuri hunting and gathering, Chiricahua and Western Apache seasonal rounds in the surrounding ranges. [H&S 1995] acknowledge the valley is archaeologically under-studied relative to the river corridors.

Mission congregación brought disease and category-dissolution: by the early-to-mid 1800s the Sobaipuri had merged into the Tohono O'odham (notably at San Xavier del Bac / Wa:k) and Akimel O'odham. The 1853–54 line then bisected O'odham lands — which ran from the Gila to the Sea of Cortez — with no consultation. For decades the line was notional and O'odham crossed freely for kinship, subsistence, and ceremony (the Magdalena pilgrimage). Today the Tohono O'odham Nation counts ~34,000 enrolled members; its 2026 issue brief puts ~3,000 residing in Sonora (earlier sources gave 1,800–2,000; the figure moves with time and counting method). The Nation's 62 border miles run under the post-9/11 Enhanced Tribal Card regime, with documented frictions up to and including the 2018 deportation of two O'odham members from ancestral land.

"There is no O'odham word for wall" is the title of the Nation's February 2017 film (dir. Frances Causey) — institutional messaging rather than one person's quotable utterance. The directly attributable statements are Verlon Jose's: "Over my dead body will a wall be built" (Jan 2017) and "We do not own the land, but we care for the land. Every stick and stone is sacred."

Resistance to militarization long predates the current project: Ofelia Rivas and O'odham VOICE Against the WALL from the early 2000s; the February 2020 blasting of Monument Hill (Hia-Ced O'odham burial ground — Chairman Ned Norris Jr.: "totally forever damaged"); the September 2020 Quitobaquito blockade (Amber Lee Ortega and Nellie Jo David, whose arrests catalyzed the women-led O'odham Anti Border Collective); Elbit Systems surveillance towers on Nation land as a standing grievance. Coverage consistently situates this in Land Back terms: the wall as ongoing dispossession, not immigration policy. The Nation's current suit — injunction denied Aug 13–14, 2026, appeal under consideration — is the same front, in court.

## 3. The Gadsden family and the Purchase

**Christopher Gadsden (1724–1805)**: Charleston merchant, Sons of Liberty founder, "the Sam Adams of the South," 42 weeks in solitary at Castillo de San Marcos after refusing parole. He enslaved people at his Charleston properties and Beneventum plantation; his will divides "my negroes" into nineteen shares. His wharf — 840 feet, the largest in the colonies, built with enslaved labor, completed 1774 — became the principal landing of the U.S. slave trade's final legal years: an 1806 Charleston ordinance *required* slave ships to land there, and public-history estimates put ~100,000 enslaved Africans — on the order of 40% of all brought to what became the U.S. — through it across 1767–87 and 1803–08 (the estimate is widely used, including by the International African American Museum that opened on the site in June 2023, but lacks a published primary count). The famous flag: Gadsden sat on the Marine Committee that gave Commodore Esek Hopkins the yellow rattlesnake standard (flown on USS *Alfred*, Dec 1775) and presented a copy to the South Carolina Provincial Congress; whether he personally *designed* it is disputed (Whitney Smith called the attribution "often, falsely" made).

**James Gadsden (1788–1858)**, Christopher's grandson (son of Philip Gadsden): Yale 1806; Army engineer; Jackson's aide-de-camp in the First Seminole War, where his captured documents fed the Arbuthnot–Ambrister executions and his batteries forced Pensacola's surrender. In 1818 he rebuilt the destroyed Negro Fort — the maroon fort on the Apalachicola — and Jackson renamed it **Fort Gadsden**: his name written over a destroyed free Black settlement decades before it touched the border. He negotiated both Seminole removal treaties — Moultrie Creek (1823, with the warning that absent bands would be "forced to comply") and Payne's Landing (1832, disavowed by most Seminole leaders as coerced, feeding the Second Seminole War); both obligated Seminoles to surrender escaped enslaved people. He presided over the South Carolina Railroad (1840–1850), called slavery "a social blessing," and after California's free-state admission schemed to split it and plant a slaveholder colony there, tied to a slave-built railroad to the goldfields.

**The Purchase**: Pierce's administration, with Secretary of War Jefferson Davis the southern-route railroad advocate (holding stock in a railroad positioned to connect to it). Gadsden's instructions ran to $50M for Baja California and much of northern Mexico; Santa Anna — bankrupt, with William Walker's filibuster "Republic of Sonora" running concurrent with the talks — sold the minimal strip. Signed Dec 30, 1853 at $15M; the Senate cut it to **$10M and 29,670 sq mi**, northern senators shrinking prospective slave territory — the line's price and shape are artifacts of the sectional conflict. Ratifications exchanged June 8, 1854. The treaty abrogated Article XI of Guadalupe Hidalgo (the U.S. obligation to police Indian raids into Mexico), and its **Article VIII secured U.S. transit across the Isthmus of Tehuantepec** — persons, merchandise, sealed mails, and "prompt transit of troops and munitions of the United States." The sale is named among the indictments in the Plan de Ayutla (March 1854); Santa Anna fell for the last time in August 1855. The Crabb filibuster (1857, executed at Caborca) shows the acquisitive pressure did not stop at the new line.

The railroad rationale was fulfilled a generation late: Southern Pacific reached Tucson in March 1880 and connected the southern transcontinental in 1881 (full Sunset Route 1883), built in large part by Chinese workers — and the Chinese Exclusion Act was signed May 6, 1882, barring the labor force that had just built the line the Purchase was for.

## 4. The grant, the Camerons, and the valley

The 1825 grant was communal — Ramón Romero and 18–30 fellow parcioneros of the Santa Cruz presidio — and it was this commons that the Camerons enclosed. Chain of title: Richardson (1880) → **San Rafael Cattle Company** (Cameron syndicate, 1883; 300 shares at $500 to Pennsylvania investors) → **William Cornell Greene** (1902/03, $1.5M) → Sharp family (to Jan 1999) → Nature Conservancy bridge purchase → **San Rafael Ranch State Natural Area** (Jan 27, 1999; NRHP district 2008).

**The name.** Colin Cameron (b. 1849, Danville PA) and his brother Brewster were sons of a Danville farmer *also named Simon Cameron* (married to Elizabeth Leinbach) — a different man from Senator Simon Cameron, Lincoln's first Secretary of War, whom popular sources conflate with their father. [H&S 1995]'s description of Colin as the senator's nephew and "son of a railroad magnate" points to the family network around the senator's brothers (William Cameron of Lewisburg was the banker/railroad man); the exact uncle-link would take county records to close. What matters for the name: the senator's Harrisburg holdings already included a **"Lochiel" estate and the Lochiel Iron Company**, named for Clan Cameron's seat in Lochaber. The Arizona name arrived as a relay — cleared Highlands → Pennsylvania iron and war-department wealth → Arizona land claim.

**The enclosure.** The Camerons spent twenty years asserting a 16-league / 152,899-acre reading of the grant against the confirmed four leagues (~17,400 acres; Wasson's 1880 survey and the CPLC's 1899 confirmation differ by ~150 acres). Documented tactics: the Salcido house-burning prosecution (1885 — press verdict: "looked upon as land grabbers"), John Jameson's arson and attempted-murder allegation, fence enclosures ruled illegal by Judge Barnes (June 20, 1887), squatters described by Cameron as "worse than Apache Indians." The Supreme Court settled it (*United States v. Green*, 185 U.S. 256 (1902); H&S give 1900) (Justice White: "No duty rests on this government to recognize the validity of a grant to any area of greater extent than was recognized by the government of Mexico") — and Cameron held roughly nine times his confirmed acreage anyway, by cattle-as-occupation, until the sale.

**The catastrophe and the irony.** Arizona cattle went from 5,132 (1870) to an official 720,940 (1891) — with experienced stockmen privately estimating 1.5 million, double the books. The 1891–93 drought killed 50–75% of herds; Pima County's count fell 116,604 → 49,500 in a year; arroyo entrenchment followed the floods of 1890–92 across grazed-bare watersheds (the definitive treatment is Webb et al., *Requiem for the Santa Cruz*; current scholarship reads climate and overgrazing as interactive causes). [H&S 1995] draw the uncomfortable conclusion in a section titled "The Ecological Legacy of Colin Cameron": his monopolistic enclosure *excluded* the fragmented overstocking that destroyed neighboring valleys — "his actions may have kept the San Rafael Valley from becoming as degraded as other desert grassland ranges in southeastern Arizona." Greene and the Sharps continued conservative centralized management. **The intactness now defended at Lochiel — grove, grassland, jaguar corridor — is downstream of concentrated, often illegal, settler landholding.** The note keeps this without softening either half.

**Greene and Cananea.** Greene's copper empire at Cananea sat just south of the line; his cattle empire (~600,000 acres) straddled it. On June 1, 1906, Mexican miners struck over the two-tier wage ($1.50 vs $5 for the same day); roughly 20–33 people died, and **275 armed American volunteers crossed into Mexico** — at the Sonoran governor's invitation, against the Arizona territorial governor's orders — to help suppress the strike. The line stops people moving north; it has never stopped capital or its armed men moving south. Villa later raided the Greene ranch for horses and reportedly crossed at Lochiel.

**The town.** La Noria → Luttrell (P.O. 1880) → Lochiel (1884); peak ~400 residents, two smelters, three saloons, five stores; post office closed 1911; school built 1918, out of students by 1920; port of entry closed **1983**; the surviving structures (schoolhouse, teacherage, church, cemetery, customs house) stand on private land, and demolition of the customs house was reportedly considered in the current project, whose crews are removing the old barrier panel by panel. *Oklahoma!* was filmed in the valley in 1955 because Oklahoma itself had visible derricks and airplane noise — the settler pastoral could only be staged on land whose intactness the Cameron/Greene monopoly produced.

## 5. The line made material

The Gadsden line was surveyed by Emory 1854–55 (52 monuments, mostly dry-laid stone; Monument 127's 4.41-mile placement error per §0) and re-monumented by Barlow-Blanco 1891–96 (258 cast-iron obelisks at ~$150 each, later 276 total).

The hardening sequence runs: cattle-tick quarantine fence (1909–11, the first federally funded border fence); the Ambos Nogales fence (1918, after the battle); Border Patrol (1924 — 450 agents, self-supplied horses, no uniforms until 1928); Operation Wetback (1954 — INS claimed 1.1 million removals; historians put actual removals nearer 300,000, the gap being apprehension-counting and propaganda); then **"Prevention Through Deterrence"** — the *Border Patrol Strategic Plan: 1994 and Beyond*, approved by INS Commissioner Doris Meissner in August 1994 after Silvestre Reyes's unilateral El Paso blockade. The strategy deliberately pushed crossings into lethal terrain. Jason De León's summary in *The Land of Open Graves*: it "weaponized the natural environment against migrants." The toll: 7,800+ deaths nationwide 1998–2019; 4,500+ documented on the Arizona border across 25 years; **July 2026 the deadliest month in two years (19 bodies) against historically low apprehensions** — the funnel operates independent of volume. The Colibrí Center, which identified the dead by DNA, was administratively dissolved December 31, 2025.

Wall eras: Secure Fence Act 2006 (~654 miles of mixed barrier); Trump-era 2017–21 (~450 miles, mostly replacement — genuinely new primary wall possibly as little as ~40–47 miles — at ~$20.4M/mile, Fisher Sand & Gravel taking $2B+ in contracts); Biden pause then 2023 Texas resumption using the same §102 waiver mechanism; **OBBBA 2025: $46.5B**, "primary wall complete" targeted late 2027. The San Rafael segment: ~27 miles of double 30-ft bollard walls four feet apart with a patrol corridor, crossing the Arizona Trail and the Santa Cruz River twice, clearing a 150-ft swath; **$309.46M to Fisher (June 2025)**; **34 federal laws waived** in Noem's notice; Fisher's record includes a 2019 DOJ suit over defective Texas wall work; VisionQuest provides private security. The CBD suit challenging the waiver delegation itself died on summary judgment in March 2026.

## 6. The monument and the myth

The Fray Marcos de Niza monument at Lochiel was built in 1939 by the National Youth Administration — Depression jobs program, local teenagers — inside the statewide Coronado Cuarto Centennial apparatus (an act of Congress, a 10,000-spectator pageant, a 1940 stamp). Dedicated April 15, 1939, governors of two states attending, its inscription declares de Niza "the first European west of the Rockies" entered here, April 12, 1539.

Both layers of the claim are contested. The route through this valley is Wilson (1987)'s minority reconstruction; Sauer (1932)'s San Pedro route is the majority view and [H&S 1995] side with Sauer. And de Niza's underlying report is itself doubted — Hallenbeck called him "a lying monk"; his golden Cíbola dissolved on Coronado's arrival into stone and mud, and the account may have covered for turning back after the Zuni killed his enslaved scout Estevanico at Hawikuh. The settler Southwest monumentalized its origin at a place that origin may never have touched, commemorating a report that may have been a lie — while the grove, a verifiable two-century archive of floods, required no monument and got a wall.

## 7. The cottonwood in Lakota lifeway; the ban

The Lakota language holds two distinct terms here, and the distinction is itself the point: **wáǧačhaŋ** ("rustling tree") names the cottonwood as species — its leaves' sound understood as the tree's prayer — while **čhaŋwákȟaŋ** ("sacred tree") names the Sun Dance pole: the tree's ritual status, not its botany. A tree becomes čhaŋwákȟaŋ by relation and ceremony. (Plains ceremony uses *Populus deltoides*; Lochiel's trees are the Southwestern relative *P. fremontii* — kin across species, and the note says so plainly.)

The rite, as long published through Black Elk (Joseph Epes Brown, *The Sacred Pipe*, 1953, Sun Dance chapter "Wiwanyag Wachipi," pp. 67–100; see also Neihardt, *Black Elk Speaks*, 1932): scouts select the tree; a ceremonial strike at the four directions before felling; the tree must not touch the ground as it falls; carried with four stops, decorated with chokecherry, cloth, and tobacco; raised as the center of the arbor, tethering the piercing dancers. Why cottonwood: the five-pointed star in the cut pith (the morning star); the leaf that folds into a tipi pattern; bark horses could eat; and Black Elk's line — "even in the slightest breeze you can hear the voice of the cottonwood tree; this we understand is its prayer to the Great Spirit." These details are attributed to Black Elk via two non-Native interlocutors; Sun Dance is living religion, and downstream writing attributes accordingly rather than citing "Lakota belief" generically.

The ban, precisely: Interior Secretary Henry Teller's letter of March 30, 1883 ("heathenish dances, such as the sun-dance") → Commissioner Hiram Price's **Code of Indian Offenses** and its agent-controlled Courts. The penalty for sacred dances was **withholding of rations** — starvation as enforcement. Administratively ended by **Collier Circular No. 2970 (1934)** ("no interference with Indian religious life or ceremonial expression will hereafter be tolerated"), though local suppression persisted informally for decades; statutory protection came only with **AIRFA, August 11, 1978** — itself a policy statement without a private right of action, as *Lyng* (1988) and *Smith* (1990) later exposed.

Borderlands nations: Quitobaquito Springs — **A'al Vaipia** (also rendered A'al Waipia; the v/w varies by O'odham orthographic convention; NPS itself offers no O'odham name) — is a cottonwood-ringed O'odham site with 16,000 years of documented use, damaged by the 2020 wall build. Beyond that, O'odham and Apache relationships to cottonwood specifically are thinly documented in accessible sources (O'odham calendar sticks are saguaro rib; basketry is beargrass, yucca, devil's claw); the tree-sit activists' statement that the cottonwoods are "sacred to the Tohono O'odham and Yaqui people" is their assertion, with no tribal statement located. Proper sourcing would go through Amadeo Rea's ethnobotany and tribal cultural-resource offices.

## 8. The tree-sit and its lineage

The full timeline is in §0. The lineage that situates it: Chipko (1973, Uttarakhand — women embracing trees; bodily interposition before arboreal occupation); the Pacific Northwest timber wars and Redwood Summer (1990); Julia Butterfly Hill's 738 days in Luna (1997–99, won by negotiation); Fairy Creek (2020–21, 1,100+ arrests — Canada's largest civil disobedience — resolved through Pacheedaht Nation authority, a template partially echoed in the Tohono O'odham Nation's trespass notices to contractors at Lochiel); Weelaunee/Stop Cop City (2021–23, including the killing of Manuel "Tortuguita" Terán in January 2023 and the domestic-terrorism charging strategy that Lochiel participants now cite for their anonymity); Standing Rock (2016) as the encampment-defense frame the "land defender" language inherits.

Framing note, per this repository's own critique of movement historiography (Citing for Containment): the sources support telling this as **a response from the watershed and its peoples** — the grandmother naming preceded the felling; O'odham resistance preceded the project by decades — not as a reaction to a wall.

## 9. Cross-cutting threads (analysis hooks)

1. **Naming as relay**: Lochaber → Simon Cameron's Pennsylvania "Lochiel" holdings → the Arizona claim. Settler nostalgia hands grief forward as deed. Compare Fort Gadsden over the destroyed Negro Fort; La Noria surviving only on the Mexican side of a split town.
2. **The line as sectional artifact**: price and shape set by a Senate fight over slavery's expansion; rationale a slaver's railroad; fulfillment built by workers excluded the same year.
3. **The filing system**: wall = "infrastructure" ($46.5B); tree = "environment" (34 laws waived in one notice). The §102 waiver makes the filing literal.
4. **Asymmetric permeability**: 1906, armed Americans cross south to break a strike; 2018, O'odham deported from ancestral land; projected 86% cut in wildlife crossings. The line filters by category, not geography.
5. **Monopoly-as-conservation**: the intactness being defended is downstream of illegal enclosure ([H&S 1995]'s own argument). Honest history holds both halves.
6. **Myth infrastructure vs. living infrastructure**: the state monumentalized the doubtful (a contested route for a doubted report) and bulldozes the demonstrable (the flood archive) — and the line itself is 4.41 miles off its own treaty at this very monument.
7. **Enforcement asymmetry as the line's constant**: rations withheld to stop the Sun Dance; deterrence-by-desert; never enforcement of the state's own obligations. (Connects to the glossary's property-as-disposition: the line as a bundle of exclusion-powers, selectively energized.)
8. **The counting crisis**: cattle books at half of reality (1891); Wetback's inflated million; Colibrí dissolved. Settler accounting inflates what justifies power and loses what indicts it.
