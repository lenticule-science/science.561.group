# The line and the other nations

The Lakota named the 49th parallel the "medicine line" — a boundary with the seeming magic of stopping U.S. cavalry cold, as it did in 1877 when it let Sitting Bull's Húŋkpapȟa cross into Canadian exile after the Little Bighorn (Utley, *The Lance and the Shield*, 1993; LaDow, *The Medicine Line*, 2001). But the Lakota were not the only nation the line was drawn through, and the parallel's history looks different from each side of it. This note gathers the comparative ground: the Dakota refugees of 1862-63, the Blackfoot Confederacy split down the middle, Big Bear's and the Cree's 1880s crossings, and the Métis whose economy the line eventually helped kill — plus a plain accounting of how each nation's crossing rights stand as of 2026. Citation status is marked inline; most claims here rest on multiply-attested secondary scholarship and named institutional sources rather than primary documents read directly.

## The Dakota flight, 1862-63

The U.S.-Dakota War broke out in southern Minnesota in August 1862 after the Dakota, driven to starvation by late and stolen annuity payments and squeezed onto a shrinking reservation strip along the Minnesota River, attacked settlements and Army posts (attested-secondary, standard account). The war ended in Dakota defeat within six weeks. A military commission tried 498 Dakota men in the following weeks — trials that in many cases ran under five minutes, without defense counsel, in a language most defendants did not speak — and sentenced 303 to death. President Lincoln personally reviewed the case files and commuted all but 39; one further man was reprieved. On **December 26, 1862**, the U.S. Army hanged **38 Dakota men** simultaneously on a single scaffold at Mankato, Minnesota — the largest mass execution in United States history, before an estimated crowd of 4,000 (attested-secondary: Minnesota Historical Society, Equal Justice Initiative, Indian Country Today all concur on the count and date; it was later established that at least one of the 38, Wičhaŋȟpi Wašteday, was hanged by mistake).

Survivors and refugees who had not been captured fled north. Roughly 1,000 Dakota reached Fort Garry (present-day Winnipeg) by November 1862; a further wave — 445 people, described in the record as being "in a state of absolute starvation" — arrived in December 1863 after a second U.S. attack on a refugee camp near the border. Estimates put the total flight across the two years at approximately 1,500 people (attested-secondary: Minnesota Historical Society *U.S.-Dakota War* project). Canada's government did not extend treaty status to these arrivals — it had no treaty relationship with them to begin with, since the Dakota's alliance had been with the British Crown going back to the War of 1812, not with Confederation-era Canada — but land was set aside beginning in **1874**, with two reserves chosen in 1875 at Oak River and Birdtail Creek in present-day Manitoba. Standing Buffalo's band, driven west to Wood Mountain after conflict with the Saulteaux, received a reserve near the Qu'Appelle Lakes in **1881**. A separate Lakota community at Wood Mountain, Saskatchewan — descended from Hunkpapa and other bands who stayed in Canada after Sitting Bull's own return south — held only a temporary reserve from 1910, formalized by Order-in-Council in 1930.

For over 150 years the Dakota and Lakota communities in Canada were administratively filed as "refugees" rather than as "Aboriginal peoples of Canada" under Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 — a filing that denied them the land and funding entitlements treaty First Nations received. That changed on **July 15, 2024**, when Crown-Indigenous Relations Minister Gary Anandasangaree delivered a formal statement of recognition and apology at a ceremony at Whitecap Dakota Nation, Saskatchewan, attended by roughly 650 people. The apology named nine communities — Birdtail Sioux Dakota Nation, Canupawakpa Dakota Nation, Dakota Plains Wahpeton Nation, Dakota Tipi, Sioux Valley Dakota Nation, Standing Buffalo Dakota Nation, Wahpeton Dakota Nation, Whitecap Dakota Nation, and Wood Mountain Lakota — as Aboriginal peoples of Canada holding constitutionally protected Section 35 rights, correcting the refugee designation (attested-secondary: Government of Canada/CIRNAC statement, CBC News, JURIST). The apology recognized status; chiefs at the ceremony pressed for it to be followed by land return and funding, which remain separate, ongoing negotiations rather than accomplished facts as of this writing (unpinned: whether specific land or funding transfers have since been concluded was not confirmed).

## The Blackfoot Confederacy, split down the middle

The Blackfoot Confederacy (Niitsítapi, also rendered Siksikaitsitapi) is composed of four nations sharing language and ceremony: the Siksika ("Blackfoot" proper), the Kainai (Blood), the Northern Piikani (Peigan), and the Southern Piikani, known in the United States as the Amskapi Piikani or Blackfeet. Before the boundary existed, the confederacy's territory ran from the North Saskatchewan River in present-day Alberta south to the Yellowstone in Montana — one people, moving as weather and buffalo dictated. The 49th parallel was surveyed through the middle of that territory by the joint British-American North American Boundary Commission between 1872 and 1874; the commission wintered at Willow Bunch in 1873-74 and placed its final marker at Waterton Lake on **August 8, 1874** — inside Blackfoot country (attested-secondary: multiple boundary-commission histories agree on dates and route).

Treaty separated the confederacy formally on each side of the new line. In the United States, Lame Bull's Treaty of **1855** set aside roughly 20 million acres in Montana for the Blackfeet and allied nations; executive orders in 1873 and 1874 and the 1888 Sweet Grass Hills agreement cut that down repeatedly, leaving the present Blackfeet Reservation at about 1.5 million acres in Glacier County, Montana. In Canada, Treaty 7 was concluded on **September 22, 1877**, at Blackfoot Crossing on the Bow River — Crowfoot (Isapo-muxika) signing for the Siksika, alongside Kainai and Piikani leaders — establishing the reserves the Siksika, Kainai, and Piikani nations hold in southern Alberta today (attested-secondary: standard treaty scholarship; Glenbow Museum, Library and Archives Canada).

The split shows in the numbers. The Blackfeet Nation in Montana has roughly 17,000 enrolled members with about 8,500-10,500 living on the reservation; across the border, the Kainai (Blood Tribe) — Canada's largest reserve by land area — has a registered population around 12,900, Siksika Nation around 7,700-7,800, and Piikani Nation around 3,900 (attested-secondary: tribal and Statistics Canada figures, cross-checked across sources). One confederacy; four nation-state-recognized governments; four separate band or tribal rolls; two federal bureaucracies neither of which administers the whole.

## Big Bear, the Cree, and the 1880s crossings

Mistahi-maskwa (Big Bear), a Plains Cree chief, refused to sign Treaty 6 in 1876 and spent the following years trying to unite Cree bands into a confederation that could negotiate as a bloc rather than accept scattered reserves. From roughly 1876 to 1882 he and his followers moved south into Montana, hunting the last of the northern buffalo herds alongside American Indians and other Canadian treaty Cree who joined him there — crossing a border neither government yet enforced against Indigenous travel (attested-secondary: Dictionary of Canadian Biography). Starvation and the collapse of the buffalo economy forced Big Bear to sign Treaty 6 in December 1882, the last major Cree leader to do so.

In 1885, during the North-West Resistance, warriors from Big Bear's band killed nine people at Frog Lake (present-day Alberta) on April 2 — an act Big Bear had opposed and did not order, though he bore responsibility for it in the eyes of Canadian authorities. He surrendered at Fort Carlton on **July 2, 1885**, was convicted of treason-felony that September, and served time at Stony Mountain Penitentiary before dying, in ill health, on the Poundmaker Reserve on January 17, 1888. His son, Ayimisīs (rendered in English-language sources as Little Bear or Imasees), led a separate band of Cree south across the border into Montana after Frog Lake, seeking refuge near Fort Assiniboine — distinct from an unrelated Cree chief, Apischiskoos (also called Little Bear), who was hanged with seven others at Fort Battleford on November 27, 1885, in the largest mass execution in Canadian history (attested-secondary: sources on this Battleford/Montana distinction are consistent but this note has not verified it against a primary trial or Indian Affairs record). In **1896** the United States deported Ayimisīs, fellow leader Lucky Man, and several hundred landless Cree and Ojibwe back to Canada. Many nonetheless drifted back south, and in **1916** the U.S. government established Rocky Boy's Indian Reservation in Montana — combining Stone Child's ("Rocky Boy's") landless Chippewa with Ayimisīs's Cree followers into what is now the Chippewa Cree Tribe, the border crossing resolved only by the border-holder deciding, after three decades of churn, to finally file these people as domestic.

## The Métis and the line

The Métis Nation's relationship to the 49th parallel predates the boundary survey: the Red River Métis buffalo-hunting brigades of the mid-1800s ranged with Red River carts from Manitoba through the Cypress Hills and into Montana Territory as a matter of course, wintering (as "hivernants") on whichever side the herds required, with no meaningful boundary in practice until the Commission's survey work of 1872-74 (attested-secondary: Manitoba Historical Society; academic literature on Cypress Hills hivernant communities). Métis guides and hunters were themselves hired by the Boundary Commission to provision and navigate the survey that would formalize the line running through their own hunting grounds. As the buffalo herds collapsed in the late 1870s, Cypress Hills Métis petitioned Canada in **1878** for a reserve running 150 miles west from the Pembina River along the border — a request the government declined.

Louis Riel, who had led the Métis provisional government during the 1869-70 Red River Resistance, spent 1879-1884 in exile south of the line in Montana — trading out of Fort Benton, becoming a naturalized U.S. citizen in 1883, and teaching at St. Peter's Mission on the Sun River. A Métis delegation led by Gabriel Dumont crossed the border to fetch him back in June 1884, and Riel returned to lead the North-West Resistance of 1885, which ended in Métis defeat at the **Battle of Batoche, May 9-12, 1885**. Riel was tried for treason and hanged at Regina on **November 16, 1885** (attested-secondary: standard Canadian historiography, cross-checked across multiple sources). Unlike the Blackfoot or the Cree, the Métis had no analog of treaty on either side of the parallel to fall back on; their border history is a slower dispossession — first mobility, then land scrip fraud in Canada, then no formal U.S. recognition at all until the 21st century.

## Crossing rights today

Article III of the **Jay Treaty (1794)** — predating the 49th parallel's own survey by eighty years — guarantees "Indians dwelling on either side of the boundary line" the right to pass freely across it, and Congress codified that right for U.S. entry in the Immigration and Nationality Act (8 U.S.C. §1359) in 1952. In practice the right is unevenly delivered:

- **Blackfoot/Blackfeet**: covered by the Jay Treaty in principle; Blackfeet members report inconsistent enforcement, with U.S. officers sometimes demanding passport-equivalent documents beyond tribal ID despite the treaty's plain language (attested-secondary: Native News reporting).
- **Cree and Chippewa Cree**: Rocky Boy's members qualify under the same Jay Treaty/§1359 framework as other federally recognized U.S.-side or Canadian status Indians with 50% documented Indigenous blood quantum.
- **Métis**: not reliably covered. U.S. border officers do not accept Métis citizenship cards as standalone proof, and Métis applicants must separately document 50% Indigenous ancestry by other means — a documentary burden the Jay Treaty's drafters did not anticipate and that leaves Métis crossing rights the least secure of any nation in this note (attested-secondary: legal-aid and immigration-law guides on Jay Treaty administration).
- **Dakota/Lakota**: crossing rights run through the same Jay Treaty framework, but only now sit on a corrected footing on the Canadian side after the 2024 apology's Section 35 recognition — 162 years after the flight that made the crossing necessary in the first place.

## The comparative point

Every nation in this note crossed a line that was, for decades, largely notional to the people living across it and increasingly solid to the states administering it. What differs is not that a line was drawn through peoples — the Gadsden Purchase line did that too, further south, and is this corpus's other worked case — but how each nation's permeability history worked out, and that difference is itself data for this corpus's argument. The [exclusion-powers](../../../../../../glossary/terms/exclusion-powers.md) at the 49th parallel were never symmetrical: the same boundary that offered Sitting Bull genuine asylum in 1877 offered Ayimisīs's Cree only twenty years of administrative limbo before deportation, and offered the Métis nothing recognizable as a treaty relationship on either side. Read through [property-as-disposition](../../../../../../glossary/terms/property-as-disposition.md) rather than as a fixed geographic fact, the medicine line's power to admit or expel was energized differently against each nation depending on what filing category the receiving state had available for them — refugee, treaty Indian, landless band, no category at all. That selective energizing, and the decades- or century-long lag between a nation's crossing and its administrative recognition (1862 to 2024 for the Dakota; 1885 to 1916 for the Cree), is exactly the mechanism [settlerism-as-filing-system](../../../../../../glossary/terms/settlerism-as-filing-system.md) names: the border's force was never primarily about where the line ran, but about which filing a state was willing to open for the people it ran through.
