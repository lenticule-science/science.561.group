# The exile in Grandmother's Land: Sitting Bull, 1877-1881

Compiled 2026-08-22 as a foundation note for the Medicine Line research corpus, ahead of the [Wood Mountain Lakota First Nation](../../../../../../glossary/terms/wood-mountain-lakota.md) glossary entry. Sources drawn on: Ernie LaPointe's *Sitting Bull: His Life and Legacy* (2009) — the account authorized by Sitting Bull's (Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake's) direct descendants, and the source this note privileges wherever it and the standard scholarship diverge; Robert M. Utley's *The Lance and the Shield: The Life and Times of Sitting Bull* (1993), the fullest single scholarly biography, whose chapters on the Canada years (pp. 183-233) are the standard citation; Joseph Manzione's *"I Am Looking to the North for My Life": Sitting Bull, 1876-1881* (1991), a monograph built on U.S. and Canadian government records and focused specifically on the diplomatic standoff the exile produced; and Beth LaDow's *The Medicine Line: Life and Death on a North American Borderland* (2001), which reads the episode as a founding case study of the border itself. Citation status is marked per claim per house method; none of these books has been read in full for this note — claims attributed to a named author come from that author's material as represented in reviews, syntheses, and excerpts located via search, not from the book cover to cover, and are flagged **attested-secondary** rather than **primary-verified** throughout. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is reported rather than resolved.

## The crossings, and why the numbers disagree

There was no single crossing. The Hunkpapa and allied Lakota bands broke apart after the June 1876 destruction of Custer's column and drifted north over roughly a year, in waves, not as one column under one leader — which is the first thing that gets flattened when the story is told as "Sitting Bull led 5,000 people across the border." **Attested-secondary** (Utley; Manzione; corroborated by the University of Saskatchewan's *Indigenous Saskatchewan Encyclopedia*): Hunkpapa chiefs Four Horns and Black Moon, with smaller followings, crossed into the North-West Territories in late 1876, ahead of Sitting Bull himself. Sitting Bull's own band crossed the line in **May 1877**, coming up from Montana past Pinto Horse Butte to the Wood Mountain country; Utley gives the specific figure for this band as roughly **1,000 people in 135 lodges** — travel-worn, many lodges patched after a Missouri River flood had swept through their village that March — accompanied by smaller allied groups, a band of Sans Arc under Spotted Eagle and Minneconjou under Swift Bird. NWMP Inspector James Morrow Walsh, meeting them, made his own count that spring at roughly **2,900 people** (500 men, 1,000 women, 1,400 children) with about 3,500 horses — a number close to but not identical with Utley's, and covering a somewhat different population (Walsh was counting everyone then in the Wood Mountain camps, not only Sitting Bull's own band). By contrast, some general-audience accounts (repeated in local and popular history pieces, e.g. SaskToday's "History Corner" column) give a round **5,000** for "Sitting Bull and 5,000 Sioux" crossing — a figure that reads as an aggregate of all the Lakota refugee bands accumulated in Canada by mid-1877, not a count of one crossing or one leader's following. The house rule applies directly here: any number attached to "Sitting Bull crossed with N people" needs to specify which count, whose band, and which month — the sources genuinely do not agree because they were not all counting the same event.

## Grandmother's Land

**Attested-secondary**, corroborated across multiple sources including Lakota-language reference material: the Lakota name for Canada was Uŋčíyapi Makȟóčhe, "Grandmother's Land" or "Grandmother's Country" — "Grandmother" (uŋčí) being the term applied to Queen Victoria, on the pattern by which the United States and its president were sometimes rendered through a parallel kinship idiom. The usage predates Sitting Bull's own crossing: Dakota bands fleeing the 1862 U.S.-Dakota War in Minnesota had already used Canada, and the Grandmother idiom, as a refuge fifteen years earlier. For Sitting Bull's people in 1877, "Grandmother's Land" carried a specific, almost legal weight: it named a polity from which they hoped to receive the protection a grandmother owes a grandchild — food, land, standing — under a kinship framing of sovereignty that the Canadian government never actually honored in practice (see below). The name is not decorative; it is a claim about what relationship the Lakota believed, or hoped, they were entering into.

## Wood Mountain and the Walsh relationship

Wood Mountain, in the North-West Territories (present-day southern Saskatchewan), was the general encampment area — not a single fixed village but a shifting complex of camps across the Wood Mountain uplands and nearby Cypress Hills, close to the NWMP's small post there. **Attested-secondary** (Utley; Manzione; Dictionary of Canadian Biography entry on Sitting Bull): NWMP Inspector (later Superintendent — the "Major" honorific attached to his name in popular usage reflects his eventual senior rank rather than a formal military commission) James Morrow Walsh met Sitting Bull's band on arrival, told them plainly that they were now on British soil, subject to British law, and could not use Canadian territory as a base for raids back across the border — terms Sitting Bull's people appear to have kept. Walsh and Sitting Bull developed a documented personal rapport over the following years; Sitting Bull is recorded as having given Walsh a war bonnet, and Walsh brokered a peace meeting between Sitting Bull and Blackfoot leader Crowfoot, longtime enemies, that held.

The house caution here is that this relationship's popular legacy — "Sitting Bull's Boss," the subject of at least one modern popular biography devoted to the friendship (Ian Anderson's *Sitting Bull's Boss: Above the Medicine Line with James Morrow Walsh*) — considerably overstates what the friendship actually accomplished or what it meant institutionally. **Attested-secondary**: Walsh's superiors in Ottawa came to regard his closeness to Sitting Bull as an obstacle rather than an asset, precisely because it was not producing the one outcome Ottawa wanted — Sitting Bull's voluntary return south. Walsh was reassigned to Fort Qu'Appelle in the summer of 1880, and his replacement, Superintendent Lief (Léval) Crozier, did not sustain anything like the same rapport. Walsh could offer Sitting Bull consistency and a degree of honesty about Canadian law; he could not, and by his own government's later judgment did not even try hard enough to, deliver what Ottawa actually wanted from the relationship, and he had no power at all over the policy question that determined the exile's outcome: whether the Lakota would get land.

## Canadian policy: refusal of a reserve, and starvation as leverage

This is the load-bearing historical fact of the exile, and it is well attested across every scholarly source consulted (Utley, Manzione, the Dictionary of Canadian Biography, and the Indigenous Saskatchewan Encyclopedia converge on it without contradiction — **attested-secondary** at high confidence). The Canadian government's position, held consistently from 1877 through 1881, was that it would not grant the Lakota refugees a reserve, would not recognize them as "British Indians," and would not feed them. This was a real distinction with teeth: Canada was bound by its own numbered treaties to provision Indigenous nations that had signed them; the Lakota, as foreign refugees who held no treaty with the Crown, fell outside that obligation as Ottawa construed it, and Ottawa was explicit that it intended to keep them outside it, precisely so as not to create an incentive for them to stay. Sitting Bull petitioned repeatedly, through Walsh and directly, for a reserve; the answer, restated across several years by successive officials, was no. The refusal was not merely administrative indifference — Manzione's monograph, built on the government correspondence itself, frames it as a deliberate three-government standoff (Washington did not want the Lakota back either, at least not on favorable terms; Ottawa did not want them at all) in which denying subsistence was the mechanism by which Canada expected the problem to resolve itself. It did.

## The buffalo collapse

**Attested-secondary**, consistent across sources: the northern buffalo herds that had sustained Plains life were already collapsing from decades of commercial hide-hunting on both sides of the border, and the process accelerated sharply during exactly the years of Sitting Bull's exile. By autumn 1878 — a little over a year after Sitting Bull's own band arrived — the herds that had still been reaching Canadian territory largely failed to appear; some accounts additionally report American traders deliberately setting prairie fires along the border in 1879 to discourage what herds remained from crossing north, though this specific claim is reported at only one remove here and should be treated as **unpinned** pending a primary or more clearly sourced secondary citation. Whatever the precise mechanism, the effect is not in dispute: by 1878-79 the food base the Lakota needed to survive independently in Canada was gone, and Canadian policy supplied nothing in its place. Small bands began peeling off to surrender at U.S. posts as early as July 1879 — roughly 200 to 300 lodges surrendering that summer at Fort Keogh — years before Sitting Bull himself gave up the position. The buffalo collapse did not cause the Canadian refusal of a reserve; it made that refusal lethal, on a timeline the government could simply wait out.

## The surrender at Fort Buford, July 19-20, 1881

By spring 1881, Sitting Bull's remaining following in Canada had shrunk to a small remnant of what had crossed in 1877. **Attested-secondary**, converging across Utley, the Wikipedia synthesis of the standard record, and contemporary press: guided south by Métis trader Jean-Louis Légaré, Sitting Bull and roughly 186-187 people (accounts differ by a person or two depending on how the census was taken and when) arrived at Fort Buford, in Dakota Territory, on **July 19, 1881**. The formal surrender ceremony took place the next morning, **July 20, 1881**, in the commanding officer's quarters, before Major David H. Brotherton.

The famous episode: Sitting Bull had his young son, Crow Foot (not to be confused with the Blackfoot leader Crowfoot, whom Sitting Bull had befriended in Canada — a coincidence of names the sources are careful to disentangle), carry his Winchester rifle forward and hand it to Brotherton, rather than surrendering it himself directly. The widely quoted words — "I surrender this rifle to you through my youngest son... I wish it to be remembered that I was the last man of my tribe to surrender my rifle" — are reported in contemporary telegraphed newspaper dispatches from the surrender, including the *Bismarck Tribune* (July 21, 1881) and the *Wheeling Intelligencer* (July 21, 1881, which also notes that no formal speeches were given that first day because "Sitting Bull & his orator" were fatigued from the journey). This gives the quote a real basis in contemporaneous press record rather than later invention — but it reaches this note only as **attested-secondary at one remove**: this note has not read the original 1881 newspaper columns directly, only modern accounts and excerpts that cite them, and the exact wording varies slightly across the retellings that circulate today. Readers should treat the sentiment and the act (son carrying the rifle; a statement about being the last to surrender) as well-attested, and the precise verbatim phrasing as reported-but-unverified against the primary telegraph text.

## After the surrender: Fort Randall, Standing Rock, 1890

Sitting Bull's surrender was not the end of U.S. custody. **Attested-secondary**, consistent across sources: rather than being sent immediately to an agency, he and a group of about 172 people were held as prisoners of war and transported by steamboat down the Missouri to **Fort Randall**, in present-day South Dakota, where they remained for roughly twenty months. He was released to rejoin his people at the **Standing Rock Agency** on **May 10, 1883** (some sources round this to "May 1883"). At Standing Rock he lived under the close supervision of Indian Agent James McLaughlin, toured for one season in 1885 with Buffalo Bill Cody's Wild West show, and in 1889-90 permitted Ghost Dance gatherings at his camp, a movement he is reported not to have danced in himself but which brought him under suspicion regardless. On **December 15, 1890**, Indian police, acting on McLaughlin's order to arrest him amid fears of Ghost Dance-related unrest, came to his cabin on the Grand River; in the ensuing struggle Sitting Bull was shot and killed, along with several others on both sides. He is buried, per long-disputed and litigated history involving disinterment and rival claims, ultimately at Mobridge, South Dakota, near the Missouri River — a detail outside this note's scope but relevant to any later note on his descendants.

## Connection to the corpus

Read through this corpus's vocabulary, the Canadian refusal of a reserve is a textbook case of an [exclusion-power](../../../../../../glossary/terms/exclusion-powers.md) exercised by omission rather than assault: Ottawa never had to remove Sitting Bull's people by force, because it held, and used, the simpler power to withhold recognition and provisioning and let the border's other exclusion-power — the one waiting on the American side, backed by an army that had already broken the Lakota militarily — do the removing for it. The two states' postures were not opposites; they were complementary halves of one machine. Washington did not want the Lakota back on favorable terms and Ottawa did not want them at all, and between those two refusals there was no ground on which "Grandmother's Land" could be anything but temporary, whatever the Lakota's own kinship framing of the relationship had hoped it would be. This is [settlerism-as-filing-system](../../../../../../glossary/terms/settlerism-as-filing-system.md) operating at the scale of a border rather than a deed: Canada's own treaty obligations to feed Indigenous nations were filed as applying only to nations Canada itself had signed treaties with, a filing that cost nothing to maintain and did not have to be enforced by a single Mountie's rifle — it only had to be *held*, while the buffalo did the rest. Wood Mountain's founding families, the subject of the companion glossary entry, are what remained on the far side of that same filing once the rest of the Lakota crossed back south into a filing system's custody of its own.
