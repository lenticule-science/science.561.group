# command

A command is the directive by which an authority moves another's conduct — the utterance "do this"
issued by someone with the standing to expect it will be obeyed. Generated by: command is the basic
exercise of authority, the act in which the right to bind others is actually put to work. Where
authority names a standing, command names the use of that standing: it is to authority what a move is
to the right to make it. Its counterpart is obedience, the compliance of the one commanded; and a
command that is standing, general, addressed to everyone rather than a single person, and backed by
enforcement, is what we call law.

The classical analysis comes from John Austin (*The Province of Jurisprudence Determined*, 1832), who
made command the foundation of law itself. For Austin a command is the expression of a wish by a
party who will inflict an evil — a *sanction* — if the wish is not complied with, and who has the
power to do so; the person commanded lies under a *duty* and is *liable* to the sanction. Law, on this
"command theory," is simply the general commands of a *sovereign* — a determinate person or body
habitually obeyed by the bulk of a society and itself obeying no one. The whole apparatus of legal
obligation, in Austin's hands, reduces to the threatened command of an uncommanded commander.

This austere picture was sharply criticized, most influentially by H. L. A. Hart (*The Concept of
Law*, 1961), who argued that the command-of-a-sovereign model, the "gunman situation writ large,"
cannot capture what is distinctive about law. The gunman's order — "your money or your life" — obliges
me in the sense that I am forced, but it does not place me under an *obligation*; obligation requires
a *rule* that is accepted as a standard of conduct, an "internal point of view" from which the rule
is treated as a reason for action and a ground for criticism. Many laws, moreover, do not command at
all but *confer powers* — to make a will, a contract, a marriage — and these cannot be squeezed into
the form of orders backed by threats. Command, on this account, is one mode of legal direction, not
its essence.

The distinction at issue here is the same one that separates authority from coercion. A command
proper is obeyed because the commander is recognized as having the standing to issue it; a mere threat
extracts compliance through fear alone. This is why the military, the paradigm case of command,
invests so heavily in the legitimacy of the chain of command and in the duty to obey lawful orders —
and why the limit of that duty, the refusal of *manifestly unlawful* orders affirmed at Nuremberg, marks
the point where the command form breaks down and naked coercion or criminality begins.

In this cluster command is the immediate exercise of authority and the seed of law: a single,
person-to-person directive becomes law when it is generalized into a standing rule addressed to all
and enforced through sanction. Its correlate, obedience, completes the relation — for a command is
only a command if there is someone positioned to comply, and the stability of any structure of
authority rests finally on whether its commands are in fact obeyed.
