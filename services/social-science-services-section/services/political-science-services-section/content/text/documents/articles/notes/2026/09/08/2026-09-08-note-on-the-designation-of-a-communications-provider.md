# The designation of a communications provider

## Why this case

Autistici/Inventati (A/I), a volunteer-run Italian collective founded in 2001, provided email, web hosting, mailing lists, blogging, chat, video, streaming, and anonymity services to activists at no charge. On 2026-08-26 the US Treasury's OFAC added A/I to the Specially Designated Nationals list as a Specially Designated Global Terrorist (SDGT). Eleven days later A/I announced it would close entirely. This note treats the sequence as one case of [state-designation-as-repression](../../../../../../glossary/terms/state-designation-as-repression.md) acting on [movement-infrastructure](../../../../../../glossary/terms/movement-infrastructure.md), read against the general political-science literature on [digital-repression](../../../../../../glossary/terms/digital-repression.md), [networked-social-movement](../../../../../../glossary/terms/networked-social-movement.md)s, and [communication-security-for-movements](../../../../../../glossary/terms/communication-security-for-movements.md). It reports facts and records where sources conflict; it does not evaluate the designation's legal merits.

## Timeline, first-party and contemporaneous sources

- **2026-08-26** — OFAC adds A/I to the SDN list as an SDGT under "section 1(b) of Executive Order 13224, as amended by Executive Order 13886," and simultaneously issues Counter Terrorism General License 36 authorizing a wind-down through 2026-09-25 [@ofacgl36_2026]. The State Department's own release, not directly fetchable (HTTP 403), is reported by outlets quoting it as stating A/I "provided digital infrastructure, tools and services" to extremist and designated organizations [@statedept2026].
- **2026-08-27** — A/I's PayPal account is suspended, per A/I's own account [@aipress2026].
- **2026-08-28** — The autistici.org domain stops resolving, reported by A/I as a `serverHold` status; A/I attributes this to the Public Interest Registry (PIR) without a public confirmation from PIR that it acted on a direct OFAC order [@aipress2026]. A/I publishes its press release the same day [@aipress2026].
- **2026-09-01** — Banca Etica, an Italian ethical bank, suspends A/I's account, which A/I's press release frames as a precaution against secondary-sanctions exposure rather than a determination that A/I itself violated Italian or EU law [@aipress2026].
- **2026-09-06** — A/I announces closure, nineteen days before the GL 36 wind-down deadline, citing the need to protect its users from potential legal and financial exposure; the closure is reported to affect roughly 16,000 mailboxes, 1,500 websites, 5,500 mailing lists, and 10,000 blogs [@aiclosure2026].

## The instruments, in the order they acted

1. A State-Department-led terrorism determination under EO 13224 §1(b) (as amended by EO 13886), naming A/I an SDGT.
2. OFAC's simultaneous SDN listing (31 C.F.R. Part 594), which blocks A/I's property within US jurisdiction and bars US persons from transacting with it, paired with General License 36 authorizing only a time-boxed wind-down.
3. No US person needed to act further for the cutoffs that followed: PayPal, PIR, and Banca Etica are the parties whose own decisions produced the domain, payment, and banking losses, each avoiding the secondary-sanctions and de-risking exposure of continuing to serve a US-designated party rather than acting on any direct order naming them.

## Economic and infrastructural cutoffs

Three independent chokepoints closed within six days of designation: payment processing (PayPal), domain resolution (PIR/`serverHold`), and banking (Banca Etica). None of these three intermediaries was itself the subject of OFAC's action; each acted to manage its own exposure. This is the mechanism [FATF's de-risking literature](../../../../../../../../../law-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/08/2026-09-08-note-on-sanctions-designation-and-hosting-provider-law.md) documents at the level of financial institutions generally, observed here operating simultaneously across three different classes of intermediary against a single target.

## What the general literature says this implies

**Digital repression.** Feldstein's and Deibert's accounts of digital repression both extend beyond content-level censorship to infrastructural and financial disruption as a repressive tool [@feldstein2021; @deibert2013]. A/I's case fits that broader category exactly: no statement A/I or its users made was censored; the capacity to communicate at all was removed by cutting the infrastructure under it.

**Movement infrastructure and networked movements.** Because A/I served roughly 16,000 mailboxes and thousands of independent mailing lists and blogs run by unrelated organizations, its closure is a [movement-infrastructure](../../../../../../glossary/terms/movement-infrastructure.md) event in McCarthy and Zald's sense [@mccarthyzald1977]: the loss falls on every group that depended on the shared resource, not only on A/I itself. Castells' and Bennett and Segerberg's accounts of digitally networked movements describe exactly this dependency structure — coordination running through shared digital infrastructure rather than through any single organization's chain of command [@castells2012; @bennettsegerberg2012] — which is the structural reason one provider's closure has effects disproportionate to its own size.

**Designation as a political instrument.** Jarvis and Legrand's comparative survey of proscription regimes finds designation power "employed extremely widely — although inconsistently — across the globe," used for ends beyond a narrow counter-terrorism predicate, and note that critiques "have noted that proscription may reflect political bias" [@jarvislegrand2018]. The White House's September 2025 designation of Antifa — a decentralized tendency, not a single organization — as a domestic terrorist entity is a comparable case by function (targeting a political tendency rather than an armed group with a chain of command) [@whitehouse2025antifa], reported here as a parallel instance in the same designation-regime literature, without this note adjudicating either designation's legal validity.

**Server seizure as a documented pattern.** A/I's closure is not the first time state action has removed shared movement-communications infrastructure. In 2004, US Justice Department action (at Italian and Swiss request under mutual legal assistance treaties) led Rackspace to surrender Indymedia's servers from its London facility, taking more than 20 Indymedia sites offline worldwide over investigations naming specific individuals [@eff2004indymedia]. In April 2012, an FBI seizure of a shared server hosting both May First/People Link and Riseup disrupted both organizations' full user bases over a single account [@eff2012mayfirstriseup]. In June 2004, Bologna prosecutors ordered a seizure at A/I's own Aruba data center that A/I was told was an "electrical problem"; A/I discovered in June 2005 that Italian postal police had instead installed a backdoor on the server, decrypting and intercepting all its traffic, not only traffic related to the underlying anarchist-conspiracy investigation [@edri2005backdoor; @micahflee2026]. In August 2013, Ladar Levison shut down Lavabit rather than surrender its master encryption key, which the US government's order would have exposed to reach one user's (Snowden's) email but which would have compromised all 40,000 Lavabit accounts [@theregister2013lavabit]. Across these cases, the shared pattern is that action addressed to or through a shared infrastructure provider exposes every user of that infrastructure, not only the investigation's actual target.

**Communication security's limits.** Tufekci's account of networked protest describes movements built on shared digital infrastructure as simultaneously harder to decapitate (no single leader to arrest) and more fragile under sustained pressure, because they often lack the deeper organizational decision-making capacity that older, more centralized movements built over time [@tufekci2017]. Costanza-Chock's study of transmedia organizing likewise treats a movement's media infrastructure as integral to, not separable from, its organizing capacity [@costanzachock2014]. Read together, both suggest that [communication-security-for-movements](../../../../../../glossary/terms/communication-security-for-movements.md) practices aimed only at message-level protection (encryption, anonymity) do not address a movement's infrastructural dependency on a small number of shared providers, each of which is independently exposed to the legal and financial pressures this note documents.

## What this implies generally, for any provider serving the same demographics

The mechanism this case documents does not depend on any fact specific to A/I: a hosting provider serving movement clients anywhere is one designation away from the same three-part cutoff (payment, domain, banking), because the cutoff is produced by intermediaries managing their own secondary-sanctions and de-risking exposure, not by direct enforcement against the provider. This note states that mechanism as fact; it offers no advice about how a provider should respond to it.
