Digital repression is the use of digital information and communication technologies by state or state-aligned actors to surveil, censor, coerce, or disrupt political opposition, civil society, and independent media, extending the classical repertoire of repression with tools — internet shutdowns, deep-packet inspection, facial recognition, coordinated inauthentic behavior, and financial or infrastructural disruption — that scale more cheaply and operate with less visible violence than earlier repressive methods (Feldstein 2021).

Digital repression is broader than the narrower category of "digital authoritarianism" studied mainly in explicitly non-democratic regimes: Feldstein's and Deibert's research documents its use by democracies as well, and both specifically identify financial and infrastructural chokepoints, not only content-level censorship or surveillance, as a repressive mechanism, since disrupting a target's hosting, payment processing, or domain resolution achieves disruption without directly touching any statement's content (Deibert 2013).

A designation-driven cutoff of hosting, banking, and domain services is a direct instance of the financial and infrastructural branch of digital repression as Feldstein and Deibert define the category: it need not censor any specific statement to remove a movement's capacity to communicate at all. See [a research note on the designation of a communications provider](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/08/2026-09-08-note-on-the-designation-of-a-communications-provider.md) for the case that documents this mechanism operating through three independent intermediaries at once [@feldstein2021; @deibert2013].
