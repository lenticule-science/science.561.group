Server seizure is the physical or administrative removal of a hosting provider's server hardware, or of exclusive control over it, by government action — through a judicial search-and-seizure warrant, through a court order compelling a hosting company to seize its own equipment, or through a mutual legal assistance treaty request routed through a third-country host — with a documented history of use against politically oriented and encrypted-communications hosting providers independent of any securities, fraud, or ordinary criminal predicate against the seized service's own operators.

Server seizure against shared movement-communications infrastructure is distinguished by its effect on parties who are not themselves the subject of the underlying investigation: the 2004 seizure of Indymedia's servers from Rackspace's London facility, executed at US Justice Department request after Italian and Swiss mutual-legal-assistance inquiries, took more than 20 Indymedia sites offline worldwide though the underlying investigations named specific individuals; the 2012 FBI seizure of a server shared by May First/People Link and Riseup disrupted both organizations' full user bases over a single account; and the June 2004 seizure at Autistici/Inventati's Aruba data center, followed by A/I's 2005 discovery of a backdoor installed during that seizure that decrypted and intercepted all server traffic rather than only the traffic named in the underlying investigation, shows access obtained for one case extending to interception of unrelated users.

Because a seized or backdoored shared server exposes every user of that server, not only the investigation's stated target, server seizure against a movement-infrastructure host is disruptive and surveillance-productive at a scale disproportionate to any single warrant's predicate, which is the documented reason movement-technology providers subsequently invested in warrant-canary practices, server hardening, and jurisdictional distribution of infrastructure across multiple hosts and countries — the same underlying vulnerability that led Lavabit to shut down entirely in 2013 rather than surrender an encryption key that would have exposed all 40,000 of its users to reach one target account. See [a research note on the designation of a communications provider](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/08/2026-09-08-note-on-the-designation-of-a-communications-provider.md) for the primary sources behind each case, and the [COINTELPRO](../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/black-philosophy-services-section/services/abolition-services-section/services/state-repression-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/cointelpro.md) term for the domestic-surveillance-program lineage of this pattern in the United States [@eff2004indymedia; @eff2012mayfirstriseup; @edri2005backdoor; @theregister2013lavabit].
