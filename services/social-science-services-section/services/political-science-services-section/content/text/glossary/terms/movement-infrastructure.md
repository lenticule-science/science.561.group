Movement infrastructure is the accumulated stock of resources — communication channels, technical systems, meeting spaces, financial instruments, trained personnel, and organizational know-how — that social movements build up over time and draw on to sustain mobilization beyond any single spontaneous protest event, which resource-mobilization theory treats as the material precondition for a movement's capacity to act (McCarthy and Zald 1977).

Movement infrastructure is distinguished from any single social movement organization: infrastructure is typically shared across many organizations and even across otherwise-unrelated movements — a shared mailing-list host, a legal defense fund, an independent media platform — so its loss is not equivalent to the loss of one organization but to a reduction in the resource base every organization that depended on it can draw from.

Because infrastructure is a shared, non-excludable resource across a movement sector, its disruption produces effects disproportionate to the size of the entity that operated it: a small, resource-constrained provider can be load-bearing for a movement sector far larger than the provider's own visible footprint, which is the resource-mobilization-theoretic reason a hosting collective's closure is a movement-infrastructure event rather than merely the loss of one vendor. See [a research note on the designation of a communications provider](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/08/2026-09-08-note-on-the-designation-of-a-communications-provider.md) for a case in which roughly 16,000 mailboxes and thousands of independently run mailing lists and blogs depended on one collective [@mccarthyzald1977].
