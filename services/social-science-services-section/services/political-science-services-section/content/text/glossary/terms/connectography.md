**Connectography**, the term coined by [Parag Khanna](../../../../../../../../content/text/documents/articles/biographies/parag-khanna.md), names the thesis that infrastructure connectivity — transportation corridors, energy pipelines and electrical grids, and fiber-optic and data cables — is superseding political borders as the primary map of global power. On this account, a city or region's economic fate is set increasingly by what it is physically and digitally connected to, rather than by which sovereign state it nominally belongs to, and continued buildout of connective infrastructure is read as a largely positive, growth-enabling, and durable process.

Connectography stands in direct tension with [demographic deglobalization](./demographic-deglobalization.md), which forecasts the same infrastructure and trade system fragmenting along demographic and security lines rather than continuing to integrate. It is also read, in house use, against the declining-net-energy position associated with [energy return on investment](../../../../../economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/economics-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/energy-return-on-investment.md): connectivity can reroute and rebuffer the effects of declining energy returns, as choke-point analysis of shipping, insurance, and clearing systems shows in detail, but rerouting is not treated as equivalent to resolving the underlying physical constraint.
