**Geopolitical forecasting**, in the method developed by [George Friedman](../../../../../../../../content/text/documents/articles/biographies/george-friedman.md) at Stratfor and later Geopolitical Futures, treats a state's geography, demography, and economic necessity as constraints strong enough that its behavior can be forecast as the outworking of "impersonal forces," largely independent of which government or leader happens to be in office at a given time.

The method's characteristic output is a specific, dated forecast — naming a state, a behavior, and an approximate timeframe — rather than a hedged probability range. This makes geopolitical forecasts of this kind unusually easy to score against outcomes once the stated timeframe elapses, and it is the feature that has drawn the most criticism: several of Friedman's dated forecasts in *The Next 100 Years* (a resurgent Poland and Turkey, a more assertive Japan) have not played out on the stated schedule, which critics read as evidence that structural-constraint reasoning alone underdetermines near-term political outcomes.

Geopolitical forecasting of this kind is distinguished from [superforecasting](../../../../../../../../../engineering-services-section/services/computer-engineering-services-section/services/computer-systems-engineering-services-section/services/computer-web-engineering-services-section/services/web-platform-services-section/services/web3-platform-services-section/web3-gambling-platform-services-section/services/kalshi-services-section/services/forecasting-service/content/text/glossary/terms/superforecasting.md), which requires calibrated probability estimates scored against a proper scoring rule rather than confident single-outcome narrative claims; the two methods can be applied to the same questions and compared directly.
