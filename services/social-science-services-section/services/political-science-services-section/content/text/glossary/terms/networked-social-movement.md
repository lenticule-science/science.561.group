A networked social movement is a form of collective action organized through digitally networked, often leaderless or multiply-led structures rather than through a single centralized organization, in which participants coordinate directly with each other through shared digital infrastructure rather than primarily through a formal organization's internal chain of command (Castells 2012).

This is distinguished from the older collective-action model, in which a formal social movement organization recruits, frames, and directs members: Bennett and Segerberg (2012) sharpen the distinction by separating "collective action," which requires the resource-intensive work of forging a shared collective identity and organizational brokerage, from "connective action," in which personalized, digitally shared content itself becomes the organizing mechanism and reduces, without eliminating, the need for formal organizational coordination.

Because a networked social movement's coordination depends on the shared digital infrastructure it uses rather than on any single organization's internal structure, disrupting that shared infrastructure — rather than targeting any one organization's leadership — degrades coordinating capacity across every group that relied on it, which is the structural reason a single shared hosting provider's closure can affect thousands of otherwise-unrelated organizing groups at once.
