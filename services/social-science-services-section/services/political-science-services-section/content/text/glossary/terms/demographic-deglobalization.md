**Demographic deglobalization** names the thesis, developed by [Peter Zeihan](../../../../../../../../content/text/documents/articles/biographies/peter-zeihan.md), that the globalized system of long, cheap, just-in-time international supply chains built after 1945 depended on two conditions that are both now receding: a US Navy guarantee of open global shipping lanes, extended to allies as a Cold War inducement rather than out of ordinary commercial interest, and a global demographic profile with enough working-age population to keep production and consumption growing.

As American strategic interest in policing global shipping fades — accelerated by reduced US dependence on imported oil — and as the working-age population shrinks across most of the industrialized and increasingly the developing world, the argument holds that globalization does not merely slow but reverses: trade and production reorganize into smaller regional blocs built around whichever powers retain the demographic weight and geographic security to sustain their own supply chains without depending on a guarantor.

Demographic deglobalization is distinguished from a general slowdown in trade growth by its claimed mechanism and direction: it predicts active fragmentation into blocs, not merely a plateau in an otherwise continuing single global market. It stands in direct tension with [connectography](./connectography.md), which reads continued infrastructure connectivity as a durable, largely independent driver of continued integration rather than something demographic and security pressures will unwind.
