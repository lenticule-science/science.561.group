Communication security for movements, often called "security culture" in movement literature, is the set of operational practices — compartmentalized information-sharing, verified identity and provenance checks, encrypted and anonymized communication tools, and threat modeling appropriate to a movement's specific adversary — that movements adopt to reduce the surveillance, infiltration, and disruption risk their communications and organizing infrastructure face from state and non-state adversaries.

These practices are distinguished from the technical capacities Tufekci (2017) analyzes as the "power and fragility" of networked protest: the same networked, digitally organized capacity that lets a movement mobilize a mass protest with unprecedented speed and low up-front organizational cost also produces a correspondingly low floor of organizational resilience once the digital infrastructure or the loosely coordinated network itself is disrupted, so security-culture practices aimed only at individual communications (encryption, anonymity) do not by themselves substitute for the deeper organizational capacities — tactical adaptability, collective decision-making structures — that more centralized movements historically built over time; Costanza-Chock's account of transmedia organizing likewise treats a movement's media infrastructure as integral to, not separable from, its organizing capacity (Costanza-Chock 2014).

Tufekci's finding that networked movements are simultaneously harder to decapitate, having no single leader to arrest, and more fragile under sustained pressure, having no established decision-making structure to fall back on once initial momentum fades, means that communication security narrowly defined as protecting message content and metadata addresses only the surveillance-and-infiltration half of a movement's vulnerability; the infrastructural half — dependence on a small number of shared hosting, payment, and domain providers, each individually exposed to the legal and financial pressure documented in the server-seizure record — is a distinct vulnerability that message-level security practices do not mitigate.
