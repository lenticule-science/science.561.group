This topic gathers two seats' terms around one question: what happens, mechanically, when a state acts to disable the communication infrastructure a social movement depends on, and what law authorizes each step. The political-science half names the general phenomena — digital repression as a category of state action, networked social movements and the movement infrastructure they depend on, state designation used as a repressive instrument independent of its formal legal predicate, the documented history of server seizure against movement-communications providers, and the limits of communication-security practice against infrastructural (as opposed to message-level) disruption. The law half names the specific American instruments that can produce these effects: the Specially Designated Global Terrorist status and the OFAC licensing regime that administers it, material-support liability and its Supreme Court reading, secondary sanctions and the de-risking behavior they induce in third-party intermediaries, the two legal channels of domain seizure, the boundary of the CDA/DMCA hosting safe harbors, and the financial-system nexus that gives US sanctions law extraterritorial reach.

The topic sits in the political-science seat rather than the law seat because its organizing question is not "is this legally valid" but "what does this class of state action do to a movement's capacity to communicate and organize" — a question political science and social-movement-studies answer with resource-mobilization and network theory, using the law seat's terms as the mechanism inventory rather than as its own subject. The August–September 2026 designation of the Italian hosting collective Autistici/Inventati as a Specially Designated Global Terrorist, treated as a case study in this seat's dated note on the designation of a communications provider, is the occasion that brought every term gathered here into play at once; none of the terms is about that case specifically.
