The Madrean Sky Islands (also the Madrean Archipelago) are a scatter of roughly 63 isolated mountain ranges rising out of the desert and grassland lowlands of southeastern Arizona, southwestern New Mexico, and northern Sonora and Chihuahua — the place where the temperate Rocky Mountain and Colorado Plateau floras meet the subtropical Sierra Madre Occidental, with Chihuahuan and Sonoran desert biomes filling the valleys between. Each range is a vertical world of its own: desert scrub at the base, then grassland, oak woodland, pine-oak, and in the tallest ranges mixed conifer and aspen near the summit, so that a single peak can cross six biotic communities in a few thousand feet of elevation. The archipelago holds more than 7,000 documented plant and animal species — sky-island endemics, the continent's northernmost jaguars, the elf owl, and assemblages found nowhere else, packed into "islands" of highland habitat separated from each other, and from the Sierra Madre proper, by seas of lowland desert too hot and dry for most of their species to cross.

That isolation is also the archipelago's vulnerability and its logic: each range is reachable by most of its resident species only by moving through the valleys between ranges, at the cooler, wetter margins where desert habitat is thinnest. The San Rafael Valley is one of those margins, and an unusually important one — a broad, largely intact grassland saddle connecting the Huachuca Mountains to the west with the Patagonia Mountains to the east, itself linking further east and south toward the Canelo Hills and the Sonoran ranges beyond the international line. Sky Island Alliance camera surveys of the valley recorded 71 species using it in a 90-day period, with 17 large-bodied species documented moving through gaps in the old vehicle barriers and a monthly average of roughly 5 human border crossings against 180 white-tailed deer crossings in the same span — the valley functioning, in practice, far more as an animal thoroughfare than a human one. It is Southern Arizona's last major wildlife corridor without a continuous wall.

The archipelago's jaguar and ocelot populations make the stakes concrete. Neither species breeds north of the border in numbers; the established breeding populations are in Sonora, and the northern individuals documented in Arizona — a male jaguar nicknamed "Cinco," photographed just north of the San Rafael Valley in June and again in late July 2026, mid-construction, and the first evidence suggestive of a female jaguar in the borderlands in early 2024 — depend on the sky-island corridor network to reach U.S. habitat at all. The 2018 *BioScience* consensus statement, signed by 2,556 scientists across 43 countries, found that the U.S.-Mexico border bisects the ranges of 1,506 native species, 62 of them already IUCN-threatened; a continuous 30-foot wall with 4-inch bollard gaps at San Rafael is projected to cut wildlife crossings there by roughly 86%. A corridor built from isolation — ranges that evolved their diversity precisely because they were hard to cross — is being closed by a second, engineered isolation running perpendicular to the first.

The valley's ecological intactness is not incidental to its history. Hadley and Sheridan's land-use history of the San Rafael Valley traces that intactness to the same nineteenth-century enclosure documented under the [San Rafael de la Zanja grant](../../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/san-rafael-de-la-zanja-grant.md): Colin Cameron's illegal sixteen-league claim, and the Greene and Sharp ownerships that followed it, excluded the fragmented overstocking that gutted comparable desert grasslands elsewhere in the region during the 1891–93 drought. What the corridor's ecology needed — a single actor able to exclude competing use of the land — is structurally the same power now exercised against the corridor: the wall waiver notice for the San Rafael segment set aside 34 federal laws, including the Endangered Species Act, under the same logic of concentrated, unaccountable exclusion-power that produced the valley's intactness in the first place. The knowledge base's line of argument holds both halves without resolving the irony: property-as-disposition conserved this corridor for a century and a half, and property-as-disposition — reasserted now as "border security," a filing category that outranks every other category of harm — is what closes it. The [Gadsden line](./la-gran-linea.md) that drew the political border through the middle of a single biological archipelago is the earlier instance of the same act.
