The Santa Cruz River rises as a set of headwater springs and cienegas in the San Rafael Valley, on the U.S. side of the international line, then does something few rivers on this border do: it flows south into Sonora, loops through the Mexican city of Santa Cruz and past Nogales, Sonora, and turns back north to re-enter the United States near Nogales, Arizona, before continuing on through Tucson toward its historic confluence with the Gila. It crosses the Gadsden line twice in its first fifty miles — a single hydrological system the treaty line was drawn across without regard for, and that the current border wall now physically crosses twice as well, once at each transit.

For most of its length the river no longer behaves as a river in the ordinary sense. Perennial surface flow — water present in the channel year-round, fed by a shallow water table close enough to the surface to sustain it — was effectively lost by 1913, the casualty of groundwater pumping, irrigation diversion, and the arroyo-cutting that tore the channel down through the valley fill across the 1880s–1890s drought-and-flood cycle described in [the San Rafael de la Zanja grant](../../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/san-rafael-de-la-zanja-grant.md) entry. Robert Webb and coauthors' *Requiem for the Santa Cruz: An Environmental History of an Arizona River* (2014) is the anchor study of that entrenchment: a "Great Mesquite Forest" and marshland bird habitat, sustained for centuries by a shallow water table, were destroyed within a generation by channel incision and groundwater mining that the historical record can date with unusual precision. Where the river appears to flow perennially today — notably the reach through downtown Tucson — it does so on treated wastewater effluent released from municipal treatment plants, not on natural baseflow; the river's visible water and its hydrological history are now two different things.

The river's water is also explicitly binational infrastructure, not merely a binational watercourse. The Santa Cruz aquifer is one of the priority transboundary aquifers studied under the congressionally authorized Transboundary Aquifer Assessment Program (TAAP), a joint U.S. Geological Survey and Mexican effort to assess groundwater shared across the Arizona-Sonora line — an acknowledgment, unusual for this border, that the two countries are drawing on the same underground water regardless of what the surface line says. That shared aquifer is precisely what wall construction threatens further: the 2019–21 build cycle's precedent includes Quitobaquito Springs drawn to record lows and San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge pumped at DHS's own estimated rate of 83,000–100,000 gallons per day for on-site wells and concrete production, and climate modeling for the region projects roughly −15% precipitation compounding into −22% streamflow and further recharge loss — pressures on the same binational water table the San Rafael segment's construction now adds to directly.

The river's fate and the [Madrean sky-island corridor](./madrean-sky-islands.md)'s fate are the same fate stated in two registers: the Santa Cruz's headwater cienegas are what make the San Rafael Valley a wildlife corridor rather than open desert, and a river already reduced to an effluent-sustained remnant for most of its length is precisely the kind of system a 150-foot construction swath, on-site wells, and a wall crossing it twice can finish off rather than merely stress. Where [La Gran Línea](./la-gran-linea.md) records the political abstraction laid across this ground in 1855, the Santa Cruz records what the ground actually does — flows south, flows north, and does not recognize the line drawn twice across its own channel.
