A cash flow statement tracks the actual movement of money into and out of a business over a defined period. Unlike the [income statement](./income-statement.md), which records revenue when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred (accrual accounting), the cash flow statement records when money actually changes hands. A business can be profitable on its [income statement](./income-statement.md) and still run out of cash — if customers pay late, if inventory must be purchased in advance, or if a large expense comes due before revenue arrives.

The statement is organized into three sections. Operating cash flow covers the money generated or consumed by core business activities — customer payments received, supplier invoices paid, wages disbursed. Investing cash flow covers purchases or sales of long-term assets: equipment, vehicles, [property](../../sociology/terms/property.md). Financing cash flow covers money raised from or returned to investors and lenders — capital contributions, loan proceeds, loan repayments, dividends.

For small and early-stage businesses, the cash flow statement is often more revealing than the [income statement](./income-statement.md). It exposes the timing gaps that cause businesses to fail even when demand exists: the weeks between paying for supplies and receiving customer payment, the seasonal fluctuations that leave cash reserves depleted at the wrong moment, the capital expenditures that front-load costs against future [revenue](../../../../services/strategy-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/revenue-model.md).
