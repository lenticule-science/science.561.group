## Why this is central

Hansmann's theory answers the question a cooperative raises: who should own a firm. Ownership carries two rights, control and residual earnings, and every class of the firm's patrons (investors, customers, suppliers, workers) could hold them; the efficient assignment gives ownership to the class for which the sum of the costs of contracting through the market and the costs of owning (collective decision-making, risk bearing, monitoring managers) is lowest. Investor ownership dominates not because capital is special but because investors are homogeneous in interest and contracting for capital is costly; cooperatives succeed where the patron class is homogeneous and the market contract with it would be worse. The house cites it for the exact test a member-owned pool must pass: its members must be alike enough in what they want from it that collective decision costs stay low.

## What it does not settle

The book is a theory of existing firm forms and their survival; it does not address an enterprise whose members are also its depositors and its borrowers at once, or one whose ownership rights are recorded on a ledger rather than in a charter. Its cost categories are qualitative, so it can say a form is plausible, not that it is optimal for a given membership.
