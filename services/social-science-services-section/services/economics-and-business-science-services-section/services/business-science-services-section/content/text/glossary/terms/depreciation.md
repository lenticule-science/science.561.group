Depreciation allocates the cost of a physical asset — equipment, vehicles, furniture, leasehold improvements — across the years it will be used. A \$15,000 commercial oven with a 10-year useful life depreciates at \$1,500 per year under straight-line depreciation: each year, \$1,500 appears as an expense on the [income statement](./income-statement.md), reducing reported profit, while the asset's book value on the [balance sheet](./balance-sheet.md) decreases by the same amount.

Depreciation is a non-cash expense — no money leaves the business when depreciation is recorded. The cash was spent when the asset was purchased. This is why the [cash flow statement](./cash-flow-statement.md) adds depreciation back to net income when calculating operating cash flow: it reduces reported profit but not actual cash.

Common methods: **straight-line** (equal amounts each year), **declining balance** (more in early years, less later), and **Section 179 / bonus depreciation** (deduct the full cost in the year of purchase, subject to IRS limits — often used by small businesses to accelerate tax deductions).
