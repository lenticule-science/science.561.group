# The eCornell Business Management Essentials Certificate as of September 2026

This note is the research phase of the fifth book in the Group's study
companion series, written to the education science section's pattern
(`education-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/27/2026-09-27-note-on-how-a-study-companion-should-teach-toward-a-credential.md`).
It lives in business science. The section's own notes are practical guides for
running a small business (market analysis, marketing plans, cash flow, hiring,
managing employees, weekly KPIs); the accounting section's glossary defines
the financial statements; the strategy section holds only a competitive
analysis term. What the book needs beyond them is the academic grounding
below.

## What the certificate publishes about itself

Read from eCornell on 2026-09-27 [@ecornell-bme-2026]. Business Management
Essentials is offered by Cornell's Dyson School, authored by Pedro Pérez
(senior lecturer, Cornell SC Johnson College of Business) and David Taylor. It
is online, about three months at four to six hours a week, $3,900, and carries
72 professional development hours (7.2 CEUs). Six two-week, instructor-led
courses are taken in order, with a six-month pass to eCornell's live AI
workshops:

| Code | Course | Published takeaways, in brief |
|---|---|---|
| DYS551 | Finance and Accounting Principles | Analyze a simple balance sheet and income statement; calculate and interpret basic ratios; examine financial trends; use financial data to inform strategy |
| DYS552 | Marketing Fundamentals | Identify target markets and segments; develop a marketing mix; plan to acquire and retain customers; a three-part strategic marketing brief |
| DYS553 | Strategic Business Planning and Forecasting | Define generic strategies and position firms; describe the value chain; create a SWOT diagram; prioritize stakeholder and shareholder expectations |
| DYS554 | Managing Organizations | Divide roles by principles of organizing; analyze and specify a job; describe the HR process from recruiting to motivating; describe organizational culture; identify uses of power; an organizational brief |
| DYS555 | Leading Without Authority | Distinguish factors that raise satisfaction from those that lower dissatisfaction; design motivation strategies; identify types of capital in teams; form high-performing teams; resolve team conflict |
| DYS556 | Navigating the Global Business Landscape | Write sound business correspondence; plan networking; generate ideas; describe disruptive technologies; identify stages of globalization; define ethics frameworks |

The course pages were read one by one on 2026-09-27. Cornell's catalog PDFs
refused automated reading.

## Where sources disagree

1. **Who invented SWOT.** Management writing often credits Albert Humphrey at
   the Stanford Research Institute in the 1960s. The documented origin is the
   Harvard business policy group: Learned, Christensen, Andrews and Guth's 1965
   text frames strategy as matching a firm's strengths and weaknesses to its
   environment's opportunities and threats [@learned1965]. *Reading:* the
   Humphrey attribution is unsupported by a primary source; the Harvard origin
   is supported. The book gives the Harvard origin and doesn't repeat the
   Humphrey story.
2. **Herzberg's two factors.** Herzberg and colleagues found that the things
   people named as satisfying (achievement, recognition, the work itself)
   differed from those named as dissatisfying (pay, policy, supervision,
   conditions) [@herzberg1959]. House and Wigdor showed the split depends on
   the interview method, which invites people to credit themselves for good
   events and blame others for bad ones [@house-wigdor1967]. *Reading:* both.
   The distinction is useful for designing work; the claim that the two sets
   are separate scales is contested. The course asks readers to distinguish
   the factors, so the book teaches them, with the critique.
3. **Whose interests a business serves.** Friedman argued that a business's
   social responsibility is to increase its profits within the rules
   [@friedman1970]; Freeman argued that managers must serve all stakeholders
   [@freeman1984]; in 2019 the Business Roundtable's chief executives committed
   to "all stakeholders" [@brt2019]. *Reading:* both are positions, not
   findings; the course asks readers to prioritize among them, and the book
   presents both and a way to decide in a case.
4. **"Stuck in the middle."** Porter argued that a firm pursuing both low cost
   and differentiation risks having neither [@porter1980]. Later studies found
   firms that combine them successfully [@miller-dess1993]. *Reading:* both;
   the risk is real and not a law. The book teaches the three strategies and
   the hybrid case.

## How the house's theories read the same material

- **Double-entry bookkeeping is a balanced ledger.** Every transaction is
  recorded twice, as a debit and a credit of equal size, so the books always
  balance (assets = liabilities + equity). The accounting section's history
  of double-entry bookkeeping holds this; the monetary economics section's
  *tally* is the same idea in the house's own economy, where every mark has
  its counter-mark.
- **The value chain is sequential composition of work.** Porter's value
  chain decomposes a firm into activities whose costs add as the product
  moves through them [@porter1985]. In the theory of work, sequential work
  composes and its costs combine by the cost quantale's tensor; the margin is
  what customers pay over the composite's cost.
- **Stakeholders are a governance question.** The theory of governance reads
  a governance act as attribution, binding and becoming: who is recognized as
  having a claim, what obligation follows, and what changes. Prioritizing
  stakeholder and shareholder expectations is that act inside a firm, and the
  book presents it as a decision with reasons, not a formula.

## What the book is

*Business Management, Course by Course: An Independent Study Companion to
Cornell's Business Management Essentials Certificate.* Series number 5. A
chapter per course (six), a guide to the course projects (the strategic
marketing brief and the organizational brief), a spaced study plan and a
glossary. One running example: a three-shop bakery-café deciding whether to
grow into wholesale. It states that it isn't affiliated with or endorsed by
Cornell University or eCornell.
