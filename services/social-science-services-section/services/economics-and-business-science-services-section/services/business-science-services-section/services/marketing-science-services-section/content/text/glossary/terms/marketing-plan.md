A marketing plan is a structured document that specifies how a business will reach, attract, and convert its [target market](./target-market.md) into paying customers. It translates the findings of [market analysis](./market-analysis.md) into actionable strategy: what channels will be used (digital advertising, local events, social media, word of mouth, partnerships), what messages will be communicated, what budget will be allocated, and what metrics will measure success.

In [American business practice](../schools/american-business-practices/index.md), a marketing plan typically covers customer acquisition (how new customers are found), retention (how existing customers are kept), and growth (how [average check](../../../../../../../../../../../operations-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/average-check.md) or purchase frequency is increased). It may distinguish between digital strategy (search engine optimization, paid advertising, social media content, email campaigns) and local strategy (community events, partnerships with neighboring businesses, physical signage, local press). The plan connects directly to the [revenue model](../../../../../strategy-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/revenue-model.md) — projected customer acquisition rates feed into revenue projections, making the marketing plan a critical input to [financial projections](../../../../../../../finance-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/financial-projections.md).

Marketing as a discipline operates on the assumption that customer attention is a scarce resource that must be competed for and captured. This framing — customers as targets, attention as a resource to be acquired — naturalizes a particular relationship between business and community. Alternatives exist: businesses embedded in existing communities may rely on relationships and reputation rather than campaigns, and cooperative models may treat marketing as information-sharing rather than persuasion. The marketing plan as a document, however, belongs to the [American business](../schools/american-business-practices/index.md) tradition of treating customer acquisition as an engineering problem.
