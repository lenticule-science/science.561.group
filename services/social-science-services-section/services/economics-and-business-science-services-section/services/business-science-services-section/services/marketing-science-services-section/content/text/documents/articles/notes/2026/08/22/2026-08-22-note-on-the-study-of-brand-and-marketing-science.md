This is a study of brand and marketing science as a subject of formal research, read for one purpose: to establish, per sub-literature, which claims trace to peer-reviewed sources versus practitioner, industry, or trade canon, so that this section's later glossary terms and notes cite the right kind of source for the right kind of claim.
Method: web-verified citations gathered directly from publisher, repository, and indexer records (crossref.org, sagepub.com, tandfonline.com, springer.com, oup.com, ecfr.gov) rather than restated from memory; every load-bearing claim below carries a markdown-linked source, and any claim this study could not verify to that standard is marked **[UNVERIFIED]** inline rather than presented as settled.

## 1. Brand equity: the founding canon

David A. Aaker's *[Managing Brand Equity: Capitalizing on the Value of a Brand Name](https://archive.org/details/managingbrandequ00aake)* (Free Press, 1991) is the founding managerial-strategy statement of brand equity, structuring it around five asset categories — brand loyalty, name awareness, perceived quality, brand associations, and other proprietary assets — as sources of competitive advantage and future earnings.
It is a practitioner-facing strategy book from a marketing academic, not a peer-reviewed journal article, and should be cited in this section as foundational textbook/monograph canon rather than as an empirical study with a testable method section.

Kevin Lane Keller's ["Conceptualizing, Measuring, and Managing Customer-Based Brand Equity"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002224299305700101) (*Journal of Marketing* 57(1), 1993, pp. 1–22) is the peer-reviewed complement Aaker's book lacks: a formal conceptual model defining customer-based brand equity (CBBE) as "the differential effect of brand knowledge on consumer response to the marketing of the brand," built from associative-network memory theory, and distinguishing brand awareness from brand image as the two components of brand knowledge.
Where Aaker (1991) supplies the managerial asset taxonomy, Keller (1993) supplies the peer-reviewed psychological mechanism, and this section's `brand-equity` glossary term cites both, at their correct evidentiary tiers, rather than treating them as a single undifferentiated "equity theory."

## 2. Brand personality and its critique

Jennifer L. Aaker's ["Dimensions of Brand Personality"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224379703400304) (*Journal of Marketing Research* 34(3), 1997, pp. 347–356) is the anchor peer-reviewed brand-personality study: a psychometric scale development (rather than a purely theoretical proposal) identifying five brand-personality dimensions — Sincerity, Excitement, Competence, Sophistication, and Ruggedness — via factor analysis of consumer ratings of real brands, explicitly modeled on the human "Big Five" personality tradition.
This is the single most cited peer-reviewed source underlying "brand personality" as a construct, and any claim in this section using the five-dimension framework should trace to this paper specifically rather than to secondary textbook restatements.

Audrey Azoulay and Jean-Noël Kapferer's ["Do brand personality scales really measure brand personality?"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/palgrave.bm.2540162) (*Journal of Brand Management* 11(2), 2003, pp. 143–155) is the field's leading peer-reviewed critique: it argues that existing brand-personality scales, including Aaker's, in practice conflate several distinct facets of brand identity — physical attributes, user-image reflections, and self-image projections among them — of which personality proper is only one, and that the scales therefore do not cleanly isolate the construct they claim to measure.
This is a direct, peer-reviewed methodological challenge to the 1997 framework, not a trade-press opinion, and this section's `brand-personality` glossary term treats it as such rather than omitting it.
**[UNVERIFIED]**: this study did not locate a peer-reviewed rebuttal or resolution settling the Azoulay & Kapferer critique against Aaker's original scale; the dispute appears to remain open in the literature as reviewed here.

## 3. The Ehrenberg-Bass empirical-generalization tradition

Andrew S. C. Ehrenberg, Gerald J. Goodhardt, and T. Patrick Barwise's ["Double Jeopardy Revisited"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224299005400307) (*Journal of Marketing* 54(3), 1990, pp. 82–91) is the peer-reviewed formalization of the "double jeopardy" law: brands with lower market share are found, across repeated empirical studies, to suffer twice — fewer buyers (lower penetration) and slightly lower purchase frequency among those buyers (lower loyalty) — a pattern the paper frames explicitly as a recurring empirical generalization rather than a single dataset's finding.
It rests on the earlier Dirichlet buyer-behavior model formalized in G. J. Goodhardt, A. S. C. Ehrenberg, and C. Chatfield's ["The Dirichlet: A Comprehensive Model of Buying Behaviour"](https://doi.org/10.2307/2981696) (*Journal of the Royal Statistical Society, Series A* 147(5), 1984, pp. 621–655), a peer-reviewed statistical model of repeat-buying patterns across product categories that underlies most Ehrenberg-Bass "law-like" claims about purchase frequency and brand-switching.

Byron Sharp's *[How Brands Grow](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/how-brands-grow-9780195573565)* (Oxford University Press, 2010) is the tradition's popularizing synthesis, written by the director of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute and built directly on the Ehrenberg/Goodhardt empirical program above.
Its central claims — that brands grow primarily by expanding penetration rather than cultivating loyalty, and that "meaningless distinctiveness" (salient, ownable physical assets: logos, colors, taglines) matters more for growth than perceived differentiation — are presented as empirical generalizations from cross-category, cross-country purchase-panel data.
This section treats Sharp (2010) as empirically grounded but institutionally partisan: it is published by an academic press and draws on peer-reviewed antecedents (Ehrenberg, Goodhardt & Barwise 1990; Goodhardt, Ehrenberg & Chatfield 1984), but the book itself is a practitioner-facing monograph advocating a specific institute's research program against a named rival tradition (positioning/differentiation), and its differentiation-versus-distinctiveness argument has drawn published pushback from marketing practitioners and some academics who dispute how far the underlying data generalizes to brand strategy prescriptions.
**[UNVERIFIED]**: this study located practitioner-level rebuttals (e.g., trade commentary defending differentiation strategy) but did not confirm a peer-reviewed journal article directly refuting Sharp's differentiation claim using comparable panel-data methods; the debate as documented here is better evidenced on the Ehrenberg-Bass side than on the rebuttal side.

Al Ries and Jack Trout's *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (McGraw-Hill, 1981) is the canonical statement of the rival positioning school this section's Ehrenberg-Bass literature implicitly argues against: that a brand's success depends on securing a distinct, differentiated position in the prospect's mind relative to competitors.
It originates from an earlier 1969 trade article and a three-part 1972 *Advertising Age* series, and the book itself, and is trade/practitioner advertising canon with no peer-reviewed empirical apparatus — this section cites it as the positioning school's founding trade text, not as a study whose claims carry empirical-generalization weight comparable to Ehrenberg, Goodhardt & Barwise (1990) or Goodhardt, Ehrenberg & Chatfield (1984).
**[UNVERIFIED]**: this study could not confirm the 1969 article's exact venue or the 1972 series' exact title against a primary source — Ries's own company history places the 1969 groundwork before the confirmed 1972 *Advertising Age* three-part series, while at least one secondary source places the 1969 piece in *Industrial Marketing* rather than *Advertising Age*; the original draft's claim that both were *Advertising Age* pieces titled "The Positioning Era" is corrected here pending primary-source confirmation.

## 4. Personal branding and self-branding scholarship

Tom Peters's ["The Brand Called You"](https://www.fastcompany.com/28905/brand-called-you) (*Fast Company*, Issue 10, August/September 1997, p. 83) is the trade originator of "personal branding" as a popular concept: its central claim — that individuals should manage themselves as "Me Inc.," a company with its own brand — is magazine business commentary, not peer-reviewed research, and this section cites it as the term's trade-canon origin point rather than as scholarship.

Alison Hearn's ["'Meat, Mask, Burden': Probing the contours of the branded 'self'"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1469540508090086) (*Journal of Consumer Culture* 8(2), 2008, pp. 197–217) is the peer-reviewed critical-theory response: it argues that the "reflexive project of the self" becomes, under post-Fordist capital, an explicit form of labor — "self-branding" — in which the self is worked up as image/meat, presented as mask/persona, and carried as an ongoing burden of continuous self-promotion.
This is a critical-cultural-studies account, methodologically distinct from Peters's trade advocacy, and this section's `personal-branding` term cites Hearn (2008) as the peer-reviewed critical counterweight to the Peters-originated trade concept, not as an empirical test of Peters's claims.

Susie Khamis, Lawrence Ang, and Raymond Welling's ["Self-branding, 'micro-celebrity' and the rise of Social Media Influencers"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/19392397.2016.1218292) (*Celebrity Studies* 8(2), 2017, pp. 191–208) extends Hearn's self-branding-as-labor account specifically to social-media influencers, arguing self-branding's growth parallels the growth of digital/social technology embedded within a broader climate of neoliberal individualism, and gives "micro-celebrity" its academic grounding as a distinct practice from mass-media celebrity.
This is the paper this section's `influencer-marketing` and `personal-branding` terms both cite as the bridge between self-branding theory and influencer practice specifically.

## 5. Corporate brand and corporate identity scholarship

John M. T. Balmer's ["Corporate identity and the advent of corporate marketing"](https://doi.org/10.1362/026725798784867536) (*Journal of Marketing Management* 14(8), 1998, pp. 963–996) is the founding peer-reviewed statement proposing "corporate marketing" as an integrative discipline distinct from product-brand marketing, arguing corporate identity should be managed as a strategic resource that underlies corporate communications and reputation.
Balmer and Stephen A. Greyser's ["Corporate Marketing"](https://doi.org/10.1108/03090560610669964) (*European Journal of Marketing* 40(7/8), 2006, pp. 730–741) extends this into a mature integrative framework explicitly unifying corporate identity, corporate branding, corporate communications, corporate image, and corporate reputation under one "corporate marketing" umbrella.
Both are peer-reviewed journal articles, giving corporate-brand scholarship (distinct from the consumer-brand-equity tradition in §1 and the personal-branding tradition in §4) its own citable academic anchor; this section's scope note treats corporate branding as adjacent to, but distinct from, both.

## 6. Influencer marketing: effectiveness and disclosure

Demetris Vrontis, Anna Makrides, Michael Christofi, and Alkis Thrassou's ["Social Media Influencer Marketing: A Systematic Review, Integrative Framework and Future Research Agenda"](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijcs.12647) (*International Journal of Consumer Studies* 45(4), 2021) is the field's anchor systematic review, synthesizing the fragmented influencer-marketing literature into an integrative multidimensional framework organized around antecedents, mediators, moderators, outcomes, and contextual factors of influencer-marketing effects, and setting a research agenda — the standard peer-reviewed entry point for influencer-marketing effectiveness claims generally, as distinct from disclosure specifically.

On disclosure specifically, Sophie C. Boerman, Eva A. van Reijmersdal, and Peter C. Neijens's ["Sponsorship Disclosure: Effects of Duration on Persuasion Knowledge and Brand Responses"](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.2012.01677.x) (*Journal of Communication* 62(6), 2012, pp. 1047–1064) is the anchor experimental study: longer disclosure duration increases viewers' activation of persuasion knowledge (recognizing an ad as an ad), which in turn produces more negative brand attitudes and lower purchase intentions — the founding peer-reviewed evidence that disclosure is not merely a compliance formality but has a measurable downstream effect on brand outcomes.
Xia Liu and Hong Zhao's ["A meta-analysis of the effects of sponsorship disclosure in influencer marketing"](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11002-024-09757-z) (*Marketing Letters* 36(3), 2024, pp. 519–532) is the field's quantitative synthesis of that experimental literature, pooling effect sizes and finding, per its abstract, that disclosure significantly increases persuasion-knowledge activation while its pooled effects on brand attitude and conative (behavioral-intention) responses are statistically insignificant — meaning disclosure reliably makes audiences recognize sponsored content as sponsored, without reliably harming brand attitudes at the pooled level, a more precise and less alarmist finding than the single-study Boerman et al. (2012) result taken alone.
**[UNVERIFIED]**: this study confirmed the paper's existence, venue, and this directional finding (288 pooled effect sizes from 37 studies, 12,721 participants) via its public abstract, but could not access the full text to confirm the specific pooled effect-size figures (reported in an earlier draft as Hedges' g ≈ 0.60 for recognition and g ≈ −0.04 for brand attitude); those numeric values are removed here pending primary-source confirmation.

The primary regulatory source for disclosure requirements is the US Federal Trade Commission's Endorsement Guides, codified at [16 CFR Part 255, "Guides Concerning Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising"](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-255), revised in 2023 to add specific social-media examples: an influencer paid to post about a product must disclose that connection "clearly and conspicuously" rather than only in a bio or "more" section viewers could easily miss, and disclosure is required even when the endorser received free product rather than cash payment.
This is primary-source regulatory text, not scholarship, and this section's `influencer-marketing` term cites it as the governing US legal standard against which the Boerman et al. (2012) and Liu & Zhao (2024) disclosure-effectiveness findings should be read.

## Gaps carried forward

This study did not resolve, and carries forward rather than launders into settled fact:

- No peer-reviewed resolution of the Azoulay & Kapferer (2003) critique of Aaker's (1997) brand-personality scale was located; the methodological dispute over whether the scale isolates "personality" from other brand-identity facets appears open in the literature reviewed here.
- The Ehrenberg-Bass/positioning-school dispute (§3) is better evidenced on the Ehrenberg-Bass side (peer-reviewed antecedents: Ehrenberg, Goodhardt & Barwise 1990; Goodhardt, Ehrenberg & Chatfield 1984) than on the positioning-school rebuttal side, where this study located only trade-level pushback, not a peer-reviewed refutation using comparable panel-data methods.
- Liu & Zhao's (2024) meta-analytic disclosure-effect finding (pooled across 288 effect sizes from 37 studies) is confirmed only at the level of its public abstract; this study did not access the full text and so could not confirm the specific pooled effect-size figures an earlier draft had reported (Hedges' g ≈ 0.60 for recognition, g ≈ −0.04 for brand attitude) or how many platforms, jurisdictions, or disclosure-format types the pooled studies span.
  Both the exact effect sizes and their cross-platform/cross-jurisdiction generalization should be treated as **[UNVERIFIED]** pending direct inspection of that meta-analysis's full text and included-study table.
- This study located Balmer's corporate-marketing framework (§5) as peer-reviewed but did not attempt a full survey of the corporate-brand sub-literature (e.g., corporate reputation measurement, corporate heritage branding) beyond the two anchor Balmer citations; a fuller corporate-brand literature study is carried forward as future work if this section's `corporate-branding` scope grows past its current adjacency note.
- Ries & Trout (1981) is cited here only as the positioning school's trade-canon origin; this study did not attempt to locate whatever peer-reviewed positioning-theory literature may exist independent of the Ries/Trout trade lineage, since none was flagged as needed by this section's scope.
