**Mental and physical availability** is the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute's account of what makes brands grow: mental availability is the propensity for a brand to come to mind in buying situations, and physical availability is the ease of finding and buying it.
The tradition's founding empirical generalization is the **double jeopardy law**, formalized in Andrew S. C. Ehrenberg, Gerald J. Goodhardt, and T. Patrick Barwise's ["Double Jeopardy Revisited"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224299005400307) (*Journal of Marketing* 54(3), 1990, pp. 82–91): brands with lower market share suffer twice — fewer buyers (lower penetration) and slightly lower purchase frequency among those buyers (lower loyalty), a pattern documented as recurring across product categories rather than as a single dataset's finding.

That law rests on the earlier **Dirichlet model**, a peer-reviewed statistical model of repeat-buying behavior formalized in G. J. Goodhardt, A. S. C. Ehrenberg, and C. Chatfield's ["The Dirichlet: A Comprehensive Model of Buying Behaviour"](https://doi.org/10.2307/2981696) (*Journal of the Royal Statistical Society, Series A* 147(5), 1984, pp. 621–655), which underlies most Ehrenberg-Bass claims about purchase frequency and brand-switching across categories.

Byron Sharp's *[How Brands Grow](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/how-brands-grow-9780195573565)* (Oxford University Press, 2010) is the tradition's popularizing synthesis, arguing from cross-category purchase-panel data that brands grow primarily by expanding penetration, and that salient, ownable "distinctive assets" (logos, colors, taglines) matter more for growth than perceived differentiation.
This section treats *How Brands Grow* as empirically grounded — built on the peer-reviewed Ehrenberg/Goodhardt program above — but institutionally partisan: a practitioner-facing monograph by the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute's director, arguing against a named rival tradition.
**[UNVERIFIED]**: this study located practitioner-level pushback against Sharp's differentiation claim but no peer-reviewed refutation using comparable panel-data methods.

The named rival is the **positioning** school of Al Ries and Jack Trout's *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (McGraw-Hill, 1981), trade/practitioner advertising canon with no peer-reviewed empirical apparatus, cited here as the founding trade text the Ehrenberg-Bass tradition implicitly argues against — not as a study of comparable evidentiary weight.
Mental and physical availability should be distinguished from [brand equity](./brand-equity.md), a knowledge-based account of brand value that the Ehrenberg-Bass tradition does not rely on and, in Sharp's framing, partly displaces as a growth explanation.

See the [study of brand and marketing science](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-the-study-of-brand-and-marketing-science.md) for the fuller literature review, including the open evidentiary status of the Ehrenberg-Bass/positioning-school dispute.
