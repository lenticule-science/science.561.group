# The eCornell Business Strategy Certificate as of September 2026

This note is the research phase of the sixth book in the Group's study
companion series, written to the education science section's pattern
(`education-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/27/2026-09-27-note-on-how-a-study-companion-should-teach-toward-a-credential.md`).
It lives in the strategy section, which before this note held only a topic
page and a competitive-analysis term. The house glossary already defines
*network effect* and *two-sided market* (filed under work science) and the
business science section's companion note on Business Management Essentials
covers generic strategies, the value chain and SWOT, which this book builds
on.

## What the certificate publishes about itself

Read from eCornell on 2026-09-27 [@ecornell-bs-2026]. Business Strategy is
offered by Cornell's Johnson Graduate School of Management and authored by
Justin P. Johnson, professor of economics and area coordinator for strategy
and business economics. Online, about three months at three to five hours a
week, $3,900; 60 professional development hours (6 CEUs), also counted toward
SHRM and HRCI recertification. Six two-week courses, plus a two-year
Leadership Symposium membership and a six-month pass to live AI workshops:

| Code | Course | Published takeaways, in brief |
|---|---|---|
| LSM311 | Value Creation and Profitability | Diagnose the market and how to create value for target customers; align resources with value creation; identify and assess threats to profits; select strategies to mitigate them; find win-win opportunities with complementors |
| LSM312 | Change, Disruption, and Growth | Assess change, disruption and uncertainty in an industry; evaluate responses and devise strategies; identify and mitigate internal challenges to change |
| LSM313 | Synergies, Mergers, and Alliances | Identify synergies; choose how to realize them (merger or acquisition, partnership, organic growth); evaluate partner fit; plan realization and track it |
| LSM314 | Getting the Most Out of Your Business Relationships | Identify key relationships and assess their health; maintain them long term; anticipate and mitigate common challenges |
| LSM315 | Game Theory and Business Strategy | Identify the players and their motivations and capabilities; map likely responses to your moves; see your business from their perspective; change the game to your advantage |
| LSM316 | Digital Platform Strategy | Evaluate a platform's potential; plan to start, grow and defend a platform; sell through or interact with someone else's platform |

## Where sources disagree

1. **How often acquisitions fail.** A widely repeated range says 70 to 90
   percent of acquisitions fail [@christensen2011]; Sirower found that most
   acquirers in his sample paid premiums that the promised synergies couldn't
   repay [@sirower1997]. The figures depend on what counts as failure
   (acquirer's share price, stated targets, later divestiture) and on the
   sample. *Reading:* that acquirers often overpay for synergies is supported;
   any single failure rate is supported only under its own definition. The book
   gives the reasons deals fail and names the range as the authors' estimate.
2. **Whether disruption theory predicts.** Christensen's theory explains how
   leaders lose to entrants starting at the low end or in new markets
   [@christensen1997; @christensen2015]. Lepore argued its case studies were
   selective [@lepore2014]; King and Baatartogtokh asked experts about the
   theory's own 77 examples and found that only a minority fit all of its
   conditions [@king-baatartogtokh2015]. *Reading:* both. The mechanism is
   supported where its conditions hold; the theory is refuted as a general
   predictor. The book teaches the conditions and asks readers to test them.
3. **Kotter's steps.** Kotter's eight steps for leading change are widely
   taught [@kotter1995]. A review found the model's parts have support but the
   sequence as a whole had little direct empirical testing [@appelbaum2012].
   *Reading:* both; the book presents the steps as a checklist of failure
   points, not a proven procedure.
4. **Do networks tip to one winner?** Strong network effects can make a
   market winner-take-all [@katz-shapiro1985], but when users can use several
   platforms at once (multi-homing) and differentiation matters, more than one
   platform survives [@eisenmann2006]. *Reading:* both, by condition; the book
   teaches the conditions for tipping.

## How the house's theories read the same material

- **Strategy is a game in the precise sense.** The house's theory of
  computing (the logic section's question note on the theory of computing)
  lands on concurrent games as the model of interaction; its theory of agents
  treats an agent as a process choosing moves against others. Game theory in
  strategy is the same object at business scale: players, moves, payoffs,
  information, and commitments that change what others will do
  [@schelling1960]. The book teaches game trees and payoff tables and, for
  the reader who wants it, names the connection.
- **Value created is a width, not a price.** Brandenburger and Stuart define
  the value a firm creates as the gap between customers' willingness to pay
  and suppliers' opportunity cost; strategy is creating that gap and capturing
  part of it [@brandenburger-stuart1996]. This is the economics section's
  *willingness to pay* term applied to a firm, and it's the frame the book
  uses throughout.
- **The Group runs a two-sided market.** The house's x402 market sells
  paid operations to agents and pays sellers; its catalog, facilitator fees
  and discovery listings are platform decisions of exactly the kind the
  sixth course teaches (which side to subsidize, how to seed supply). The
  book's platform chapter uses a neutral example; the house note on the x402
  economy is the Group's own case.

## What the book is

*Business Strategy, Course by Course: An Independent Study Companion to
Cornell's Business Strategy Certificate.* Series number 6. A chapter per
course, a strategy toolkit summary, a spaced study plan and a glossary. One
running example: a regional online marketplace connecting small farms with
restaurants, facing a national food distributor. It states that it isn't
affiliated with or endorsed by Cornell University or eCornell.
