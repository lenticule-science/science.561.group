Financial statements are structured claims about a reporting entity's financial
position and changes over a period. They are produced from a ledger together
with adjustments, consolidation, classification, measurement, and disclosure;
they are not raw ledger dumps.

Before reading a statement, identify the entity, reporting period or date,
accounting basis, presentation currency, consolidation scope, and level of
assurance. The same event can be classified differently from another entity's
perspective or under another reporting purpose.

## Balance sheet

The balance sheet (or statement of financial position) shows the accounting
equation at a point in time:

    Assets = Liabilities + Equity

- **Assets** are recognized resources controlled by the entity under the
  governing basis.
- **Liabilities** are recognized present obligations of the entity.
- **Equity** is the residual interest after liabilities are deducted from
  assets under the statement's measurement bases.

Book equity is not automatically liquidation proceeds, enterprise value, or the
amount owners could withdraw. Realization costs, unrecognized items, market
values, restrictions, priority claims, and tax effects can make those amounts
different.

## Income statement

The income statement (or profit and loss statement) covers a period — a month,
a quarter, a year:

    Profit or loss = Recognized income − Recognized expenses

- **Income** records recognized increases in economic benefits other than owner
  contributions, subject to the governing terminology and basis.
- **Expenses** record recognized decreases in economic benefits other than
  owner distributions.

Profit or loss measures recognized performance under the selected basis. It
does not by itself measure cash generation, solvency, economic welfare, or
whether distributions are prudent or permitted.

## Cash flow

A statement of cash flows explains the change between beginning and ending cash
and cash equivalents through operating, investing, and financing activities.
The classification requires the entity's account and transaction structure;
subtracting any two cash balances or listing changes in all asset accounts does
not produce a classified statement.

The report connects cash movement to accrual performance and financing, but it
does not forecast liquidity by itself. Forecast receipts, restrictions,
commitments, maturities, and available funding also matter.

## Other statements and notes

Depending on the reporting framework and entity, a complete set may also
include a statement of changes in equity, comprehensive income, comparative
periods, and notes. Notes describe policies, judgments, measurement uncertainty,
commitments, contingencies, related parties, and disaggregations that the face
statements cannot safely compress.

## Connections among the statements

- Ending cash in the cash-flow statement ties to the corresponding balance-sheet
  population.
- Profit or loss affects equity, but owner transactions and other recognized
  components also change equity.
- Purchases, disposals, borrowing, repayment, noncash exchanges, and valuation
  changes help explain movement between opening and closing balance sheets.
- A trial balance can feed statement preparation, but presentation still
  requires classification, elimination, adjustment, and disclosure judgments.

## Reading the reports

- Compare periods only after checking changes in entity scope, policy,
  classification, units, and material estimates.
- Investigate composition and maturity, not only totals. Restricted cash is not
  interchangeable with operating cash, and long-dated debt differs from an
  immediately due obligation.
- Explain profit-to-cash differences through receivables, payables, inventory,
  prepayments, noncash charges, capital activity, financing, and other supported
  reconciling items.
- Treat missing cost, uncertain valuation, and unresolved reconciliation as
  information to disclose, not as zeros that make the statements appear
  complete.
