After completing this lesson, you'll be able to read the three primary [financial statements](../../../glossary/terms/financial-statements.md) — the balance sheet, the income statement, and the cash flow statement — identify what each one tells you about a business, and explain how the three statements connect to form a single interlocking picture.

## From trial balance to financial statements

In the previous lesson's exercises, you built a trial balance for a small consulting business:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | \$14,800 | |
| Equipment | \$3,000 | |
| Inventory | \$2,000 | |
| Accounts Payable | | \$2,000 |
| Loans Payable | | \$5,000 |
| Owner's Equity | | \$10,000 |
| Service Revenue | | \$4,000 |
| Rent Expense | \$1,200 | |
| **Total** | **\$21,000** | **\$21,000** |

A trial balance establishes aggregate debit-credit agreement for its selected
ledger population, but it does not establish correct classification,
measurement, or completeness. Financial statements transform the adjusted
ledger into several connected views:

1. **Balance sheet** — What does the business own and owe right now?
2. **Income statement** — Did the business make or lose money over a period?
3. **Cash flow statement** — Where did cash come from and where did it go?

Accounts do not simply “land on exactly one” statement. Balance-sheet accounts
present position, revenue and expense accounts present performance, and
transaction and account mappings classify cash movements. Profit also affects
equity, comparative balances support cash-flow preparation, and material policy
or uncertainty may require notes.

## The balance sheet

The [balance sheet](../../../glossary/terms/balance-sheet.md) is a snapshot of the business at a single point in time. It's the accounting equation in report form:

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}$$

Using the trial balance above, here's the balance sheet for the consulting business as of March 31:

**Balance Sheet — As of March 31**

| **Assets** | |
|---|---|
| Cash | \$14,800 |
| Inventory | \$2,000 |
| Equipment | \$3,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **\$19,800** |

| **Liabilities** | |
|---|---|
| Accounts Payable | \$2,000 |
| Loans Payable | \$5,000 |
| **Total Liabilities** | **\$7,000** |

| **Equity** | |
|---|---|
| Owner's Equity | \$10,000 |
| Net Income (from income statement) | \$2,800 |
| **Total Equity** | **\$12,800** |

| | |
|---|---|
| **Total Liabilities + Equity** | **\$19,800** |

Total assets (\$19,800) equal total liabilities plus equity (\$19,800). The
example also presents the period's \$2,800 profit within equity. Profit changes
the residual interest, but that does not mean the amount is immediately owed or
distributable to owners.

## The income statement

The [income statement](../../../glossary/terms/income-statement.md) covers a
period and presents recognized income and expense under the reporting basis.
“Spent” is a cash-flow concept and need not occur in the same period as expense.

The structure is straightforward: revenues minus expenses equals net income.

**Income Statement — For the Month Ended March 31**

| | |
|---|---|
| Service Revenue | \$4,000 |
| Less: Rent Expense | (\$1,200) |
| **Net Income** | **\$2,800** |

That \$2,800 is the number that flows into equity on the balance sheet. If expenses had exceeded revenue, the business would've reported a net loss, and equity would've decreased.

A real business will have many more line items — cost of goods sold, salaries, utilities, depreciation — but the logic stays the same. Revenue minus expenses gives you the bottom line.

## The cash flow statement

The [cash flow statement](../../../glossary/terms/cash-flow-statement.md)
reconciles beginning and ending cash and cash equivalents through classified
cash flows. Noncash transactions are excluded from the reconciliation but may
require separate disclosure. The common classes are:

- **Operating activities** — cash generated or spent running the core business (collecting from customers, paying suppliers, paying rent).
- **Investing activities** — cash spent on or received from long-term assets (buying equipment, selling property).
- **Financing activities** — cash from or to owners and lenders (owner investments, loan proceeds, loan repayments, dividends).

Here's the cash flow statement for the consulting business:

**Cash Flow Statement — For the Month Ended March 31**

| **Operating Activities** | |
|---|---|
| Cash received from clients | \$4,000 |
| Cash paid for rent | (\$1,200) |
| **Net cash from operating activities** | **\$2,800** |

| **Investing Activities** | |
|---|---|
| Purchase of equipment | (\$3,000) |
| **Net cash from investing activities** | **(\$3,000)** |

| **Financing Activities** | |
|---|---|
| Owner's investment | \$10,000 |
| Loan proceeds | \$5,000 |
| **Net cash from financing activities** | **\$15,000** |

| | |
|---|---|
| **Net increase in cash** | **\$14,800** |
| Cash at beginning of period | \$0 |
| **Cash at end of period** | **\$14,800** |

The ending cash of \$14,800 matches the corresponding Cash line in this example.
In a full statement, the defined cash-and-cash-equivalents population must tie
to the same balance-sheet population, including any required reconciliation for
restricted or differently presented amounts.

### Why profit doesn't equal cash

This simple example happens to show equal operating cash flow and net income (\$2,800), because the business collected all its revenue in cash and paid all its expenses in cash during the same period. Real businesses aren't that tidy.

Consider what happens if the consulting business completed \$4,000 of work in March but the client won't pay until April. Under accrual accounting, the income statement still shows \$4,000 in revenue and \$2,800 in net income — the business earned that money. But the cash flow statement would show \$0 received from clients, making operating cash flow negative \$1,200 (just the rent payment going out).

The business reports profit while operating cash decreases. Receivable growth,
capital purchases, debt service, owner distributions, prepayments, inventory,
and other balance changes can produce this divergence. Some are timing effects;
others are investing or financing decisions rather than future reversals of
income.

## How the three connect

The three statements form an interlocking system:

1. **Profit or loss** affects **equity**, alongside owner transactions and any
   other recognized components of equity.
2. **Ending cash** from the cash flow statement matches the **cash** line on the balance sheet. The cash flow statement explains how cash moved from its beginning balance to its ending balance.
3. The balance sheet must balance after incorporating both. If you change a number on one statement, at least one number on another statement must also change to keep the system consistent.

Think of it this way: the balance sheet is a photograph — it shows where things stand at one moment. The income statement and cash flow statement are videos — they show what happened between two photographs. Together, they give you both position and motion.

## Self-check exercises

**Exercise 1**: Using the trial balance below, prepare a balance sheet. Does it balance?

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | \$8,500 | |
| Accounts Receivable | \$3,000 | |
| Equipment | \$6,000 | |
| Accounts Payable | | \$1,500 |
| Loans Payable | | \$4,000 |
| Owner's Equity | | \$7,000 |
| Service Revenue | | \$9,000 |
| Salaries Expense | \$3,500 | |
| Rent Expense | \$500 | |
| **Total** | **\$21,500** | **\$21,500** |

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

First, calculate net income: \$9,000 revenue - \$3,500 salaries - \$500 rent = \$5,000 net income.

**Balance Sheet**

| **Assets** | |
|---|---|
| Cash | \$8,500 |
| Accounts Receivable | \$3,000 |
| Equipment | \$6,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **\$17,500** |

| **Liabilities** | |
|---|---|
| Accounts Payable | \$1,500 |
| Loans Payable | \$4,000 |
| **Total Liabilities** | **\$5,500** |

| **Equity** | |
|---|---|
| Owner's Equity | \$7,000 |
| Net Income | \$5,000 |
| **Total Equity** | **\$12,000** |

Total Liabilities + Equity = \$5,500 + \$12,000 = \$17,500. It balances.

</details>

**Exercise 2**: For each item, identify its primary face-statement location and,
where the item describes a cash transaction, its cash-flow class:

1. Accounts Receivable
2. Salaries Expense
3. Cash paid to purchase a truck
4. Loans Payable
5. Service Revenue
6. Owner's investment of cash into the business

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. **Accounts Receivable** — balance sheet asset.
2. **Salaries Expense** — income statement expense; cash paid for salaries also
   affects operating cash flow, but expense and payment timing can differ.
3. **Cash paid to purchase a truck** — the truck appears as a balance-sheet
   asset and the payment is commonly an investing cash outflow.
4. **Loans Payable** — balance sheet liability; borrowing and principal
   repayment commonly affect financing cash flow.
5. **Service Revenue** — income statement income; collection commonly affects
   operating cash flow in its collection period.
6. **Owner's investment of cash** — increases balance-sheet cash and equity and
   is a financing cash inflow.

</details>

**Exercise 3**: A business reports net income of \$20,000 for the quarter. During the same quarter, cash decreased by \$5,000. Give at least two reasons that could explain why a profitable business saw its cash decline.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Several timing and investment factors can cause cash to drop even when a business is profitable:

1. **Uncollected receivables** — The business recognized revenue but hasn't received cash yet. If it billed \$15,000 of work that clients won't pay until next quarter, that revenue boosted net income but not cash.
2. **Capital purchases** — The business bought long-term assets (equipment, vehicles, property) with cash. These purchases don't appear on the income statement as expenses (they're assets), but they drain cash immediately.
3. **Loan repayments** — The business repaid principal on a loan. Loan repayments reduce cash but aren't an expense on the income statement (only interest is an expense; principal repayment is a financing activity).
4. **Prepaid expenses** — The business paid cash up front for something it'll use over several periods (like an annual insurance premium), reducing cash now while the expense hits the income statement gradually.

Any two of these (or similar timing explanations) would be correct.

</details>

**Exercise 4**: If profit increases by \$500 from a cash service sale with no
associated unrecorded cost or tax effect, what happens on the balance sheet?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Two things change on the balance sheet:

1. **Cash (an asset) increases by \$500** — because the sale was collected in cash.
2. **Equity increases by \$500** — because the additional recognized profit
   increases the residual interest.

Total assets go up by \$500, total equity goes up by \$500, and the balance sheet stays balanced. On the cash flow statement, operating cash flow also increases by \$500, and ending cash matches the new cash balance on the balance sheet.

</details>
