After completing this lesson, you'll be able to calculate and interpret the most commonly used financial ratios, perform horizontal and vertical analysis, and explain what these tools reveal (and don't reveal) about a business.

## Why raw numbers aren't enough

A business with \$500,000 in revenue sounds impressive — but is it? That depends on the industry, the company's size, last year's revenue, and the profit left after expenses. A \$500,000 top line means something very different for a solo consultant than for a manufacturing plant with 50 employees and \$480,000 in costs.

Financial statement analysis converts statement amounts into relationships and
trends that can support comparison across periods or entities. It does not make
unlike accounting policies, scopes, business models, or measurement bases
comparable by arithmetic alone. Three common tools are horizontal analysis,
vertical analysis, and ratio analysis.

## Horizontal analysis (trend analysis)

Horizontal analysis compares the same line item across two or more periods. You calculate the dollar change and the percentage change for every major line item, then look for patterns.

**Worked example.** Here's a simplified [income statement](../../../glossary/terms/income-statement.md) for two years:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | $ Change | % Change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | \$400,000 | \$500,000 | \$100,000 | 25.0% |
| Cost of Goods Sold | \$240,000 | \$320,000 | \$80,000 | 33.3% |
| Gross Profit | \$160,000 | \$180,000 | \$20,000 | 12.5% |
| Operating Expenses | \$100,000 | \$115,000 | \$15,000 | 15.0% |
| Net Income | \$60,000 | \$65,000 | \$5,000 | 8.3% |

Revenue grew 25%, while
[cost of goods sold](../../../glossary/terms/cost-of-goods-sold.md) grew 33.3%,
compressing gross margin. Operating expenses rose 15% and net income 8.3%.
Those relationships identify questions about price, volume, mix, cost behavior,
classification, and scope; the percentages alone do not label the changes good,
bad, efficient, or controllable.

**Formulas:**

- Dollar change = Current period amount − Prior period amount
- Percentage change = Dollar change / Prior period amount × 100

## Vertical analysis (common-size statements)

Vertical analysis expresses line items as percentages of a selected base. Income
statements commonly use revenue and balance sheets commonly use total assets.
Common-size statements remove one scale difference, but they do not remove
differences in business model, policy, period, entity scope, or data quality.

**Worked example.** Here's a common-size income statement:

| | Amount | % of Revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | \$500,000 | 100.0% |
| Cost of Goods Sold | \$320,000 | 64.0% |
| Gross Profit | \$180,000 | 36.0% |
| Salaries | \$60,000 | 12.0% |
| Rent | \$24,000 | 4.8% |
| Marketing | \$16,000 | 3.2% |
| Other Operating Expenses | \$15,000 | 3.0% |
| Total Operating Expenses | \$115,000 | 23.0% |
| Net Income | \$65,000 | 13.0% |

The 64% COGS share can now be compared numerically with a competitor's 70%.
Explaining the difference still requires compatible COGS definitions and
evidence about product mix, pricing, production model, inventory accounting,
capacity use, and supplier terms; it does not prove greater efficiency.

## Liquidity ratios

Liquidity ratios answer a simple question: can the business pay its short-term obligations?

**Current ratio** = Current Assets / Current Liabilities

This compares recognized current assets with recognized current liabilities at
one reporting date. A company with \$150,000 in current assets and \$100,000 in
current liabilities has a current ratio of 1.5, or \$1.50 of current assets per
\$1.00 of current liabilities. It does not establish that those assets will
convert to cash before the obligations fall due.

**Quick ratio** = (Cash + marketable securities + qualifying receivables) /
Current Liabilities

The quick ratio excludes inventory and other current assets that are not treated
as quick assets under the selected definition. Definitions vary, so the report
must state whether restricted cash, doubtful receivables, or other items are
included.

**Interpretation.** A current ratio below 1.0 means current liabilities exceed
current assets. That can indicate liquidity pressure, but it is not a default
prediction: collection timing, inventory conversion, committed facilities,
seasonality, and the composition of both numerator and denominator matter.

## Profitability ratios

Profitability ratios measure whether the business generates adequate returns relative to its revenue, assets, or equity.

**Worked example.** Consider a company with these numbers:

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | \$500,000 |
| Cost of Goods Sold | \$320,000 |
| Net Income | \$65,000 |
| Total Assets | \$400,000 |
| Total Equity | \$250,000 |

**Gross profit margin** = (Revenue − COGS) / Revenue = (\$500,000 − \$320,000) / \$500,000 = 36.0%. This states the recognized gross profit remaining per revenue unit under the entity's COGS classification. Price, volume, mix, costing policy, and capacity use all can affect it.

**Net profit margin** = Net Income / Revenue = \$65,000 / \$500,000 = 13.0%. This is the bottom line — what percentage of each revenue dollar becomes profit after all expenses.

**Return on assets (ROA)** commonly uses profit divided by average total assets
for the period. Using closing assets here gives \$65,000 / \$400,000 = 16.3%, a
simplified return relative to one point-in-time denominator.

**Return on equity (ROE)** commonly uses profit available to the relevant equity
holders divided by their average equity. Using closing total equity here gives
\$65,000 / \$250,000 = 26.0%.

ROE exceeds the simplified ROA in this example partly because equity is a
smaller denominator than assets. Leverage can magnify returns or losses to
equity, but explaining the gap also requires compatible numerators, average
balances, financing cost, tax effects, and noncontrolling interests.

## Leverage ratios

Leverage ratios reveal how the business is financed — the mix of debt and equity.

**Debt-to-equity ratio** = Total Liabilities / Total Equity. Using the same company: total liabilities are \$150,000 (\$400,000 total assets − \$250,000 equity), so debt-to-equity = \$150,000 / \$250,000 = 0.6. The business has \$0.60 of debt for every \$1.00 of equity.

**Liabilities-to-assets ratio** = Total Liabilities / Total Assets = \$150,000 /
\$400,000 = 37.5%. It compares recognized liabilities with recognized assets;
it does not trace which particular asset was “financed by” which claim.

Borrowing can expand available resources and magnify equity outcomes. It also
creates contractual claims whose timing, rate, security, covenants, currency,
and refinancing exposure may not move with operating results. Risk depends on
those terms and cash-flow resilience, not on a leverage ratio alone.

## Limitations

Financial statement analysis is powerful, but it has blind spots:

- **Mixed measurement bases.** Statements may combine historical cost,
  amortized cost, fair value, net realizable value, impairment, and other bases.
  A ratio can therefore combine amounts that do not share one valuation date or
  economic meaning.
- **Accounting standards.** Different companies might make different (but equally valid) choices about [depreciation](../../../glossary/terms/depreciation.md) methods, [revenue recognition](../../../glossary/terms/revenue-recognition.md) timing, or inventory costing. These choices affect the numbers ratios are built on.
- **Non-financial factors.** Ratios don't capture management quality, employee morale, pending lawsuits, competitive dynamics, or technological shifts.
- **Operating-model differences.** The same ratio can imply different risks for
  entities with different collection cycles, inventory liquidity, contractual
  cash flows, asset lives, and access to financing. Compare definitions,
  accounting policies, business models, and multiple periods before comparing
  the numbers.

Ratios are a starting point for questions, not a source of final answers.

## Self-check exercises

**Exercise 1.** A company has these balance sheet items: Cash \$40,000, Accounts Receivable \$55,000, Inventory \$35,000, Prepaid Expenses \$10,000, Accounts Payable \$60,000, Short-term Loans Payable \$30,000. Calculate the current ratio and quick ratio. Interpret the results.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Current Assets = \$40,000 + \$55,000 + \$35,000 + \$10,000 = \$140,000

Current Liabilities = \$60,000 + \$30,000 = \$90,000

**Current ratio** = \$140,000 / \$90,000 = 1.56. The business reports \$1.56 of
current assets for every \$1.00 of current liabilities. This static relation
does not prove that conversion and payment timing align.

Using cash and receivables as the stated quick assets, **quick ratio** =
(\$40,000 + \$55,000) / \$90,000 = 1.06. Inventory and prepaid expenses are
excluded because neither is a quick asset under this definition.

The gap shows that inventory and prepayments materially affect the current
ratio. Collection quality, restrictions, maturity timing, and available funding
still require separate analysis.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** A business has Revenue of \$800,000, Cost of Goods Sold of \$520,000, and Net Income of \$84,000. Calculate the gross profit margin and net profit margin. What does the gap between them tell you?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Gross profit margin** = (\$800,000 − \$520,000) / \$800,000 = \$280,000 / \$800,000 = 35.0%

**Net profit margin** = \$84,000 / \$800,000 = 10.5%

The 24.5 percentage-point gap contains every recognized item between gross
profit and net income under this presentation. It may include operating expense,
financing cost, tax, gains, losses, and other items; the three supplied totals do
not support attributing the entire gap to operating expense or declaring the
production side satisfactory.

</details>

**Exercise 3.** Company A has ROE of 25% and a debt-to-equity ratio of 3:1. Company B has ROE of 15% and a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.5:1. Which company is "better"?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Neither is objectively better — it depends on how you weigh return against risk.

Company A's higher ROE is partially driven by heavy leverage. A 3:1 debt-to-equity ratio means the company has \$3 of debt for every \$1 of equity. Borrowed money is amplifying the owners' returns, but it also amplifies risk. If revenue drops, Company A still owes interest and principal payments on all that debt.

Company B's ROE is lower, but it comes with much less financial risk. A 0.5:1 debt-to-equity ratio means the company relies primarily on equity financing. It's more resilient to downturns.

An investor who prioritizes stability might prefer B. An investor who prioritizes returns and tolerates risk might prefer A. The key insight is that you can't evaluate ROE without also looking at how much leverage produced it.

</details>

**Exercise 4.** Perform vertical analysis on the following income statement by expressing each line as a percentage of revenue.

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | \$250,000 |
| Cost of Goods Sold | \$150,000 |
| Gross Profit | \$100,000 |
| Salaries | \$40,000 |
| Rent | \$12,000 |
| Utilities | \$3,000 |
| Marketing | \$10,000 |
| Net Income | \$35,000 |

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

| | Amount | % of Revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | \$250,000 | 100.0% |
| Cost of Goods Sold | \$150,000 | 60.0% |
| Gross Profit | \$100,000 | 40.0% |
| Salaries | \$40,000 | 16.0% |
| Rent | \$12,000 | 4.8% |
| Utilities | \$3,000 | 1.2% |
| Marketing | \$10,000 | 4.0% |
| Net Income | \$35,000 | 14.0% |

The business retains 40 cents of every revenue dollar after COGS. Salaries are the largest operating expense at 16% of revenue. The net profit margin of 14% means the business keeps 14 cents of every dollar earned.

</details>
