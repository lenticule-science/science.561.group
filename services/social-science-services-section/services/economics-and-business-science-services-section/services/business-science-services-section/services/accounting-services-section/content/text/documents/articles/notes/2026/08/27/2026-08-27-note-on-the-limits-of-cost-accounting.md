Cost accounting can measure every monetary amount, labor interval, material
movement, and overhead allocation that its recognition rules admit. That is a
real and useful capability. It does not follow that the resulting account
captures every form of labor, every causal contribution, every avoided harm,
or every condition that made production possible.

The boundary is easiest to state as a distinction:

> Cost accounting allocates recognized costs. It does not perfectly attribute
> the production of value.

Allocation applies a rule to a defined population of costs. Attribution makes
a causal claim about how an outcome came to exist. A job-cost report may show
which paid hours and purchased materials were assigned to a job. It cannot, by
that fact alone, establish which relationships, knowledge, care, coordination,
or prevented failures made the job possible.

## Accounting is a recognition system

No accounting system encounters an unclassified world. It begins with a
reporting entity, a unit of account, recognition criteria, measurement rules,
account categories, time periods, and evidence requirements. Those choices
make some events legible as transactions and leave others outside the ledger.

Precision operates after recognition. A system can calculate a recognized
labor cost to the cent while remaining silent about labor that was never coded
as paid time, assigned to the job, or admitted as an accounting event. Greater
decimal precision does not repair a missing category or an incorrect boundary.

This is not a defect unique to poor software. Classification makes coordinated
action possible by reducing variation. The same reduction necessarily creates
residual cases: work classified as miscellaneous, work recorded only in notes,
work visible only when it fails, and work that the formal schema cannot express.

## Work can disappear before costing begins

Arlene Kaplan Daniels's account of invisible work identifies a prior social
selection: ordinary understandings of “real work” center paid employment, so
unpaid domestic, community, and institution-maintaining labor can disappear
from view before measurement starts. An accounting system commonly inherits
that boundary. If labor means a wage, timecard, billable interval, or cost-center
assignment, socially necessary activity outside those forms does not become a
zero-cost line item; it is absent from the population being costed.

Arlie Hochschild's emotional labor exposes a related gap inside paid work.
Managing expression, patience, reassurance, deference, and composure may be
constitutive of service, care, teaching, hospitality, administration, and
conflict management. Yet its successful product is often that no visible
breakdown occurs. The shift and wage appear in the records; the regulation that
made the shift succeed may not.

These omissions are not randomly distributed. Gendered and racialized divisions
of domestic, reproductive, care, migrant, and service work influence which
contributions are treated as background conditions, personal qualities, or
professional demeanor rather than labor with claims on institutional resources.

## Cooperative work depends on articulation work

Anselm Strauss describes articulation work as the activity required to make
distributed tasks cohere: sequencing dependencies, negotiating handoffs,
repairing disruptions, translating between specialties, remembering context,
and aligning people and materials. Kjeld Schmidt and Liam Bannon carry this
problem into computer-supported cooperative work: supporting many formal tasks
is insufficient when the interdependence among those tasks remains unsupported.

An organization that accounts only for assigned tasks and final deliverables
will tend to recognize articulation indirectly—as delay when it is missing, or
as smooth operation when it succeeds. Adding a coordination field to a database
can expose part of the work, but it also creates new work: deciding what belongs
in the field, maintaining its categories, resolving exceptions, and making its
entries intelligible to other people.

The system has not eliminated background work. It has moved the boundary
between foreground and background.

## Visibility is an intervention

Lucy Suchman's analysis of making work visible blocks a tempting conclusion:
that hidden work need only be exposed and measured. Visibility requires a
representation. Someone chooses what counts as evidence, who may describe the
work, which audience receives the description, and what consequences follow.

Susan Leigh Star and Anselm Strauss therefore treat visible and invisible work
as an ecology rather than a binary. Work may be visible to coworkers but not
managers, to clients but not payroll, or to auditors only after failure. It may
appear as helpfulness or personality rather than as labor. A new measurement can
redistribute voice and attention, but it can also increase surveillance,
discipline, or uncompensated documentation.

“Make the work visible” is consequently a governance decision, not a neutral
database operation.

## Commensuration produces useful sameness

Wendy Espeland and Mitchell Stevens describe quantification as the production
and communication of numbers and analyze commensuration as the comparison of
different qualities through a common metric. Cost accounting depends on this
power. Machine operation, mentoring, conflict repair, routine administration,
and crisis prevention can all become labor hours or currency amounts that may
be compared and allocated.

The comparison is produced by the measurement rule; it is not proof that the
underlying activities are interchangeable. Context is removed so that budgeting,
pricing, variance analysis, and control become possible. The resulting number
answers the question defined by the rule. It should not be promoted into an
answer to every question about contribution or worth.

## A finite model cannot absorb unlimited work variety

W. Ross Ashby's law of requisite variety holds that a regulator needs sufficient
variety to absorb the variety of the disturbances it must regulate. Roger Conant
and Ashby's good-regulator theorem links effective regulation to a model of the
regulated system. Applied cautiously to accounting, the lesson is that a labor
model cannot distinguish differences its schema does not represent.

Adding categories can improve a model. It cannot make a practical accounting
system coextensive with the world. A mentoring category requires a boundary for
mentoring. A safety category requires rules for preventive work whose principal
result is a non-event. A coordination category still depends on uncategorized
work that coordinates the category. The residual moves as the schema improves;
it does not disappear.

## Consequential metrics change the work

Measurement becomes reflexive when it governs pay, promotion, discipline,
staffing, investment, or status. Workers and managers reorganize activity around
what the metric rewards, neglects, or makes gameable. The measure stops merely
describing a labor process and becomes one of the forces producing it.

Michael Power's account of audit society adds a material consequence: an
organization increasingly oriented toward audit must produce auditable traces.
Documentation, compliance, dashboard maintenance, self-reporting, and exception
handling become part of the labor process. Some of that accounting-work is
itself measured; some becomes a new invisible remainder.

A control system therefore needs to ask not only whether a measure correlates
with the desired activity, but how behavior will change once consequences attach
to the measure and who will perform the additional work needed to sustain it.

## Prevented events have value without revenue

The statement that safety, compliance, maintenance, or care “generates no
revenue” already assumes that transaction revenue is the relevant theory of
value. Preventing an injury, shutdown, lawsuit, equipment failure, burnout, or
loss of trust often appears as nothing happening. The counterfactual event is
not a sale, and the avoided loss is not automatically recognized as income.

Calling the preventive function an expense may be correct financial-statement
classification. Treating that classification as proof that the function creates
no value is a category error. A bridge support generates no traffic, but the
traffic depends on it.

## Design consequences

A responsible cost system does not abandon quantification. It bounds the claims
made from quantified data and preserves channels for information the schema
cannot absorb. In practice:

- state the reporting entity, recognition boundary, allocation base, and purpose
  of every cost model;
- distinguish direct observation from allocation, estimate, and causal claim;
- retain “other” and exception records as evidence about model limits rather
  than automatically treating them as data-cleaning failures;
- let affected workers contest classifications and supply situated knowledge;
- evaluate who performs data-entry, reconciliation, and exception-handling work;
- pair output and labor metrics with qualitative review of coordination, care,
  safety, and prevented harm;
- monitor how behavior changes after a metric acquires consequences; and
- revise the model when residuals show a recurring distinction relevant to the
  decision the model is meant to support.

The conclusion is not that labor cannot be counted. It is that every count
contains a theory of labor. Capital can count every bean it has defined as a
bean; the count cannot prove that nothing else is food.
