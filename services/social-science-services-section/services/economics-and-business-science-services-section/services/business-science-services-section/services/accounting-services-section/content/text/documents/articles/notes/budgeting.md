A budget is a governed plan expressed in financial and operational measures. It
states an entity, horizon, assumptions, expected activity, resource constraints,
and baseline against which compatible actual results can be compared.

## Why budget

- It connects strategy to operating volume, capacity, commitments, capital
  expenditure, financing, and liquidity.
- It exposes whether assumptions made by different responsibility centers can
  coexist—for example, whether purchasing, staffing, fulfillment, and cash
  collection support the same sales plan.
- Comparison with actuals can reveal timing, volume, price, mix,
  classification, and execution differences.
- Scenario and sensitivity analysis make uncertainty visible instead of
  burying it in one point estimate.

## Structure

A budget maps to the chart of accounts but need not mirror it one-for-one.
Operational drivers, projects, responsibility centers, cash timing, and
commitments may require dimensions that do not belong in account names. A
simple operating view might include:

| Account                     | Monthly Budget |
|-----------------------------|---------------|
| Income:Salary               | 4,000.00      |
| Expenses:Housing:Rent       | 1,200.00      |
| Expenses:Food:Groceries     | 400.00        |
| Expenses:Food:Dining        | 100.00        |
| Expenses:Transport          | 150.00        |
| Expenses:Utilities          | 120.00        |
| Expenses:Insurance          | 200.00        |
| Planned operating surplus   | 500.00        |

Saving is normally a transfer or retained surplus, not an expense. An accrual
budget must reconcile expected income and expense, while a cash budget
separately proves that opening cash, receipts, payments, financing, and minimum
reserves are coherent over time.

## Methods

- **Incremental budgeting** adjusts a prior baseline. It is inexpensive but can
  preserve obsolete assumptions.
- **Zero-based budgeting** requires proposed activity and cost to be justified
  from a stated baseline. It can expose assumptions but consumes analysis and
  does not guarantee better allocation.
- **Flexible budgeting** restates variable components at actual activity so
  volume effects are not confused with price or efficiency effects.
- **Rolling budgeting** extends the horizon as periods expire. It preserves a
  forward view while keeping the original approved baseline distinct.

## Budget vs. actual

The value of a budget is in the comparison. At period close, generate the
income statement and compare each line to the budget:

- A lower expense can reflect efficiency, lower activity, deferred maintenance,
  or an omitted invoice; “favorable” is not self-explanatory.
- A higher expense can reflect excess unit cost, higher productive volume, a
  classification difference, or an unplanned obligation.
- Revenue variance should be decomposed into price, volume, mix, timing, and
  collectability where the evidence supports it.
- Reforecast prospectively when assumptions change; never revise the original
  baseline merely to erase past variance.

## In plain-text accounting

In a plain-text system, keep the approved baseline and each reforecast as dated,
versioned records. Map them explicitly to the actual ledger rather than
overloading journal entries with planned transactions. The comparison must use
the same entity, period, units, and classification basis on both sides.
