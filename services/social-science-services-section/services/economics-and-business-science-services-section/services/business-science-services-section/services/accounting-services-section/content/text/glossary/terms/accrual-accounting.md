Accrual accounting is a method that records revenues when earned and expenses when incurred, regardless of when cash changes hands.

Under accrual accounting, a company that satisfies the applicable
revenue-recognition conditions in December can record revenue in December even
if the customer pays in January. An expense recognized in March likewise does
not wait for the check to clear. This assigns recognized economic activity to
the period in which its recognition conditions are satisfied. It answers a
different question from a cash record; whether it is the appropriate basis
depends on the reporting purpose and governing requirements.

Cash-basis accounting records revenue and expense effects by cash receipt and
payment. It can combine collection of old activity with payment for current
activity, so it does not by itself measure period performance. General-purpose
IFRS financial statements, other than cash-flow information, use the accrual
basis under [IAS 1](https://www.ifrs.org/content/dam/ifrs/publications/pdf-standards/english/2021/issued/part-a/ias-1-presentation-of-financial-statements.pdf).
Other frameworks and tax regimes define their own scope and exceptions.

The gap between accrual performance and cash movement is one reason the
[cash flow statement](./cash-flow-statement.md) is necessary. The income
statement reports recognized performance under its governing basis; the cash
flow statement explains changes in cash and cash equivalents. Neither is a bank
reconciliation, and neither alone describes every economic event or liquidity
constraint.
