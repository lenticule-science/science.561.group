Double-entry bookkeeping records a settled transaction as equal
[debits and credits](./debit-and-credit.md) across two or more
[accounts](./account.md). A [trial balance](./trial-balance.md) tests the
resulting debit-credit equality. A mismatch proves that something is wrong; a
match does not prove that every event was recorded or correctly classified.

For a single reporting currency, the familiar equality can be checked directly.
For multi-commodity books, native units are not added across unlike commodities:
postings balance within a commodity or through evidenced cost or price weights.
Quantity, historical cost, transaction price, and current market value remain
different accounting facts.

Luca Pacioli's 1494 *Summa de arithmetica* contains the first printed
description of the Venetian method, but surviving medieval ledgers predate it.
The [history of double-entry bookkeeping](./history-of-double-entry-bookkeeping.md)
separates the surviving evidence from invention claims.

Double-entry bookkeeping rests on the accounting equation:

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}$$

Every settled transaction maintains this equation. Buying equipment for cash
increases one asset and decreases another; borrowing cash increases an asset and
a liability. A one-sided proposal is incomplete, but a balanced proposal can
still be economically false. Source evidence and recognition rules decide what
should enter the books; double-entry constrains how the accepted conclusion is
carried.
