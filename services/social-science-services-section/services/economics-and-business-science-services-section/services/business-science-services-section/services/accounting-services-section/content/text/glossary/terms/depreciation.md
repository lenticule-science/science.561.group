Depreciation is the systematic allocation of a tangible asset's depreciable
amount over its useful life. It is a cost-allocation process, not an estimate of
the asset's current market value.

A \$30,000 vehicle with no residual value and a five-year useful life produces a
\$6,000 annual expense under straight-line depreciation. The example depends on
those estimates and on the asset qualifying for capitalization. Depreciation
begins under the applicable reporting basis when the asset is available for its
intended use, not merely when cash is paid.

The selected method should represent the expected pattern in which economic
benefits are consumed. **Straight-line depreciation** allocates the depreciable
amount evenly. **Declining-balance depreciation** allocates more in earlier
periods. **Units-of-production depreciation** follows a measured output or use
base. Useful life, residual value, impairment, disposal, and changes in use must
remain separate from mechanically applying a rate.

Recording depreciation does not itself move cash. The asset may originally have
been paid for in cash, financed, contributed, leased, or acquired through
another exchange, so “the cash already left” is not universal. In an indirect
[cash flow statement](./cash-flow-statement.md), depreciation included in the
starting profit measure is commonly reversed as a non-cash adjustment before
cash effects are classified; that add-back does not make depreciation income or
undo its effect on carrying amount.
