Cost of goods sold (COGS), or cost of sales, is the carrying amount of inventory
or other costs recognized against the goods or services whose related revenue
is presented for a period.

For a manufacturer, inventoriable cost can include raw materials, conversion
labor, and allocated production overhead under the governing basis. It is
therefore not simply “direct cost.” Selling and general administrative costs are
ordinarily period costs, while unusual scope rules and service cost-of-revenue
presentations require their own analysis. COGS appears on the
[income statement](./income-statement.md) as a deduction from revenue:

- Revenue minus COGS equals **gross profit**.
- Gross profit minus operating expenses equals **operating income**.

COGS matters because it separates the cost of what was sold from the cost of running the business. This makes it possible to assess whether the core product or service is profitable before overhead enters the picture.

What counts as cost of sales depends on the entity, contract, inventory and
costing policy, and reporting framework. Service and software entities may
present cost of revenue even without conventional merchandise inventory.

In a simple periodic merchandise system, beginning inventory plus net purchases
and other inventoriable costs minus ending inventory yields COGS. A perpetual
system records relief as sales occur, but physical counts, cutoff, write-downs,
returns, and reconciliation remain necessary. [FIFO](./fifo.md) is one possible
cost-flow assumption, not the rule governing every inventory.
