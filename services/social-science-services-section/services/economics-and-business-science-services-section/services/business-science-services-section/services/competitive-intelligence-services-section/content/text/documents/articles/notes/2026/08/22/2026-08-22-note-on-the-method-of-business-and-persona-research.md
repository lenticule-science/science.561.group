This is a method note: it describes how this section researches a real business and the public persona of the person running it, grounded in three linked research passes conducted as this section's founding applications — a product/app teardown, a business and channel-footprint analysis, and a founder-identity/public-persona profile, all of the same subject (Feral Foraging, its app "Gather: The Foraging App," and founder Jesse Akozbek). Method: those three documents were read in full and are cited below by their actual findings, not summarized from memory; every mechanism claimed below is one the three documents actually demonstrate doing, and every place those documents themselves flagged a gap, a conflicting figure, or an unreachable source is carried forward here rather than smoothed over. Where this note draws a general lesson from a specific instance in those documents, the instance is named so the generalization can be checked against it.

## 1. Researching the business: what gets read, in what order

The three founding documents establish a working order for profiling a business, not a fixed checklist executed in isolation:

**Entity and legal pages first, for grounding.** Before anything else, the researcher establishes what legal entity is actually behind the product, distinct from its marketing brand name. In the Gather research, this came from two independent, cross-checking sources: the app's own privacy policy (which discloses controlling-entity name, jurisdiction, registered address, and support contact for GDPR/CCPA-compliance reasons — a legally-motivated, and therefore unusually reliable, self-disclosure) and the App Store/Google Play listings' independent "developer of record" field, both of which agreed on "Feral Foraging, LLC." This cross-check matters: a privacy policy is self-disclosed, not registry-verified, and the note explicitly recorded that the LLC's Alabama registration was **not** independently checked against a state business registry in that pass — a real limit on the entity-verification claim, stated rather than implied away.

**App stores and their attached surfaces next, for the product itself.** The App Store and Google Play listings, their structured review data, and — the specific addition this section names as its own evidence class — the app's public roadmap/feedback board (a Featurebase instance, in the founding case) together yielded the product's confirmed feature list, its pricing (with two slightly different price pairs found across separate fetches and reported as an unresolved discrepancy rather than picked), its rating and review-mined complaint pattern, and — the sharpest example of this surface's value — a direct, dated confirmation that a user-requested feature (photo-based plant identification) was marked "Not planned" by the developer, meaning the product's central "no AI ID" positioning is a *stated, deliberate choice*, not something the researcher had to infer from its absence.

**Roadmap/help boards read as the developer's own voice.** Distinct from reviews (the users' voice) and from marketing copy (the curated voice), a Featurebase-style board is where a developer states, in writing and often dated, what shipped, what's declined, and why — the Gather board's "Does this app identify plants and mushrooms for me?" help article, quoted directly ("at this time, we do not"), is the clearest primary-voice evidence in the entire research pass, and it came from this surface, not the store listing or the marketing site.

**Social channels, mapped as a funnel, not a list.** Each platform (YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, Facebook, Patreon, Discord, a newsletter) was checked individually for follower/member counts, but the more load-bearing work was tracing how the channels connect to each other and to the paid product — an Instagram bio's literal link-in-bio text, an affiliate-tagged tracking link, and Patreon's own about-page copy explicitly redirecting supporters toward app-subscription revenue. This is the content-to-app-funnel pattern this section names as its own term; the mechanism worth restating here is that the funnel's shape was established from the businesses' own public copy (what a bio says, what an about-page says), not inferred from follower counts alone.

**Podcasts and press, for third-party corroboration and trajectory.** Podcast guest appearances (four identified, across foraging, homesteading, and community-library programming niches) and one food-media feature (Garden & Gun) served two functions: corroborating the founder's self-presented identity and timeline against an independent party's framing, and dating milestones (an app feature launch, a press mention) that the business's own channels don't always timestamp precisely.

## 2. Researching the founder's public persona: verification-first

The founder-persona document is structured differently from the app/business documents because its subject is a person, and the method note's central discipline here is **verification-first, not aggregation-first**: build outward from what the subject or a clearly-primary source states directly, and treat search-engine-aggregated summaries as a weaker, separately-flagged tier.

**Identity resolution before biography.** The founding research opened by resolving a spelling discrepancy — "Jesse Akozbek" versus a single podcast's "Jesse Akosbek" — by cross-checking the name against the founder's own TikTok, an App-Store-listed LLC name, a self-created Permaculture Global profile, and multiple independent third-party credits, before concluding the single divergent spelling was very likely a transcription error by one show, not a genuine alternate spelling. This is the pattern worth generalizing: a name, a credential, or a biographical claim is treated as resolved only once it has been checked against more than one source that did not copy each other, with the disagreement itself reported rather than silently resolved in the note's own voice.

**A bio timeline built primary-source-first, with each entry's confidence stated.** The founder's bio timeline (youth introduction to permaculture, an engineering degree, a pre-foraging engineering career, an origin-story pivot, current base of operations, brand-building years, the app launch) was assembled with each row tagged to its actual source and, critically, to a stated confidence level — "High" (his own TikTok, listing data), "Medium" (search-index-only access to a LinkedIn/professional profile whose direct fetch failed with a 404), "Medium-low" (an origin story that appears only in aggregated search summaries, never a directly-quoted primary statement). **This claim could not be verified to the sentence level in the founding pass**: the founder's own primary "About" page, presumably his most authoritative self-written bio, returned a live server error (HTTP 500, a WordPress/Cloudflare-plugin fatal error, confirmed via a raw fetch showing the actual PHP stack trace) throughout the entire research window, and the note says so explicitly rather than filling the gap from indexed snippets presented as settled fact.

**Persona-as-asset analysis, argued from evidence, not asserted.** The founding document's central analytical move — treating the founder's persona as a business asset with four functions (primary trust mechanism, low-CAC distribution channel, ownable differentiator, single-founder dependency risk) — is argued from specific, cited mechanisms in the earlier sections (the "engineer turned forager" framing recast as a deliberate trust device; a stated ~800K+ combined cross-platform following as the pre-existing distribution channel; a marketing tagline used as both claim and literal product-architecture choice; the observed absence of any co-founder, advisory board, or institutional affiliation across all sources checked), not stated as a general truism about founder-led businesses. See [`founder-public-persona`](../../../../../../glossary/terms/founder-public-persona.md) for the term this generalizes to.

## 3. Evidence grades

Reading the three founding documents together, four distinct evidence grades recur, and keeping them visually and textually distinct (rather than flattening every claim to the same confident prose register) is the single practice this note treats as most load-bearing:

- **Primary self-disclosure** — something the subject discloses about themselves, especially where they have a legal or reputational incentive for it to be accurate (a privacy policy's entity disclosure, an App-Store developer-of-record field, a direct first-person statement in a caption or a help-center article). Highest confidence, but still not registry-verified unless a registry was actually checked.
- **Store-measured** — a figure a platform itself computes and displays (a Google Play `aggregateRating` block, an install-count band, a structured price-range field). High confidence for what the platform measured, explicitly caveated for promotional/regional variation where two fetches disagreed.
- **Secondary** — independent third-party coverage that did not come from the subject (a podcast interview, a press feature, a forum thread, a review). Valuable specifically because it is independent, but reported as "a reviewer states..." or "a 2020 blog post references...", not generalized past what was actually said.
- **Unverified** — a claim that reached the researcher only through search-engine aggregation or indexing, with the underlying primary page unreachable by direct fetch (a 403, a 404, a 500). The founding documents' practice — and this section's binding practice going forward — is to keep this tier's claims in the document (an unreachable page is not evidence a claim is false) but to mark them plainly rather than let them read with the same authority as a directly-fetched primary source.

## 4. The ethical boundary, restated

The founding research passes demonstrate the [privacy and consent posture](../../../../../../../../README.md) in practice, not just in principle. Twice in the founder-persona pass, information surfaced that was excluded on sight: a business address surfaced in a search snippet was left out of the report because it might be a home address, and a birthplace/ethnicity claim surfaced from the subject's own public profile was "deliberately minimized... not business-relevant" and kept out of the synthesized findings even though it had technically been found. Both exclusions were recorded as a named line in the document's own gaps section — the omission is visible and accountable, not silent. That is the standard this section holds every document to: research the public, professional persona in full; when a research pass surfaces something outside that scope, name the exclusion rather than either publishing it or hiding that a choice was made.
