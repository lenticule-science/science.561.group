# The economic, financial, and industrial technologies of narcotics empires

A synthesis of measured facts (rows.json, 89 rows) on six cases -- the British
East India Company's opium trade into China; the Medellín and Cali
organizations; the Sinaloa organization and the Mexican trade; the Golden
Triangle and Afghan opium economies; the legal pharmaceutical opioid industry
(Purdue Pharma); and the fentanyl/precursor-chemical trade -- organized as a
value chain from territory to population. All figures below are cited to a
row by its `sourceTitle`; full URLs and access dates are in rows.json.

## 1. Agrarian technology

The raw-material stage looks structurally similar across plant-based cases
and structurally absent in the fully synthetic one. Under the East India
Company's Bengal monopoly (from 1773) growers were compelled to deliver all
opium to Company godowns, with cultivation induced by advance loans/bonuses
rather than open-market purchase (*Opium - Captions, Cambridge University
Library*); Bombay opium was folded into the same monopoly by 1830 (*Opium
Trading in Mumbai*). In Colombia, coca leaf sells for roughly $400-500/ton,
and paste made by the farmer for about $900/kg (*Legendary drug lord Pablo
Escobar...*, Yahoo Finance); cocaine-HCl yield per hectare rose from 6.5 to
7.9 kg/ha 2016-2020 (*Challenging the Cocaine Figures*, InSight Crime/UNODC).
In the Golden Triangle, Shan State supplies 92% of Myanmar cultivation, and
poppy farmers earn roughly $584 versus $336 for non-poppy farmers in Kachin
State (*Opium economy in Golden Triangle...*, UNODC); the 2023 regional
farm-gate price averaged $355/kg. Afghan farm-gate prices swung from $63/kg
(2019) to $408/kg (2023) after the Taliban's cultivation ban, and yields have
ranged 18.3-44.5 kg/ha across 2011-2022 (UNODC Afghanistan Opium Surveys);
2025 cultivation fell to 10,200 hectares / 296 tons, down 32% (*Afghanistan
Opium Survey 2025*, UNODC). Sinaloa-linked marijuana growers saw their price
collapse from $60-90/kg to $30-40/kg as U.S. legalization expanded (*Legal
Pot In The U.S. May Be Undercutting Mexican Marijuana*, NPR). The legal
pharmaceutical industry has its own licit poppy-growing base -- Australia
(Tasmania's thebaine/oripavine cultivar), India, and Turkey supply 80% of
required U.S. narcotic raw material (*Welcome to the Australian Island That
Fueled America's Opioid Crisis*, Vice). Fentanyl breaks the pattern entirely:
it is fully synthetic and has no cultivation stage (*China Primer: Illicit
Fentanyl and China's Role*, CRS). Eradication/substitution economics show
extreme dispersion: per-hectare Colombian eradication cost estimates range
$800-$70,000 depending on cost-accounting boundary, and a $340 million,
99,097-family substitution program saw only 0.4% of enrolled farmers actually
replant coca before cultivation rose 13% again (*A Death Foretold*, InSight
Crime).

## 2. Industrial technology

Refinement is where the chain concentrates the least labor and the most
value-jump risk. Company-era opium volumes into China rose from 200 chests
(1729) to 40,000 chests (1838) (*Opium trade*, Britannica), averaging 900
tons/year by the 1820s (*Opium Wars*, New World Encyclopedia). The cocaine
price ladder runs from ~$900/kg paste to $1,654-2,000/kg Colombian
export-grade, to $10,000-20,000/kg landed in transit countries, to
$70,000-80,000/kg U.S. wholesale, to a street-cut equivalent of ~$150,000/kg
(*Legendary drug lord Pablo Escobar...*, Yahoo Finance; Statista). Afghan
heroin manufacture processes about 60% of opium domestically, requiring an
estimated 475 metric tons/year of smuggled acetic anhydride (*Afghanistan
opium/heroin production*, GlobalSecurity.org/UNODC). Risk-pricing research
(Caulkins and Reuter, *Journal of Drug Issues*, 1998) finds over 50% of
cocaine's retail price is compensation for trafficking risk rather than
production cost -- cocaine and heroin are worth more per gram than gold.
Markup is heaviest at final retail repackaging: Italian trafficker interviews
found 121% markup at retail, 57% at wholesale, only 26% at high-level
multi-kilo trafficking (*Uncertainty and risk...*, International Journal of
Drug Policy). Fentanyl's industrial stage has been deliberately relocated:
after China's 2019 class-wide scheduling of fentanyl-related substances, DEA
assesses Chinese traffickers shifted from exporting finished fentanyl to
exporting precursors, with Mexican TCOs now doing the manufacturing (*China
Primer*, CRS); a $995 pill press plus $115-130 die molds let one kilogram of
fentanyl become hundreds of thousands of $10-20 pills, worth $10-20 million
in retail sales (*Pill Press Fact Sheet*, LAPPA/DEA). Purdue's OxyContin
wholesales at roughly $7.75 per 30mg capsule (*DrugPatentWatch*); a specific
manufacturing-cost-per-pill figure was not found.

## 3. Logistics and distribution technology

Distribution technology recurs as tiered, franchised, or arm's-length
networks designed to insulate upstream actors from downstream legal risk.
The Company sold at Calcutta auction to licensed private "country traders"
who carried opium to the Chinese coast and sold to local smugglers, keeping
the Company's monopoly formally clean of the illegal import itself
(*Opium trade*, Britannica); Chinese rural distribution then ran through a
hierarchical shareholding enterprise of founders, investor-salesmen, and
retail networks (*The Opium Business*, Stanford UP). Colombian cocaine moves
by artisanal narco-submarines built along the Pacific coast (over half of
seized vessels), and increasingly through container shipping chosen for
minimal handoffs (InSight Crime; OCCRP). Sinaloa's "plaza system" divides
Mexico into franchised territories under jefes de plaza who pay a percentage
to central leadership -- explicitly compared by analysts to Walmart- or
McDonald's-style subsidiary structures (*Journal of Strategic Security*,
USF), with plaza bosses holding real day-to-day autonomy inside a
consensus-run top leadership (InSight Crime). The fentanyl chain is
described by researchers as one adaptive supply chain spanning overseas
precursor sourcing, Mexican manufacture, and U.S. distribution corridors
(*Precursors, Ports, and Pills*, UVA National Security Data and Policy
Institute). For OxyContin, specific pharmacy/PBM distribution-margin figures
were searched for and not found beyond a general statement that rebating
erodes most list-price gains.

## 4. Financial technology

This is the deepest and most contested heading. The Treaty of Nanking (1842)
imposed a 21-million-tael (~27 million silver dollar) indemnity on Qing
China, funding mechanisms for confiscated opium, war ransom, merchant debts,
and reparations (*Treaty of Nanking*, Wikipedia); Company opium revenue ran
about £5 million/year, second-largest British Indian revenue source by 1843
(Cambridge University Library). Medellín-era revenue estimates are sharply
contested: $2-4 billion/year at the low end versus $21.8-25+ billion/year at
the high end (Yahoo Finance), set against a Colombian GDP of only $25-30
billion in the early 1980s. BCCI laundered Medellín proceeds, pled guilty in
1990 ($14.8 million fine, then-record), and collapsed in 1991 as a $20
billion, 73-country lender (Market Histories; U.S. Congress Kerry-Brown
Report). Colombian money-laundering-to-GDP estimates range from 0.4% to 14%
depending on year and methodology (InSight Crime), and $5-15 billion/year
reportedly flowed back to Colombia via the Black Market Peso Exchange
(Colombia Reports). HSBC's December 2012 settlement ($1.9 billion total,
$1.3 billion forfeiture plus $650 million civil fine) followed admissions
that at least $881 million in Sinaloa-linked transfers moved through its
accounts, with no individual indicted (NPR). Chinese underground banking
("Operation Fortune Runner") laundered over $50 million for Sinaloa
2019-2023 (NBC Los Angeles), and cryptocurrency-tracking firms found over
$55.5 million flowing into cartel-linked wallets since 2019, mostly Bitcoin
and USDT, moved through cross-border "spinner" networks (TRM Labs). FinCEN
has formally flagged Chinese money-laundering networks as a distinct,
growing typology for fentanyl proceeds. Afghanistan's overall opium economy
has been valued at $1.4-2.7 billion (9-14% of GDP) in 2021, and farm-gate
income alone ran $600 million-$1.4 billion (7-13% of GDP) across 2004-2017
(UNODC; Modern War Institute). Purdue's legal settlements escalated from
$634.5 million (2007 guilty plea) to a $5.5 billion 2021 plan (invalidated
by the Supreme Court in June 2024) to a $7.4 billion 2025 settlement -- the
largest opioid settlement to date, covering roughly 9,300 local governments,
with New York alone due up to $250 million.

## 5. Demand technology

Markets were built, not merely found. Chinese opium smokers grew from about
12 million (First Opium War era) to 40 million by the 1880s as cheaper grades
extended a once-elite habit downward (Harvard Art Museums). The 1980s U.S.
crack epidemic began as a supply-side response to an 80%-price collapse in
powder cocaine, converted into a smaller, cheaper retail unit (as low as
$2.50/dose at 80%+ purity versus $100/gram at 55% purity for powder), spread
to 28 states within two years (Into the Shadows; Wikipedia). Purdue's
OxyContin demand-creation program is the most granularly documented case in
this set: a sales force doubled to pursue 70,000 "committed" physicians,
reps expected to make 35 calls/week, with a bonus structure that in 2001 paid
an average $71,500 bonus atop a $55,000 salary (total average income over
$120,000), explicitly rewarding higher-dose prescribing over the company's
other product (STAT; NPR); a former rep alleges deceptive "pseudoaddiction"
messaging continued after the 2007 guilty plea (CBS News). Fentanyl's demand
side operates through adulteration rather than marketing: it has been mixed
into heroin, cocaine, and methamphetamine supplies, and 69% of 2023 U.S.
overdose deaths involved synthetic opioids (CDC); the 27% overdose-death
decline from 110,037 (2023) to 80,391 (2024) is attributed substantially to
falling fentanyl purity/potency (medRxiv preprint, "the fentanyl
hypothesis"). RAND estimates total U.S. illegal-drug retail spending at
$120-150 billion/year (2006-2016), rivaling U.S. alcohol spending -- the
scale against which fentanyl's low per-pill price operates as a competitive
strategy.

## 6. Governance and legitimacy technology

Every case shows territorial control paired with a legitimacy-manufacturing
program. The Company's 1600 royal charter granted not just a 21-year
monopoly but sovereign functions -- military force, treaty-making, coinage,
law-making (EBSCO); opium was under 9% of British Indian revenue 1792-1856
but became one of the Raj's chief revenue sources by 1947, second only to
salt and land taxes (History Reclaimed), with net revenue figures around
£3.5-4.65 million/year in 1905-1910 (Whitman College). "Plata o plomo"
originated as Escobar's ultimatum to officials and is now itself an object of
formal political-economy research (*American Political Science Review*).
Escobar's "Medellín sin Tugurios" program built 2,800 homes for 12,700
people (1984) cementing a benefactor image, and he was elected an alternate
congressman in 1982 for parliamentary-immunity protection before Justice
Minister Lara-Bonilla's exposure forced his resignation and preceded
Lara-Bonilla's assassination (Colombia Reports; InSight Crime). Cali,
distinctively, ran "more on the model of a multinational corporation than a
criminal enterprise" (DEA). Sinaloa cultivates the same benefactor pattern in
Badiraguato and Culiacán -- churches, roads, electricity, and openly stated
gratitude for jobs -- underwritten by an unspoken social pact that visibly
broke in the 2019 "Culiacanazo" (EBSCO; LA Times). The Taliban runs an
explicit tax code on the trade: 10% ushr at the farm gate (yielding roughly
$44 million from $440 million gross farmer revenue), 2.5% zakat, and further
10% levies on labs and traders, totaling about $160 million/year under
finance-ministry oversight (USIP); the CIA's Air America is documented (per
Alfred McCoy's research, initially denied by the State Department) as having
tolerated and facilitated Hmong opium trafficking in Laos during the Vietnam
War (Wikipedia, summarizing McCoy). The Taliban's own 2022 cultivation ban
shows a governance actor voluntarily suppressing the trade it had taxed. For
Purdue, the 2025 settlement's permanent ban on Sackler opioid participation
is itself a state-imposed legitimacy withdrawal; against this sits an
overdose death toll that rose roughly tenfold 1999-2023, peaking at 81,806 in
2022 (KFF). The fentanyl trade's governance response has been to treat
supply-chain actors as sanctionable, narco-terrorism-linked networks (U.S.
Treasury/OFAC).

## 7. Modern-era continuations and analogies (scholars' claims, not this
research's conclusions)

Historian David Courtwright's "limbic capitalism" thesis explicitly groups
Purdue's opioids, McDonald's engineered food, and Tencent's video games as
one category of brain-reward-targeting business design (*The Age of
Addiction*, discussed in After Babel). Chinese state media labeled online
gaming "spiritual opium" in August 2021 (a term used since 1961 for foreign
cultural products), triggering an 11% single-day Tencent stock drop
(Kotaku); commentators have since extended the framing to short-video
platforms as "electronic fentanyl," "electronic opium," and "electronic
cocaine," warning of a new "era of opium wars" (Frontiers in Psychology).
Douyin's regulatory response -- a 40-minutes/day "adolescent mode" -- treats
platform design, not content, as the addiction vector (MIT Technology
Review). Separately, Marx's 1843 "opium of the people" (about religion) has
been academically re-read (McKinnon, 2005) and later, in different secondary
literature, applied by other commentators to social media as a "safety
valve" for unrest. TikTok scholarship analyzes the platform within an
explicit "attention economy"/"virality system" framework (Gerbaudo, *New
Media & Society*, 2024). These are reported here as the cited authors' own
claims and metaphors, not as this research's independent assessment of
whether social media functions like a narcotic.

## 8. Separability: which parts are narcotic-specific versus general
value-chain technology

Scholarship directly addresses this question in places, and the case
material itself supports an answer elsewhere by extension. A published
coffee-versus-cocaine value-chain comparison treats the two Colombian export
commodities as structurally comparable agricultural chains differing chiefly
in legal status (NSF Public Access Repository); a comparative study of
cocaine, wildlife, and (construction) sand trafficking networks explicitly
tests supply-chain structure against non-narcotic illicit commodities (LSE,
*Journal of Illicit Economies and Development*); and a hashish value-chain
study applies generic legal-commodity value-chain methodology, framing its
~7,000% end-to-retail markup as a function of prohibition/risk economics
general to any banned good, not cannabis specifically (Nature/HSSC).
Scholars separately find that profit concentrates in trafficking/wholesale
rather than farm-level production across opium/heroin and cocaine alike,
reducing traffickers' incentive to control the production stage -- a
structural claim about illicit distribution economics in general (LSE). By
extension from the case evidence: the Sinaloa plaza system is described in
ordinary corporate-franchise vocabulary (Walmart, McDonald's), indicating the
franchise-territory-with-tribute design is a general scaling technology for
any decentralized, discovery-resistant distribution business, not a
narcotics-specific invention (*Journal of Strategic Security*). The East India
Company's compelled-cultivation-plus-sovereign-powers model is documented
across its whole trade portfolio (textiles, tea, saltpeter), not only opium,
supporting that colonial commodity-extraction governance is general and
opium was simply the commodity applied to it in this instance (*International
Organization*, Cambridge Core) -- this is this research's synthesis of that
documented breadth, not the cited article's own separability framing.
Fentanyl offers the sharpest natural experiment: by removing the agrarian
stage entirely and relocating the industrial stage from China to Mexico
within a few years of a single regulatory change (2019 PRC scheduling), the
case shows that "where value is added" in a value chain is separable from
which narcotic (or whether a narcotic at all) is being moved -- an inference
this research draws from the documented facts of the CRS/DEA reporting,
which does not itself use separability language.
