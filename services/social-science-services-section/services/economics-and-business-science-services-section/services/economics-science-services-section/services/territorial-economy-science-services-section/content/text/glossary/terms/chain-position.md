A chain position is a named position on a value chain's price ladder — producer, refiner, exporter, wholesaler, retailer — together with its share of the retail price. The producer's share is the first number a chain-position reading reports.

The cocaine price ladder is the clearest measured instance: coca leaf sells for roughly $400-500 a ton and farmer-made paste for about $900/kg; the same kilogram reaches $1,654-2,000/kg at Colombian export, $10,000-20,000/kg landed in transit countries, $70,000-80,000/kg at United States wholesale, and about $150,000/kg cut on the street ("Legendary drug lord Pablo Escobar lost $2.1 billion in cash each year — and it didn't matter," Yahoo Finance; Statista; cited in "The economic, financial, and industrial technologies of narcotics empires," §2, Industrial technology). The farm gate holds under one percent of the retail value.

Scholarship treats this position-holding, not the commodity, as the general structure: "profit concentrates in trafficking/wholesale rather than farm-level production," a claim its source states as general to illicit distribution economics rather than specific to any one drug (*Comparative Analysis of Illicit Supply Network Structure and Operations: Cocaine, Wildlife, and Sand*, Journal of Illicit Economies and Development, LSE); and coffee and cocaine are "structurally comparable agricultural chains differing chiefly in legal status" (*Spatialising illicit commodity chains: Comparing coffee and cocaine*, NSF Public Access Repository). A [territorial economy](./territorial-economy.md) is measured by which chain position it holds and could hold; a [prohibition rent](./prohibition-rent.md) is the part of a narcotic's chain-position share that a licit chain never carries.
