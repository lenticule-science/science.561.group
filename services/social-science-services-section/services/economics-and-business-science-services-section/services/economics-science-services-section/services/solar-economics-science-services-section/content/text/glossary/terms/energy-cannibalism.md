**Energy cannibalism**, coined by Joshua Pearce in a 2008 conference paper, names an effect distinct from any single installation's own [energy return on investment](../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/energy-return-on-investment.md): during a period of rapid, exponential growth in a technology's cumulative installed capacity, the energy that must be invested to build the *next* wave of units can consume most or all of the energy the *currently* installed base is delivering, so that the industry as a whole delivers little or no net energy to the rest of the economy during the growth period — even though every individual unit, once built and operating over its full lifetime, has a perfectly respectable EROI.

The mechanism is a timing effect, not a per-unit quality problem: a technology whose energy payback time is short relative to its growth rate largely escapes it, while a technology whose payback time is long relative to how fast the fleet is expanding can spend years running an aggregate energy deficit even as it eventually pays back many times over per unit once growth slows. This is why energy cannibalism and a technology's own EROI or [energy cliff](../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/energy-cliff.md) position must be kept distinct: a technology can simultaneously have a high, healthy per-unit EROI and be, in aggregate, an energy sink during a period of very fast deployment, and the two facts do not contradict each other.

The house keeps this distinction explicit because it is easily and often conflated in popular discussion of whether solar deployment "pays for itself": Pearce's own claim is about the industry's growth-rate-relative-to-payback-time arithmetic, not about whether any given panel or plant is a good energy investment over its own life. This section's founding field note applies the distinction directly, and separately records that a further citation named in its commissioning brief (a 2022 paper by Jarmo Kikstra applying energy cannibalism to the renewable-energy transition specifically) was searched for and not found in this pass — stated there as a search miss, not as evidence the paper does not exist.
