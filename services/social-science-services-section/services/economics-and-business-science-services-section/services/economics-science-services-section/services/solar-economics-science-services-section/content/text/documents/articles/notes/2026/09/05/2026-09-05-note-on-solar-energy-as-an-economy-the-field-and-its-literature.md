## 1. The field: net energy analysis of photovoltaics and storage

### 1.1 EROI itself is not redefined here

[Energy return on investment](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/energy-return-on-investment.md) and [energy cliff](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/energy-cliff.md) are already ruled terms in this section's parent glossary, citing Hall's own 2009 minimum-EROI paper [@hall2009] (bibliography carrier in the parent economics-science section) and Nate Hagens's energy-blindness critique; [emergy](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/emergy.md) is likewise already ruled, citing Odum 1996 [@odum1996]. This note does not restate those definitions. What follows is the photovoltaic- and storage-specific literature layered on top of them, which the parent terms do not cover.

### 1.2 A real-world PV fleet's EROI, and why it is so much lower than the harmonized literature

Pedro Prieto and Charles Hall's 2013 book, the first complete energy analysis of a large, real-world grid-connected PV deployment (3.5 GW installed across Spain) rather than a laboratory or manufacturer's-datasheet extrapolation, calculates an EROI of **2.45:1** for Spain's PV fleet as of 2008, "at the juncture with the distribution system," assuming a 25-year system lifetime [@prietohall2013]. Hall's own account of the study (quoted directly, not paraphrased, from his 2016 public exchange on the figure) states the reason this is so much lower than most other published PV EROI figures: "the panels and inverters, which are the only parts measured in most studies, were only about a third of the energy cost of the system." Prieto and Hall instead followed all money spent per installed GW and converted it to energy using energy-intensity-of-money accounting for more than twenty activities outside module production that most EROI studies exclude — permitting, land, security, insurance, financing, labor, grid-connection infrastructure, and O&M over the plant's life among them (source: Hall's own statement in "The Real EROI of Photovoltaic Systems: Professor Hall Weighs In," resilience.org, 2016-05-27, observed 2026-09-05; this is Hall's own public restatement of the book's method, not this note's paraphrase of the book itself, which was not independently re-read cover-to-cover in this pass).

This is a **boundary dispute**, not a factual disagreement about Spain's panels: studies that measure only module-and-inverter manufacturing energy against lifetime electricity output report PV EROI figures in the high single digits to double digits; studies that additionally account for balance-of-system, land, labor, financing, and grid-integration energy at the point the electricity actually reaches a consumer report figures several times lower. Hall names Ferroni and Hopkirk's finding that panel lifetimes run closer to 18 years in practice than the industry-standard 25-30 year assumption as a second, compounding source of divergence (same 2016 source).

### 1.3 The 2022 harmonization review, its method, and its own live boundary dispute

Murphy, Raugei, Carbajales-Dale, and Rubio Estrada's 2022 review in *Sustainability*, "Energy Return on Investment of Major Energy Carriers: Review and Harmonization," is the most recent attempt to make PV's EROI comparable across studies rather than resolve which single figure is "true" [@murphyraugei2022] (read directly from the published PDF, pages 1-14, retrieved 2026-09-05). Its own abstract states the headline result plainly: "PV, wind and hydropower have EROIs at or above ten while the EROIs for thermal fuels vary significantly, with that for petroleum oil notably below ten." Two methodological moves produce that number, and both are boundary choices, not discoveries:

- **Point-of-extraction versus point-of-use.** The paper's own worked example (its Table 1 and Figure 2) shows that a primary energy resource with an EROI of 100 at the point of extraction can fall to an EROI of 10 once downstream processing and delivery energy are included — "an initial EROI difference at the point of extraction (i.e., a factor of four) is reduced to being only a very marginal difference at the point of use," in the paper's own words. The paper argues point-of-use is the only fair comparison; Prieto and Hall's 2.45 figure is already a point-of-use (indeed point-of-delivery) figure, which is part of why it does not rise to meet the harmonized PV range even after this correction is applied to fossil fuels.
- **Primary-energy-equivalence (η_G).** To compare an electricity-only technology (PV, wind, hydro) against a thermal fuel on one basis, the paper converts electrical output to "primary energy equivalent" by dividing by an assumed grid conversion efficiency η_G, sensitivity-tested at 0.3 (a thermal-dominated grid) and 0.7 (a decarbonized grid). The paper's own Figure 7 shows harmonized PV EROI spanning a wide range under this method — the paper states this spread is "primarily due to intrinsic differences in the assessed supply chains and technologies," compounded by real irradiance differences from roughly 1,000 kWh·m⁻²·yr⁻¹ (Germany) to over 2,300 kWh·m⁻²·yr⁻¹ (Chile) — and states plainly that "it is unreasonable to expect to arrive at a single value (or a very tight range of estimates)" for PV's harmonized EROI.

The review's own methodology is itself contested by other EROI researchers on first-party grounds: Carlos de Castro and Iñigo Capellán-Pérez state, in their own public comment on the review (GEEDS research group, observed 2026-09-05), that their 2020 paper computing "Standard, Point of Use, and Extended EROI" for wind, solar, and hydro [@decastrocapellanperez2020] met every stated inclusion criterion of the Murphy et al. review but was excluded from it, and that Murphy et al.'s own choice to require "the same type of energy carrier" (crediting renewable electricity with distribution costs the way fossil fuels are credited with refining costs) is not, in de Castro and Capellán-Pérez's view, a neutral methodological convention — selecting different reference points (electricity versus liquid fuel) yields results the excluded authors call "not robust" for cross-technology comparison. This note does not adjudicate the dispute; it states, per this office's duty, that a live, first-party-documented boundary dispute exists over how PV's EROI should be harmonized against fossil fuels, and that Pyranometer's own downstream instruments (the joule ledger, joules per settled row) will need to declare which boundary they use rather than borrow a bare number from either side.

Bhandari, Collier, Ellingson, and Apul's 2015 systematic review and meta-analysis in *Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews* is the pre-harmonization literature base both later papers draw on [@bhandari2015]: of 232 candidate references on PV energy payback time (EPBT) and EROI published 2000-2013, only 11 passed the review's screening for EPBT/EROI (23 for embedded-energy analysis), across crystalline-silicon and thin-film technologies. The paper's contribution is establishing that even within the pre-2013 literature, the number of methodologically transparent, screenable EROI studies for PV is small — a fact both later disputes (harmonization versus boundary) inherit rather than resolve.

### 1.4 Storage lowers the number, and the energy cliff bites harder once it does

Two questions are distinct and this note keeps them distinct: what is the EROI of a storage technology on its own, and how much does adding storage to a PV system lower the system's own EROI?

**The storage-specific metric.** Charles Barnhart and Sally Benson's 2013 paper in *Energy & Environmental Science* introduces **ESOI** (Energy Stored on Invested): the lifetime energy a storage technology can cycle through, divided by the energy required to build it [@barnhartbenson2013]. Their finding, corroborated by two independent secondary science-press accounts of the paper (Green Car Congress and ScienceDaily, both 2013-03-08, observed 2026-09-05 — this note did not independently retrieve the Royal Society of Chemistry's own abstract page, which returned an access-blocked response in this pass, so the exact numeric ESOI values per battery chemistry are **not found** in this pass and are not restated here): lithium-ion, sodium-sulfur, and lead-acid batteries have markedly lower ESOI than pumped hydro storage (PHS) or compressed air energy storage (CAES), meaning batteries need more energy to build per unit of energy they will ever store and discharge over their life.

**The system-level effect on PV.** Raugei, Leccisi, and Fthenakis's 2020 paper in *Energy Technology* is the paper this note leans on for a quantified, verified figure, because its own abstract was read directly [@raugeileccisifthenakis2020] (PDF retrieved 2026-09-05 from Oxford Brookes University's own repository, radar.brookes.ac.uk): a life-cycle assessment of a 100 MW ground-mounted PV system paired with 60 MW of lithium-manganese-oxide battery storage finds that "energy pay-back time and life-cycle global warming potential increase by 7% to 30% (depending on storage duration scenarios), with respect to those of PV without storage." A 7-30% increase in energy payback time is, for a fixed system lifetime, a corresponding fall in EROI — meaning a PV-plus-storage system's EROI sits measurably below PV-alone's already-disputed number (§1.2-1.3), not above it. Davidsson Kurland and Benson's 2019 paper in *Sustainable Energy & Fuels* studies the same question for a residential-scale system across "a wide range of climatic conditions" [@davidssonkurlandbenson2019]; this note did not independently retrieve that paper's own numeric results tables in this pass (search-engine synthesis only), so its specific figures are **not found** here and this note cites it only for its scope and method, not for a number.

Read against the house's own [energy cliff](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/energy-cliff.md) term: because the net-energy-cliff curve is flat near EROI 20-30 and steepens sharply below about 10, a PV system whose already-disputed EROI sits anywhere in the single digits to low teens (§1.2-1.3) loses a materially larger share of its net energy to a 7-30% EPBT increase from storage than a system starting from a high-double-digit EROI would. This is this note's own reading of the two literatures together, not a finding either cited paper states in these terms — stated here as the house's synthesis, not the literature's.

### 1.5 Energy cannibalism: a growth-rate effect, not a per-unit EROI figure

Joshua Pearce's 2008 conference paper coins **energy cannibalism**: during a period of rapid exponential growth in a technology's deployment, the energy invested in building the *next* wave of capacity can consume most or all of the energy delivered by the *current* installed base, so the industry as a whole delivers little or no net energy to the rest of the economy even though each individual installed unit has a perfectly respectable lifetime EROI [@pearce2008] (citation corroborated via ResearchGate's listing and Wikipedia's "Energy cannibalism" article, both observed 2026-09-05; the original Climate 2008/Klima 2008 conference proceedings page was not independently retrieved in this pass — **not found** as a live first-party URL). This is a distinct claim from anything in §1.2-1.4: it is about the growth *rate* of an industry relative to the energy payback time of its own units, not about any single installation's EROI.

The commissioning brief for this note named "Kikstra 2022" as a further source applying energy cannibalism to the renewable transition specifically. This note searched for that citation directly, by author name combined with "energy cannibalism," "storage," "renewable transition," and "batteries," across five separate queries, and fetched Wikipedia's own "Energy cannibalism" article to check whether it cited any 2022 Kikstra work — it does not, its most recent citation being a 2014 wind-turbine study. **No such paper was found in this pass.** Per this office's duty to record a search miss as data rather than as evidence of absence: this is stated as not found, not as nonexistent: Jarmo Kikstra is a real, active energy-transition researcher at IIASA (confirmed via his own IIASA staff page), so a 2022 publication by him could exist under a title this note's queries did not match; it is not treated here as a citable source until a first-party bibliographic record is found.

## 4. The three dance lineages

### 4.1 The whirling dervish: the house's protected defect

The world's own whirling-dervish tradition, independent of the house's mathematics, is the Mevlevi Sema ceremony of the Mevlevi Sufi order, founded 1273 in Konya, inscribed by UNESCO on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008 (originally proclaimed 2005). UNESCO's own listing states dancers ("semazens") "rotate on their left feet in short twists, using the right foot to drive their bodies around the left foot," with "eyes open but unfocused," and that traditional training required "1,001 days of reclusive training" in a mevlevihane before all such houses were closed by Turkey's 1925 secularization law, with public performance resuming in the 1950s — the listing itself notes that today "many sema ceremonies are no longer performed in their traditional context but for tourist audiences" (UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage, "Mevlevi Sema Ceremony," observed 2026-09-05, https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/mevlevi-sema-ceremony-00100). The specific hand posture the founder's movement names — one palm up, one palm down — is not stated on UNESCO's own listing page; it is stated consistently across multiple independent cultural and Sufi-order sources describing the semazen's right palm turned skyward "to receive Divine grace" and the left palm turned toward the earth "to convey that grace to all humanity" (multiple corroborating secondary and Sufi-order-affiliated accounts, observed 2026-09-05; this note attempted but could not complete a direct fetch of the Mevlevi-affiliated Threshold Society's own explanatory page, sufism.org, which timed out in this pass — the posture's description is corroborated, not confirmed against a single pinned first-party source).

### 4.3 John Trudell's stance on sundances: a research order answered as not found

This is the research order the program carrier names explicitly. **None of the three contains a statement from Trudell specifically about the sun dance.** His Wikipedia biography, read directly, states his early life on the Santee Reservation in Nebraska and his role in the Wounded Knee occupation (1973, on the Pine Ridge reservation) but makes no statement about his relationship — participation, avoidance, or public position — to sun dance ceremony specifically. Neither statement names the sun dance, and this note does not extend either into a stance on it — per the office's duty, a search miss is recorded as data, not as evidence that no such statement exists: Trudell gave decades of interviews, several book-length essay collections (*Lines from a Mined Mind*, 2008), and the full 2005 documentary was not watched in its entirety in this pass. **Seating Trudell's actual stance on sundances remains open and is not settled by this note.**
