**Energy storage return on investment**, most often abbreviated **ESOI**, is Charles Barnhart and Sally Benson's 2013 storage-specific analogue of [energy return on investment](../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/energy-return-on-investment.md): the total lifetime energy a storage technology can cycle through (charge and discharge, across its full service life), divided by the energy required to build it. Where EROI asks how much energy a *generating* process returns for the energy invested in building it, ESOI asks the parallel question for a *storing* process — a battery, a pumped-hydro reservoir, a compressed-air cavern — which has no generation of its own to compare against, only cycles of energy passed through it over its lifetime.

Barnhart and Benson's own finding is that storage technologies differ sharply on this measure: electrochemical batteries (lithium-ion, sodium-sulfur, lead-acid, and the vanadium-redox and zinc-bromine flow chemistries) have markedly lower ESOI than pumped hydro storage or compressed air energy storage, meaning a battery requires more energy to build per unit of energy it will ever store and discharge over its life than a mechanical storage technology of comparable duration. This is a distinct question from the one Raugei, Leccisi, and Fthenakis's 2020 system-level study answers: how much does *adding* battery storage to an existing generator (their case, a 100 MW ground-mounted photovoltaic plant with 60 MW of lithium-manganese-oxide battery storage) lower that combined system's own net-energy return? Their answer, read directly from the paper's own abstract, is that energy payback time (and life-cycle global warming potential) rise by 7% to 30% relative to the same PV system without storage, depending on the assumed storage-duration scenario — which, because energy payback time and EROI move inversely for a fixed system lifetime, states plainly that a PV-plus-battery system's own EROI sits measurably below PV-alone's already-disputed figure.

The house reads ESOI and the storage-inclusive EROI penalty together as the reason Soft Dyson's own instruments (the joule ledger, the joule-denominated barrier) must treat "a household's solar" and "a household's solar-plus-battery" as two different net-energy claims rather than one, and must read the [energy cliff](../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/energy-cliff.md) against whichever of the two a given household actually has installed.
