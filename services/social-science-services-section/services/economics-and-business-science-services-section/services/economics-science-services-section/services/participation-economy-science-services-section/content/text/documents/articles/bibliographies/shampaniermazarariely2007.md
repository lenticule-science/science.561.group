*Marketing Science* 26(6), 2007, 742-757.

## Why this is central

This is the founding experimental study of the zero-price effect: a controlled demand study found that dropping a product's price to exactly zero produces a demand shift out of proportion to the equivalent discount at any positive price, and that the effect is best explained by an affective response to "free" rather than by a purely rational cost-benefit recalculation. It is the mechanism-level literature behind why "no-purchase" is a meaningfully different tier from "cheap" for No Buy's own catalog, and why free-tier AI inference and free-to-earn crypto surfaces attract participation that a merely-cheap version of the same product would not.

## What it does not settle

The study is a laboratory and field demand experiment on ordinary consumer goods (chocolates, gift cards); it says nothing about digital platforms, attention-for-reward schemes, or crypto-native earn programs, and it does not itself address labor supplied in exchange for a free good (that is the digital-labor literature — Gray and Suri 2019; Casilli 2019 — held separately in this note). It also predates Chris Anderson's popularization of "freemium" business models built on the same effect (Anderson, *Free*, 2009), which is cited in this note's body but does not have its own bibliography carrier here.
