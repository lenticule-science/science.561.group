## 1. The question this note answers

The founder's own bootstrapped run — a custodial Lightning wallet (ZBD),
daily polls and capped ads paying a few sats, a bridge (v4v.app) into Hive,
framed explicitly as "less about immediate value and more about learning
how to interact with Bitcoin and Lightning" — is the seed for the No Buy
program (source: [No-Buy Sat Stacking: Starting with ZBD](https://lumensocial.net/hive-167922/@michael561/nobuy-sat-stacking-starting-with-zbd-cw),
observed 2026-09-05). The founder's phrase for what this names is
"participation economy." This note asks the literal question the phrase
raises: **is there a studied field that name actually belongs to, and if
so, what does it call itself?**

The short answer, stated first per house method (state the finding, then
the evidence): **no single field is named "participation economy" in the
literature.** Nothing under that exact phrase is an established academic
literature — what exists is a scatter of adjacent, better-established
literatures, each covering one facet of what the founder's post describes,
plus two literatures that share vocabulary with the phrase but are a
different thing entirely (§8, §12). The rest of this note names each
literature by the term it actually uses for itself, states what it
settles and does not, and where each of No Buy's surfaces falls among
them.

That finding settles only whether an existing literature already uses the
phrase; it does not settle whether the house may found the term itself.
The founder ruled on 2026-09-05 that it does: "participation economy, gig
economy, wage economy, contract economy, salary economy, are five
different economies I believe the American economy to have... so we can
have participation economy and American economy next to each other, and
then an American participation economy." §13 states the house's own
definition as house coinage, not a literature's finding, and §14 places it
beside the other four economies the founder names.

## 3. Attention economy

The oldest and best-established name for "a person's engagement is the
scarce, monetizable resource" is the **attention economy**. Herbert
Simon's 1971 essay states the founding move: "a wealth of information
creates a poverty of attention," and that an information system should be
judged by how much it economizes the attention of the humans who use it,
not by how much information it produces [@simon1971]. Michael Goldhaber's
1997 essay names the economy itself — not merely the design problem —
"the attention economy," arguing that in a network-saturated world
attention, not material wealth or labor, becomes the resource that
structures production and status [@goldhaber1997]. Davenport and Beck's
2001 book brought the term into management practice, framing it as "the
new currency of business" and giving methods for measuring and
protecting attention inside firms [@davenportbeck2001]. Tim Wu's 2016
history, *The Attention Merchants*, traces attention monetization from
19th-century penny newspapers and patent-medicine posters through
television and into the smartphone-era platforms whose entire business
model is reselling human attention to advertisers [@wu2016].

This is the literature ZBD's polls-and-ads surface and Brave Rewards
belong to directly: a platform pays a person a small amount for looking at
or interacting with something, and the payment is a share of what the
platform in turn sold that attention for (advertiser spend, offer-wall
commissions). The measurement record's rows for ZBD and Brave Rewards
carry the specific rates and identity requirements; this section supplies
only the literature that explains why such a small, capped payment is
economically rational for the payer.

## 4. Zero-price and free economics

A second, narrower literature explains why *exactly zero* behaves
differently from *merely cheap*. Shampanier, Mazar, and Ariely's 2007
controlled demand experiments found that dropping a price to zero shifts
demand out of proportion to the discount an equivalent price cut would
produce, and that the effect is best explained as an affective response to
"free" rather than a purely rational recalculation of cost and benefit
[@shampaniermazarariely2007]. Chris Anderson's 2009 book popularized the
business implication under the name "freemium": a product given away to a
mass tier can be cross-subsidized by a minority who pay, or by a third
party (an advertiser, a data buyer) who values the attention or data the
free tier generates [@anderson2009]. This is the literature that explains
why a "free tier" (Groq, Cloudflare Workers AI, Gemini's free API, Ollama
running with no account at all) is not merely "the cheap plan" — a
provider offering literal zero cost expects a specific, different behavior
(higher signup, more experimentation, less price-sensitivity to the
tier's own limits) than a provider charging even a token amount would get,
which is part of why the free tiers this note measures exist as marketing
and onboarding instruments rather than charity.

## 5. Digital labor, microwork, and crowdwork

A third literature studies work that a platform assigns, piece by piece,
to a distributed and often invisible human workforce. Mary Gray and
Siddharth Suri's *Ghost Work* documents the on-demand microwork —
content moderation, transcription, labeling, identity confirmation — that
underlies services marketed as fully automated [@grayandsuri2019]. Antonio
Casilli's *En attendant les robots* gives a three-part typology this note
adopts directly: **digital labor on demand** (task-assigned gig work, e.g.
ride-hailing dispatch), **microwork** (piece-rate platform tasks, e.g.
Amazon Mechanical Turk), and **networked labor** (unpaid or
nominally-rewarded activity performed by ordinary platform users —
"produsers" — posting, reacting, curating) [@casilli2019]. Lilly Irani's
2015 essay "Justice for 'Data Janitors'" and her Turkopticon research make
the same invisibility argument from inside the worker's own view of the
platform: the platform's interface is designed so the requester need never
see the worker as a worker [@irani2015].

This typology matters for classification, not just vocabulary: Hive
posting and curation, and ZBD's polls and ads, are Casilli's **networked
labor** — no third-party requester is paying for a specified deliverable
— not microwork or gig labor. No Buy's surfaces are therefore not, in this
literature's terms, a gig-work or crowdwork product; the house's separate
gig-work science section (`gig-work-science-services-section`) already
covers task-assigned platform gig work, and this note does not duplicate
that ground.

## 6. Prosumption and playbour

Two further terms name the blurred line between using a product and
producing value for its owner. George Ritzer and Nathan Jurgenson's 2010
paper "Production, Consumption, Prosumption" argues that the producer and
the consumer are, on platforms like these, frequently the same person —
their example set runs from ATMs and self-service gas pumps to
user-generated social media content — and that this "prosumption" is
increasingly central to platform capitalism rather than a marginal case
[@ritzerjurgenson2010]. Julian Kücklich's 2005 essay coins "playbour" for
labor that is framed as play specifically so that it escapes recognition,
and payment, as work — his case is unpaid game-modding communities whose
work is later captured as value by publishers [@kucklich2005]. This note
uses playbour as the diagnostic for one of the anti-bot economics'
questions in §15: a surface framed as a game or a small treat (a poll, a
daily ad, a Lightning game) is, by playbour's own logic, licensed to pay
far below any wage floor and to impose streaks, captchas, and other
human-only frictions that an ordinary wage relationship would not require.

## 7. Participatory culture

Henry Jenkins's 2006 book *Convergence Culture* names "participatory
culture" as one of three components (with media convergence and
collective intelligence) of an environment where "the power of the media
producer and the power of the media consumer interact in unpredictable
ways" [@jenkins2006]. This is the closest-sounding literature to
"participation economy" that is genuinely adjacent to No Buy's ground —
Hive's vote-and-curate mechanics and ZBD's poll-answering are both
participatory-culture activities in Jenkins's sense — but Jenkins studies
fan culture, media franchises, and collective sense-making; he does not
study direct monetary payment for participation, which is the feature
that defines every surface this note measures. Participatory culture is
therefore adjacent to, not identical with, whatever field name the founder
reaches for.

## 8. Commons-based peer production — a different thing

Yochai Benkler's 2002 "Coase's Penguin" coins **commons-based peer
production**: large numbers of people coordinating, usually online, on a
non-market, non-hierarchical basis to build a shared, openly-licensed good
— his running example is Linux [@benkler2002]. This is the literature
whose name is most easily mistaken for "participation economy," and it is
not it: commons-based peer production describes jointly building a shared
good with no individual payout expected from the act of contributing.
Every surface this note measures pays the individual participant directly
(sats, HIVE, free inference credits); No Buy is not commons-based peer
production even where its surfaces (Hive posting, open-source AI tooling)
sit adjacent to peer-production communities.

## 9. Sharing economy and gig economy

Juliet Schor's *After the Gig* and Arun Sundararajan's *The Sharing
Economy* study platform-mediated exchange of labor, assets, and time —
Uber and Lyft rides, Airbnb rentals, TaskRabbit tasks, Turo car rental
[@schor2020; @sundararajan2016]. Sundararajan frames the shift as "the end
of employment and the rise of crowd-based capitalism"; Schor's own
fieldwork (2011-2017, Boston-area platform users) finds the sharing
economy's promises of earnings, equality, community, and sustainability
give way in practice to exploited workers, racial discrimination, and
rising emissions, and proposes cooperative, user-owned platforms as a
partial remedy. This is the literature the house's `gig-work-science-services-section`
already carries in depth (rideshare and delivery platforms, net hourly
rate, self-employment tax); it is named here only to mark the boundary —
No Buy's surfaces are not asset-rental or task-dispatch gig work, so this
literature is adjacent context, not this note's own ground.

## 10. Loyalty and rewards economics

A market-practice literature studies points-and-rewards programs
directly, without any of the above vocabulary. Kim, Steinhoff, and
Palmatier's 2021 synthesis in the *Journal of the Academy of Marketing
Science* models loyalty-program effectiveness across acquisition,
onboarding, expansion, and retention stages, and reports a documented
paradox: programs built primarily around concrete monetary rewards can
train customers into purely transactional behavior, which can undermine
the very loyalty the program was built to produce
[@kimsteinhoffpalmatier2021]. This is the correct literature for
"get-paid-to" sites (Swagbucks and its class) and cash-back apps (Fold,
Lolli) measured in arena E of the accompanying record: these are loyalty
and rewards programs, not attention-economy or digital-labor surfaces,
and several of them are purchase-gated (Fold and Lolli require spending
through a linked card) rather than no-purchase, which the measurement
record's `requiresPurchase` field states plainly per row.

## 11. Crypto-native earn programs

A cluster of terms, none from peer-reviewed literature but each with a
clear first-use event, names crypto-specific versions of "getting paid to
participate." **Faucets** originate with Gavin Andresen's Bitcoin Faucet,
launched in June 2010, which gave 5 BTC to any visitor who solved a
CAPTCHA and distributed roughly 19,700 BTC before closing in 2012; this
history is reported by MIT Technology Review and Bitcoin.com and was not
independently re-verified against a surviving primary Andresen page in
this pass (**unverified** in the sense of not first-party-retrieved,
though corroborated across two independent secondary accounts)
[@andresen2010faucet]. **Airdrops** are commonly dated to Auroracoin's
March 2014 distribution to Icelandic citizens and permanent residents, a
claim resting on secondary crypto-news reporting (Cryptovest, Airdrop
Alert) rather than a surviving Auroracoin Foundation primary page
retrieved in this pass — marked **unverified** on the same basis
[@auroracoinfoundation2014]. **Play-to-earn** is the term the industry
itself uses for blockchain games that pay players in tradeable tokens or
NFTs, popularized by Axie Infinity (launched 2018, its play-to-earn model
driving mass adoption in 2021) — this note did not independently retrieve
Axie's own first-party statement of the term's coinage, and treats the
dating as **unverified** pending that check. **Learn-to-earn** is the
term platforms use for their own quiz-for-crypto programs (Coinbase's own
product is measured directly in the accompanying record's arena B rows;
this note does not restate its specific mechanics here to avoid citing a
platform fact without the record's own dated observation). **Engage-to-earn**
is a looser industry label covering the attention-economy-style
poll/ad/streak surfaces (ZBD) once denominated in crypto rather than fiat
or platform points — it names no single founding text, only a genre of
first-party product marketing, and this note treats it as a descriptive
label rather than a literature.

## 12. Participatory economics (parecon) — a different thing

Michael Albert and Robin Hahnel's **participatory economics** ("parecon"),
first formally presented in their 1991 *The Political Economy of
Participatory Economics*, is the second literature whose name risks being
mistaken for "participation economy," and like commons-based peer
production (§8), it is not it [@albert1991]. Parecon is a proposed
whole-economy planning alternative to both market capitalism and central
planning: worker and consumer councils democratically negotiate a
decentralized annual plan, "balanced job complexes" redistribute
empowering and disempowering work across every worker, and remuneration
follows effort and sacrifice rather than output or property ownership.
Nothing in this note's field — earning sats from a poll, routing a free
AI request, claiming a Hive account — is parecon in Albert and Hahnel's
sense; parecon has never been implemented at economy-wide scale and says
nothing about any specific earning surface. It is named here only so the
name collision is closed rather than left to recur.

## 13. The participation economy — the house's own definition

Sections 3 through 12 establish that no existing literature is named
"participation economy." What follows is not a literature's finding but
the house's own founded term, ruled by the founder on 2026-09-05 and
stated here as coinage, never dressed as an external field's result.

**The participation economy is the economy in which a person earns by
participating** — through attention, engagement, play, movement, learning,
curation, resource-sharing, quest-completion, or supplying training data —
**rather than by wage, salary, contract, or gig**, with the payout taking
the form of sats, tokens, credits, or inference rather than dollars paid
for a specified deliverable or a scheduled shift. Every surface this note
has measured is a participation-economy surface under this definition: a
ZBD poll, a Hive vote, an X-2-Earn quest, a free AI inference request are
each a person participating in a platform's own activity, with the
platform's payout scaled to that participation rather than to an agreed
wage, salary, or contract price.

Free-tier AI access is this economy's zero-price side, not a separate
phenomenon: the provider's consideration for giving away inference is not
money but the person's own usage, data, and lock-in. The accompanying
measurement record states this directly for Google's Gemini API free
tier — its own terms state that on the free ("Unpaid Services") tier,
"Google uses the content you submit to the Services and any generated
responses to provide, improve, and develop Google products and
services... human reviewers may read, annotate, and process your API
input and output," a posture the same terms document explicitly reverse
once billing is enabled ("Google doesn't use your prompts...or responses
to improve our products") — the free tier's price is paid in data and
product-improvement rights, not dollars (row: arena `free-inference-gemini`,
capability "data-use/training terms on the free (Unpaid Services) tier",
`561-group-no-buy-services-section/data/records/2026-09-05-no-buy-provider-and-incumbent-measurement.json`).
This is the same zero-price mechanism §4 describes (Shampanier, Mazar, and
Ariely's finding that exactly-zero price shifts behavior disproportionately
[@shampaniermazarariely2007]), applied to a provider's own consideration
rather than the participant's psychology: the provider is not acting out
of charity, it is being paid in a different currency.

## 14. The five economies of earning

The founder's own framing, verbatim: "participation economy, gig economy,
wage economy, contract economy, salary economy, are five different
economies I believe the American economy to have... so we can have
participation economy and American economy next to each other, and then
an American participation economy." Placed beside each other, with each
one's own studied literature named or its absence stated plainly:

| Economy | What earns the payout | Studied literature | Status here |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wage economy** | Hours worked at an hourly rate | Labor economics broadly; Mincer's human-capital earnings function models how schooling and experience determine wages [@mincer1974] | Established field, not this note's ground — the house's separate work-science custody carries it |
| **Salary economy** | A fixed period (typically annual) regardless of hours-in-task | The same labor-economics literature as the wage economy; salary is a payment-schedule variant labor economics treats within its general wage/earnings models, not a separate field [@mincer1974] | Established field, not this note's ground |
| **Contract economy** | A specified deliverable under an agreed price, the relationship ending at delivery | The economics of contracting: Coase's 1937 transaction-cost theory of why firms exist rather than contracting every task on the open market [@coase1937], extended by Grossman and Hart's 1986 incomplete-contracts theory of ownership and control (2016 Nobel-recognized contract theory) [@grossmanhart1986] | Established field, not this note's ground |
| **Gig economy** | A platform-dispatched task or trip, paid piece-by-piece, no ongoing employment relationship | Schor's and Sundararajan's sharing/gig-economy literature (§9) [@schor2020; @sundararajan2016], and in depth, the house's own `gig-work-science-services-section` | Established field; adjacent to, not this note's ground |
| **Participation economy** | Attention, engagement, play, movement, learning, curation, resource-sharing, quests, or training data | None (§1-§12 of this note) — this is the house's own founded term, not a literature's | This note's ground; the term this program studies |

The table's point is structural, not competitive: these are five distinct
mechanisms by which the American economy pays a person, observed to
coexist rather than to have replaced one another, and "American
participation economy" (the founder's own compound) names the specific,
scoped claim that the participation economy is one of several the
American economy holds — not a claim that it is the only one, or that it
supersedes the other four.

## 15. The anti-bot economics: what a machine may lawfully do

Two questions, read directly off each surface's own terms of service in
the accompanying measurement record, decide whether a No Buy companion
may act on a surface at all:

1. **Is the surface human-labor-gated by design?** A CAPTCHA, a
   phone-verification step, a required video watch, or a streak mechanic
   that resets on missed days is the platform's own anti-bot control,
   and — per the playbour literature's own logic (§6) — is precisely what
   licenses the platform to pay so little: the friction is the point, not
   an accident. A surface with these controls is a surface a person must
   clear themselves; a companion may schedule the reminder and record the
   result, never clear the control itself.
2. **What does the surface's own terms of service say about automation,
   scripting, or multiple accounts?** Where a first-party ToS explicitly
   forbids automated interaction (bots, scripts) or multiple accounts per
   person, that prohibition is dispositive regardless of whether the
   platform's technical controls could be defeated — the accompanying
   record's rows carry the exact ToS language found for each surface
   under the capability "ToS on bots/automation" or equivalent, and a row
   marked "not disclosed" or "unknown" is a standing question, not a
   license to automate.

Under these two tests, a companion may lawfully: track which surfaces
exist and their published rates and caps; remind a person of a streak or
reset window; record what was claimed against what actually landed in a
wallet; convert and ledger earnings; and route a person's own AI requests
across free-tier keys the person themselves obtained and holds (arena D
and C of the accompanying record), because holding and presenting one's
own already-issued API key is not evading an anti-bot control in the same
sense that scripting a poll answer or a CAPTCHA would be. A companion may
not: solve a CAPTCHA, answer a poll, watch an ad, or otherwise perform the
human-gated action itself; and it may not create or operate multiple
accounts on a person's behalf.

## 16. What this means for the program

No Buy Crypto's core surfaces (ZBD's polls and ads, Hive posting and
curation, Stacker News, Fountain) sit in the attention-economy (§3) and
networked-labor (§5, via Casilli's third category) literatures, with the
zero-price effect (§4) explaining why free-to-join is itself a deliberate
design choice by the platform, not an oversight. No Buy AI's surfaces
(Groq, OpenRouter's free models, Cloudflare Workers AI, Gemini's free
tier, local Ollama) sit in the free/zero-price literature (§4) on the
provider side and are, from the person's side, simply unpaid inference
capacity a companion may route across (§15). The two literatures whose
names most resemble "participation economy" — commons-based peer
production (§8) and participatory economics (§12) — are both, on
inspection of their own founding texts, a different thing: neither is
about an individual earning a direct payout for participating, which is
the one feature every surface in the accompanying measurement record
shares.

No existing literature is named "participation economy" (§1-§12); the
house nonetheless founds it as its own term (§13), placed beside four
other economies the American economy holds — wage, salary, contract, and
gig (§14) — three of which (wage, salary, contract) are established
literatures this note is not the ground of, and one of which (gig) the
house's `gig-work-science-services-section` already carries in depth. The
glossary terms this note admits (no-buy, crypto faucet, custodial wallet,
know-your-customer, free tier, learn-to-earn, attention economy,
