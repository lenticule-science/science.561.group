# About this book {.unnumbered}

In May 2025 a status code that had waited nearly thirty years for a job
finally got one. HTTP 402, "Payment Required", has been part of the web's
basic protocol since the 1990s, and until recently it did nothing. The
specifications that define the web's plumbing have said the same five words
about it since 1997: "reserved for future use" [@rfc2068; @rfc9110].

x402 is the name of a protocol that gives 402 a meaning. A server that wants
to be paid answers a request with 402 and a price. The client pays, in a
digital dollar, and asks again. The server checks the payment and answers.
There is no account, no subscription and no card number. The payment is the
permission.

This book explains that protocol and the economy growing around it. It is
written for readers who use the web but do not build it. It assumes no
knowledge of programming or of cryptocurrency, and it explains the terms it
needs when it first uses them. A glossary at the end collects them.

## What the book covers

The first five chapters explain the thing itself:

- where 402 came from and why earlier attempts to charge small amounts on the
  web failed;
- how one paid request works, step by step;
- why it became possible now, and not in 1997;
- who built x402 and who governs it;
- the other payment protocols built for software buyers, and how they fit
  together.

The next four chapters look at the market:

- how big it is, and why published figures disagree by a factor of ten;
- what has actually sold, and what did not;
- how software buys safely;
- what can go wrong.

The last chapters cover the law and what would have to be true for the x402
economy to become large, stated as conditions rather than predictions.

## How to read the numbers

Most numbers about x402 come from reading public blockchains, where every
payment leaves a permanent record. That makes the market unusually easy to
count and unusually easy to miscount. A payment from a wallet to itself, a
developer's test, and a purchase by a stranger all look alike on the chain.

This book gives every figure with its source and its date, and says what the
figure counts. Where sources disagree, the book shows the disagreement rather
than picking the largest number. Chapter 6 is about this problem.

## Who wrote it

The 561 Group wrote this book. The Group is a small company that builds
software and sells some of it through x402, so it has a stake in the subject.
Its own sales appear in Chapter 7 as a case study: they are small, and the
book reports them as they are. The Group's research notes, with every source
this book cites, are kept in its research records.

Nothing in this book is legal, tax or investment advice. The law around
stablecoins and payments is changing, and Chapter 10 says where it is
unsettled.

The facts in this edition are current to 27 September 2026.
