## Why this is central

Geertz's essay, built from the Javanese arisan and a survey of the same institution across Asia, Africa, and the Caribbean, is the earliest general account of the rotating credit association as one institution appearing independently in many societies: a fixed group, fixed periodic contributions, a fund handed in rotation to each member. His argument is that the association is a "middle rung" between traditional reciprocity and modern financial institutions, an instrument through which people accustomed to obligation among kin learn to handle money as a commercial object, and that it works because the social relations among members enforce contributions the law does not. The house cites it for the claim that the pool is an old and widespread institution, not an invention, and for the observation that its enforcement is social.

## What it does not settle

The developmentalist frame, that the association is a stage on the way to banks, is the essay's own thesis and not a finding; the associations persisted alongside and inside banked economies, which the later literature records. Geertz has no model and no numbers on default, size, or return, and his survey relies on secondary ethnographies of uneven quality. The essay describes fixed-rotation associations only; bidding and priced-draw variants are covered by Ardener and by Besley, Coate, and Loury.
