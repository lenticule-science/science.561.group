The cost of abuse handling is the recurring labor and operational expense a communication-service operator bears to receive, triage, and act on reports of account or content misuse, a cost that scales with the number of users and the number of distinct service capabilities the operator runs rather than being a one-time engineering expense. Roberts's ethnographic study of commercial content moderation documents this labor concretely: more than 100,000 people are employed globally as commercial content moderators, typically as low-wage, outsourced, or contracted labor rather than direct core staff, bearing a documented psychological toll from repeated exposure to reported material.

The cost of abuse handling differs from the technical practice of abuse handling in what each names: abuse handling, in the networking-science seat, is the operational process of receiving a report, verifying it, and acting on it, while the cost of abuse handling is the labor-economics fact that this process consumes paid or unpaid staff time in proportion to usage, a cost that exists whether the process is well-designed or poorly designed. It differs from content-moderation cost broadly construed in scope: Gillespie's account of platform moderation treats moderation as constitutive of what a platform is at any scale, while abuse handling as the networking-science seat defines it is the narrower, complaint-triggered subset of that broader moderation function.

A large commercial provider prices this labor into its business model, typically through outsourced commercial content-moderation contracts of the kind Roberts documents; a small volunteer-run collective offering the same capability set, mail, hosting, lists, chat, carries the same per-user abuse-handling burden without that provider's paid staffing, which converts directly into volunteer hours or into capability limits the collective imposes on itself, fewer users accepted, fewer capabilities offered, to keep the burden inside what its volunteers can absorb. This cost is the labor-economics counterpart to the technical abuse-handling term in the networking-science seat, and is a standing input to any funding-model analysis of a donation-funded collective alongside hosting and bandwidth cost.
