Sell-through rate is the share of acquired inventory that actually sells, usually stated over an explicit time window, since almost anything sells eventually at a low enough price — the number only means something once the window and the price floor are both fixed.
It is the practical, measured counterpart to [inventory risk](./inventory-risk.md): a low sell-through rate is what inventory risk *becoming real* looks like, after the fact.

The same metric takes very different shapes across the goods-arbitrage variants surveyed in [the goods-arbitrage-mechanism survey](../../../../../../../../../../data/records/2026-08-28-goods-arbitrage-mechanism/goods-arbitrage-survey.md), and the contrast is instructive.
Liquidation-pallet sell-through is graded by item condition and measured in weeks: new/shelf-pull pallets run 80-90% sellable, general returns pallets 60-80% (55-75% specifically for customer-returned items), and salvage pallets 40-60% — a range where the seller directly controls the outcome by choosing which grade to buy and how quickly to relist, and where moving from 50% to 75% sell-through on the same pallet raises net profit roughly 50%, because storage time and markdown pressure both fall together, per the same record.
Expired-domain sell-through is a different order of magnitude and measured in years, not weeks: an average portfolio name sells at roughly 2.4% per year, meaning a single domain has, on that figure, only a low-single-digit percent chance of selling in any given year, even though names that do sell often move inside 30-90 days once a real buyer appears — the same record's own point that "most domains never sell" and a domain bought today may sit for one to three years, or indefinitely, while a small renewal fee keeps accruing.

This contrast is the practical reason [inventory risk](./inventory-risk.md) cannot be assessed from a single average figure: a 60% sell-through rate over four weeks (liquidation pallets) and a 2.4% sell-through rate per year (domains) describe structurally different kinds of bets even though both are, formally, "a sell-through rate."
The pallet number describes a business a small operator can iterate on quickly — buy again next week with a better bid, based on this week's actual result.
The domain number describes something closer to a single-draw lottery, where the operator's $150 either resolves inside the observed distribution's better cases (30-90 days for a well-priced, in-demand name) or sits, without feedback, for years.
