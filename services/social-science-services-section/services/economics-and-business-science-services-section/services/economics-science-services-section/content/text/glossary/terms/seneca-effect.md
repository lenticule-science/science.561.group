**The Seneca effect**, named by Ugo Bardi for the Roman philosopher's line that "fortune is of sluggish growth, but ruin is rapid," is the argument that decline in many real physical, ecological, and economic systems is faster than the growth that preceded it, rather than a mirror image of it. Bardi's 2017 book, *The Seneca Effect: Why Growth is Slow but Collapse is Rapid*, builds simple system-dynamics models (a stock depleted by extraction and additionally degraded by pollution or self-reinforcing feedback) and shows that when a decline mechanism compounds on itself, the resulting curve is a slow rise followed by a steep, asymmetric fall — a **Seneca cliff** — rather than the roughly symmetric bell shape a simple depletion model alone would produce.

The mechanism generalizes beyond any single resource: a financial system that adds leverage on the way up and forced-selling cascades on the way down, an empire that expands by absorbing cheap peripheries and then loses them in a rush once maintenance costs exceed what the core can extract, or a society whose accumulated complexity (see [diminishing returns on complexity](../../../../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/diminishing-returns-on-complexity.md)) is shed faster than it was built once the energy subsidy that built it withdraws.

Bardi reads the Seneca effect as compatible with, but structurally distinct from, [Limits to Growth](./limits-to-growth.md)'s World3 overshoot-and-decline runs: World3's decline slopes are not perfectly symmetric with its growth slopes either, and Bardi's own account cites the World3 standard run as an example of the pattern rather than a competitor to it. The house reads the Seneca curve alongside [catabolic collapse](../../../../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/catabolic-collapse.md) and [five stages of collapse](../../../../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/five-stages-of-collapse.md) as three different accounts of the same observed texture — collapse arriving faster and less evenly than growth did — argued from system dynamics, capital-maintenance economics, and social-domain sequencing respectively; see [peak everything](./peak-everything.md) for the resource-depletion account the Seneca curve sits downstream of.
