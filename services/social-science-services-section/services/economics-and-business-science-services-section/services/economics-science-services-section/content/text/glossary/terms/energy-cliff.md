The **energy cliff**, named from Charles Hall's research (developed with Balogh and Murphy in the 2009 *Energies* paper "What is the Minimum EROI that a Sustainable Society Must Have?"), is the nonlinear relationship between [energy return on investment](./energy-return-on-investment.md) and the net energy fraction actually available to society. If a fraction 1/EROI of gross energy output must be reinvested to obtain that energy, the net fraction remaining is 1 − 1/EROI: at EROI 30:1 that fraction is about 97%, at 10:1 it is 90%, but at 5:1 it has fallen to 80% and at 2:1 to only 50% — the curve is flat at high EROI and steepens sharply as EROI approaches the low single digits, so the same absolute drop in EROI (say, from 10:1 to 5:1 versus from 100:1 to 95:1) removes a far larger share of usable surplus at the low end.

Hall's paper argues this nonlinearity is exactly what makes the low end of the EROI range dangerous for a complex society: the energy needed merely to run extraction and delivery infrastructure, before any net energy is available for food, transport, construction, or discretionary activity, consumes a rising share of gross output as EROI falls, so a society's transition from historically high-EROI conventional oil toward lower-EROI alternative and late-stage sources does not cost it a proportionate amount of prosperity — it costs disproportionately more the further the ratio has already fallen.

The house reads the energy cliff as the quantitative mechanism behind the house's declining-EROEI position (see [energy return on investment](./energy-return-on-investment.md) for the house's citation of Nate Hagens's "energy blindness" critique), and alongside [peak everything](./peak-everything.md) as the reason a gradual, near-term decline in average EROI across an economy's energy mix can produce an abrupt, cliff-like fall in the surplus available to fund everything the [Seneca effect](./seneca-effect.md) and [catabolic collapse](../../../../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/glossary/terms/catabolic-collapse.md) describe as happening to that surplus once it is gone.
