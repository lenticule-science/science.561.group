## Why this is central

Stiglitz's paper explains why a lender would make borrowers liable for each other: it transfers monitoring from the lender, who cannot see how a loan is used, to peers who can, and it does so by shifting risk onto those peers. The paper shows that this shift can raise welfare even though it makes each borrower bear risk that is not their own, because the peers' information advantage lowers the cost of the moral hazard the lender would otherwise price into the interest rate. It is the house's source for the claim that a group's own observation of its members is an economic asset the group can lend against, and for the price of that asset: risk borne by the observers.

## What it does not settle

The paper's welfare result depends on the peers' monitoring being both cheap and effective, and on their willingness to bear the transferred risk; it does not model what happens when the observed information is itself recorded and made available to the lender, which changes who needs to bear the risk. It is a theory of loan repayment, not of savings or pooling, and it does not consider a pool that is its own lender.
