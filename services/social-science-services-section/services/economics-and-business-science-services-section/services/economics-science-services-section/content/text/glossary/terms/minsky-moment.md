Hyman Minsky's **financial instability hypothesis** (developed across his work and summarized in his 1992 Levy Economics Institute working paper "The Financial Instability Hypothesis") argues that capitalist economies do not drift into financial crisis from outside shocks alone; stability itself is destabilizing. During a period of sustained prosperity, both lenders and borrowers revise their view of acceptable risk upward, because recent experience has not punished increased leverage, and financing structures across the economy drift through three postures: **hedge finance**, where expected income covers both interest and principal; **speculative finance**, where expected income covers interest but principal must be rolled over rather than repaid; and **Ponzi finance**, where expected income covers neither, and the position depends on the financed asset's value continuing to rise so that refinancing remains possible at all. An economy that has drifted predominantly into speculative and Ponzi financing is fragile to any rise in interest rates or reversal in asset prices, because refinancing — the mechanism the whole structure has come to depend on — stops being available exactly when it is most needed.

The **Minsky moment**, a term coined later by economist Paul McCulley (1998), names the point at which this fragility resolves: over-leveraged asset values stop rising, refinancing becomes unavailable, and Ponzi and speculative positions are forced into distress selling to meet obligations, often triggering a sharp, sudden reversal after a long period of apparent calm.

The house reads a Minsky moment as the triggering event for the [debt deflation](./debt-deflation.md) spiral once it begins, and as the microeconomic, financing-structure-level account of what Ray Dalio's [long-term debt cycle](./long-term-debt-cycle.md) describes at the level of a whole economy's aggregate debt-to-income ratio: Dalio's cycle names the multi-decade arc, Minsky names the financing-posture mechanism that makes the arc's ending abrupt rather than gradual, and a [financial cascade](./financial-cascade.md) is one channel through which a Minsky moment in one sector's financing structure propagates into another's.
