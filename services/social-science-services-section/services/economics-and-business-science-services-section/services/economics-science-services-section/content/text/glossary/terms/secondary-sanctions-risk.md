Secondary-sanctions risk is the economic exposure a third-party intermediary — a bank, a payment processor, another financial institution — perceives from continuing to serve a client, once that client or a party connected to it has been sanctioned or designated, even where the intermediary itself has not been named in any sanction and has committed no violation. FATF's own guidance names the resulting institutional behavior de-risking: financial institutions terminating or restricting relationships with clients or entire categories of clients to avoid, rather than case-by-case manage, perceived risk, which FATF's own standards state should not happen on a wholesale, non-individualized basis but documents happening anyway.

Secondary-sanctions risk differs from a direct sanction in that a direct sanction names a specific target and prohibits transacting with it under legal penalty, while secondary-sanctions risk is the intermediary's own anticipatory response to the possibility of future liability, reputational harm, or correspondent-relationship loss, and can lead an intermediary to withdraw service from a party never itself named in any sanction. It differs from a payment chokepoint in level: a chokepoint is the network position that makes coercion possible at all, while secondary-sanctions risk is one specific reason an intermediary occupying or adjacent to that position chooses to exercise self-protective caution; see [payment-chokepoint](./payment-chokepoint.md).

Because de-risking is a risk-avoidance decision the intermediary makes unilaterally rather than a legal compulsion, an organization can lose banking and payment access as a second-order effect of a sanction or designation aimed at someone else, or, per FATF's own account of wholesale de-risking, from an intermediary's blanket policy toward an entire client category rather than any individualized finding about the organization itself. This is the mechanism a donation-funded communication collective is exposed to independent of whether it has itself been designated: its bank or payment processor's own risk appetite, not a court's finding, determines whether service continues, and the specific American designation and licensing mechanisms capable of triggering this exposure are a matter for law, not economics, to name in full.
