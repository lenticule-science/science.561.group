**Thermoeconomics**, in the lineage Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen set out in his 1971 book *The Entropy Law and the Economic Process*, treats the economic process as a special case of a thermodynamic one: economic activity takes in low-entropy matter and energy (concentrated ores, fossil fuels, structured materials) and irreversibly transforms them into high-entropy waste (dispersed heat, diluted pollutants, degraded materials), governed by the second law of thermodynamics rather than by the closed, reversible-flow assumptions of standard neoclassical production functions, which model an economy as a circular flow of value with no necessary throughput of matter and energy at all. Because the second law makes this transformation irreversible and because usable low-entropy stocks (fossil fuels, high-grade ores) are finite, Georgescu-Roegen argued that continuous material economic growth is bounded by physical law, not merely by the current state of technology or policy — a claim stronger than the resource-scarcity arguments of contemporaries, since it applies even under optimistic assumptions about substitution and recycling. His proposed "fourth law" — that matter, like energy, cannot be recycled with perfect (100%) efficiency — extended the argument to material as well as energy throughput, though it is contested among physicists as an overextension of the entropy concept from energy to matter.

Herman Daly, Georgescu-Roegen's student, built the **steady-state economics** program on the same thermodynamic premise: rather than treat perpetual GDP growth as the default policy goal, a steady-state economy deliberately stabilizes population and physical capital stock at a level the underlying ecological throughput can sustain indefinitely, treating further "growth" beyond that scale as uneconomic once its physical and social costs exceed its benefits.

The house reads thermoeconomics as the theoretical root beneath [energy return on investment](./energy-return-on-investment.md), [emergy](./emergy.md), and [Limits to Growth](./limits-to-growth.md): where those three each measure or model one operational aspect of the same physical constraint, Georgescu-Roegen's entropy-law argument is the reason a physical constraint of this general kind should be expected to exist at all, independent of any particular resource's measured depletion rate.
