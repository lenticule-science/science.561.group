The importer of record is the party legally responsible for goods entering the United States. It is the owner or purchaser of the goods, or a licensed customs broker it designates; it makes the customs entry or has its broker make it, posts the bond, pays the duties, and answers to CBP and to every other agency with rules at the border (FDA, CPSC, FCC, EPA) for the goods' compliance. For a regulated consumer product it is the importer, not the foreign factory, that issues the certificate of compliance.

A foreign company can be its own importer of record, as a non-resident importer, by posting a bond through a US surety. Executive Order 14411 (2026-06-03) makes that harder: it bars foreign importers from informal entries and requires foreign importers on formal entries to be CTPAT-validated or to file through a CTPAT-validated broker. This is one reason Chinese sellers form US companies and look for US people to stand in the importer's place (see [the law note](../../../../../../../law-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/26/2026-09-26-note-on-us-law-for-doing-business-with-and-for-chinese-firms.md), §3).

Importing for one's own account needs no license. Filing entries as a service for someone else is customs business, which only a licensed [customs broker](../../../../../../../law-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/customs-broker.md) may do (19 CFR 111.2). The two roles are therefore distinct offers: a US company that buys goods and resells them is its own importer; a US company that helps a Chinese seller import arranges the broker, the bond and the warehouse and leaves the filing to the broker.

The concept's boundaries: the importer of record is not the consignee (the party goods are shipped to), not the customs broker (its agent), and not the ultimate consignee named for export-control purposes, though one company can be all four.
