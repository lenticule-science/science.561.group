**Energy return on investment** (EROEI, also EROI) is the ratio of usable energy an energy-producing process delivers to the energy invested in extracting, processing, and delivering it. A ratio of 10:1 means ten units of usable energy are returned for every unit invested in producing it; the surplus above 1:1 is what is available to do everything else a society does — grow food beyond subsistence, build and maintain infrastructure, support specialists who produce nothing directly consumable, and fund complexity generally.

Charles Hall's research, developed further by [Nate Hagens](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/documents/articles/biographies/nate-hagens.md), identifies a **net energy cliff**: because the energy needed to sustain a complex industrial society is not linear in the EROEI ratio, a decline from a high ratio (conventional oil historically well above 10:1) toward a low one (many alternative and late-stage conventional sources in the single digits) removes a disproportionate share of the surplus available to society, even though the ratio itself has fallen by what looks like a modest amount. Hagens's "energy blindness" critique is that mainstream economics treats energy as one substitutable input among many rather than as this surplus-generating master resource, which leads it to systematically underprice the consequences of a falling ratio.
