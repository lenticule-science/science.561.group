**Bretton Woods III**, a term coined by Credit Suisse strategist Zoltan Pozsar in a March 2022 research note published days after Western sanctions froze a large share of Russia's foreign-exchange reserves, names a proposed third monetary era following the two Bretton Woods regimes: Bretton Woods I (1944–1971), the fixed exchange-rate system with the US dollar convertible to gold; and Bretton Woods II (roughly 1971 onward), an era in which commodity-exporting and export-surplus states recycled their surpluses into holdings of the reserve-issuing state's own sovereign debt and other financial claims, treating "outside money" (claims on another sovereign) as functionally as good as gold. Pozsar's argument is that freezing a major reserve-holding state's foreign-exchange assets — proving that outside money can be made unusable by political decision rather than only by default — gives every state holding reserves in another sovereign's currency a reason to reduce that exposure and to hold a larger share of reserves in commodities, gold, and other assets that do not depend on another state's continued willingness to honor a claim. Bretton Woods III, on this reading, is a shift toward commodity-backed or commodity-adjacent money as the credible reserve asset for states unwilling to bear confiscation risk on financial claims.

Pozsar's note is a bank research publication rather than a peer-reviewed paper, and its predictive claims (a durable, structural shift in reserve composition and in commodity pricing power toward exporting states) remain contested and only partially borne out in reserve-composition data as of the note's writing.

The house reads Bretton Woods III as the monetary-regime reading of the same 2025-12-25 oil-sanctions log the house's declining-EROEI position is drawn from, and as the argument, alongside Ray Dalio's [long-term debt cycle](./long-term-debt-cycle.md), that a reserve currency's late-cycle phase (devaluation, capital controls, and here, weaponization of reserve access) favors assets whose value does not depend on the currency's — or the issuing state's — continued promise, which the house's finance doctrine reads as one more argument for an energy-denominated rather than currency-denominated unit of account.
