A capability demo is an activity undertaken not primarily to earn the income it visibly generates, but to demonstrate a capability to a different, higher-value buyer — the visible income is a side effect or marketing cost, not the actual business model.
The category is recognizable by a specific pattern: a large, publicly visible achievement (a leaderboard position, a headline metric, a prolific output count) paired with an economic outcome, on close inspection, that does not justify the resources spent producing it — the gap is the diagnostic signal, not an anomaly to be explained away.

**XBOW** is the clearest documented instance in the security-bounty context, worked through in full in the [2026-08-27 note on bounty markets hardening against autonomy](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/27/2026-08-27-bounty-markets-hardened-against-autonomy-as-payouts-fell.md): it topped HackerOne's US leaderboard in June 2025 with roughly **1,060 reports**, the most prolific submitter by report count, while its own founder stated on record that **bounty earnings are less than the compute cost of running the tool**.
Read as an attempt to earn bounty income, this is a failure; read as a capability demo, it succeeds completely — XBOW has raised roughly **$237-270 million** in funding and sells **enterprise penetration testing at $4,000-$8,000 per test**, and a public, verifiable, best-in-class leaderboard position is exactly the credibility signal an enterprise security buyer would want before paying that price.
The leaderboard was never the revenue mechanism; it was the sales collateral, and the bounty earnings figure only looks like a disappointing result if the leaderboard achievement is mistaken for the business's actual product.

The term generalizes past this one example.
Any time a headline achievement (most reports submitted, fastest time, largest scale) is reported alongside a separately-disclosed, much smaller or negative income figure, the correct question is not "why did this fail to monetize" but "what is this actually selling, and to whom" — a capability demo aimed at enterprise buyers, investors, or a platform's own credibility economy can be a complete success by its real objective while looking, by the surface metric a casual reader would apply, like a loss.
The practical caution this term exists to hold in place, for anyone using a leaderboard or headline figure to estimate whether solo bounty-hunting is a viable income strategy: a leading position on a public leaderboard is evidence of technical capability, and is not, by itself, evidence that the activity earned money — those are two different claims, and conflating them is exactly the mistake XBOW's own founder went on record to correct.
