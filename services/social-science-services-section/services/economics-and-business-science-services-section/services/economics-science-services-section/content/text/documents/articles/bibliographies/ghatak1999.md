## Why this is central

Ghatak and Guinnane organize the joint-liability literature by the four problems a lender faces and show how group liability addresses each: adverse selection (borrowers sort themselves into groups of like risk, so the safe pay less), moral hazard (peers monitor effort), costly state verification (peers know whether a partner truly failed), and enforcement (peers pressure a partner who could pay). They read this against the nineteenth-century German credit cooperatives as well as Grameen, so the mechanism is shown to be old and to have worked under written rules, not only under village norms. The house cites it as the map of what a pool's draw rule has to do: select, monitor, verify, and enforce, and for the observation that the historical cooperatives did these with records.

## What it does not settle

The survey reports mixed empirical evidence on whether joint liability outperforms individual liability once other features of group programs (frequent meetings, dynamic incentives, public repayment) are separated out, and later field experiments weakened the case further. It does not model a pool whose members are also its depositors, and it treats punishment of a defaulter's partners as the enforcement mechanism, which the house pool deliberately does not use.
