## Why this is central

Ostrom is the house's citation for the claim that a shared resource can be governed by its own users without either privatization or a central authority, and for the eight design principles that the long-enduring cases share: clearly defined boundaries; congruence between the rules of appropriation and provision and local conditions; collective-choice arrangements in which most of those affected can modify the rules; monitoring by monitors accountable to the users; graduated sanctions; low-cost conflict-resolution mechanisms; minimal recognition by outside authorities of the users' right to organize; and, for larger systems, nested enterprises. The book is built from field cases (Swiss alpine meadows, Japanese village commons, Spanish huertas, Philippine zanjeras, California groundwater basins) rather than from a model, and it is the direct rebuttal of Hardin's argument that a commons must end in ruin. For a pool of members' directed yield the eight principles are a checklist a governance specification is measured against, which is how the house uses it: a [common-pool resource](./../../../glossary/terms/common-pool-resource.md) is governed well or badly on those principles, and the pool is one.

## What it does not settle

Ostrom's cases are small, long-lived, place-bound communities with repeated face-to-face interaction; the book does not show that the principles transfer to a dispersed membership whose only shared surface is a ledger, and later work on online commons is extrapolation, not the book's claim. The design principles are descriptive regularities of surviving cases, not a proof that satisfying them produces survival. The book also treats monitoring and sanctioning as social acts; it has nothing to say about a default held as a recorded contradiction rather than punished, which is the house pool's rule, so the fit between graduated sanctions and a mismatch held open is an open question for the house, not a reading of Ostrom.
