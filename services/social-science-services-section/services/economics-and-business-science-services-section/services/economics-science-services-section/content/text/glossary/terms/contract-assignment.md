Contract assignment is the transfer of one party's rights and obligations under an existing contract to a third party, before the contract's own performance (closing, delivery, completion) occurs.
As a general legal mechanism it is broader than brokerage — see [the legalism section's contract glossary term](../../../../../../../law-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/contract.md) for contract law's own general treatment — but this term covers its specific, economically distinct use as a [brokerage](./brokerage.md) mechanism in real-estate wholesaling, which is the use case this term was founded from.

**Mechanism.** A wholesaler signs a purchase agreement with a property seller as the buyer of record, typically backed by an earnest-money deposit rather than the full purchase price.
Before that agreement closes, the wholesaler assigns their contractual rights under it — the right to buy at the agreed price — to an end buyer, for a fee equal to the difference between the contracted price and the assignment price.
The wholesaler never takes title to the property at any point; the earnest-money deposit, not the purchase price, is the only capital at risk, which is the structural reason this mechanism matches brokerage's no-inventory-ownership pattern even though real estate itself is a capital-intensive asset class.
A 1,000-plus-wholesaler industry survey (search synthesis, not independently verified in this pass; see [the 2026-08-28 record](../../../../../../../../../../data/records/2026-08-28-brokerage-mechanism/brokerage-survey.md#2-real-estate-wholesaling-contract-assignment)) puts the average assignment fee at $13,000, with a broader range of $2,500 to $40,000-plus depending on deal size and property condition.

**This is the one form of brokerage where the mechanism's legal status is itself the live, contested question, not a settled backdrop.**
As of the 2026-08-28 research pass this term draws from, no US state has outright banned contract assignment in real estate, but the regulatory direction across nearly every state that has legislated on it in 2025-2026 is toward tighter restriction: several states (North Carolina, Oklahoma, Kentucky) now require a real-estate broker's license to publicly market an assigned contract; Illinois caps unlicensed assignment at one transaction per twelve months; and a wider group (Connecticut, Maryland, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Virginia, Indiana, Iowa, Wisconsin) have added or tightened mandatory disclosure requirements that a seller is contracting with an assignor, not a buyer who intends to take title.
Florida, as of the same pass, does not require a license to assign a real-estate contract, but confines legal marketing narrowly: advertising must describe the *contractual interest* being assigned, never the underlying property's address or condition, and fair-dealing disclosure to the seller is required (search synthesis; see the record for the full state-by-state detail this term deliberately does not duplicate).

**The mechanism's legality is necessary but not sufficient for the mission screen this estate applies.**
Predatory use of contract assignment — unsolicited cash offers targeting financially distressed or elderly homeowners for below-market prices — is separately documented as disproportionately harming Black and Brown homeowners and communities specifically, and is the named rationale behind Oklahoma's, Seattle's, and Philadelphia's recent homeowner-protection legislation (search synthesis; see the record and [the note on brokerage as a market mechanism](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/28/2026-08-28-note-on-brokerage-as-a-market-mechanism.md) for the full sourcing and the judgment this estate draws from it).
A legally compliant assignment is not, on that evidence, automatically a mission-neutral one; who is being sourced from, and under what pressure, is a separate question from whether the disclosure paperwork is correct.
