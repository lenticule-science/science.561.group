## Why this is central

Bardi supplies the house's citation for the asymmetric shape of decline: growth is slow, ruin is rapid. The [seneca effect](../../../glossary/terms/seneca-effect.md) term rests on Bardi's system-dynamics models showing that a decline mechanism which compounds on itself (pollution, debt, loss of institutional trust) produces a steep, asymmetric fall distinct from a mirror-image reversal of the growth curve. It is one of the house's structural reasons to expect any of the collapse sequences named elsewhere in this bibliography to arrive faster than the corresponding buildup did.

## What it does not settle

The Seneca curve is a model shape fit to simple system-dynamics equations and a handful of historical case studies (the fall of Rome, fisheries collapse, corporate decline), not a measured universal law; Bardi does not claim every decline is asymmetric in this way, and cases of gradual, non-catastrophic decline exist alongside the cases the book emphasizes. The book is aimed at a general audience and argued mostly through illustrative models rather than through a single unified empirical test across domains, so the strength of the asymmetry in any one domain (finance, a specific resource, a specific institution) has to be checked domain by domain rather than assumed from the general argument.
