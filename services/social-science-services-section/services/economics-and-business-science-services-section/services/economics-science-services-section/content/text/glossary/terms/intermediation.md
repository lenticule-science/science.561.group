Intermediation is the economic function of standing between two parties who could, in principle, transact directly, and doing something that makes the transaction happen — finding the counterparty, verifying terms, carrying information one side lacks, bearing a risk one side will not — without becoming a principal to the underlying exchange.
An intermediary's income comes from performing that function, not from buying low and selling high on its own account; the moment an intermediary takes title to the good or the risk being exchanged, the function shifts from intermediation to dealing, and the income shifts from a fee for service to a margin on ownership.
This distinction is the reason [two-sided markets](../../../../../../../sociology-science-services-section/services/work-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/two-sided-market.md) exist as a named category at all: a platform that connects buyers and sellers without ever owning the inventory passing through it — a credit-card network, a job board, a freight-matching platform — is doing pure intermediation, and its revenue model (fees on facilitated transactions) is structurally different from a retailer's (margin on owned inventory), even when both sit in the same supply chain.

Three conditions make intermediation valuable, and each names a real cost the intermediary is being paid to remove.
**Search cost**: the parties cannot cheaply find each other on their own, which is the condition [search-and-matching theory](./search-and-matching.md) formalizes.
**Information asymmetry**: one party knows something material the other does not, and the intermediary's reputation or expertise substitutes for the missing information — an insurance broker assessing which policy actually fits a buyer's risk, a real-estate agent certifying a listing is what it claims to be.
**Trust and enforcement cost**: the parties do not trust each other to perform, and the intermediary's standing (or an escrow-like structure) substitutes for that missing trust.
Where none of these three costs is real, intermediation adds nothing and a direct transaction is strictly cheaper — which is exactly why platforms that remove search cost at near-zero marginal cost (search engines, open marketplaces) put fee-charging human intermediaries out of work in the segments where search was the only cost being solved, while intermediaries who also carry information asymmetry or trust functions survive disintermediation attempts that pure search-cost intermediaries do not.

**Brokerage** is the specific form of intermediation defined by two further constraints: the intermediary is paid contingent on a successful match (see [contingency fee](./contingency-fee.md)), and the payment is for the *match itself*, not for ongoing management of the relationship afterward — see [the brokerage glossary term](./brokerage.md) for the full definition and [the note on brokerage as a market mechanism](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/28/2026-08-28-note-on-brokerage-as-a-market-mechanism.md) for the applied case this term was founded to support.
