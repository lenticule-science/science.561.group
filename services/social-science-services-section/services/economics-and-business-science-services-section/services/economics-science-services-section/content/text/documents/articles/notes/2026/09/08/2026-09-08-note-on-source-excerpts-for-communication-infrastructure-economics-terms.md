## benkler2002 / benkler2006 — Benkler, commons-based peer production

"Coase's Penguin, or, Linux and The Nature of the Firm," Yale Law
Journal 112 (2002), and *The Wealth of Networks: How Social Production
Transforms Markets and Freedom* (Yale University Press, 2006). The
2002 article coins "commons-based peer production": a mode of
production in which large numbers of people coordinate, usually over
the Internet, on a non-market, non-hierarchical basis, to build a
shared, openly licensed good, with Linux as the running example. The
2006 book generalizes the same mechanism to the "networked information
economy" broadly, arguing that a low-capital-cost communication
network lets nonmarket, decentralized production compete with
firm-based and market-based production of information goods. This
section's participation-economy seat already holds a full bibliography
carrier for benkler2002
(`../../../services/participation-economy-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/bibliographies/benkler2002.md`),
reused here rather than restated.

## eghbal2016 — Eghbal, Roads and Bridges

Nadia Eghbal, "Roads and Bridges: The Unseen Labor Behind Our Digital
Infrastructure," published by the Ford Foundation, 2016. Documents
that the software libraries underlying most commercial and public
digital infrastructure are frequently produced and maintained by small
numbers of unpaid or under-resourced volunteers, names this a
collective-action and funding problem (public code lacking the
institutional funding routes public roads and bridges receive), and
recommends decentralized, community-fit, proactive, long-term funding
support rather than one-off centralized grants.

## eghbal2020 — Eghbal (Asparouhova), Working in Public

Nadia Eghbal (publishing as Nadia Asparouhova), *Working in Public:
The Making and Maintenance of Open Source Software*, Stripe Press,
2020. An ethnographic study, drawn from work at GitHub and independent
research 2015-2019, of how open-source projects are actually
maintained: most production is not large equitable collaboration but
concentrated in a small number of maintainers managing a much larger
number of users and occasional contributors, a structure the book
states scales the maintainer's exposure (to burnout, to being a single
point of failure) faster than it scales the project's funding.

## farrellnewman2019 — Farrell & Newman, Weaponized Interdependence

Henry Farrell and Abraham L. Newman, "Weaponized Interdependence: How
Global Economic Networks Shape State Coercion," International
Security 44(1), Summer 2019. States that global economic and
information networks are structurally asymmetric: a small number of
hub nodes carry disproportionate traffic, and a state with
jurisdiction over a hub can weaponize the network through two
mechanisms — the "panopticon effect" (using the hub's position to
surveil information flowing through it) and the "chokepoint effect"
(using the hub's position to deny network access to a targeted actor,
cutting it off from the network entirely, or coercing third parties
who route through that hub to cut the target off themselves).
Financial clearing and correspondent-banking networks are the paper's
central example of a chokepoint-susceptible network.

## fatf2023 — FATF, drivers of de-risking

Financial Action Task Force, "Drivers for 'De-risking' Go Beyond
Anti-Money Laundering/Terrorist Financing," FATF publication, cited
2023 update of ongoing FATF de-risking guidance. Defines de-risking as
financial institutions terminating or restricting business
relationships with clients or categories of clients to avoid, rather
than manage, risk, and states that FATF's own standards require
case-by-case risk assessment, not wholesale termination of an entire
class of customer — a gap between the standard and observed bank
behavior the same guidance documents as a persistent driver of
de-risking in practice.

## roberts2019 — Roberts, Behind the Screen

Sarah T. Roberts, *Behind the Screen: Content Moderation in the
Shadows of Social Media*, Yale University Press, 2019. An eight-year
ethnographic study of commercial content moderation (CCM) work,
documenting that more than 100,000 people are employed globally as
CCM workers, typically as low-wage, outsourced, or contracted labor
rather than direct core staff, and documenting the psychological toll
repeated exposure to reported material takes on these workers.

## gillespie2018 — Gillespie, Custodians of the Internet

Tarleton Gillespie, *Custodians of the Internet: Platforms, Content
Moderation, and the Hidden Decisions That Shape Social Media*, Yale
University Press, 2018. Argues that content moderation is not
peripheral but constitutive of what a platform is — a platform must
moderate to remain usable and advertiser-safe, so moderation capacity
is a standing operating cost every platform carries regardless of
scale, and the book states this cost has historically received far
less public and scholarly scrutiny than platforms' other technical or
business functions.

## wikileaksblockade2010 — WikiLeaks, Banking Blockade

WikiLeaks's own account of the December 2010 financial blockade: Bank
of America, Visa, MasterCard, PayPal, and Western Union stopped
processing donations to WikiLeaks following its November 2010
publication of US diplomatic cables, cutting the organization's
donation-based funding by a reported 95 percent; MasterCard later
reversed its position, other intermediaries maintained the block for
years. Cited here (corroborated by contemporary press coverage in
Forbes and CBS News) as one documented case of a payment
intermediary — none of which were themselves the target of a formal
legal designation — withdrawing service from a controversial
organization.

## fdicoig2022 — FDIC OIG, Operation Choke Point

Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, Office of Inspector General,
"The FDIC's Role in Operation Choke Point," 2022. Documents the
2013-2017 US Department of Justice initiative, joined informally by
FDIC and FTC pressure on banks, that led banks to terminate
relationships with merchants in categories (payday lending, firearms
dealers, pawnbrokers) regulators characterized as high-risk, using
administrative subpoenas and informal reputational-risk pressure
rather than case-by-case adjudicated findings against individual
merchants; the FDIC subsequently committed to Congress to end
"informal" and "unwritten" suggestions to banks after the practice was
challenged.
