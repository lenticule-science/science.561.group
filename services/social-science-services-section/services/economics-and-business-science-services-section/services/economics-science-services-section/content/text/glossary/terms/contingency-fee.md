A contingency fee is payment owed to an intermediary only if a specified outcome actually occurs; if the outcome does not occur, no fee is owed regardless of how much effort the intermediary spent trying to bring it about.
This is the defining compensation structure of [brokerage](./brokerage.md): a contingency recruiter is paid only when a candidate is hired, a real-estate wholesaler is paid only when a [contract assignment](./contract-assignment.md) closes, a freight broker is paid only when a load actually moves.
It is the structural opposite of a **retainer**, where an intermediary is paid a fixed amount up front (or on a schedule) regardless of outcome, in exchange for exclusive or prioritized effort — retained executive search is the standard counterexample inside recruiting itself, and the two models are frequently contrasted directly by the same firms that offer both.

**Who bears the risk of failed search is the entire content of this distinction.**
Under a retainer, the client bears the risk that the search fails — they have already paid for the effort, win or lose.
Under contingency, the intermediary bears that risk — effort spent on a search that does not close is a pure loss to the intermediary, recovered from nowhere.
This is why contingency fees run structurally higher, as a percentage of the deal, than a comparable retained arrangement would charge per successful outcome: the fee on a completed match has to cover, on average, the cost of the matches that did not complete, the same way an insurer's premium has to cover claims across a pool of policies that mostly do not claim.

**Why this compensation structure is the whole reason a capital-poor, labor-abundant operator can enter brokerage at all**: a contingency fee converts what would otherwise be pure sunk search cost — hours spent sourcing candidates, hours spent finding a distressed seller, hours spent finding an available truck — into a cost the intermediary only incurs against outcomes that pay for themselves.
Failure under a contingency model costs the intermediary a fee, never a reputation debt owed to a client who prepaid for a guarantee (see [the 561 Group brokerage operations service](../../../../../../../../../../../561-group-services-section/services/561-group-operations-services-section/services/561-group-brokerage-service/README.md) for how this estate specifically weighs that asymmetry, given abundant parallel search labor and a hard constraint against reputation-damaging failure modes).
This is also precisely the property [search-and-matching theory](./search-and-matching.md) predicts should command the largest fee: the harder and more consequential the search — an executive placement, a distressed property under time pressure, a time-sensitive freight load — the larger the contingency fee a client will accept, because the client's own cost of failed search (a bad hire, a foreclosure, a missed delivery window) is larger too.
