Commons-based peer production is a mode of production in which large numbers of people coordinate, typically over the Internet, on a non-market, non-hierarchical basis, to build a shared, openly licensed good, without an individual payout expected in return for a specific contribution. Benkler coined the term in 2002 using free and open-source software, with Linux as his running example, as the paradigm case, and generalized the mechanism in 2006 to the wider networked information economy, arguing that low-cost, decentralized communication infrastructure lets nonmarket production compete with market-based and firm-based production of information goods.

Commons-based peer production differs from a common-pool resource in what is being coordinated: a common-pool resource is a finite stock whose units are subtractable and must be allocated among users under a governance rule, while commons-based peer production is the joint construction of a non-rival good whose use by one participant does not subtract from another's. It differs from the house's own participation economy in payout structure, since a participation-economy surface pays an individual participant directly for engagement, while commons-based peer production, in Benkler's sense, names contribution with no individual payout expected at all; see [common-pool-resource](./common-pool-resource.md).

A volunteer-run communication collective that builds and maintains its own server software, documentation, and operational practice in the open, without paying its contributors a wage tied to their contribution, is engaged in commons-based peer production for its own infrastructure even where the service it delivers to end users is not itself commons-based peer production. This is the structural reason the labor documented in [donation-funded-infrastructure](./donation-funded-infrastructure.md) is uncompensated by market wage even when the resulting software underlies commercially valuable systems: the production mode Benkler names does not price contribution by the market mechanisms that would otherwise compensate it.
