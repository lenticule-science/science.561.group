## Why this is central

Hardin's essay is the canonical statement of the claim that a resource open to all is overused by each user acting rationally, the herders' pasture: each herder gains the whole benefit of one more animal and bears only a fraction of the cost of the grazing it removes, so "freedom in a commons brings ruin to all." It is cited here because it is the argument every pooled instrument is measured against by an outside reader, and because Ostrom's answer to it (that Hardin described open access, not a commons with rules) is the reason a member-governed pool is a defensible design rather than a naive one. The house keeps the essay as the objection, stated in its strongest original form.

## What it does not settle

The essay assumes no communication, no rules, no boundary on who may appropriate, and no memory between rounds; Ostrom's field cases violate every one of those assumptions and survive. It is also a policy essay about population and pollution as much as a theory of resources, and its proposals ("mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon") are not derived from the model. It makes no quantitative prediction that a pool could be tested against.
