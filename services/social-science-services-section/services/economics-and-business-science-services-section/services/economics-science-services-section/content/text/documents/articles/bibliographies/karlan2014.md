## Why this is central

This review organizes what is known about why the poor save less than they say they want to, under five constraints: transaction costs, regulatory barriers, lack of trust in institutions and information gaps, social constraints (claims of kin on any visible balance), and behavioral biases (present bias, inattention). It then reads the evidence on the products that address each, including commitment accounts and savings groups. The house cites it for the social constraint above all: a visible balance attracts claims, so an instrument whose value is held in circulation rather than as a balance is not only a benefit-rules workaround but an answer to a documented constraint on saving, and for the finding that the poor pay to hold money safely.

## What it does not settle

The review's evidence is from low-income countries and its authors say the effects of most interventions on income and consumption are small or unmeasured; it does not cover a high-income country's benefit asset tests or fee schedules. It reviews rather than establishes, so any figure taken from it must be traced to the underlying study.
