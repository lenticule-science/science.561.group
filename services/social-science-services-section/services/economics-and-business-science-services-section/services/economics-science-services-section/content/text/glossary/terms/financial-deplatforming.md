Financial deplatforming is the withdrawal of payment-processing, banking, or donation-handling service from an organization or individual by a financial intermediary, cutting off the target's access to that intermediary's payment network regardless of whether the target has been found to have violated any law. [Farrell and Newman's account](../../documents/articles/bibliographies/farrellnewman2019.md) of network-structured state coercion states the mechanism a deplatforming action typically exercises: a state or a coercive actor positioned at a network hub node can deny network access to a targeted actor directly, or induce third-party intermediaries who route through that hub to sever the target's access themselves, without the state itself needing to transact with the target at all.

Financial deplatforming differs from a payment chokepoint in that deplatforming names the action taken, the actual withdrawal of service from a specific target, while a payment chokepoint names the structural network position that makes such an action possible in the first place; a chokepoint can exist, and be documented, without ever being exercised against a given target. See [payment-chokepoint](./payment-chokepoint.md).

The documented case series is longer than any one incident: WikiLeaks lost roughly 95 percent of its donation-based funding within days when Bank of America, Visa, MasterCard, PayPal, and Western Union stopped processing its donations in December 2010, following no judicial finding against the organization; the 2013-2017 US Operation Choke Point led banks to terminate relationships with entire merchant categories, payday lenders and firearms dealers among them, based on regulators' informal reputational-risk pressure rather than case-by-case adjudication against individual merchants; and Autistici/Inventati's PayPal account was suspended and its bank account frozen within days of its 2026 designation, before any judicial process had run. Across all three cases the deplatforming intermediary was never itself found liable for the conduct attributed to its former customer; the intermediary's own risk calculus, not a legal finding against the target, is what produced the loss of service.
