A **cash-secured put** is a written put option for which the seller reserves enough cash or cash equivalents to meet the contract's purchase obligation if assigned. Required cash is approximately the strike price multiplied by the contract multiplier, less any treatment of premium allowed by the account and broker.

The contract multiplier is part of the option specification. Standard listed US equity options commonly represent 100 shares, but adjusted contracts and other option classes can use different deliverables. A short put supported by margin rather than the full purchase amount is not cash-secured in the same economic sense.

The seller receives the premium and must buy the deliverable at the strike if valid exercise results in assignment. The position's maximum gain is limited to the premium. Its loss grows as the underlying falls, approaching the strike-value obligation less premium if the underlying becomes worthless. The breakeven at expiration is the strike minus premium per unit, before fees and taxes.

Assignment is an ordinary contract outcome, not proof that the strategy succeeded or failed. American-style options may be exercised before expiration. Closing or rolling the option changes the position at current market prices; it does not erase an existing gain or loss and requires sufficient liquidity and account capacity.

A cash-secured put can express a willingness to acquire the underlying at an effective price, but that framing does not remove concentration, gap, liquidity, or opportunity-cost risk. Capital feasibility depends on the actual contract specification and strike, not a universal account threshold.
