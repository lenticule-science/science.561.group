Prize-linked savings is a savings product whose interest is pooled and paid out as prizes drawn by lot among depositors, leaving every depositor's principal intact. Britain's Premium Bonds have worked this way since 1956, South Africa's Million-a-Month Account and United States credit-union pilots followed, and Kearney, Tufano, Guryan, and Hurst surveyed the form and its demand ([Kearney 2010](../../documents/articles/bibliographies/kearney2010.md)). In the United States the product was blocked by state lottery law until the American Savings Promotion Act of 2014 permitted it for banks and credit unions in states that opted in.

It differs from a lottery in that no ticket is ever lost, and from an interest-bearing account in that the return's distribution is a lottery's while its expectation is the account's. The [Kelly criterion](./kelly-criterion.md) would size a stake in the prize draw at zero, since its expected return equals the ordinary account's while its variance is larger; the product's value is behavioral, not growth-optimal ([Kearney 2010](../../documents/articles/bibliographies/kearney2010.md)).

A pool that receives its members' yield has to decide what the yield buys, and a prize drawn among members is one lawful answer beside distribution to enterprises and reserve for floats, with the measured advantage that it draws deposits from people who do not otherwise save. The evidence in the paper is that people who do not save but do buy lottery tickets deposit into prize-linked accounts; whether this creates saving or moves it was thin then and is mixed since ([Kearney 2010](../../documents/articles/bibliographies/kearney2010.md)). The on-chain form, a no-loss prize pool, is the same instrument with the yield source changed; see [savings group](../../../../../economics-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/savings-group.md) for the form whose share-out the prize replaces.
