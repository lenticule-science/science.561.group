**Premium selling** means writing an option and receiving its premium in exchange for the contingent obligation defined by the contract. The premium is received when the trade settles, but it is not earned free of later liability: the written option is a position whose fair value and closing cost change with the underlying price, volatility, time, rates, dividends, and liquidity.

Common collateralized forms include a [cash-secured put](./cash-secured-put.md) and a covered call. Other short-option structures can be partially hedged, spread-defined, margined, or economically uncovered. Their loss bounds and collateral requirements differ.

At expiration, a short option's gross result is the premium minus its intrinsic payoff. Before expiration, assignment, exercise style, early-exercise incentives, spread costs, and changing implied volatility also matter. Maximum premium is generally small relative to the contingent loss a seller may accept, producing negatively skewed returns: frequent limited gains can coexist with infrequent large losses.

Option prices can contain compensation for bearing volatility and crash risk, discussed under [volatility risk premium](./volatility-risk-premium.md). That aggregate observation does not establish that every option is overpriced or that an unhedged seller has positive expected return after costs. Contract selection, diversification, sizing, collateral, execution, and tail-risk controls determine the realized strategy.
