## Why this is central

The report is the measured statement of the poverty premium: the extra cost low-income households pay for the same goods and services, itemized (energy paid by prepayment meter or not on the best tariff, insurance priced by postcode, higher-cost credit, rent-to-own goods, fees for cash access and money transfer, and area-based costs of access) and summed to an average of about £490 a year per low-income household in Britain, with a wide range around it. Its method, a fee schedule reconstructed item by item from what the households actually use, is the method the house applies: a pool's return on fronting a member's shortfall is the fee that member would otherwise have paid, read off such a schedule.

## What it does not settle

The figures are British and from 2016; a United States schedule (overdraft, payday, check cashing, deposits held at no interest) has to be measured separately, and the report itself notes that most of the premium comes from a few items that vary sharply by household. The report measures cost, not the households' alternatives, so it does not say how much of the premium a pool could actually remove.
