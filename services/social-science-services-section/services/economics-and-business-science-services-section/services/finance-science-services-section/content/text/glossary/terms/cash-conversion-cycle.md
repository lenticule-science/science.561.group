The **cash conversion cycle** (**CCC**) estimates the number of days between committing resources to inventory and collecting from customers, net of supplier financing:

**CCC = days inventory outstanding + days sales outstanding − days payable outstanding**

The components are usually calculated from period flows and average balance-sheet amounts:

- **Days inventory outstanding (DIO)** relates average [inventory](../../../../../../../../../../../operations-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/inventory.md) to cost of sales.
- **Days sales outstanding (DSO)** relates average [accounts receivable](../../../../../business-science-services-section/services/accounting-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/accounts-receivable.md) to credit sales.
- **Days payable outstanding (DPO)** relates average [accounts payable](../../../../../business-science-services-section/services/accounting-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/accounts-payable.md) to the purchases or cost base that generated those payables.

The exact formulas must use consistent periods, averages, and denominators. Using ending balances or revenue where purchases are required can distort the result. Seasonal businesses often need monthly or rolling calculations rather than one annual average.

A shorter CCC can reduce operating financing needs, but shorter is not automatically better. Excessively low inventory can cause stockouts, aggressive collection can damage customer relationships, and delayed supplier payment can forfeit discounts or strain supply. Negative CCC can be structurally healthy when customers pay before suppliers are due.

CCC diagnoses operating timing; it does not measure cash itself. The resulting financing need is managed through [working capital](./working-capital.md), payment terms, operating design, or facilities such as a [line of credit](./line-of-credit.md).
