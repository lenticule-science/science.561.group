## Colorado: a statute built for patron and investor members

The Colorado Uniform Limited Cooperative Association Act describes an LCA as "an autonomous, unincorporated association of persons united to meet their mutual interests through a jointly owned enterprise primarily controlled by those persons" that permits combining ownership and benefits for patron members with "separate investments in the association by investor members who ... may receive returns on their investments and a share of control" (Colorado LCA Nature 2024, C.R.S. 7-58-104). It may be organized "for any lawful purpose, whether or not for profit" (7-58-105), and relations with members are "consensual and contractual", with the articles and bylaws free to provide for any matter not limited by law.

A patron member made a contribution and is required or permitted to conduct patronage with the association to receive or retain the interest; an investor member made a contribution and is not required or not permitted to; patronage is business that entitles the person to financial rights "based on the value or quantity of business done" (Colorado LCA Definitions 2024, 7-58-102). Patron and investor status are therefore set by the articles and bylaws, not by the kind of person.

Voting (Colorado LCA Voting 2024): two patron members are required to begin business unless the sole member is a cooperative (7-58-501); each patron member has one vote unless the articles or bylaws allocate it by one member one vote, use or patronage, equity, or a cooperative member's own patron count, alone or combined (7-58-511, 512(1)); a use-based allocation must still give a non-using member at least the minimum vote (7-58-512(2)); investor members have one vote each unless the articles say otherwise, with no cap stated (7-58-513).

Profits (Colorado LCA Profits 2024): all profits and losses go to patron members unless the articles provide otherwise; with investor members, patrons may not be reduced below fifty percent of profits (7-58-1004(3)); the board may first set aside unallocated capital and reasonable reserves and then allocate "to patron members in the ratio of each member's patronage to the total patronage" (1004(4)-(5)); patron allocation may use pooling arrangements (1004(6)); distributions may be money, capital credits, allocated patronage equities, revolving fund certificates or the association's own securities (7-58-1005); a distribution is barred if the association then could not pay debts as they fall due or assets would be less than liabilities (7-58-1007).

## Wyoming is not an LCA state

Title 17 of the Wyoming Statutes contains no limited cooperative association act: the phrase does not occur in the 2026 compilation. Its cooperative article is the Wyoming Processing Cooperative Law, formed "to market, process, or otherwise change the form or marketability of crops, livestock and other agricultural products" (Wyoming Processing Cooperative Law 2026, 17-10-205), with patron and nonpatron members and a patron member "only entitled to one (1) vote", the patron vote cast collectively by majority (17-10-230). The house drafts cite W.S. 17-10-230 as Wyoming's LCA voting default; it is the agricultural cooperative's section. A Wyoming LCA is not an available form on the statute text; Colorado's is.

The Wyoming DUNA Act 2026 (17-32) requires at least one hundred members, a common nonprofit purpose, and bars dividends or distributions of income or profits to members, administrators or outsiders except reasonable compensation, benefits conforming to the nonprofit purpose, repurchases and winding-up distributions (17-32-104). Governing principles may include smart contracts and membership interests may be read from the ledger (17-32-102), and the DUNA is a separate legal entity with member liability limited (17-32-107). A DUNA cannot pay a patronage dividend. The Wyoming DAO Supplement 2026 (17-31) makes a DAO a Wyoming LLC whose articles state its algorithmic share of management and list the public identifier of each smart contract that manages it, but requires that "[a]ll smart contracts utilized by a decentralized autonomous organization shall be capable of being updated, modified or otherwise upgraded" (17-31-109), and lets articles reduce or eliminate fiduciary duties (17-31-110). It has no patron or investor classes and no patronage rule. The upgradeability requirement is the opposite of the immutability that Van Loon rests on, so a DAO LLC's own governance contracts cannot be immutable.

## Courts treat unwrapped DAOs as partnerships

The Lido Order 2024 (N.D. Cal., ECF 115) held it adequately alleged that Lido DAO is a general partnership under California law and not immune from suit, that Paradigm and Andreessen Horowitz were plausibly general partners through governance participation, and rejected the argument that Lido is autonomous software because "its actions are the actions of an entity run by people". It is a ruling on motions to dismiss accepting the complaint. The Ooki Order 2023 recites a prior finding that Ooki DAO "was an unincorporated association as defined by California and federal law". The bZx Order 2023 (S.D. Cal.) found the complaint sufficiently alleges the DAO is an association of two or more persons operating as a business for profit. In all three the defendant was a tokenholder-governed DAO treated as an association or partnership of its participants. A statutory entity that is "an entity distinct from its members" (7-58-105) supplies a legal person to sue and to hold the contracts, which is the structural answer to the partnership premise; the text of the Act's member-liability section, 7-58-504, was not read.

## Where the house drafts stand

The draft articles' Colorado citations hold: patron and investor definitions (7-58-102), organizer (7-58-301), two patron members (7-58-501), one-vote default and override (7-58-511, 512(1)), investor vote (7-58-513), patronage-ratio allocation (7-58-1004(5)(a)). Three corrections follow. The Wyoming LCA alternative does not exist on the text. The draft states Colorado carries no cap on investor members without noting the patron fifty-percent profit floor in 7-58-1004(3) that applies once investor members exist. The draft's reserve and tally-credit retentions fit 1004(4) only to the extent they are unallocated capital or reasonable unallocated reserves, and the solvency test in 7-58-1007 governs every distribution.
