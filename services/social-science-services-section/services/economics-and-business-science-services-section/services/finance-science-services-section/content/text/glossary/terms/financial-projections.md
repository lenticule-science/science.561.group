Financial projections are forward-looking estimates of a business's financial performance over a defined period chosen for the decision being modeled. An integrated projection commonly links a [pro forma income statement](../../../../../business-science-services-section/services/accounting-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/income-statement.md), a [cash flow statement](../../../../../business-science-services-section/services/accounting-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/cash-flow-statement.md), and a [balance sheet](../../../../../business-science-services-section/services/accounting-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/balance-sheet.md). Together these model performance, cash movements, and financial position under a consistent set of assumptions.

The term "pro forma" (Latin: "as a matter of form") signals that the numbers are hypothetical — constructed from assumptions about pricing, volume, costs, and timing rather than from historical accounting. This distinction matters. Financial projections are arguments, not records. They encode a set of claims about the future: that customers will arrive at a certain rate, that costs will hold within a range, that [revenue](../../../../../business-science-services-section/services/strategy-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/revenue-model.md) will grow on a particular curve. Investors evaluate these claims for internal consistency and for whether the assumptions reflect actual market conditions.

Financial projections serve a dual function. They are planning tools that force quantitative reasoning about costs, pricing, timing, working capital, and financing, and they may also be persuasion instruments presented to capital providers. Because those purposes create different incentives, a useful projection states its assumptions, distinguishes a base case from sensitivities or scenarios, and keeps the linked statements internally consistent rather than presenting a single growth narrative as certainty.
