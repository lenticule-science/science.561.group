## Why this is central

The paper defines prize-linked savings: a savings product whose interest is pooled and paid out as prizes drawn by lot, so the principal is never at risk and the expected return is the same as an ordinary account while the return's distribution is a lottery's. It surveys the products (Britain's Premium Bonds since 1956, South Africa's Million-a-Month Account, credit-union pilots in the United States) and the evidence that they draw deposits from people who do not otherwise save and who do buy lottery tickets, and it lays out the state lottery laws that blocked the product in most of the United States at the time. The house cites it because a pool that receives its members' directed yield has to decide what the yield buys, and a prize drawn among members is one lawful answer beside distribution to enterprises and reserve for floats.

## What it does not settle

The evidence on whether prize-linked saving creates new saving or only moves it is thin in the paper and mixed since. The legal survey is dated: the American Savings Promotion Act of 2014 later permitted the product for banks and credit unions in states that opted in, which the paper predates. It is about deposit products; whether the same demand exists when the "interest" is on-chain yield is not addressed.
