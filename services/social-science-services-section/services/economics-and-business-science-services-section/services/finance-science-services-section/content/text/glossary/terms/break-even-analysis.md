Break-even analysis determines the sales volume at which a business's total revenue equals the costs included in the model. Below this threshold the modeled activity produces a loss; above it, each additional unit contributes toward profit while price, variable cost, fixed cost, and sales mix remain within the model's relevant range.

The calculation divides operating costs into two categories. Fixed costs persist across the relevant sales-volume range: rent, insurance, and salaried compensation. Variable costs scale with production or sales: ingredients, hourly [labor](../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/labor.md), packaging, and transaction fees. Interest may be included when the question is whether operating profit covers financing cost, but loan-principal repayment is a financing cash flow rather than an expense and does not belong in an accounting break-even calculation. The break-even point in units equals fixed costs divided by the contribution margin per unit (selling price minus variable cost per unit). The break-even point in revenue equals fixed costs divided by the contribution margin ratio (contribution margin per unit divided by selling price).

This analysis is a standard component of [financial projections](./financial-projections.md) and operating plans. Managers and capital providers use it to assess how much sales volume an activity needs before it covers the modeled costs, and how sensitive profitability is to changes in price, costs, or volume. Holding revenue capacity constant, a business with higher fixed costs needs more contribution margin to break even; after that threshold, a high contribution margin causes profit to increase more rapidly with volume.

The model assumes that costs divide cleanly into fixed and variable, that selling prices remain constant across volume, and that the product mix doesn't change. These assumptions are often violated in practice — costs are frequently semi-variable, prices shift with competition, and product mix responds to demand. The analysis remains useful not because it predicts a precise number but because it forces explicit reasoning about cost structure.
