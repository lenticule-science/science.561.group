Unclaimed property is financial property a holder (a bank, an insurer, an employer, a corporation issuing shares or dividends) has lost contact with the owner of, and which the holder is required by state law to report and transfer — escheat — to the state treasury or comptroller after a statutory dormancy period elapses without owner contact.
The property is not forfeited by escheat: the state holds it in trust indefinitely, and the rightful owner, or their heir, can claim it back directly from the state at any time, at no cost, by proving ownership.
Every U.S. state, the District of Columbia, and several U.S. territories maintain a public unclaimed-property database and claims process, and the aggregate sums involved are large enough that a specialized commercial practice — the **finder** — exists to locate specific owners and charge a fee, capped by statute in every state that regulates it, for doing the location and filing work on the owner's behalf (search synthesis; see the [2026-08-28 claims recovery mechanism record](../../../../../../../../data/records/2026-08-28-claims-recovery-mechanism/claims-recovery-survey.md) for state-by-state figures).

Because the owner can always claim directly from the state for free, a finder's entire value proposition is locating property the owner does not know exists, and every state that permits the finder business regulates it against exploiting that information asymmetry: a percentage [finder's fee](./finders-fee.md) cap, a waiting period before a finder may contact an owner, and, in some states, a requirement that the finder hold a specific occupational license or file a state registration before charging for the service.
Four states were checked against primary or near-primary sources in the 2026-08-28 pass: California (10% cap, CCP §1582), Texas (10% cap plus a Class A/C Investigations Company License requirement from the Texas Department of Public Safety, confirmed by reading the agency's own page), Florida (tiered 15-30% cap, §717.135, read directly from the Florida Senate's own text), and New York (15% cap, Abandoned Property Law §1416, read directly from the New York Senate's own codified-laws page, with no personal license requirement found in the section itself).
