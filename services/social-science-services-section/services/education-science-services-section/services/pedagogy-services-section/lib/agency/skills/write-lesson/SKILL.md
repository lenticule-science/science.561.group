# Write Lesson

A lesson teaches one core idea well. Structure carries most of the work — voice and citation finish it.

## When to use

You are writing a new lesson (not revising — see `review-lesson`) and want the structural shape settled before drafting prose.

## How it works

### Before writing

1. Read the destination domain's existing texts/curricula to understand what's already covered and where this lesson fits in the sequence.
2. Read any prerequisite lessons the new lesson will build on.
3. Identify the lesson's *one core idea*. If you find yourself writing "now we turn to a different topic," it's two lessons. Split it.
4. Identify the worked example before drafting — what concrete case will the lesson walk through end-to-end?

### Required structure (in order)

- **What this lesson covers** — 1–2 sentences. No jargon. A reader should know whether the lesson is for them.
- **Why it matters** — Motivation before definition. Use a concrete scenario or historical example, not "this is important because." Show the problem the concept solves.
- **Prerequisites** — Linked. For each, state what specifically the reader needs (not "familiarity with X" but "the definition of X and how Y works").
- **Core concepts** — One at a time. For each: intuitive explanation → concrete example → formal definition (if applicable). Do not introduce the next concept until this one is grounded.
- **Worked example** — A full walkthrough applying the lesson's concepts to a specific case. Show each step. Name what concept is being used at each step. The reader should be able to follow along and reproduce the reasoning.
- **Check your understanding** — 2–3 self-check questions testing application, not recall. Include brief answers or hints in a collapsed details block.
- **Common mistakes** — What learners typically get wrong with this material. Be specific: "Confusing X with Y" not "misunderstanding the concept."
- **What comes next** — 1–2 sentences linking to the next lesson in the sequence.
- **Sources** — Sources the lesson draws on. Note where the reader can find the original treatment for deeper study.

### Rules

- One core idea per lesson.
- Concrete before abstract — every definition is preceded by an example of what it defines.
- The reader does something — without exercises or a worked example the document is a reference, not a lesson.
- First mention of a person uses their full name; subsequent mentions may use surname only.
- Don't frame the topic instrumentally ("this matters because the project needs it"). Teach the subject as genuine knowledge.
- Cite sources. Every scholarly claim points back to where it came from.

### After writing

Run the prose-style review (vague adverbs, telling-the-reader-how-to-feel, etc.). Update the surrounding sequence index to include the new lesson.
