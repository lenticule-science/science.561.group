# Writing Curricula

Educational prose differs from the broader prose default in a few specific ways. This skill names those differences. It does not duplicate the structural rules in `write-lesson` or the design rules in `learn-lesson-design`; it covers the voice and citation conventions distinctive to lessons and curricula.

## When to use

You are writing or revising a lesson or curriculum and want to follow the corpus's conventions for educational voice. Use alongside `write-lesson` (structure) and the broader prose style guide (general rules).

## How it works

### Voice in lessons

Lessons relax two of the broader prose defaults:

- **Second person is acceptable.** "You" can address the learner directly. This is the one context where the corpus's usual third-person default relaxes.
- **Imperatives are acceptable.** "Consider a poset with three elements" is fine in a lesson.

Other prose rules still apply: active voice, no vague adverbs, no telling the reader how to feel, plain technical English.

### Citation that serves the learner

Lessons cite their sources, but the citation serves the learner, not just the scholar. When citing, note what the learner can find there. "For a full treatment, see [Lattice Theory, ch. 3]" is more useful than a bare citation. The reader should be able to follow the reference forward to deeper material.

### Prerequisites stated specifically

Each prerequisite states *what specifically* the reader needs from it — not "familiarity with partial orders" but "the definition of a partial order and the ability to draw a Hasse diagram." Edge annotations on dependency graphs apply at the lesson level too.

### Exercises that test application

Self-check exercises test application, not recall. "Define X" is a recall exercise. "Given Y, determine whether X applies and explain why" is an application exercise. Include answers in a collapsible details block.

### Recognize references masquerading as lessons

A lesson with no exercises and no worked example is acting as a reference document, even if it's structured like a lesson. The mark of a teaching document is that the reader does something. If the reader is only reading, restructure.

## Prerequisites

- `learn-lesson-design` — backward design, concrete-before-abstract ordering, scaffolding.
- `learn-decolonial-pedagogy` — the banking model (what to avoid) and problem-posing education (what to aim for).

## Verification

You have this skill if you can: (1) take a topic and produce a lesson that opens with a worked example before any formal definition; (2) write exercises that test application rather than recall; (3) cite sources in a way that serves the learner; (4) identify where a draft is depositing information rather than building understanding.
