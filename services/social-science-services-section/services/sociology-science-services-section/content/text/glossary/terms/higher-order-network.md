name: higher-order network
description: "A network representation — typically a hypergraph or simplicial complex — that records interactions among three or more units directly, rather than reducing every interaction to a collection of pairwise edges."

A higher-order network is a generalization of the ordinary graph (nodes and pairwise edges) that represents interactions among groups of three or more units as first-class objects rather than decomposing them into pairwise ties. The two standard formalisms are the hypergraph, in which a single "hyperedge" can join any number of nodes with no further structural requirement, and the simplicial complex, a more constrained hypergraph in which every subset of a joined group (a "simplex") must also be included as a face of the complex — a mathematical closure property borrowed from algebraic topology that makes tools like homology and Betti numbers applicable. Federico Battiston and collaborators, along with a broader physics-of-complex-systems community, consolidated the case for these formalisms in influential review articles arguing that many real social, biological, and technological systems — collaboration, co-attendance, joint decision-making, group communication — are misrepresented when forced into pairwise-edge form, because a genuine three-or-more-way interaction is not the same object as three separate pairwise ties.

The added expressive power matters most for exactly the kind of "redundant" structure the coordination-cohomology theory is concerned with. A graph reduction of a group event (say, a shared meal or a council meeting) into pairwise edges between every attendee can look identical to a graph built from several independent one-on-one meetings among the same people, even though the group event and the sequence of dyadic meetings are functionally different processes. Simplicial complexes preserve this difference, and once a social system is represented that way, standard tools from applied topology — persistent homology, Betti numbers, Čech and Vietoris-Rips constructions — become available to quantify features like cycles and cavities that a pairwise graph cannot represent at all.

Applications of this toolkit to social systems remain concentrated in modeling contagion and homophily on simplicial complexes and in adjacent fields such as network neuroscience, where persistent one-dimensional cycles ("cavities") in brain connectomes have been shown to be functionally meaningful, non-random structural features. As of this audit, no published study has computed the first Čech cohomology or Betti-1 of a kinship, congregational, or community-affiliation network with the specific aim of using it as a coordination-cost or held-structure proxy — the tooling exists, the target social datasets (congregational studies, cooperative membership panels, time-use-linked affiliation data) exist, but the specific measurement has not yet been made.

The higher-order network is the formal object the coordination-cohomology theorem is stated about: a relational nerve built from a federated cover, whose Ȟ¹ rank equals its cycle rank beyond a spanning forest. The audit grades the underlying mathematics as settled and the tooling for computing it on social data as available, but grades the empirical claim that follows — that measured Ȟ¹ on a real community's higher-order network tracks its "held structure" and predicts its trajectory — **[unsupported-so-far]**: a genuine, currently open, and concretely executable gap rather than a tested and failed prediction.
