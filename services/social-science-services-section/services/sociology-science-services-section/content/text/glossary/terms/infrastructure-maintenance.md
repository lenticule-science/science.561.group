Infrastructure maintenance is the ongoing, unglamorous labor of keeping an already-built sociotechnical system running — patching software, renewing certificates, moderating abuse, restoring from backup, answering user support requests — as distinct from the labor of building a new system or feature.

Infrastructure maintenance is distinguished from innovation by visibility and by whose labor is asked to justify itself: Star's ethnographic program on infrastructure treats maintenance as work that becomes visible mainly at the moment it fails, meaning the labor that prevents failure is structurally harder to recognize, measure, or fund than the labor of building something new (Star 1999); Russell and Vinsel argue this asymmetry is not incidental but a cultural preference for celebrating innovators over the far larger population of people who keep existing systems working (Russell and Vinsel 2016).

The consequence for a volunteer-run tech collective is that maintenance labor competes poorly for recognition, succession planning, and resources against the more visible work of adding new services, so a collective that does not deliberately account for maintenance as real, ongoing labor risks losing its capacity to keep existing infrastructure running even while it appears externally successful.
