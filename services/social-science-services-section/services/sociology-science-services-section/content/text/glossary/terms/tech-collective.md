A tech collective is a volunteer-run, horizontally organized group that builds, hosts, and maintains communication infrastructure — email, web hosting, mailing lists, chat, video, streaming, and anonymity services — for a political or activist population, funded by donations and small-scale fundraising rather than commercial revenue, and governed without a formal ownership hierarchy or shareholder structure. Milan's study of the "liberated technologies" social movements build documents that this infrastructure is rarely produced from nothing: it typically grows out of an existing movement's own communicative practice, and its maintainers are themselves movement participants rather than hired technical staff (Milan 2013). Wolfson's ethnography of the Indymedia network shows the same pattern at larger scale, tracing how the Zapatista movement's network-organizational form and participatory-governance practices were carried directly into the Independent Media Center network's own infrastructure and decision-making (Wolfson 2014).

A tech collective is distinguished from a commercial internet service provider or platform by governance and accountability, not merely by size or politics: a commercial provider is accountable to owners or shareholders through market discipline, while a tech collective is accountable to the movement it serves through direct participation, consensus process, or informal trust. It is distinguished from an individual system administrator's personal server by its collective, multi-person governance and succession capacity, and from a nonprofit "tech for good" program by not being funded through grant-making institutions with their own reporting and compliance requirements.

The consequence of this structure is that a tech collective's capacity is bounded by volunteer labor and by dependency on commercial and state-regulated infrastructure it cannot fully route around — banking rails, domain registries, and hosting colocation remain outside the collective's own governance even when everything else it runs is self-hosted and self-governed. This residual dependency is the mechanism through which a state or commercial actor can disable a tech collective's services without directly compromising the collective's own self-hosted systems at all.
