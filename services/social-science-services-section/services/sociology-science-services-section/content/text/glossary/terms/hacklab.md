A hacklab is a physical, place-based collective computing space organized around explicit political commitments — anti-capitalism, autonomy from the state, opposition to intellectual-property enclosure — and typically embedded in a squatted or self-managed social center rather than operating as an independent venue.

A hacklab is distinguished from a hackerspace by genealogy and politics: Maxigas traces hacklabs and hackerspaces as two forks of the same hacker-culture lineage, with hacklabs predating hackerspaces and organized around an explicit political agenda, while the hackerspace model that spread from the mid-2000s onward is typically organized around a depoliticized, membership-fee-funded maker and prototyping function (Maxigas 2012).

The consequence of a hacklab's embeddedness in a squatted or occupied physical space is that its survival is tied to the fate of that space: eviction, building sale, or the loss of the host social center can end a hacklab even when its computing equipment, volunteers, and technical practice remain intact — a failure mode distinct from the network-level failures, such as domain seizure or banking cutoff, that end a purely networked tech collective.
