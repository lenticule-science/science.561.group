Technological purism is the practice of refusing to adopt a widely-used tool, platform, or technical standard on the grounds of political or ethical principle — commonly a commitment to free and open-source software, to avoiding proprietary or centralized platforms, or to minimizing dependency on corporate infrastructure — regardless of whether the refusal is justified by a stated threat model or maintenance-capacity argument for the specific case at hand.

The literature gives two distinct readings of this practice, and they are distinguished by whether the refusal is grounded. Read as free-software ethics, refusal is a substantive political and legal claim, not an aesthetic preference: Stallman's foundational essays ground the free-software commitment in the user's freedom to run, study, modify, and redistribute the software that controls their own infrastructure (Stallman 2002), and Coleman's ethnography of free-software communities documents hackers reformulating free speech, transparency, and freedom from restrictive licensing as sustained, load-bearing ethical commitments rather than arbitrary style choices (Coleman 2013). Read as technological solutionism in reverse, refusal can instead be an unreflective dogma: Morozov's critique of "technological solutionism" names the general error of treating a technology choice — adopting one, or here refusing one — as inherently virtuous or inherently compromised, independent of whether it actually improves outcomes for the problem at hand (Morozov 2013).

The consequence for a tech collective is that refusal is sometimes threat-model-correct and sometimes dogmatic, and the evidence does not license collapsing the two: an empirical study of activist adoption of secure communication tools found that adoption failure was driven overwhelmingly by fragmented user bases, tool incompatibility, and users not understanding what a given tool's encryption actually protected against, not by principled refusal of available technology (Abu-Salma et al. 2017) — evidence that when refusal does occur, it is more often explained by usability and threat-model mismatch than by dogma, though this does not rule out dogmatic refusal occurring alongside these better-evidenced causes.
