---
name: "complex contagion"
description: "The spread of behaviors or beliefs that require reinforcement from multiple independent contacts before adoption, contrasted with simple contagion, which spreads on a single exposure."
date-created: 2026-08-26
authors:
  - Claude Sonnet
---

The term and its formal treatment come from Damon Centola and Michael Macy's 2007 paper "Complex Contagions and the Weakness of Long Ties," published in the *American Journal of Sociology*. Centola and Macy set out to explain an anomaly in the classic diffusion literature: Mark Granovetter's famous "strength of weak ties" result showed that novel information — job leads, in Granovetter's original study — travels farthest through long-range, weak, bridging ties that connect otherwise separate clusters, because those bridges reach people a person's own dense cluster cannot. But Centola and Macy observed that not everything spreads the way information does. Costly, risky, or socially contested behaviors — joining a strike, adopting an unproven technology, taking on a norm your community does not yet endorse — often fail to spread across exactly the same long weak ties that carry information so efficiently. Their explanation: these behaviors require independent confirmation from multiple sources before an individual will adopt them, because the behavior's costliness makes a single contact's endorsement insufficient evidence that adoption is safe, credible, or normatively sanctioned. A single weak tie, however long-range, delivers only one signal; it cannot deliver the multiple independent confirmations complex contagion requires.

Centola formalized the distinction with the metaphor of a "wide bridge": where simple contagion (a fact, a piece of gossip, a joke) needs only a single-strand bridge — one tie — connecting two clusters to cross between them, complex contagion needs a bridge built from several parallel, redundant ties simultaneously, so that a person in the receiving cluster gets reinforcing exposure from more than one independent contact who has already adopted. Centola's later book, *How Behavior Spreads: The Science of Complex Contagions* (2018), extended the finding with networked laboratory experiments and real-world case studies — health behavior adoption, political mobilization campaigns, contested workplace norm changes — consistently showing that these outcomes spread fastest and farthest through networks with redundant, clustered, overlapping ties, and stall or fail entirely in networks optimized for the long, sparse, weak-tie bridges that are ideal for simple contagion.

The theoretical stakes are practical as much as academic: an organization or movement trying to spread information wants a network of many weak bridging ties, minimizing redundancy to maximize reach per edge — a spanning-tree-like structure is close to ideal. An organization or movement trying to spread costly cooperation, trust, or a genuinely new norm wants the opposite: dense, overlapping, redundant clusters, even at the cost of total reach, because the wide bridges those redundant ties create are the only structures capable of delivering the reinforced exposure complex contagion requires. Network interventions designed on the simple-contagion model — for instance, "influencer" seeding strategies that rely on a few highly connected individuals with many weak ties — can therefore actively fail at spreading exactly the behaviors, like sustained cooperation or trust-based coordination, that most matter for durable collective action.

This is the piece of network science that comes closest to a direct empirical vindication of the "cycles hold what trees cannot" thesis audited in this section. A spanning tree — the minimal structure that keeps a network connected with zero redundant edges — is precisely optimized for simple contagion and precisely incapable of the redundant reinforcement complex contagion needs; every wide bridge is, by construction, a set of parallel paths that only exist because the network contains cycles beyond the spanning tree. The audit's verdict on this specific point was correspondingly strong: complex contagion research is treated as near-direct confirmation, at the mechanism level, that redundant relational structure — cycles, not trees — is what trust-dependent coordination and costly cooperation actually run on, even though no study has yet applied this specifically to ceremony, kinship, or gift-exchange networks with the cohomological framing the audited theory proposes.
