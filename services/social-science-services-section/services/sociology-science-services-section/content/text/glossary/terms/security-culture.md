Security culture is a set of shared, mostly informal practices — compartmentalized disclosure, need-to-know information-sharing, resistance to speculative gossip about who is doing what, and deliberate limits on what is documented or discussed outside a trusted circle — that a community whose members may be targeted by state repression adopts to reduce the risk that surveillance, infiltration, or a legal process against one member exposes others (CrimethInc 2005).

Security culture is distinguished from paranoia as an individual affective disposition by being a shared, negotiated set of group norms rather than a private feeling, and from formal operational-security procedure by being informal and cultural rather than written and enforced. McCormick and Owen's formal model of clandestine organization states the structural reason such practices exist at all: operational capacity and operational security trade off against each other, so compartmentalization that protects against infiltration necessarily also slows coordination and raises the cost of bringing in new members (McCormick and Owen 2000).

The consequence of this tradeoff is that security culture is threat-model-correct only when its restrictions are actually calibrated to a real, stated adversary; practiced as an unreflective norm rather than a deliberate response to a stated threat, it degrades a collective's operational capacity — recruitment, onboarding, and coordination speed — without a compensating security gain, compounding the maintenance-labor burden documented for infrastructure maintenance generally.
