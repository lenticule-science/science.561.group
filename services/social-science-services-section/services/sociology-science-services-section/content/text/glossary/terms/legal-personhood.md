Legal personhood is the category that determines who or what can hold rights, bear obligations, and appear in legal proceedings. In American law, legal personhood extends beyond human beings to corporations, trusts, governmental bodies, and certain other entities. The sociological interest lies not in the doctrinal details but in what the category produces: a division of the social world into entities the legal system can recognize and entities it can't.

The extension of legal personhood to institutions — corporate personhood, in particular — creates a structural asymmetry between institutional and non-institutional forms of social life. An incorporated organization can sue, be sued, hold property, enter contracts, and present [character evidence](./character-evidence.md) in its own name. An unincorporated community, an informal network, or a subcultural scene can't. This doesn't mean such communities are legally prohibited; it means they are legally invisible. The legal system has no category for a [zine](../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/communication-science-services-section/services/media-science-services-section/services/zines-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/zine.md) scene, a mutual aid network, or a local culture. It has categories for the organizations that claim to represent them.

This invisibility is a specific form of [legibility](../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/black-philosophy-services-section/services/abolition-services-section/services/state-repression-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/legibility.md) failure — or, more precisely, a legibility demand. The legal system doesn't refuse to see informal communities; it requires them to become something it can see. The path to legal recognition runs through incorporation, formalization, and institutional structuring — the very processes that transform informal communities into the kind of entities whose character can be evidenced and whose compliance can be monitored. The demand for legal personhood is thus a demand for institutional formatting.

The implications extend to how legal proceedings reshape subcultural ecology. When a trial like the [prairieland case](../../../../../law-science-services-section/services/american-law-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/prairieland-trial-as-cultural-governance.md) establishes that certain institutional practices demonstrate good faith, the incentive falls entirely on entities that possess legal personhood — incorporated organizations, registered nonprofits, accredited institutions. These entities adopt the practices as [credentials](../../../../../law-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/evidentiary-credentialing.md). The informal communities that originated the practices have no legal standing to contest the appropriation, because they have no legal standing at all.

Legal personhood thus operates as the gateway through which [governmentality](../../../../critical-theory/theories/foucauldian/terms/governmentality.md) reaches social life. The state doesn't govern communities directly; it governs the legal persons that mediate between the state and communities. The formatting pressure falls on those mediating institutions, and through them, on the communities they claim to serve.
