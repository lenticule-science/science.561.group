The tyranny of structurelessness is Jo Freeman's argument, first delivered as a talk in 1970 and widely circulated in print from 1972 onward as "The Tyranny of Structurelessness," that a group's refusal to adopt formal structure, offices, or decision procedures does not produce a leaderless or egalitarian group. It produces a group whose actual leadership is informal, unnamed, and — crucially — unaccountable, because no member can be said to hold power they were never formally given, and therefore no member can be formally challenged, voted out, or bound to a process for exercising it.

Freeman wrote from direct experience organizing in the women's liberation movement of the late 1960s, much of which was explicitly and principledly anti-hierarchical, favoring leaderless consciousness-raising groups and consensus process over elected office. She argued that this commitment, however well-intentioned, produced a predictable failure mode: informal friendship networks, communication access, confidence, and free time became the real currency of influence, distributed unevenly and invisibly, while the group's official ideology of structurelessness made that influence impossible to name, contest, or hold to any standard of accountability. "Structurelessness," she wrote, "becomes a way of masking power."

## The mechanism

1. **Someone always ends up coordinating.** Meetings need agendas, communications need drafting, decisions need summarizing — someone does this work, repeatedly, and thereby accumulates informational and procedural advantage, whether or not the group names them a leader.
2. **Informal networks substitute for formal channels.** In the absence of official process, decisions get made in side conversations, friend groups, and pre-meeting alignment among whoever already knows and trusts each other — systematically advantaging early joiners, socially confident members, and those with the free time to be informally present.
3. **Unaccountability by design, not accident.** Because no one holds formal office, no one can be formally removed. A named leader who abuses trust can be voted out; an informal one who does the same can only be gossiped about, since no legitimate process exists to challenge them.
4. **The ideology of structurelessness delegitimizes the complaint.** A member who names the informal hierarchy is often accused of being divisive, power-hungry, or failing to understand the group's egalitarian values — the absence of formal structure becomes a shield against the charge that informal structure exists at all.

Freeman's proposed remedy was not to abandon egalitarian aims but to formalize enough structure to make power visible and contestable: rotating and time-limited tasks, distributing skills and information rather than concentrating them, delegation with explicit authority and explicit accountability, and written process that any member could invoke against any other.

## Relation to the iron law of oligarchy

Freeman's argument runs parallel to, and was developed independently of, Robert Michels' [iron law of oligarchy](./iron-law-of-oligarchy.md) (1911): both hold that concentrated leadership is a structural default of collective action rather than an aberration produced by bad actors. Michels studied formally democratic organizations (parties, unions) whose written constitutions and elected offices nonetheless drifted toward entrenched leadership; Freeman studied organizations that had no formal offices to drift *from* in the first place. Read together, the two describe a single underlying pattern with two entry points: formal structure does not reliably prevent oligarchy (Michels), and the absence of formal structure does not prevent it either — it only prevents the oligarchy from being named or held accountable (Freeman). A governance form combining Michels' concentration with Freeman's informality — power that is both entrenched *and* undocumented — is among the least accountable configurations either theorist describes, since it offers neither the friction formal office imposes on entrenchment nor the visibility formal office at least requires of it.
