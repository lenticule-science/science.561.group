# Status

*The role concept for a position in a social structure — and, in the Weberian sense, for the honor, prestige, or social estimation that attaches to a way of life. The two senses (positional and prestige-based) are not the same and the distinction matters.*

In the positional sense, a status is a slot — "doctor," "child," "citizen," "defendant" — to which [roles](./role.md) attach. Linton and Parsons used the term in this sense: status is the position, role is the conduct expected of someone in that position. Linton distinguished *ascribed* statuses (assigned at birth — race, gender, family of origin) from *achieved* statuses (attained through action — occupation, marital status). The distinction is sharper in theory than in practice; many "achieved" statuses are heavily structured by ascribed ones.

In the Weberian sense (*Stand*), status is one of three orthogonal axes of stratification — class (economic position), status (social honor / prestige / consumption-style), and party (organized political power). Weberian status groups share a *style of life*, a basis of social honor, and patterns of consumption that mark them off from others. Weber's contribution was to refuse the orthodox Marxist reduction of all stratification to class: status hierarchies (caste, religious community, ethnic group) do not reduce to economic position and have their own dynamics.

Bourdieu's [capital](./capital.md) framework can be read as a generalization of Weber: economic capital, cultural capital, social capital, and symbolic capital map (with revisions) onto Weber's class–status–party plus the symbolic transformation that converts the others into legitimacy.

In the Black-radical and intersectional traditions, the analysis of status is inseparable from the analysis of how race, gender, and class intersect to assign positions in a hierarchy that is then naturalized as descriptive rather than imposed.
