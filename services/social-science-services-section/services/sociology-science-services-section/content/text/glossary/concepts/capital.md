# Capital

*The role concept for resources whose possession structures position in a [field](field.md) and whose distribution organizes the field's hierarchy. Bourdieu generalized Marx's economic concept of capital to a family of capitals — economic, cultural, social, symbolic — that govern position across different fields and partially convert into one another.*

Marx's capital is a specific historical relation: money used to buy means of production and labor power in order to extract surplus value and accumulate more money. It is not a thing but a process — value in motion, valorizing itself by passing through production. The Marxist analysis of capital is the analysis of that process and the social arrangement that sustains it.

Bourdieu's generalization keeps the structural logic — capital is what gives position in a structured space — and abstracts from the specific economic form. *Economic capital* is roughly Marx's, materialized as money or productive assets. *Cultural capital* is dispositions, knowledges, and credentials whose recognition is restricted (the right accent, the elite degree, the ability to read the right paintings). *Social capital* is the actual or potential resources accruing from membership in a durable network of mutual recognition. *Symbolic capital* is what any of the others becomes when it is recognized as legitimate — when it stops looking like power and starts looking like prestige, taste, or merit.

The capitals are partially convertible. Economic capital can purchase cultural capital (private schools, expensive credentials). Cultural capital can be cashed for economic capital (the elite degree gets the high-paying job). Social capital can mediate both. The conversion is not free — there are exchange rates, costs, and sometimes refusals — and field-specific dynamics govern when conversion succeeds.

The Black-radical and Black-feminist traditions have pressed important critiques. Capital theory tends to assume the field as given and to analyze position within it; it can underestimate the prior question of whose entry into the field is conditioned on race, gender, citizenship. Patricia Hill Collins's analysis of intersectional positions, the Combahee River Collective's analysis of Black women's situation, and the analysis of the carceral state as a system that *strips* certain populations of capital all push the framework beyond a competition-within-the-field picture.

In the Marxist tradition, returning the analysis to capital-the-process — the specific historical relation of capitalist accumulation — remains important. Bourdieu's generalization is an analytical tool; it does not displace the historical-materialist account of what capitalism specifically is.
