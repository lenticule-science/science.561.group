# Participant observation

*The method in which the researcher takes part in the activities of the studied world — as worker, member, congregant, neighbor — while simultaneously recording observations of those activities. The dual position is the method's signature: the researcher is at once inside the world (participating) and producing knowledge about it from outside (observing).*

Participant observation is the core technique of [ethnography](./ethnography.md), but the two are not identical. Ethnography is a method family with characteristic outputs (thick description) and commitments (sustained presence); participant observation is one of its constitutive techniques. Pure observation without participation, and pure participation without systematic observation, are the limit cases that participant observation occupies the middle of.

Gold's classic typology of researcher roles — complete participant, participant-as-observer, observer-as-participant, complete observer — names a continuum the researcher chooses a position on. Each position has trade-offs. Complete participation gets the deepest access but raises ethical questions about deception and analytic distance; complete observation preserves analytic distance but loses access to what is only legible from inside.

What the technique produces. Field notes, written as soon after the observed scene as practical, recording behavior, talk, setting, and the researcher's own reactions. Over months and years, the field notes accumulate into the corpus from which analysis is built. The notes are not raw data — they are already interpreted — but they are the closest the method comes to direct evidence of the studied world.

Critical and feminist extensions have made the question of what the researcher *owes* the studied — beyond consent forms and IRB approval — methodologically central. Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies* and Tuck and Yang's "Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor" press analogous questions for Indigenous communities: who is the research for, who controls what is published, who benefits.

Limits. Reactivity — the researcher's presence changes what is observed — is unavoidable; the question is how to track and analyze it rather than eliminate it. Generalization beyond the observed setting is constrained: participant observation builds detailed knowledge of one site, and claims about other sites require separate evidence.
