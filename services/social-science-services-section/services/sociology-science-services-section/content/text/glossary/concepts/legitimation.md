# Legitimation

The process by which a polity's authority comes to be experienced as binding — accepted as appropriate, not merely effective. Legitimation is the answer to *why* a polity's commands have force: not because they coerce, but because the people they bind reason themselves into compliance.

The canonical sociological account is Max Weber's three types of legitimate domination — traditional, charismatic, and legal-rational ([weber1919](../../documents/articles/bibliographies/weber1919.md), and the more developed treatment in *Wirtschaft und Gesellschaft*). Each type names a different basis on which obedience is given: ancestral custom, personal magnetism that breaks ordinary expectations, or impersonal rules administered through office. Weber's typology is a starting point in this domain, not a finishing point.

## Beyond Weber's three

Polity-forms outside the Weberian set legitimate themselves differently:

- **Kinship-based legitimation**: in polities where kinship is the organizing relation rather than territorial citizenship, authority is legitimate insofar as it conforms to kin-relations and their obligations. Ella Deloria ([deloria-ella1944](../../../../../../../../../public-services-section/services/personal-services-section/services/emsenn-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/reference-articles/bibliographies/deloria-ella1944.md)) is the foundational source on Dakota kinship-as-organization. The naca's authority among the Lakota is legitimate not by office in a Weberian sense but by the relational standing of one who has demonstrated wowačhantognaka (generosity) and woksape (wisdom) toward relatives.
- **Ceremonial legitimation**: authority is constituted through participation in ceremony, not through procedural appointment. The pipe ceremony binds council decisions; the hunka ceremony enacts political kinship. Authority that is not ceremonially grounded is not authority.
- **Refusal as legitimation-mechanism**: in state-refusing polities, the right of withdrawal *is* the legitimation mechanism — what makes a decision binding is that those who remain have chosen to remain.

## Delegitimation

A polity's legitimation can decay. The relevant question is what conditions destabilize it: charismatic exhaustion, ceremonial neglect, demographic disruption, occupation by an external state. The 561 library's [cybernetic-postliberalism](../../cybernetic-postliberalism/) theorizes a specific contemporary delegitimation crisis of liberal-state authority.

## What legitimation is not

Legitimation is not synonymous with consent in the liberal-contractual sense; consent is one of many possible legitimation mechanisms. Nor is it synonymous with *effectiveness*: a polity can be effective at extracting compliance through pure coercion (the Weberian limit case of domination without legitimation) and a polity can be legitimate without being effective.
