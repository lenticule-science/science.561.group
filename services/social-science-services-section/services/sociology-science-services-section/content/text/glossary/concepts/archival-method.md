# Archival method

*The method family in which evidence is recovered from documents — state records, organizational papers, personal correspondence, court filings, newspapers, propaganda, ledgers, plats — produced for purposes other than the researcher's analysis, and read against the grain of those original purposes.*

Archival sociology overlaps with social history but is not the same: the archival sociologist is typically asking a structural question (how did this institution come to take this form, why did this movement succeed where another failed, how did this category get constituted) and using the documentary record as the evidence base. Skocpol's *States and Social Revolutions*, Tilly's *Contentious Performances*, Bourdieu's *State Nobility*, and a great deal of comparative-historical work depend constitutively on archival evidence.

The method's commitments. Documents are themselves social products, made by particular actors for particular purposes; they are not transparent windows onto the past. The researcher must reconstruct the document's conditions of production — who made it, for what audience, under what constraints — before reading it. Silences in the archive (whose voices are absent, what was destroyed, what was never recorded) are themselves data: archives are records of who had the power to record. Reading against the grain — taking a document produced for one purpose as evidence of something else — is one of the method's signature moves; reading along the grain (Stoler) — taking the document's own categories and anxieties seriously as evidence of how the producing institution thought — is the complementary one.

Critical and decolonial extensions have sharpened the method's politics. Trouillot's *Silencing the Past* (1995), Hartman's *Lose Your Mother* (2007), and Stoler's *Along the Archival Grain* (2009) each press: the colonial and slave archives are themselves instruments of erasure; the method must reckon with what cannot be recovered, not just with what can. Hartman's *critical fabulation* — narrating around the archive's silences with explicit acknowledgment that the narration is speculation — is one response.

Limits. Survival bias: only the documents that were preserved are available, and what was preserved reflects the priorities of the preservers. Provenance: many archives carry contested ownership, especially Indigenous materials in colonial repositories. Access: state archives have classification regimes that shape what can be studied at all.
