# Discourse analysis

*The method family in which the object of study is talk and text — how language constitutes its objects, organizes positions for speakers and addressees, and carries the categories through which social life is intelligible — and in which the analytic move is to read patterns at the level of discourse rather than at the level of individual speakers' intentions.*

The method family is wide and the differences between its branches are real. Conversation analysis (Sacks, Schegloff, Jefferson) studies the sequential organization of talk-in-interaction at the millisecond scale: how turn-taking is organized, how repair works, how categories are deployed in the unfolding of an exchange. Critical discourse analysis (Fairclough, van Dijk, Wodak) reads texts and talk for how they reproduce power relations, ideology, and inequality. Foucauldian discourse analysis (after Foucault, taken up by sociologists like Hall and by genealogically-inclined historical sociology) studies discourses as systems of statements that produce and govern their objects — what can be said, by whom, with what authority — across whole institutional formations.

What the method shares across branches. Language is not a transparent medium reporting on a pre-existing reality; it is one of the things that produces social reality. Categories — "criminal," "patient," "citizen," "immigrant," "terrorist" — do work; they sort populations and authorize differential treatment. Reading discourse analytically means asking how the categories were produced, what they enable, and what they exclude.

The method has been particularly important for analyses of state and institutional categorization (Bowker and Star's *Sorting Things Out*), of media and representation, of policy formation, of professional knowledges, and of how marginalized populations are spoken about by those with the authority to speak.

The Black-radical and Black-feminist traditions have used discourse analysis to track the production of race as category — Hartman on the discursive structure of slavery, Spillers on the grammar of "ungendering," Hortense Spillers on the symbolic order of racial slavery — work that often refuses to remain inside the institutional politeness of academic discourse-analytic vocabulary while using its core moves.

Limits. The method works on what is said and recorded; what cannot be said in a discursive formation is by construction harder to study from inside it. Discourse analysis generally cannot adjudicate between competing accounts of why a discourse takes the form it does — that is a job for historical, ethnographic, or comparative work that the discourse analysis can inform.
