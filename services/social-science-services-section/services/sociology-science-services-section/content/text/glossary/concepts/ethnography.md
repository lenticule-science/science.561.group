# Ethnography

*The method family in which the researcher produces understanding of a social world by sustained, embedded presence within it — typically over months or years — and writes that understanding into a description thick enough to convey how the world looks from inside.*

Ethnography began in anthropology (Malinowski, Boas) as the method for studying small-scale, non-Western societies through long fieldwork. Sociology adopted and reworked it: the Chicago School (Park, Burgess, Whyte's *Street Corner Society*) used it to study urban worlds; later generations used it for institutions (Goffman's asylums, Becker's marijuana users), workplaces (Burawoy's factory), social movements, and digital communities.

The method's commitments. Sustained co-presence — the researcher must be there long enough to be present at the unscripted moments, to develop the relationships through which the world becomes intelligible, and to be challenged by the participants on what the researcher thought she saw. Thick description, in Geertz's sense — not just what happened but the layered meanings that make what happened intelligible to those who did it. The researcher's own positionality as data — who the researcher is shapes what she is shown and told, and this is something to be analyzed, not abstracted away from.

Critical extensions. Feminist ethnography (Stacey, Behar) made the question of relationship — what we owe the people we study — methodologically central rather than incidental. Decolonial and Indigenous-methodologies critiques (Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies*) have insisted that classical ethnography was an instrument of empire and that the method must be rebuilt under accountability to the studied. Autoethnography (Ellis, Bochner) turned the method on the researcher's own experience as the primary site.

The method's characteristic limits: ethnography produces deep accounts of specific worlds and is correspondingly cautious about generalization beyond them. Comparison across ethnographies is hard. The method's evidentiary standards — coherence, depth, the participants' own recognition of what is described — are different from but not weaker than survey or experimental standards.
