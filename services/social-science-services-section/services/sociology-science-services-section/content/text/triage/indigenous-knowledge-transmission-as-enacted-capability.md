# How do Indigenous knowledge-transmission traditions account for capability that must be actively practiced to remain alive?

Many Indigenous traditions hold that knowledge is not a static asset that can be stored and retrieved — it is alive and must be continuously practiced, transmitted, and enacted to remain vital. A ceremony not performed, a language not spoken, an ecological practice not enacted loses something essential that cannot be recovered by documentation alone; it must be re-enacted by living practitioners.

The predicated-work [enacted-capability-staleness concept](../../../../../../../humanities/theories/sociology/theories/work/theories/predicated-work/concepts/enacted-capability-staleness.md) formalizes a weaker version of this: capability witnessed at time $\tau_1$ may be stale at time $\tau_2$ if not demonstrated in the interval. This captures the organizational reality of skills that degrade without practice. But it may not capture the stronger claim some traditions make — that certain knowledge is constitutively absent when not performed, not merely uncertain.

## Specific questions

### On the nature of knowledge-in-practice

1. Do specific Indigenous traditions (with named concepts or documented accounts) articulate a distinction between knowledge that *persists* between enactments and knowledge that *exists only in* enactment? If so, what vocabulary or framework do they use?

2. Is there a recognized category of knowledge in any tradition that, once a community stops practicing it, is understood to be *gone* — not merely degraded or uncertain, but genuinely absent in a way that future enactment would constitute creation rather than retrieval?

### On consequences of lapsing

3. What are the recognized consequences in specific traditions of allowing a practice to lapse — ceremonially, socially, and in terms of the community's standing or wellbeing?

4. Are there traditions with explicit protocols for *revitalizing* lapsed practices — and what does revitalization involve? Does it require direct transmission from someone who still carries the practice, or can it be reconstructed from records?

### On substrate implications

5. The [enacted-capability-staleness](../../../../../../../humanities/theories/sociology/theories/work/theories/predicated-work/concepts/enacted-capability-staleness.md) concept uses settling monotonicity: the capability cell at $\mathbf{t}$ (historical record of past enactment) stays at $\mathbf{t}$; a staleness cell is added when the capability is not practiced. But the stronger claim — knowledge that is constitutively absent when not performed — would require a different substrate representation: either the capability cell itself must be able to decline (which settling monotonicity forbids), or the model of knowledge must be fundamentally different.

If any tradition's account requires the stronger representation, this is a finding that the predicated-work substrate may need to be extended or a new object introduced.

## Notes for the answering researcher

The goal is to ground the enacted-capability-staleness concept in actual traditional knowledge frameworks rather than using Western analogies (organizational skills decay) as the primary model. The substrate should be able to represent what specific traditions say about knowledge-in-practice, not just what Western organizational theory says. Sources should prioritize Indigenous scholars and authorized accounts.
