Insurrectionary anarchism is the current within anarchism that takes the insurrectionary moment — attack, refusal, the rupture in the order of the day — as the site at which the anarchist project actually becomes real. The position is not that the everyday work of mutual aid, organization, and constructive practice is unimportant; it is that the work is anarchist insofar as it carries forward the insurrectionary commitment, and that organizational forms which displace the commitment in favor of representational politics or stabilized institution-building have, historically, ceased to be anarchist in any operative sense.

The school's lineage runs through nineteenth-century Italian anarchism (Errico Malatesta, Luigi Galleani), through Alfredo Bonanno's late-twentieth-century theoretical work (*Armed Joy*, 1977; *The Anarchist Tension*, 1995), and into the contemporary international insurrectionary milieu (the Invisible Committee, the Conspiracy of Cells of Fire, the broader European and North American insurrectionary networks). It overlaps with [`anarcho-nihilism/`](../anarcho-nihilism/) without being identical — insurrectionary anarchism is positively committed to the practical work of attack, where anarcho-nihilism's commitments are more closely tied to a position about what is and is not recoverable from the present order.

The signature organizational form is the *affinity group*: small, intimate, self-selected, informal, and project-specific. The affinity group dissolves when its work is done. Federation between affinity groups is informal and ad hoc; permanent organization with delegated authority is rejected on the grounds that it reproduces the structures of the state at smaller scale. *Insurrectionary projectuality* names the practice of identifying specific targets, organizing specific attacks, and making the attacks legible in their political content without subordinating them to the long arc of party-building.

The current has been pressed by social-anarchist and platformist critics on whether attack-without-organization can produce social transformation rather than only individual or small-group acts of refusal. Insurrectionary responses cite the recurrent failures of mass-organizational anarchism (the Spanish CNT-FAI's compromises during 1936–1939; the trajectory of post-war anarchist federations) as the empirical case the critique has to answer. The disagreement is internal to the tradition.
