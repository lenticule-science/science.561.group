## **Introduction: Collapse Is the Continuation of Structure by Other Means**

Collapse is not rupture. It is culmination.

What looks like breakdown is often only the realization of trajectories long in motion—trajectories rendered illegible by the very systems they sustain. Joseph Tainter called it complexity collapse: when societies demand more energy to maintain their structures than their structures return. Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen pushed further: in thermodynamic terms, the economy is a heat engine. Growth is not indefinite. It is entropy postponed by extraction.

To name collapse as future is to misread its temporal structure. It is not coming. It is already underway.

Peter Gelderloos, in _The Solutions Are Already Here_, insists that collapse is not a theoretical horizon, but a present condition—visible in food system precarity, institutional rot, climate instability. But rather than confront this, political discourse reroutes collapse into theater. When systems begin to fray, the narrative insists they were sabotaged. When infrastructure cracks, the culprit becomes a personality. When the state fails, it’s explained not as entropy, but incompetence.

This is not analysis. It is displacement.

The spectacle of collapse is not collapse itself—it is its camouflage. Trump's second presidential administration, with its absurdist policy stunts and maximalist incoherence, is not a deviation. It is the form collapse takes when it becomes visible but remains ungrievable. Its chaos is not accidental. It is systemic consequence rendered in slapstick: Greenland buybacks, “Liberation Day” tariffs, crypto-farm subsidies announced via meme.

Deborah Bird Rose names this dislocation _slow violence_: harm distributed across space and time, unrecognizable to systems that only register drama and speed. The institutions that produce collapse are structured to detect only its cinematic version. They cannot see entropy unless it comes with explosions. They do not understand a world ending in silence.

This paper proceeds from the opposite orientation. It does not treat collapse as impending crisis. It treats it as ambient condition. A present tense with no climax. A structure without repair.

To do so requires more than policy adjustment or rhetorical correction. It requires confronting the cultural cosmology that framed collapse as impossible in the first place. It requires abandoning the story that promised growth, abundance, and control—not because it failed to deliver, but because it was never true.

We write, then, not to predict what is next, but to name what is already here.

## **I. Spectacle as Interpretive Collapse**

Collapse rarely arrives in a form recognizable to the system it collapses. When the machinery of state begins to sputter, the screen does not go black. It plays louder. It animates. It improvises. Collapse, when misread, becomes theater.

Trump’s second administration—absurd, incoherent, performative—has been treated as a grotesque deviation: a glitch in an otherwise legible order. But this interpretation requires faith in an order that no longer exists. Trump is not a break in the system. He is the genre collapse adopts when it becomes undeniable.

The spectacularization of dysfunction performs two ideological labors.

First, it converts systemic exhaustion into a **morality tale**. When policies falter, the story becomes one of bad actors. When institutions decay, the script demands villains. Collapse becomes a question of character, not condition. Responsibility is recast in interpersonal terms: who failed, who lied, who must be replaced. The system survives as long as its failures can be made legible as mistakes.

Second, the spectacle creates a **temporal buffer**. It suggests that collapse is an outcome we might still avert—if only we elect better people, pass better laws, ban the right app. The fantasy is not just that history has a correct direction, but that it has not yet occurred. Collapse becomes something the future might do to us, rather than what the present already is.

Lauren Berlant’s “cruel optimism” haunts this imaginary. We remain attached to the systems that prevent our survival because they promise it. The attachment is not naive—it is infrastructural. The only tools available are the ones that will fail. The only exits are decorative. The only futures are nostalgic.

Mark Fisher called this _capitalist realism_: the inability to imagine alternatives because even critique is built from within the system’s aesthetic. Spectacle collapses critique into parody. Even opposition becomes content. The meme becomes the strategy. The absurd becomes the truth that can be said.

What results is a kind of interpretive paralysis: an inability to name collapse unless it wears the mask of apocalypse. A climate disaster is legible if it floods a city, not if it erodes the topsoil. Fascism is noted if it declares itself, not if it rewrites procurement contracts. A system can fail entirely while appearing to function, so long as the spectacle continues to perform governance.

This is not incidental. As Peter Gelderloos shows, the spectacle is not the opposite of the state—it is its camouflage. Bureaucracies survive by converting loss into policy, grief into metrics, rage into grants. The theater is part of the structure.

To treat collapse as spectacle is to miss its core character: **that it does not interrupt the system—it fulfills it**. The entropy is not hidden. It is procedural. It is polite. It signs the forms. Collapse has no climax. Only a budget.

## **II. Degrowth and the Cosmology of Refusal**

Degrowth was never unproven. It was unpermitted.

As a concept, it was precise: an intentional reduction in material and energetic throughput, pursued within planetary limits and distributed with ecological justice. As a praxis, it was heretical. Not because it lacked reason—but because it lacked redemption.

The civilizational logic of the settler state cannot metabolize degrowth. Its cosmology is linear, extractive, salvific. Progress is not an option—it is the story. Within this myth, every hardship earns a reward, every sacrifice seeds abundance, every contraction is a prelude to expansion. The system isn’t just productive. It is theological.

Degrowth violates this sequence. It refuses the upward arc. It proposes that salvation is not coming. That the future might be smaller than the past. That contraction is not a crisis—it is the condition of continuity.

Serge Latouche, Jason Hickel, and Kate Raworth all name degrowth as civilizational correction. But correction implies adjustability. The system they diagnose is not correctable. It is governed by a **cosmological contract**: the world will provide, the state will deliver, and the future will redeem the present.

Peter Gelderloos, in _Worshipping Power_, shows how the state emerged not as a neutral organizer, but as a machine for extraction. Its legitimacy depends on expansion. Its contracts are written in surplus. Degrowth is not just undesirable—it is structurally unintelligible. A state cannot administer descent. It has no syntax for it.

This is why degrowth was refused. Not by argument, but by grammar. It was positioned outside the timeline, outside the myth. And when collapse arrived—not as shock, but as settling—the refusal became fatal. Not technologically, but metaphysically. Society was unprepared for descent not because it lacked tools, but because it had no story in which descent could be honorable.

This is not a failure of planning. It is a failure of **permission**.

The myth could imagine apocalypse. It could imagine rebirth. But it could not imagine _enough_. It could not imagine **stopping**.

And so, collapse entered—not as deviation, but as default. Not because degrowth was impossible—but because it was **inadmissible**.

## **III. Solarpunk and the Aesthetics of Continuity**

Solarpunk promised something rare in collapse culture: hope with texture. It imagined futures shaped by resilience, decentralization, ecological restoration, and aesthetic care. Its worlds were post-crisis, post-carbon, and post-hierarchy—lush with permaculture, solar panels, mutual aid, and vivid clothing.

But this was not a vision born from rupture. It was a **mood board of continuity**.

In the aftermath of Trump’s first term, solarpunk surged as counter-spell. It refused despair, refused techno-dystopia. But in refusing grief, it performed an avoidance. It offered futurity without betrayal, restoration without rupture, ecological justice without institutional death.

Zachary Loeb and Joanna Zylinska diagnose this pattern precisely: the speculative genre that imagines transformation without attending to loss. Holly Jean Buck warns of its political anesthetic: a greenwashed future in which power remains unexamined because it’s now “clean.” Aesthetic continuity substitutes for material contradiction.

Solarpunk gestures toward degrowth but skips its antagonism. It offers collapse without conflict, descent without cosmological fracture. It reframes survival as style. The garden grows. The city powers itself. The future remains livable. The promise is intact—just solar-powered.

This is not a failure of vision. It is a refusal of **cost**.

In many solarpunk imaginaries, the future is built by design—not by refusal. It is made beautiful, but not illegible. It invites the reader to live in the future, not to live _without_ the futures they were promised.

Peter Gelderloos reminds us: the solutions already exist. But they are often messy, insurgent, and culturally dissonant. They do not resemble concept art. They are not stylized. They are not soft. They are shaped by refusal, scarcity, and improvisation.

The solarpunk fantasy is not just visual. It is temporal. It keeps the arc. It reanimates the myth. The world will still work. The transition will still be managed. The aesthetics will still signal coherence.

What is avoided is not realism. What is avoided is **unbelonging**. The possibility that the settler future has not just malfunctioned—it has ended.

Solarpunk, in its gentlest forms, does not imagine the death of the myth. It rebrands it. And in doing so, it offers not escape, but **emotional buffering**: collapse softened into color. The myth continues, but now it composts.ut because it was **inadmissible**.

## **IV. The Settler Myth and Its Unmaking**

Collapse hurts more than it should because it violates something deeper than stability: it violates a faith.

This faith was never empirical. It was not about evidence, not about trend lines or policy efficacy. It was cosmological. Sylvia Wynter names it precisely: the overrepresentation of Man as if it were human. Western humanism—not merely as philosophy, but as civilizational software—embedded a worldview in which time moved forward, the earth produced endlessly, and the subject stood apart from consequence.

Collapse violates this worldview. Not by destroying it, but by revealing that it was never reciprocal. That the future promised was never promised to everyone. That the arc was never bending—it was extracted.

This is why collapse is not just experienced as danger. It is experienced as _betrayal_. The world does not just end. It ends _wrong_. The rewards do not arrive. The redemptive arc drops offscreen.

The system’s spokespeople and media interpreters cannot name this. And so, they narrate the betrayal in cartoon: as aberration, as farce. Which brings us to the figure of Trump—not as exception, but as mask.

Trump is not the cause of collapse. He is the form collapse takes when it must be acknowledged without being understood. His incoherence is not deviation. It is exposure. The fantasy of competent rule, rational governance, procedural legitimacy—these were always theater. Trump merely performed them off-script.

Eduardo Viveiros de Castro’s perspectivism helps here. Collapse, like myth, does not mean the same thing to every subject. For some, collapse is a loss of coherence. For others, it is the end of a fantasy imposed by force. Trump’s absurdity is not unique—it is merely unfiltered. The myth is collapsing under the weight of its own continuity.

And that collapse is ungrievable. Because to grieve it, one must first admit that the future was never shared. That for many, collapse was already ordinary. Already historic. Already lived.

Those most bound to the settler myth are often the last to see its end. And when they do, they look for technocratic sequels: reboots, reform, infrastructure bills. What they cannot face is that the dream itself has died. That collapse has already written its sentence. That what remains is to live without its grammar.

## **V. Left Spectacle and the Retreat from Structure**

Collapse does not only disorient power. It disorients critique.

Between 2016 and 2023, left discourse achieved unprecedented structural clarity. Books, panels, movement documents traced the machinery of racial capitalism, ecological breakdown, settler futurity, and algorithmic governance. Power was no longer a puzzle—it was a system. And yet, when collapse thickened into condition, much of this clarity dissolved.

The terrain shifted from structure to story. From pattern to plot. And the Left—like the center, like the right—regressed into spectacle.

Trump’s second term did not just produce bad governance. It produced **a totalizing interpretive trap**: every absurdity demanded decoding, every policy stunt became a content object, every incoherence a provocation for moral theater. Collapse became a series of dramas, villains, redemptions, narrative arcs. The material system was background. Personality became plot.

But the personality was never the engine. As Mark Fisher warned, the dominance of capitalist realism was not the absence of critique, but its containment. Even our opposition has a platform aesthetic now. Even the callout is algorithmically legible. The system permits critique because it knows how to metabolize it—into content, into careers, into affective capital.

Peter Gelderloos writes, in _Anarchy Works_, that much of the NGO and activist infrastructure is built to simulate resistance while preserving governability. The Left, too, has institutions that reward performance over antagonism. As systems collapse, these institutions offer visibility in place of transformation. They tell the story of collapse, as long as the story leaves the frame intact.

This is not about hypocrisy. It’s about **conditions of legibility**. Platform capitalism rewards affect, not structure. Bureaucratic funding rewards metrics, not disobedience. Crisis amplifies urgency, not analysis. And so, even the most radical analysis begins to shrink into spectacle: a gesture, a quote, a figurehead.

Personality politics is not the failure of the Left. It is its fallback—when the structural horizon becomes too costly to hold. When the system will not move, the story must. And so we narrate collapse as if it were a novel, waiting for a better protagonist.

But collapse is not narrative. It does not arc. It does not climax. It does not resolve. It will not be fixed by better casting. And the spectacle, even when wielded with righteous intent, cannot name a system it depends on for distribution.

When critique loses its grip on structure, it does not disappear. It goes viral.

## **VI. Living Refusal: Not Prediction, but Position**

Collapse is not a riddle to be solved. It is a grammar to be disobeyed.

By the time collapse becomes visible to institutional eyes, others have already adapted. Not through foresight, but through refusal. Not through policy, but through position.

Indigenous communities, anarchist networks, subsistence economies—these are not “preppers” for a coming crisis. They are communities for whom the myth of progress never arrived, or arrived as violence. Their survival is not the result of predictive analytics. It is the result of **ungovernability**.

Peter Gelderloos, again in _The Solutions Are Already Here_, insists: resistance is not waiting to be invented. It is present. It is messy, local, illegible to centralized power. And crucially, it is not aesthetically satisfying. It does not map cleanly onto the futurisms sold by either capital or critique. It looks like informal infrastructure, cultural contradiction, strategic opacity. It looks like people who were never promised the future surviving anyway.

What these ways share is not a blueprint. It is an ontological refusal. A refusal to organize time around salvation. A refusal to believe that collapse is what happens _after_ the system fails. For them, the system is the collapse. Its failure is not exceptional—it is procedural.

To live otherwise is not to predict what comes next. It is to **position oneself outside the cosmology that needs prediction to act**.

Refusal here is not romantic. It is not anarchist aesthetics or communal longing. It is disloyalty to the frame. Refusal does not seek to fix the system. It seeks to become **unreadable to it**.

It is not merely oppositional. It is **orthogonal**—it moves in a different direction, through a different logic, on a different timeline.

And that’s what makes it powerful.

Because the system cannot co-opt what it cannot perceive. And the future cannot save what it cannot imagine.

These lifeways do not offer us a way out. They offer **a way through**—without spectacle, without continuity, without promises. Not because they lack imagination. But because they never believed that collapse was the end of the story.

They knew it was the setting.

## **Conclusion: The Myth Dies, But We'll Still Be Here

This paper has argued that collapse is not a rupture but a condition. It is not a singular event caused by incompetence or sabotage, but the material outcome of long-standing energetic, ecological, and political trajectories. Framing collapse as a crisis obscures its structural nature and displaces responsibility onto spectacle and personality.

Degrowth was never politically viable within the dominant cosmology because it violated the foundational myth of progress—that time moves forward, sacrifice yields reward, and the future will always be more abundant. This refusal was not rational; it was cultural. The systems that maintained that myth were incapable of adapting to its loss.

In response to growing instability, cultural forms like solarpunk offered aesthetic visions of ecological resilience, but often substituted visual optimism for structural confrontation. These futures preserved the feeling of continuity without addressing the real dynamics of breakdown or power.

Beneath these responses lies the deeper crisis: the collapse of a worldview. As Sylvia Wynter and Eduardo Viveiros de Castro help us understand, the dominant frame—settler humanism, linear futurity, control as destiny—has been exposed not just as insufficient, but as fundamentally false. Trump’s incoherence is not an interruption of the system, but its logical extension made visible.

Even the political left, once fluent in systemic critique, has largely retreated into spectacle, abandoning structural analysis in favor of narrative drama and personality-based opposition. This is not a lack of analysis—it is the absorption of critique into a media ecosystem that rewards performance over transformation.

Still, outside the dominant frame, refusal endures. Indigenous, anarchist, and subsistence lifeways persist not because they predict collapse, but because they never relied on the myth in the first place. These are not models of the future, but practices of survivance beyond the grammar of progress.

The death of the myth does not mean the death of the world. What comes next depends not on restoring what was lost, but on whether we can stop mistaking the myth for reality. Collapse has already happened. The question now is how honestly we are willing to live with what that means.
