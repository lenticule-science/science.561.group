# Horkheimer 1937 — "Traditionelle und kritische Theorie"

Max Horkheimer. "Traditionelle und kritische Theorie." *Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung*, vol. 6, no. 2 (1937), pp. 245–294. English: "Traditional and Critical Theory," in *Critical Theory: Selected Essays*, trans. Matthew J. O'Connell (Continuum, 1972).

## Status

Read locally (English translation); foundational reference for the [critical-theory](theories/critical-theory/) school. The German original is the citation; most readings here have used the O'Connell translation.

## Annotation

The 1937 essay that named what the Frankfurt School was doing. *Traditional theory* — Horkheimer's name for positivist, value-neutral social science — accepts its own categories as given and theorizes inside them. *Critical theory* refuses that acceptance. It treats the categories of social analysis (class, value, exchange, the individual, society itself) as historical products to be analyzed alongside what they classify, and it insists that social analysis is itself a social act with implications for the world it studies.

The methodological commitments. Critical theory is *self-reflexive*: it asks how its own questions and categories were produced. It is *interdisciplinary*: the separation of economics, sociology, psychology, philosophy is itself an artifact of the bourgeois division of intellectual labor and must be refused. It is *practical*: the goal is not knowledge of society in the abstract but transformation, and the standpoint of the critique is the human capacity for emancipation.

The essay is the methodological charter for the work that became Adorno and Horkheimer's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1944), Marcuse's *One-Dimensional Man* (1964), Habermas's communicative action, and — at considerable distance — the contemporary neo-Frankfurt currents (Honneth, Forst, Jaeggi). It is also the reference point Foucault and the post-structuralist critical theorists wrote against, in arguments about whether critical theory's dialectical and humanist commitments could survive the post-war turn.
