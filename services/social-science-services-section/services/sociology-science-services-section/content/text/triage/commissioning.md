Commissioning is the act by which an authority constitutes a vessel for a purpose. A navy commissions a warship — the hull goes from being a construction project to being a ship in service, with a captain, a crew, and a mission. A [confederacy](../../../../../terms/confederacy.md) commissions a ship by issuing a [charter](./charter.md) — the vessel goes from being a container to being a ship with an [endeavor](../../../terms/endeavor.md).

Commissioning is a constitutive speech act. Before commissioning, there is a vessel (a structure capable of holding things) and an authority (a group with the standing to purpose it). After commissioning, there is a ship (a vessel with a charter, a captain, and a crew). The act creates something that did not exist before — not the physical structure, but the institutional identity.

## What commissioning does

Commissioning performs three acts simultaneously:

1. **Installs the charter.** The commissioning authority declares the vessel's purpose, governing policies, and scope. The charter is the document; commissioning is the act that makes it binding. A charter sitting in a drawer is a blueprint. A charter installed by commissioning is a constitutive document.

2. **Names the captain.** The commissioning authority designates who commands. This is the [investiture](./investiture.md) of the [captain](../../../terms/captain.md) — the formal act that creates command authority. The captain's authority flows from the commissioning, not from personal qualities or consent of the crew.

3. **Constitutes the mandate.** The commissioning creates the authorization under which the [crew](theories/military-command/terms/crew.md) operates. Before commissioning, people may be assigned to the vessel but they have no mandate. After commissioning, the crew's work serves the charter's purpose and falls under the captain's command.

## What distinguishes commissioning

**From [delegation](../../../terms/delegation.md).** Delegation transfers existing authority from one party to another within an already-constituted structure. Commissioning creates a new structure. A captain delegates watch authority to a duty officer (the ship already exists). A confederacy commissions a ship (the ship comes into institutional existence through the act). Delegation is intra-structural; commissioning is constitutive.

**From [investiture](./investiture.md).** Investiture installs a person into an existing [office](../../../terms/office.md). Commissioning creates the office, the vessel, and the mandate all at once. When a new captain is installed on an already-commissioned ship, that is investiture. When the ship is first constituted for service, that is commissioning. Commissioning includes investiture of the first captain but is not reducible to it.

**From chartering.** Chartering is the document; commissioning is the act. A charter can be drafted, revised, and debated without the vessel being commissioned. Commissioning is the moment the charter becomes binding — the speech act that constitutes the institutional reality. In admiralty: a charter party is a contract; commissioning is the ceremony that places the ship in service.

## The commissioning authority

Not just anyone can commission. The act requires standing — the authority to constitute institutional reality. A [confederacy](../../../../../terms/confederacy.md) has standing to commission ships because its member groups have delegated upward the authority to coordinate action. A [sovereign](../../../../legalism/terms/sovereignty.md) has standing because sovereignty includes the power to constitute institutions. A private party can commission a venture (chartering a merchant ship) because the legal system recognizes their standing to contract.

The commissioning authority retains certain powers after commissioning: the power to relieve the captain, the power to amend the charter, and the power to decommission (withdraw the ship from service). These are not delegated to the ship — they are retained by the authority that constituted it. The captain commands the ship; the commissioning authority commands whether there is a ship.

## Decommissioning

Decommissioning is the inverse act: the withdrawal of a vessel from service. The charter is dissolved, the captain is relieved, the crew is discharged. The vessel persists — the hull is still there — but the institutional identity is extinguished. A decommissioned ship is a vessel again: a container without a purpose.

Decommissioning requires the same authority that commissioned. A confederacy that commissioned a ship can decommission it. The captain cannot decommission the ship — that would be the captain dissolving their own authority, which is incoherent. The crew cannot decommission — they serve the ship, not the other way around.

## Etymology

From Latin *commissio* — "a joining together, an entrusting." *Com* (together) + *mittere* (to send, to let go). The [commissioner](../../../../military-command/terms/commissioner.md) is the person entrusted; commissioning is the act of entrusting. The naval commissioning ceremony — reading the orders, raising the ensign, the captain's first command — ritualizes the constitutive moment: from hull to ship, from document to charter, from appointment to command.
