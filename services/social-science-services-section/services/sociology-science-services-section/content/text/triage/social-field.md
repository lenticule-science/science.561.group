# Social field

A structured domain of differentiated positions in which agents compete over field-specific stakes, governed by field-specific rules of play. Bourdieu's concept: a field is constituted by the positions it contains and the objective relations between them — not by the intentions or experiences of the agents who occupy the positions.

In predicated sociology, a social field $\mathcal{F}$ is realized as a [social settling](../objects/social-settling.md) $\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{F}}$ over a social base $B_{\mathcal{F}}$ whose predicate category $\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{F}}$ encodes the field-specific relational vocabulary (the predicates of competition, recognition, and capital conversion that constitute *this* field rather than social reality in general).

## What makes a field a field

Three structural conditions:

1. **Positional differentiation.** The entity set $E_{\mathcal{F}}$ contains agents whose positional sections $\pi_e$ differ — agents are not all equivalent in the field's predication profile. Positions are distributed, not uniform.

2. **Competitive closure rules.** The closure-rule subsystem $\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{F}}$ contains rules that make the recognition of some entities contingent on the non-recognition of others. Field-entry does not add equally to all: a new agent's recognition at $\mathbf{t}$ on some positional predicate may trigger a downgrade (toward $\bot$ or $\top$) on existing agents' recognition cells. This mutual conditioning is the substrate-algebraic content of *competition*.

3. **Illusio.** Agents in the field treat the field's stakes as worth competing over — they are invested in the field's specific form of capital. Substrate-algebraically, illusio is the presence of active closure rules connecting field-specific capital predicates to agent-action predicates: agents with higher positional profiles fire rules that generate further capital-accumulating actions, sustaining the game. Without illusio, the closure rules go dormant: agents no longer fire rules oriented toward the field's stakes, and the field's competitive structure dissipates.

## Field boundaries

The boundary of a field is the set of entities for which field-specific positional predicates are defined. Substrate-algebraically, $e \in E_{\mathcal{F}}$ if the entity set of the social base $B_{\mathcal{F}}$ includes $e$ and at least some field-specific predicate cells are non-$\bot$ for $e$. An entity with all field-specific cells at $\bot$ is not yet *in* the field — it has no positional profile there.

Field entry is the morphism [field-entry](../morphisms/interpellation.md) that moves an entity from outside the field (all field cells $\bot$) to an initial position (some cells move to $\mathbf{f}$ or $\mathbf{t}$ via the entry-witnessing events). Field exit is the settling of all cells back toward $\bot$ or $\mathbf{f}$.

## Relation to the social base

The [social base](../objects/social-base.md) covers all of social reality. A field $\mathcal{F}$ is a restriction of the social settling to a subset of predicates (the field-specific vocabulary) and a subset of entities (the field participants). Different fields may share entities (one person in multiple fields) but have non-overlapping predicate vocabularies (different rules of play).

The autonomy of a field is the degree to which its closure rules are self-contained — firing only on field-internal predicate cells, without requiring inputs from other fields' cells. A fully autonomous field's settling is independent of other fields' dynamics. In practice, fields are partially autonomous: some closure rules cross field boundaries (economic capital converts to cultural capital via specific rules), but the conversion rates are not fixed by the fields themselves.
