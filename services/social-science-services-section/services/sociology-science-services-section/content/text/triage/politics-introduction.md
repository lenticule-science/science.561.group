# Introduction: Why politics, here

The 561 library carries political content already — in [`critical-theory/`](../../critical-theory/) (Marxism, feminism, abolition, Black radical tradition, postliberalism), in [`legalism/`](../../legalism/) (how law produces social order), in [`work/governance/`](../../work/theories/governance/) (decision procedures within organized productive activity), in [`cybernetic-postliberalism/`](../../cybernetic-postliberalism/) (a theory of the late-liberal conjuncture). The `politics/` slot does something different. It takes *political life as such* as its sociological object: the polity-form, sovereignty, legitimation, authority, contestation — the structural features that distinguish a polity from a community or an organization.

## Why this slot was missing

The library's political content grew up around specific committed traditions and around law and governance as siblings of work. What was missing was a slot for comparative political sociology — a place where polity-forms could be studied side by side, where the modern Westphalian state would not be the unmarked case against which other polities are measured, and where Indigenous political life could be treated as itself rather than as a residual category. This slot fills that gap.

## The scoping move

The choice that does the most work here is to treat *polity-form* as the genus and the modern state as one species among many. The dominant tradition of political sociology (Weber, Tilly, Skocpol, Mann) treats the state as the central object and other polity-forms as either developmental precursors or contemporary anomalies. That framing is empirically wrong — Pierre Clastres ([clastres1974](../references/clastres1974.md)) demonstrated this decades ago, and David Graeber and David Wengrow ([graeber-wengrow2021](../references/graeber-wengrow2021.md)) have extended the empirical case across the human archaeological record.

The framing is also politically loaded. If political sociology takes the state as its central object, then Indigenous polities can only appear as failures, as victims, or as folk-objects. Treating polity-form as the genus restores Indigenous polities (and stateless polities generally) as peers — distinct polity-forms with their own legitimation regimes, their own authority structures, their own modes of contestation.

## What follows from this scoping

Three things follow:

First, the politics domain treats *state-refusal* as a polity-form, not as the absence of politics. Many Indigenous polities of the Americas were organized to actively prevent the emergence of coercive centralized authority. Clastres' chief-as-servant is the canonical statement; the Lakota structural mechanisms (right of withdrawal, distributed prestige among naca, omniciye, akicita, wicasa wakan, and eyapaha; ceremony-as-binding rather than command-as-binding) are a parallel and historically continuous case. See [state-refusal](../teaching/explanations/state-refusal.md) and [lakotaean-politics](../theories/lakotaean-politics/_index.md).

Second, the domain treats *sovereignty* as a property a polity can exercise, not a status another polity confers. Audra Simpson ([simpson2014](../references/simpson2014.md)) and Glen Coulthard ([coulthard2014](../references/coulthard2014.md)) have made the structural case from Indigenous experience: a politics of refusal — refusing the settler state's recognition as the basis for one's political existence — is itself a sovereignty-exercising act. The contemporary Lakota refusal to accept the Black Hills compensation award is one of the clearest cases.

Third, the domain treats *legitimation* as polity-form-relative. Weber's three types (traditional, charismatic, legal-rational) are useful for analyzing the modern European state but do not exhaust the genus. Kinship-based legitimation (Ella Deloria [deloria-ella1944](../references/deloria-ella1944.md)) and ceremony-based legitimation (Black Elk via Brown; Walker's ethnographic record) are distinct legitimation principles. They are not exotic variants of Weber's three — they are full peers.

## What this domain is preparing for

The politics domain exists in the library partly to host [`lakotaean-politics/`](../theories/lakotaean-politics/) — the political life of the Lakota people, drawn from traditional practice and from contemporary Lakota scholarship. The captain of this library is Lakota and is vested with the proper authority by their people to do this work; the lakotaean-politics subtheory is authored under that authority, with the broader sociology-of-politics content cited rather than imposed.

The slot is also open. Other peoples' political traditions can be hosted as additional subtheories on the same terms: their own polity-forms, their own legitimation regimes, their own custodial authorities, with the comparative political-sociology canon cited in support rather than in dominance.

## The relationship to predicated-politics

The library also carries a [`predicated-politics/`](../../predicated-politics/) sibling — a substrate-algebraic theory of political life on the [predication-categories](../../../../math/theories/categories/theories/predication-categories/) mathematics. The two are siblings, not duplicates. Politics here is the sociology of political life; predicated-politics gives that sociology a formal substrate. The choice of which polity-form, which legitimation regime, which closure rules to encode in the predicated form is a *political* choice grounded in this domain — the mathematics does not make those choices.
