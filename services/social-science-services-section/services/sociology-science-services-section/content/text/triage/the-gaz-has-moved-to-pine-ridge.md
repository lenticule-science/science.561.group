## Draft 1
For a couple weeks now, my family and I have been living on the [[Pine Ridge Reservation]], where we were invited by the headsman of the Swallow family to cultivate practices of food autonomy. It's been a busy and incredibly fulfilling couple of weeks, where we've been getting to know our new family, the land they live with, and the wider community.

It's been hard to write an update to y'all for a few reasons. It was getting cold enough at night to discharge most of our batteries, so I haven't had my laptop, and I've never bothered to get proficient at typing on my phone. 

There's also been so much to do. Not in a busy way, but as in, there are more good opportunities available to me for what to use my energy on, every moment, than I can actually do. In discussions, that is one definition for freedom me and those close to me have found: having multiple things you want to do, that you can do. 

I've been spending a lot of time getting to know beavers, who are busy reworking a stretch of phahin sinte wakpala. I've been talking with folk about the ecology, how its changed in the last 200 years, and taking tree ring samples from the abundant chansuska growing along the wakpala. I've been getting taught more about my people's relationships with the land as medicine: the importance of tasasa here, Inonotus obliquus as wanagi phezuta, and more that will take some time and care to explain.

The amount of support toward the work I'm here to do is kind of shocking. In Durham, the city government was forcing us to cut down corn because it technically violated the regulations of grass length. (Corn is, botanically, a grass.) Our neighbors hated us for not having a turf lawn like them... even as they appreciated the resurgence of birdsong along the creek, facilitated by our riparian stewardship.

It was... demoralizing in a way I couldn't fully appreciate while living in it. But the other day, we drove to fetch some gasoline for the community, and a now-familiar conversation happened. 

Someone asked, prompted by our out-of-state license plates or accents, asked where we're from, and then, what we're doing here. I get to answer that I'm from Cheyenne River, my mom was adopted off, but I've come how to help people grow food.

And every time, the person we're talking to, their eyes light up, they usually say "wopila" as their first reply, and before much more is said, they're making sure we know we can ask them for help, and start telling us about their own aspirations toward land stewardship.

An attitude of immediate gratitude and encouragement, followed by an exploration of existent and potential material relations, is just so different than the vaguely hostile confusion that was the normal response before we came here.

It hasn't been as hard as I'd worried to not look at things through a lens of the theories informed by what I've learned and experienced. Even with spring here, there are endless occasions for splitting wood, hauling water, or doing any number of other chores to keep life going, and these have been filling up the time I might've found for reflecting on our experiences so far.

What reflection I've manged, with my immediate family, and then discussed with some of the other folk we work with, is that I *have* to figure out how to offer those reflections - and other information - because as much as community might drive the daily chores, are critical to organizing our praxis, and sharing them gets us the outside support we need to have the tools and broader base of materials necessary to develop good practices of autonomy.

It was one thing we knew would be different here: a lower population and vastly different relationship to colonialism means there's not an abundance of dumpsters to search for food, tools, and more. What that difference would look like, on-the-ground, is something that we weren't sure of. There's a lot of moving pieces to providing a good life for folk, and we decided we'd have to wait until we got here to make any sort of plans about stuff. Blenders are great for processing the medicinal roots that we knew grew in abundance around here, but would the herbalists have electricity? We didn't know.

We had a way of coming to understand that we planned to apply, where we would look toward the origins of things, to try to understand how the complex web of contemporary relations came to be. I don't want to distract myself trying to present either Lakota creation stories here, or explanations of soil science, so will say, we've found it critical to start by learning the stone and wind of a land. A lot of the other relationships, we've found, cascade from there.

We planned to look toward those relationships, and how they related to the fullness of the contemporary life on the land, including its relationship to colonialism, and figure out how those relationships might better support the lives of human communities living there.

Put simply, I have to do what I do for income, more, so I have more income, because now that we're here, I need more income.
