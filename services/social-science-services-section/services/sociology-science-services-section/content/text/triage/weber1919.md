# Weber 1919 — Politics as a Vocation

**Weber, Max. (1919). *Politik als Beruf*. Lecture delivered at Munich University. (English: "Politics as a Vocation," in *From Max Weber: Essays in Sociology*, trans. & ed. H.H. Gerth and C. Wright Mills, Oxford University Press, 1946.)**

## Annotation

The canonical sociological statement of the modern state and its political life. Two claims structure the lecture and continue to organize the field. First, the state is defined as "that human community which (successfully) lays claim to the *monopoly of legitimate physical violence* within a certain territory." Second, political authority in the modern state can be grounded in one of three types of *legitimate domination*: traditional (authority of "the eternal yesterday"), charismatic (the authority of personal extraordinary qualities), or legal-rational (authority of impersonal rules administered through office).

The lecture also distinguishes the ethic of conviction from the ethic of responsibility, and treats the vocation of the politician as a specific ethical formation organized around the willingness to use violence in pursuit of ends.

## For the politics domain

Weber is the canonical starting point for [legitimation](teaching/explanations/legitimation.md). The three-type schema (traditional / charismatic / legal-rational) is a useful baseline for analyzing modern-state legitimation, but the politics domain treats it as a *starting* point: polity-forms outside the European modern state legitimate themselves through mechanisms Weber's three types do not capture (kinship, ceremony, refusal). The schema is cited as part of the canon to be extended, not as the closed list of legitimation types.

The definitional move that ties *state* to *territorial monopoly on violence* is also load-bearing in this domain — but precisely because the politics domain refuses to equate state with polity, the Weberian definition becomes one species-level specification within a broader genus of polity-forms.

## Caveats

Weber wrote in the immediate aftermath of WWI and the German revolution, with the dissolution of European empires fresh and the contours of the modern state in flux. His framework is profoundly shaped by that European-state moment and should be read as such, not as a universal grammar of political life. The politics domain's wider scoping — see [polity-form](teaching/explanations/polity-form.md) — explicitly resists Weber's tendency to read non-state political life through state categories.
