Two strands of contemporary critical thought converge on a structurally similar argument: that politics organized around being recognized by the institutions of power reproduces the very structure it claims to challenge, and that the alternative is not invisibility but a different mode of presence — fugitive in [Fred Moten](../figures/fred-moten.md) and [Saidiya Hartman](../figures/saidiya-hartman.md)'s register, refusal-as-resurgence in [Glen Coulthard](../../../../../../../../references/people/glen-coulthard.md)'s. This essay tracks the convergence, marks where the two strands genuinely meet and where they diverge, and notes what the convergence implies for the subdomain.

## Recognition as governance technology

Coulthard's *Red Skin, White Masks* (2014) argues that the politics of recognition operates as a settler-colonial governance technology. By offering recognition on the settler-state's terms, recognition requires the colonized subject to internalize the categories of the colonizer to become intelligible — and so reproduces the colonial relation in the very form of acknowledgment that purports to redress it. The argument extends [Frantz Fanon](../../../../../../../../references/people/frantz-fanon.md)'s critique of recognition in *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) into the Indigenous-resurgence register.

The Black radical tradition's analogous critique works through a different vocabulary. The tradition is suspicious of recognition not principally as a governance technology of the settler-colonial state but as the form through which racial capitalism captures Black life into its own categories — the citizen, the worker, the consumer, the cultural identity that can be marketed. The structure is the same: recognition demands intelligibility on the dominant system's terms; intelligibility requires assuming the dominant system's categories; the categories were built to manage the population they now offer to recognize.

## Fugitivity as the Black radical alternative

Moten and Stefano Harney's *The Undercommons* (2013) names the alternative as fugitivity — a mode of being-in-and-against the institution that refuses the institutional capture-and-recognition that resistance-within-institutions presupposes. Fugitivity is not absence; the fugitive is somewhere. It is presence that refuses the registration the institution requires to extend its grasp.

Hartman's fugitivity, developed across *Lose Your Mother* (2007) and *Wayward Lives, Beautiful Experiments* (2019), is closer to historical practice. The wayward Black women of early-twentieth-century Philadelphia and New York refused work, refused family, refused the narratives prepared for them — and they did so not by being invisible but by inventing forms of life that the social-scientific apparatus could not classify. The refusal is itself the politics.

The convergence with Coulthard is real. Both traditions reject the framing of refusal as merely negative. For Coulthard, refusal of recognition is paired with the constructive politics of grounded normativity — Indigenous ethical-political life rooted in land-based practice. For Moten and Hartman, fugitivity is paired with the constructive politics of black study, the undercommons, the wayward forms of life that fugitive practice generates.

## Where the strands genuinely diverge

The convergence is real but is not identity. Three points of genuine divergence are worth marking:

1. **Land vs. movement.** Coulthard's grounded normativity is rooted in specific territories and the obligations that arise from relations to specific places. The tradition's fugitivity is, in part, an inheritance from the historical practice of fugitive enslaved persons — a practice of leaving place, of crossing borders, of the underground railroad. The two political-existential registers — to remain on the land vs. to flee — are not contradictory, but they are not the same.
2. **The settler state vs. racial capitalism.** Coulthard's primary analytic object is the settler-colonial state as a structure of accumulation; the tradition's primary analytic object is racial capitalism as a global structure. The two structures overlap massively but not completely. The tradition has work to do thinking through the relation; Coulthard's [grounded-normativity](../../povinellian/theories/settler-colonialism/_index.md) frame is one of the resources available for that thinking.
3. **The status of the subject.** Coulthard's resurgence presupposes a recoverable Indigenous subjectivity to be turned toward, however attenuated; Moten and Hartman's fugitivity is more skeptical that there is a stable subject to recover and more attentive to the ongoing production of the subject through fugitive practice.

## What the convergence implies for the subdomain

Three implications for the subdomain's working frame:

- **The critique of recognition is not unique to either tradition.** Both arrive at it from inside the conditions they describe — settler colonialism in one case, racial capitalism in the other. The convergence is evidence that the structure of the critique is right, not just that two thinkers happened to share an idea.
- **The constructive content of refusal is what distinguishes serious critique of recognition from a romance of marginality.** The traditions both refuse the move that makes refusal an end in itself; both pair refusal with the construction of alternative forms of life (grounded normativity; the undercommons; the wayward).
- **The subdomain's relation to the [povinellian](../../povinellian/_index.md) and [abolition](../../abolition/_index.md) sibling subdomains needs to be made more explicit.** Each of those subdomains is working a version of the same problem. The convergence here suggests the boundaries between them are curatorial rather than substantive, and a future pass should make the cross-references denser.
