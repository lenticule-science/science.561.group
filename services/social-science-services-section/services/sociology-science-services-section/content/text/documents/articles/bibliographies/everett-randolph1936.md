# Sam and Louisa Everett with Pearl Randolph (1936) — Federal Writers' Project interview

**Canonical cataloguing:** University of South Florida, *Narratives of Formerly Enslaved Floridians*, item **SFS0021855_00001**, six pages, Box 2 Folder 4: https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/formerly_enslaved_narratives/5/ .

**Original collection:** FWP *Slave Narrative Project*, Volume III: Florida, *Sam and Louisa Everett*; Library of Congress item \`mesn030\`, https://www.loc.gov/item/mesn030/ ; large full collection PDF at https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/mss/mesn/mesn-030/mesn-030.pdf .

**Accessible text read:** FWP original typed content transcribed by Genealogy Trails, https://www.genealogytrails.com/fla/SlaveNarratives.htm, section immediately after “October 8, 1936 / SAM AND LOUISA EVERETT” (web-view lines 949–981). The USF item's scanned six-page image text has *not* been collated character by character with the public transcription.

## Primary-source metadata and conflict

The interview is dated **8 October 1936** in the FWP header and USF archival record. Pearl Randolph is identified as field worker and John A. Simms as editor. Louisa and Sam were later interviewed in Mulberry, Florida about events under slavery near Norfolk, Virginia; the account was collected more than seventy years after emancipation. Aisha Djeliid (2024), first paragraph, introduces Louisa's recollection as **1937**. This discrepancy remains unresolved at publication-grade archival image comparison; do not overwrite the dated original record with the later article's date.

## Observation with distinct voices

**Quoted first-person voice attributed to Louisa (FWP transcription, paragraphs after the introductory story):** She describes Jim McClain directing her and Sam into a sexual relationship, his threat of whipping, their compelled action in his presence, subsequent children, and a later relationship in which Sam cared for her and she came to love him. This is a testimonial account of **a specific act of sexual and reproductive coercion**, not a statistical count of all slave births. We do not copy the dialect passage here; the period transcription itself is mediated by literacy, racism, editorial choices and unequal power.

**Third-person interviewer/editor narration:** A longer section claims McClain held more than 100 people enslaved and compelled numerous pairings, sometimes separating prior family unions. Those passages are editorial synthesis, **not a verified plantation inventory** and not identical to Louisa's own quoted words. No verified count of forced conceptions, subsequent pregnancies, live births or consent is obtainable from that passage.

**Post-emancipation family continuation:** The narrative recounts migration to Georgia, sharecropping, purchasing a small farm, and later living with descendants in Florida. These details matter because the family continues to exist as actors; neither the violent origin of one pairing nor the master's property motives define all of their later life.

## Ethical and analytical annotation

This case provides a **historically located positive example of directed sexual/reproductive coercion**, the type earlier discussions wrongly left only as a speculative “breeder plantation” trope. It does **not** establish how common the practice was; that requires a defined sampling frame and other primary records. Survivors' later affection is not evidence of prior free consent. Reassess the FWP oral-history editing and temporal distance before using verbatim direct quotations in a publication.

**Connected scholarship:** [Djeliid 2024](djeliid2024.md) frames the practice as part of reproductive expropriation; [Reader 2023](reader2023.md), p. 484, argues coercion and emotional attachment should not be reduced to an either-or explanation.
