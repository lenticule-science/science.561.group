## Why this is central

Spade's short book is the definition of post-2020 mutual aid the house writes toward: projects that meet survival needs directly while building shared understanding of why people lack what they need; that mobilize people, expand solidarity, and build movements; and that are participatory, solving problems by collective action rather than waiting for saviors. Its distinction from charity (eligibility tests, hierarchy, moralism, saviorism) and its warnings about co-optation, burnout, informal hierarchy, and the nonprofit form are the practical side of the theory, and its chapters on decision-making, conflict, and "no masters, no flakes" are the nearest thing the literature has to a governance handbook for a group that is not a firm. The house cites it because a pool's rules have to say who decides and what happens when someone does not do what they said, and Spade's answers are the ones the pool's members are likeliest to recognize.

## What it does not settle

The book is about volunteer projects distributing goods and care, not about money held in common; it does not address a fund, a draw, a default, or a ledger, and its consensus practices are for rooms of people who meet. Its critique of charity is a political argument, and the house takes only the operational distinction from it.
