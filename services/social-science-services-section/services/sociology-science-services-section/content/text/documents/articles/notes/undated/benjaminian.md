## Benjamin

Walter Benjamin (1892-1940) was a German-Jewish literary critic and philosopher whose work crosses historical materialism with messianic theology, French Symbolist poetry with Marxist political economy, and the technical-reproductive condition of the artwork with the political stakes of mass culture. His thought refuses the purification that would assign him to one tradition; this refusal is itself part of his method.

The framework draws on three veins. *Historical materialism against progress* — Benjamin's "Theses on the Philosophy of History" (1940) breaks with the social-democratic faith in progress, treating the doctrine as an apparatus that the victors use to narrate their continuity with prior victors. The angel of history sees only catastrophe; the historian's task is to redeem what has been buried under the ruins. *Messianic time* — *Jetztzeit*, the now-time, breaks the homogeneous empty time of progress; redemption is not at the end of history but flashes up in moments of danger. *Weak messianic power* — every generation is endowed with a power, weak rather than sovereign, to recognize the claim of the dead on the living and to act on that claim.

The framework is adjacent to Frankfurt-school critical theory (Benjamin's correspondence with Adorno is constitutive of both), to Jewish messianic thought (Scholem was Benjamin's interlocutor), and to French Surrealism (Benjamin's *Arcades Project* takes the Surrealist method as a point of departure). The cybernetic-postliberal framework draws on Benjamin for the resistance to closure-as-fate: even an operationally closed apparatus is read against the grain by recovering the claim of what it has buried.
