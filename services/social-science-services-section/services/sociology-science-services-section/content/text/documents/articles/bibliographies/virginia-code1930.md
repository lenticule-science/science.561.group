# Virginia General Assembly (1930) — Colored Persons and Indians Defined

**Original enacted text:** https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/colored-persons-and-indians-defined-1930/ . **Original bibliographic source:** *The Code of Virginia as Amended to Adjournment of General Assembly 1930* (Charlottesville: Michie Company, 1930), page 26, §67. **Reading grade:** Full short original 1930 primary law transcription inspected. Virginia Humanities reproduces and images the original printed code; independent archival manuscript and state session journals not collated.

## Textual rule and what it did

Section 67 defined as “colored” a person with any *ascertainable* African ancestry; it also classified people with one-fourth or more American Indian ancestry and otherwise excluded from the “colored” class as Indian. A narrow proviso made legally recognizable **tribal Indians** of people living **on reservations granted by Virginia**, with at least one-fourth Indian ancestry and less than one-sixteenth African ancestry, **so long as they remained domiciled there**. This exception did not automatically include Pamunkey, Mattaponi and other Virginia Indians living away from reservations.

This is **enacted** legislation, unlike the Virginia House's proposed one-eighth amendment of 1926 which the Senate postponed. The distinctions between legal classification of *Indigenous identity* and the politics of elites invoking distant Indigenous ancestry to remain “white” are crucial. Neither is a valid biological definition of tribal nationhood.

## Critical original-source boundary

The law's racial fractions are ideology and administrative assignments, not a scientific genetic taxonomy. The statute assigns state categories; it does not create or exhaust Indigenous nationhood.
