## Source-located evidence

Abstract, pp. 21–43; start with discussion of ‘stock men’, the ages of girls and women, forced pairing, and the author’s treatment of agency.

## What the source supports

Directly addresses a central element that prior conversation repeatedly sidelined: enslavers sometimes coerced particular people into reproducing, sometimes designated enslaved men for reproductive exploitation, and treated descendants as available labor/property. The abstract explicitly distinguishes a spectrum of coercion and uses cases rather than a universal count.

## What it does not establish

The abstract alone cannot quantify prevalence, prove purpose-built ‘breeder plantations’ were ordinary, determine the proportion of births under coerced pairing, or establish consent. The article is evidence against denying documented intentional forced reproduction, not grounds for flattening every enslaved birth into it.

## Primary-case provenance recovered (2026-10-17)

The article's opening case identifies formerly enslaved Louisa and Sam Everett and the violent reproductive coercion of enslaver Jim McClain. It draws upon the Federal Writers' Project interview under the [USF archival identity SFS0021855_00001](everett-randolph1936.md), field work by Pearl Randolph. The archival document and USF metadata date that interview **8 October 1936**; Djeliid's opening states **1937**, a source-date disagreement to preserve until original image/edition reconciliation. The primary transcript's narrative about plantation-wide forced pairing belongs to the **interviewer/editor voice**, while quoted first-person passages are attributed to Louisa. The transcript does not provide a verified census of directed pregnancies or a population prevalence estimator.

[Xavier Reader (2023)](reader2023.md), p. 484, independently examines Louisa's account of later affection within a coercively imposed relationship. This rules out the false choice between recognizing reproductive violence and recognizing enslaved people's emotional/kinship activity. Djeliid's **other** cases and any claimed prevalence remain unreviewed in this inquiry.
