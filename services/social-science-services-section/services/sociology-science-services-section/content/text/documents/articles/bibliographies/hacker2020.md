# Hacker, J. David (2020) — From ‘20. and odd’ to 10 million: The growth of the slave population in the United States

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1080/0144039X.2020.1755502
**Publication:** Slavery & Abolition 41(4): 840–855
**Review level (2026-10-17):** Article is available on PubMed Central and its publisher/university metadata was inspected.

## Annotation

Estimates enslaved population growth, births and imports by decade. Supplies the crucial captive-birth numerator and uncertainty structure for a historical share calculation; the all-birth denominator and Indigenous census exclusions must be reconstructed separately. Does not show that all captive births were directed industrial breeding.

## Commission-specific source audit (2026-10-17)

**Why this source is essential:** Demography of enslavement.

**Recoverable evidence and locators:** Read the reported decennial *births* in Table 1 separately from the study's slave-import and persons-ever-enslaved columns. 1790–99 = 403,648; 1800–09 = 583,395; 1810–19 = 759,768; 1820–29 = 1,003,859; 1830–39 = 1,280,680; 1840–49 = 1,539,820; 1850–59 = 1,865,898. The 1790–1849 sum is 5,571,170, and 1790–1859 is 7,437,068. These integers are a mathematical transcription of the author's retrospective estimator, **not census enumeration of each live birth**.

**Inferential boundary / competing evidence:** Hacker's methodology depends on historically reconstructed enslaved population sizes, growth rates and imports. His table cannot determine how often specific pregnancies were arranged, the proportion due to assault, or the material profitability of each new captive child. A model's publication does not license certainty in its last digit.
