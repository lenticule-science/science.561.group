There is concern that the recent shift in corporate DEI policy, shaped by the resurgence of right-wing governance under the Trump administration, marks the beginning of a broader sequence of violence that may foreclose the possibility of queer liberation in the United States.

This concern reflects what may be called a **somatic ontoeidic kyriogenizer**: an act or process that coerces reality into conformity with a projected world through bodily, image-forming, and hierarchical mechanisms.

A somatic ontoeidic kyriogenizer is a practice or formation that generates a kyriarchal world by conditioning reality to conform to recognizable norms of being. It operates through the body (_somatic_), structures perception and intelligibility through stabilized image-forms (_ontoeidic_), and recursively produces and maintains systems of stratified domination (_kyriogenizer_). It may be instantiated in a subject, system, or recursive social process that converts bodies, desires, and relations into forms compliant with the demands of liberal modernity. This does not merely enforce order; it stabilizes order as a lived and perceptible reality.

Somatics and kyriarchy are established concepts, developed respectively by Thomas Hanna in 1970 and Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza in 1992. The study of **ontoeidics** must be defined more precisely. Ontoeidics refers to the production and enforcement of coherence through eidolons—image-forms that determine what kinds of being are rendered legible, actionable, and real within a given system of meaning. It studies how ontological regularity is established through eidic projection, and how certain modes of being are naturalized while others are excluded from the domain of intelligibility. Unlike formalism, which concerns logical structure, or semiotics, which studies the operation of sign systems, ontoeidics examines the operations by which configurations of identity and embodiment are made to appear as _reality_.

When applied to the present dominant formation, ontoeidics identifies a consistent structural grammar: **directionality**. The dominant liberal order does not simply classify or stratify beings—it conditions their coherence by situating them in relation to an anticipated eidolon. This grammar derives from **Platonic formalism**, which holds that ideal forms organize the real. Under conditions of governance, this logic becomes temporal: the real must become its form. This directional imperative constitutes the arc of **kyriarchal ontoeidizing**.

This arc does not begin with repression. It begins with a structure of anticipation: the sense that a certain coherent form is imminent and rightful. From this, entitlement is generated, establishing a moral claim to that form as already-aligned. When reality fails to confirm this anticipation, the misalignment is interpreted as a kind of theft or dislocation. This sense of loss justifies acts of possession: the queer eidolon is asserted, performed, or imposed, even when the world resists it. When possession cannot restore coherence, the system turns to violence. This violence does not function as enforcement of order in response to disorder, but as the restitution of a world that was presumed to have already been ordered. Each stage—anticipation, entitlement, possession, and violence—acts to stabilize the queer eidolon and convert expectation into structure.

This arc forms the temporal core of kyriarchal ontoeidic practice. It structures not only experience but also the conditions under which instances of being are recognized as coherent. Temporality becomes loaded with eidic demand. In the dominant formation, to exist is to move toward form. Those instances that resist or deviate from this trajectory are rendered incoherent and increasingly subject to corrective force. This is not simply a cultural tendency; it is a general mechanism of world-formation under liberal modernity.

This arc is visible in the eidolon of queer liberal life. The good queer liberal entity is not defined by identity alone, but by its trajectory: coming out, performing inclusion, aligning with institutional norms. Queerness is rendered coherent only insofar as it conforms to the projected arc. Liberal queerness is not a stable category but a managed process of becoming intelligible through movement toward a prefigured form. Its legibility is conditional on the maintenance of that trajectory.

This paper examines how queerness has been formed through this arc, and how this arc has established  eidic technologies that emerged to secure queer coherence under crisis have been generalized across other domains of governance. In doing so, it traces how liberal queerness has not simply survived crisis, but has been structured as a mode of managing it. The queer eidolon, refined through repeated exposure to ontological insecurity, has become a repository of technologies for converting precarity into provisional legibility—technologies which are now instrumentalized by the liberal order itself.

## I. **Queer Eidolon as Living Infrastructure**

What liberal modernity offered queer life was never freedom, only form. That form—stylized, repeatable, and coherent—was not drawn from queer life itself, but imposed through a grammar of visibility structured by governance. As Michel Foucault argued in _The History of Sexuality_, modern power does not repress identity; it produces it. The queer entity is not liberated by visibility, but disciplined through it—named, formatted, and rendered actionable within systems of recognition. This is not freedom but **ontoeidizing**: the coercive shaping of being through the projection and enforcement of recognizable form.

The primary operation of this logic is the generation and stabilization of an **queer eidolon**—a socially maintained image-form of queerness, legible under liberalism. The queer eidolon is not a representation of queer life; it is the condition for that life to become visible, survivable, and administrable. It does not describe but prescribes. And it does so not neutrally, but through a recursive machinery of **affective, aesthetic, and institutional reproduction**.

Lauren Berlant’s theory of **genre** provides the necessary framework to understand this operation. In _The Female Complaint_, Berlant defines genre not as a mere narrative category, but as an **affective contract**: a collectively held expectation about what kinds of events and emotions are possible, and in what order. Pride, under liberal governance, became such a genre. Its rituals—parades, slogans, disclosures—shaped not only what queerness could look like, but what queerness could feel like. Queerness was sutured to celebration, resilience, progress. Through this genre, queer life was not just made legible; it was **formatted**.

The eidolon that emerged through this genre was not a neutral representation of queer life, but a sorting mechanism. As Jasbir Puar argues in _Terrorist Assemblages_, liberal nationalism grants conditional recognition to queerness only when it is aligned with the interests of the state—white, cis, patriotic, productive. This is not an expansion of inclusion but a re-stratification. The queer eidolon, under these terms, becomes a filtering technology: those whose embodiment or relation exceeds the eidic form are not merely excluded, but **disqualified**—rendered incoherent within the system of governance.

To describe this process as **ontoeidic** is to clarify that it is not merely aesthetic, nor ideological. It is ontological. The queer eidolon is not a surface-level stereotype: it is a deep formatting of what kind of entity queerness is allowed to become. Judith Butler’s theory of **performativity** makes clear that identity is not preexistent but citational: an entity comes into legibility through repeated acts that align with recognized norms. But performativity under liberal governance is not free play—it is **eidic citation**. It demands not just iteration, but fidelity.

These performances are affectively policed. Sara Ahmed’s work on **affective economies** shows how emotions “stick” to bodies and circulate within regimes of expectation. In the context of queer legibility, this means that joy, gratitude, and pride are not just encouraged—they are required. Resilience becomes mandatory. Rage, opacity, ambivalence—these threaten the form and must be suppressed or redirected. The eidolon disciplines affect as much as behavior.

The result is not a liberated queer entity, but a **stabilized infrastructure of recognition**. This infrastructure spans social media, policy, pedagogy, and pop culture. It offers rewards for alignment and disincentives for deviation. It does not merely represent queerness; it **conditions the ontological terms under which queerness can appear**. In this way, queerness under liberalism becomes an administrative project: a form of being that must continually demonstrate its coherence in order to remain intelligible.

It is within this ontoeidic infrastructure that queer life becomes not only governable, but also **governance itself**. Pride was not simply an event—it was an annual reaffirmation of eidic fidelity, a mechanism for checking alignment and updating image. Its performative and affective economies allowed the state to outsource the labor of discipline to the queer subject themself, who in turn became a node through which the queer eidolon was maintained.

This section thus reframes the queer eidolon not as a representational artifact but as an **infrastructural ontology**—a system of formatting being that preconditions what queer life is allowed to mean, become, and affect. The queer eidolon is not merely present; it is **operative**. And its operation is not liberation, but legibility.

## II. **Anticipation: Genre as Temporal Orientation**

If the eidolon functions as an infrastructural ontology, then its mechanism of projection is **anticipation**. The queer subject under liberalism does not simply appear—they are cast forward, formed in relation to a future coherence that has already been imagined. This is not anticipation as desire, nor even hope, but as ontological orientation: a structuring of reality through the expectation of form.

Anticipation is the first movement in the arc of **kyriarchal ontoeidizing**, and it is temporal in structure. It begins not with presence, but with promise. Within the liberal regime of governance, queerness is not validated as it is, but as it may become—if it can be rendered more coherent, more productive, more aligned. The form has already been projected; what remains is for the instance to move toward it. This is what Berlant, again drawing on genre theory, describes as the **fantasy of normative convergence**. Genre trains not only feeling, but futurity. To participate in a genre is to enter into its world-shape, with its built-in arcs, resolutions, and conditions for coherence.

In this way, liberal queerness is made to move: toward progress, toward pride, toward legibility. The eidolon offers a fantasy of becoming—not of becoming-otherwise, but of becoming-acceptable. And crucially, this movement is not optional. It is compelled through what we might call **ontological incentives**: legibility, safety, inclusion, visibility, care. These are not neutral goods—they are selectively distributed to those who anticipate correctly, who perform as if they are already aligned.

Foucault’s analysis of disciplinary time in _Discipline and Punish_ is instructive here. Governance does not simply shape behavior in the present—it organizes **temporal life**: training subjects to expect certain futures and to correct themselves according to their distance from them. In the case of queer life, the promise of recognition—legal, social, affective—operates as a deferred reward. But this reward is always conditional, and always incomplete. It is not designed to arrive. It is designed to structure movement.

Thus, anticipation becomes not only an orientation, but a **modality of control**. The queer entity is bound to a projected eidic future and is held responsible for approaching it. This anticipation governs action and interpretation alike. A child’s gender variance is not read as an expression of being, but as a precursor to a transition narrative. A same-sex relationship is not validated in itself, but as part of a larger arc toward marriage, stability, or prideful self-actualization.

This is what Elizabeth Povinelli, in her theory of **endurance governance**, names as the liberal management of suffering and becoming. Endurance is not the right to suffer, but the obligation to wait—to defer present incoherence in the name of future alignment. Queer subjects under liberalism are not freed from harm; they are asked to metabolize it in the name of a later form. The harm is legitimized by the promise of later coherence, and the failure of that promise is individualized as personal failure.

Here anticipation begins to morph into **entitlement**: the sense that the promised form _should_ arrive, that its coherence is not only natural but owed. But before that, it must be clarified that the anticipation itself is already productive. It is the anticipation of coherence that structures behavior, that generates affect, that binds the instance to the eidolon. The queer subject does not simply wait for recognition—they act in its name, feel in its name, disqualify others in its name.

In this way, anticipation is not simply hope—it is **eidic labor**. The genre of liberal queerness requires the subject to anticipate correctly, to align affect and action with the form that has been preprojected. When this is done successfully, it is not called anticipation at all—it is called being “on the right path,” “growing,” “healing,” “coming into oneself.” These are not neutral markers. They are signs that the subject is tracking toward the eidolon.

But anticipation does not remain affective—it becomes **infrastructural**. Institutions reward anticipatory fidelity: those who align with the genre receive support, visibility, funding, security. Those who do not are abandoned, pathologized, or erased. Anticipation, then, becomes a sorting mechanism. It distinguishes not between identities, but between temporal orientations. Not what one is, but **what one is becoming**—and whether that becoming moves in the right direction.

This anticipatory structure is not unique to queerness; it is a general function of liberal ontopolitics. But in the case of queer life, its effects are acute. Because queerness is so often marked by temporal disruption—nonreproductive life, nonlinear kinship, nonteleological desire—the demand for eidic alignment is especially violent. It restructures queerness not to eliminate it, but to make it move: toward form, toward legibility, toward discipline.

The next section will explore how this anticipatory movement calcifies into **entitlement**—how the promise of eidic coherence is mistaken for possession, and how the failure of reality to confirm that possession becomes a justification for violence, lateral or institutional. But first, we must understand that anticipation is not a beginning—it is already a form of governance. It is the gesture through which queerness is made into a project, and the world is coerced into becoming the proper backdrop for its realization.

## III. Entitlement: The Affective Claim to Form

Anticipation creates orientation, but entitlement fastens it to moral certainty. When the eidolon enters consciousness as destiny, its arrival becomes not only expected but required. The subject begins to feel not only shaped by the projected form, but authorized by it. This is the moment at which alignment with genre becomes possession—of identity, of future, of meaning.

Entitlement emerges when the projected arc of coherence becomes a personal claim. Not only will the form arrive—it must. Its non-arrival reads as an error in the world, a betrayal of promise. In the context of liberal queerness, entitlement sounds like: _We earned this visibility. We deserve safety. Our progress should be protected._ These are not demands for survival; they are articulations of betrayal in the face of perceived disruption.

This structure draws from the racial and colonial logics Saidiya Hartman and Sylvia Wynter have analyzed. In Hartman’s formulation of “burdened individuality,” the liberal subject is produced through the coupling of freedom and responsibility. Any failure to achieve the promised life becomes legible as personal failure, rather than systemic betrayal. Entitlement, under this formation, becomes the affective residue of coerced self-making. The subject is inducted into a genre of existence that teaches them to want the form, work for it, and feel it slipping away just as they begin to touch it.

The queer liberal subject, shaped through performative visibility and conditioned to treat their own coherence as a social achievement, absorbs this logic with precision. Alignment with the eidolon is taken as proof of investment. The subject becomes emotionally tethered to the promise of the form, and this tethering justifies the claim: _I have done what was asked. I have given the performance. The world now owes me coherence._

This is not entitlement in a casual sense. It is structural—an affective infrastructure through which projected forms turn into expected outcomes. Entitlement names the moment when projection is no longer framed as a conditional hope, but as a rightful expectation. The eidolon becomes retroactively owned.

This shift reframes obstacles as injustices. Misrecognition becomes theft. Disruption becomes sabotage. The subject who cannot sustain their projected coherence begins to read those around them through the lens of obstruction. _Who is keeping me from the future I was promised?_

This question activates the punitive dimensions of genre. Entitlement is not a passive structure; it seeks restitution. Its grammar demands that someone be held responsible for the interruption of the arc. The subject who aligned with the eidolon now begins to look for the reason the alignment failed.

This is the atmosphere in which lateral violence is born. The failure of the world to fulfill the projected image becomes intolerable. Someone must be made to answer. The queer person who performs incorrectly, who resists genre, who fails to move toward legibility—they become suspect. The very existence of incoherence becomes a threat, because it disrupts the anticipated possession of form. _We were doing so well until you made us look bad._

Sara Ahmed’s concept of “affective economies” offers insight here. Entitlement is not just a feeling—it circulates. It attaches to bodies, speech, space. The subject who expresses grief at the loss of visibility is not simply mourning. They are enforcing an economy in which queer recognition must remain legible, coherent, and morally justifiable. That economy cannot tolerate reminders of its contingency.

In this affective regime, entitlement appears as concern, sadness, anger—but it operates as a disciplinary force. It demands the restoration of the image, and it names those who disrupt it as barriers. These subjects are not cast outside the genre. They are framed as distortions within it—errors to be corrected.

Entitlement, then, is not an extension of anticipation. It is a change in register. The subject no longer waits; they insist. They no longer orient toward form; they act in its name. They hold the eidolon as proof of their own legitimacy, and that legitimacy becomes the measure of others.

What remains is to trace the movement from entitlement to enforcement. When the claim to the form fails to produce stability—when coherence continues to falter—the final move is to restore the form by force. This is not deviation into violence. It is the culmination of a structure in which coercion was always latent. The eidolon demands confirmation. When alignment cannot be secured through anticipation or performance, it will be pursued through discipline, expulsion, or destruction.

The next section examines this shift. How the failure of possession produces the fantasy of theft, and how that fantasy licenses correction. The eidolon does not disappear. It is reasserted—through whatever means remain.

## IV. Proto-Possession and the Fantasy of Theft

Entitlement structures expectation. When unmet, it initiates a search for error. The subject no longer seeks coherence in themselves but scans the environment for whatever obstructs the form they were taught to expect. The eidolon remains central, but its relation shifts: it is no longer ahead, calling the subject forward. It is now imagined as already rightful, already owed, already delayed. This temporal displacement converts form into property.

This moment activates proto-possession. The subject begins to relate to the eidolon as something that should already be theirs. The queer liberal formation teaches this relation early. Coming out narratives, for example, assign pain as prelude and recognition as reward. The subject who completes these rituals expects access to safety, intimacy, and public coherence. When these outcomes fail to arrive, the absence does not signal an error in the eidolon. The subject instead registers a theft.

The world, in this grammar, becomes the thief. It is accused of withholding the promise. The queer subject feels robbed of their legibility. The violence of the world’s refusal is reframed as betrayal. This betrayal is not experienced as abstract injustice, but as a rupture of a specific contract. The subject has performed; the system has failed to deliver. The performance becomes retroactively weaponized. The memory of having earned coherence sharpens into grievance.

This grievance takes form through narrative. It attributes the delay of the promised form to interference. Someone, somewhere, prevented its arrival. Often, this someone is not the institution or the state. It is another queer subject, one whose difference is treated as destabilizing. The noise of incoherence is read as sabotage. The failure of the form is displaced onto the bodies and actions of those who do not align with it. These others are not regarded as unrelated. They are figured as internal traitors who contaminated the form from within.

This is where proto-possession matures into reaction. The subject claims the eidolon as already theirs and blames others for its absence. Proto-possession does not wait for authority to affirm the claim. It acts from within the structure of disappointment. It calls for correction, exclusion, or explanation. It directs attention away from the instability of the form and toward the visibility of the deviant.

Wendy Brown’s analysis of ressentiment is useful here. Subjects shaped by liberal inclusion carry expectations of recognition. When recognition is denied, the subject redirects anger not at power, but at those who seem to destabilize the conditions of recognition. The incoherent subject becomes the reason coherence failed. Punishment is framed as restoration. Expulsion becomes defense.

This mechanism is familiar. It operates not only in policy but in mutual aid collectives, housing networks, informal kinship spaces. Proto-possession turns orientation into adjudication. It rewrites collective precarity as personal grievance. The subject feels harmed by proximity to deviance. They narrate their own decline in recognition as caused by those who do not perform properly. This story justifies control.

Proto-possession does not end with affect. It becomes method. It seeks to restructure the field around a lost coherence. What had been experienced as anticipated safety is now demanded as reparation. This leads to violence framed as clarification. Correction becomes a moral imperative. The queer subject who cannot stabilize the genre must be corrected to secure the form’s return.

The arc continues. The next phase is enforcement—not as the imposition of law, but as the normalization of coercion. The fantasy of theft, sustained through proto-possession, opens the way for punishment to appear as care, or as necessity. The eidolon reasserts its authority through its loss.

## V. Justified Violence as Eidic Clarification

When proto-possession fails to restore coherence—when the eidolon still does not arrive—the world becomes unintelligible in a new way. The subject no longer sees themselves as progressing toward form, or as deserving of it. They now inhabit the world as one who has been wronged by its collapse. The form was not simply withheld; it was desecrated. This desecration does not elicit reflection. It elicits retaliation.

In this moment, violence becomes clarifying. The subject, shaped by anticipation and hardened through proto-possession, turns to enforcement—not to secure a future, but to restore an image of the real. What must be restored is not order in the abstract, but the presence of the eidolon in the field. The form, now treated as sacred, demands purification.

This structure is recognizable within many liberal queer formations. When state recognition is withdrawn, or social legitimacy falters, the reaction is often not refusal but internal policing. Rage is directed laterally. Queers who disrupt the genre, who do not perform the emotions or appearances assigned to the eidolon, are marked as dangers. They are not only seen as incoherent. They are held responsible for the destruction of coherence itself.

This is the entry of justified violence—not as a breakdown of governance, but as its fulfillment. The eidolon, no longer supported by institutions, becomes enforced through informal coercion. Hate does not require hatefulness. It requires conviction. The conviction is that something good was lost, and someone is to blame.

Saidiya Hartman’s theory of “burdened individuality” clarifies this structure. Liberal governance frames freedom as a reward for performance. Subjects are told that their recognition depends on their capacity to internalize norms, to navigate structures, to endure legibly. When that recognition fails, the subject experiences themselves not as misrecognized, but as betrayed. This betrayal retroactively recodes their prior discipline as injury. What they endured was supposed to mean something. If it meant nothing, then those who refused to endure—those who were incoherent from the beginning—become unbearable.

This moment is not just psychopolitical. It is ontological. The subject finds the field of reality contaminated. They feel unmoored not because of material change alone, but because the world no longer affirms the image that had once sustained their being. The problem is not simply misalignment. The problem is that the form is absent, and its absence is intolerable.

In _Necropolitics_, Achille Mbembe describes the modern state not as one that governs through life alone, but through the management of death—who may live, who may die, and what forms of being are allowed to persist. This principle does not apply only to war zones or colonial regimes. It applies to any system in which the loss of form produces a mandate to kill, expel, erase, or isolate. The queer subject, under conditions of eidic collapse, becomes one such figure of excess. Their incoherence threatens the reconstruction of form. The only response that makes the world safe again is removal.

This removal can take many shapes. It may look like hate crimes. It may look like school bans, prison sentencing, family estrangement, or moral denunciation. But it also takes softer forms: exclusion from spaces, withdrawal of care, refusal to speak names, neglect. These are not errors of community. They are techniques of eidic restoration. The aim is not justice. The aim is to re-stabilize the appearance of coherence by ejecting what confuses it.

Julia Kristeva’s theory of abjection helps explain why the incoherent queer is not simply avoided but expelled. Abjection is the reaction to that which blurs the boundary of the self. It is not disgust at the foreign, but fear of what destabilizes the frame of the known. The abject threatens the subject with dissolution. It must be cast out to preserve internal integrity.

In a kyriarchal ontoeidic system, abjection becomes a mechanism for worldmaking. Those who do not fit the form are not simply misunderstood. They are positioned as contaminating forces whose continued presence undoes the legibility of others. The violence they endure is framed as necessary, even sacred. The world cannot move forward until they are gone.

This is not fringe behavior. It is a feature of liberal governance. The liberal order sustains itself through cycles of projection and purification. Its coherence depends on the appearance of progress. When progress halts, or appears reversed, it must be explained. The explanation is rarely systemic. It is individual. It locates failure in persons, communities, identities. Those identified as the site of failure are then subjected to violence that reestablishes the narrative of advancement.

Justified violence, then, is not only the final phase of the arc—it is its recursive motor. It retroactively affirms the logic of anticipation, entitlement, and proto-possession. It treats collapse as deviation, and deviation as sabotage. The world is cleansed, not to create peace, but to recover a lost promise. The eidolon is not rebuilt. It is demanded through harm.

## VI. From Arc to Apparatus: Governance through Ontoeidic Infrastructures

The arc of ontoeidic formation—anticipation, entitlement, proto-possession, justified violence—does not remain confined to the psychology of individual actors. It is encoded into institutional processes, social norms, and infrastructural designs that mediate access to recognition and care. In this translation from subjective posture to systemic logic, the arc becomes a durable mechanism of governance.

Liberal institutions organize themselves around legibility. Schools, healthcare systems, legal codes, and media platforms all require subjects to present themselves in recognizable form to access resources or protection. This requirement is not neutral. It presupposes that the world contains templates of coherent being, and that entities are knowable only insofar as they approximate those templates. The eidolon becomes the default interface: a stabilizing image of identity, performance, and affect against which recognition is calibrated.

Within queer life, this becomes apparent in the proliferation of infrastructures oriented around visibility, resilience, and upward movement. Pride events, mental health services, educational curricula, and media representation projects often rely on a model of queerness that conforms to an implicit script: open, affectively managed, publicly disclosed, and future-oriented. These infrastructures are not inherently coercive. They may offer support, community, or resources. But their support is often conditional on alignment with the genres of coherence they maintain.

As Lauren Berlant's theory of genre reminds us, infrastructures do not merely respond to social life—they shape it by organizing expectations. Genre is not only a form of narrative, but a mode of orientation: it tells subjects how the world will unfold and what role they might play within it. The infrastructures that manage queer life under liberal modernity enforce genre at the level of relation. They reward those who narrate their queerness in familiar forms and marginalize those whose life paths diverge from the genre's temporal logic.

This marginalization does not always appear as rejection. It may take the form of omission, deferral, or misdirection. Queer subjects who do not fit the template may be advised to "wait their turn," "reframe their story," or "focus on resilience." These gestures function as soft techniques of correction. They realign subjects with the projected form by inviting them to adjust their presentation, not their substance.

Sara Ahmed’s theory of orientation clarifies how this mechanism works. Institutions orient subjects by extending certain paths as available and others as blocked. The subject’s capacity to move is shaped by what is perceived as straight, smooth, or promising. Queer lives that diverge from the projected arc—those shaped by disability, precarity, or forms of relationality that resist commodification—encounter not hostility per se, but friction. Their movement does not align with the infrastructure’s grammar. Over time, this misalignment produces attrition.

The governance here is infrastructural rather than legalistic. It works not by punishing divergence directly, but by making certain forms of life difficult to sustain. Queer subjects are encouraged to internalize the demands of the form in order to remain within the system’s field of recognition. This is not an aberration of liberal governance. It is one of its defining techniques: to shape behavior not through force, but through access.

Foucault’s analysis of governmentality helps name this pattern. Power under modernity is not deployed primarily through overt repression, but through the cultivation of subjectivities aligned with institutional aims. The goal is not to constrain life arbitrarily, but to render it productive and intelligible. The liberal queer subject is invited to become legible in exchange for inclusion. But that legibility is not open-ended. It is shaped by the eidic forms encoded into institutional design.

This dynamic extends to material distributions. Housing programs for queer youth may require narrative coherence about trauma. Health care systems may require gender conformity for access to transition-related care. Funding for queer organizations may privilege visibility campaigns over mutual aid. These patterns are not the result of individual bias. They reflect a broader alignment between resource distribution and eidic clarity. The more a subject or organization conforms to the recognizable image of legitimate queerness, the more support they can expect to receive.

In this structure, incoherence becomes a practical liability. Those who do not align with the dominant form may be unable to access care, recognition, or security. Their condition is not criminalized outright, but it is made untenable. This is governance through form: the regulation of life by its distance from a projected ideal.

As this pattern replicates, it creates an infrastructural field in which the arc of ontoeidic formation becomes self-sustaining. Anticipation is cultivated through media and schooling; entitlement is framed as empowerment; proto-possession appears as visibility or representation; and justified violence takes institutional form, manifesting as exclusion from systems of care. The arc does not merely operate at the level of affect—it organizes the spatial, temporal, and relational structures of queer life.

Infrastructures of legibility do not require overt coercion. They govern by modulating relation to the eidolon—amplifying certain trajectories, minimizing others. Over time, these modulations accumulate, producing landscapes of possibility that privilege the aligned and leave others unacknowledged. What appears as neutrality is often a highly specific orchestration of coherence.

This section sets the stage for the next: an inquiry into the collapse of these infrastructures, and the consequences of eidic withdrawal. The following section will trace how disalignment with the queer eidolon produces not only institutional failure, but ontological instability.

## VII. Collapse and Eidic Disalignment

When infrastructures governed by eidic clarity begin to lose coherence, their breakdown is not limited to policy or program. It alters the field of sense itself. The eidolon—once serving as the stabilizing image that aligned subjects with institutions, narratives, and expectations—no longer holds. This disalignment does not only affect institutional recognition. It disrupts the ontological grammar by which being itself is made coherent.

Lauren Berlant’s concept of _cruel optimism_ is relevant here. A cruel optimism is a relation to an object or structure that promises flourishing but, in its very form, obstructs it. The queer eidolon, in this frame, was never simply an image of liberation. It was a structuring condition of possibility: an image of coherence that organized access to safety, visibility, and belonging. When it collapses—when the conditions for appearing as coherent within its frame are withdrawn or reclassified—the subject is not merely left without support. They are left without orientation.

This produces what Berlant elsewhere calls an _impasse_: a stalled state in which the genre that once governed affective and temporal life no longer functions, but no new form has yet emerged. The queer subject—particularly the one whose sense of self was formed through recursive alignment with the eidolon—experiences this not only as social instability, but as a suspension of the conditions for appearing. The disalignment is not between self and state alone, but between being and form.

Sara Ahmed’s theory of _orientation_ helps clarify the phenomenology of this moment. Orientation is not a neutral condition; it is the organization of desire, action, and attention around specific objects and horizons. The eidolon served as such a horizon. It told queer subjects what future was available, what feelings were sanctioned, and what forms of relationality were intelligible. When it dissolves, orientation fails. The subject no longer knows how to move, or toward what.

This is not simply disaffection. It is the failure of an ontoeidic infrastructure to sustain relational intelligibility. For many, particularly younger queers whose lives have been shaped entirely within this genre, the eidolon did not feel like ideology—it felt like reality. Its removal is experienced as ontological interruption, not ideological disillusionment. The world no longer offers the same cues for how to be a recognizable instance of life.

Judith Butler’s work on grievability and precarity helps extend this point. In _Frames of War_, Butler argues that not all lives are equally recognized as lives; some are made grievable, others not, depending on their alignment with normative frames. The collapse of the queer eidolon does not leave all queer lives equally unsupported. Those most closely aligned with the previous form—those who had built proximity to normativity through visibility, productivity, and performance—experience the collapse as a loss of recognition. Others, who had never been coherently included, may find the field unchanged in its disregard.

This differential effect is important. The breakdown of the eidolon does not equalize abandonment. It intensifies existing distributions of precarity, while generating new dynamics of blame and misattribution. Those whose alignment was most secured often interpret the collapse not as systemic shift, but as betrayal—sometimes attributing that betrayal laterally, to those queer subjects who failed or refused to participate in eidic maintenance. This introduces a recursive pattern of disorientation and attribution: a search for coherence through the identification of disruptive figures.

The moment of collapse, then, is not an emptying. It is a reordering of the conditions of coherence. The eidolon loses its organizing power, but the need for structure persists. What follows is not merely grief, but substitution. New images—often disciplinary, corrective, or exclusionary—are projected into the space once held by the eidolon. These new forms are not necessarily coherent themselves; they may emerge as caricatures, threats, or warnings. What matters is their capacity to absorb the vacuum of intelligibility left behind.

Julia Kristeva’s theory of _abjection_ provides further conceptual traction here. In _Powers of Horror_, Kristeva identifies abjection as the psychic mechanism by which that which threatens the coherence of the self or the social is cast out, rendered other, and stripped of form. The collapse of the eidolon produces precisely this condition. Queer subjects who cannot now be made to align are not simply ignored. They are rendered excessive—too opaque, too loud, too ambivalent. Their non-alignment is interpreted not as divergence, but as threat.

This abjection is not necessarily intentional. It emerges from the structure’s need to resolve incoherence. Where eidic clarity once organized relation between subject and world, the absence of that clarity becomes felt as disorder. That disorder must be explained. Often, it is explained through the construction of figures who are held responsible for the disalignment. These figures are not simply non-normative. They are marked as obstructive to coherence itself.

At this stage, the failure of the eidolon is no longer experienced as institutional loss. It becomes a site of contestation, redirection, and disciplinary projection. The field does not settle into pluralism or ambiguity. It seeks new grounds for legibility, often through punitive or exclusionary means. This is the condition under which the arc of justified violence re-enters the scene—not through spectacular repression, but through everyday mechanisms of redifferentiation and institutional sorting.

In the next section, we will trace how that redifferentiation occurs—how the collapse of one eidic form gives rise to another, and how ontological threat becomes articulated not only through individual rejection, but through re-infrastructuring. We turn now to examine **eidic substitution**, and the mechanisms through which crisis is resolved not by multiplicity, but by reimposed clarity.

## VIII. Eidic Substitution and the Reassertion of Clarity

The dissolution of an eidolon does not terminate its effects. Instead, it initiates a process of **eidic substitution**—the projection of new image-forms into the space once governed by the previous template of coherence. These emergent eidola are not necessarily coherent or stabilized. They often operate through contrast: not to define what queerness is, but to mark what it must not be.

This substitution process functions through **eidic overcoding**, a term we use here to describe the strategic reassignment of meaning to disoriented symbols, practices, or embodiments in order to restore institutional directionality. Overcoding does not clarify; it asserts legibility by narrowing possibility. The rainbow flag, once tethered to an optimistic teleology of inclusion, now circulates as a signal of provocation or excess in some public discourse. The same images that previously enabled recognition are reclassified as destabilizing or corruptive.

This reclassification occurs across multiple institutional scales. In law, queer embodiment is increasingly subjected to redefinition through policy—trans healthcare recategorized as harm, queer family structures as deviance, inclusive curricula as indoctrination. In education and media, narrative representation shifts from positive visibility to perceived overexposure or threat. In digital environments, moderation protocols begin to code queerness itself as political excess. These shifts do not occur uniformly, but the pattern is clear: the eidic infrastructure is recalibrated to make sense of instability through restriction.

To frame this, we return to Achille Mbembe’s work on _necropolitics_. In a necropolitical regime, governance over life is exercised through decisions about exposure, abandonment, and elimination. When applied to queerness post-eidolon, necropolitics does not necessarily operate through direct repression. Instead, it emerges through **reontologizing**—recasting the queer subject not as deviant from an inclusive norm, but as structurally incompatible with the stability of the social.

Reontologizing transforms the queer subject from a figure of possible integration into a figure whose continued existence appears to threaten the coherence of others. This is not only rhetorical. It materializes in policy exclusions, funding withdrawals, and surveillance expansions that recode queer life as a site of instability requiring control. In doing so, the state and associated institutions do not relinquish ontological governance. They shift its vector—from inclusion through conditional performance to exclusion through managed disorder.

Foucault’s concept of _biopolitical rationality_ can be adapted here. If the first mode of queer legibility was a biopolitical one—where the subject was made to participate in its own governance through visibility, health, and productivity—then its successor is a necropolitical rationality that identifies disalignment as a threat to be neutralized. This neutralization does not always look like repression. Often, it takes the form of **selective infrastructural absence**—the withdrawal of care, the slowing of support, the diffusion of blame.

Eidic substitution is also affective. What was once a site of celebration becomes one of anxiety or shame. Public queer gatherings are reframed as dangerous. Queer self-description is monitored for tone. Affiliation becomes burdened with implication. The substituted eidolon operates not as aspiration but as calibration: it guides subjects away from particular expressions through an expanding sense of what is unsafe or inappropriate to be seen as.

This shift also marks a deeper transition in the liberal mode of governance. In the earlier eidic formation, coherence was produced through performance. In the substituted one, coherence is imposed through filtering. The goal is no longer to produce the good queer subject, but to extract any instances that might be framed as undermining a fragile social stability. Surveillance is not heightened by new mechanisms, but by new expectations: that institutions and individuals will police alignment proactively.

Understood in these terms, substitution is not a symptom of chaos but a controlled response to the collapse of a prior genre of governance. It is a way to avoid pluralization of ontological forms by reaffirming the necessity of one dominant image. The cost of this reaffirmation is the formalization of non-alignment as _ontological excess_—a category with no trajectory, no institutional support, and no legitimizing discourse.

In the following section, we will explore how this condition of excess becomes stabilized as a new governance object. That is, we will analyze how queerness, once managed through conditional inclusion, is recoded as a form of crisis—an ever-present disruption that must be continuously regulated, but never resolved. This mechanism, we suggest, is foundational to the persistence of kyriarchal worlding under conditions of ideological failure.

## IX. Queerness as a Technology of Ontoeidic Crisis Governance

The intelligibility of queerness without Pride does not mark a rupture in governance. It marks a shift in its modality. The decline of Pride as a dominant eidic form has not resulted in the disappearance of queer legibility. It has resulted in its redistribution. Forms of life previously peripheral to liberal queerness—withdrawal, opacity, incoherence, refusal—are now situated as objects of governance under a new frame: **crisis response**.

This is not a novel structure. The figure of the incoherent or destabilized queer has long served as a functional object for the management of ideological tension. What is notable in the current configuration is that **the very conceptual apparatuses used to theorize queer life “without form” have become central to the reproduction of form as such**. Queerness without Pride is not ungoverned. It is governed as a **persistent exception**—a category that both affirms the failure of prior legibility and stabilizes a new mode of governance around that failure.

In Lisa Guenther’s terms, such a figure is a **crisis object**: an entity whose unresolved condition legitimates ongoing intervention. The queer subject disaligned from institutional legibility is not excluded; they are positioned as in need of management. This management takes discursive and affective forms—community care practices, trauma discourse, mutual aid frameworks—but it also takes infrastructural form: research funding, institutional programming, and curricular realignment toward emergent modes of queer recognition. These are not counter-hegemonic projects. They are examples of **ontoeidic reorientation**, in which the stabilization of being is pursued not through performance toward an image, but through the formalization of incoherence as actionable instability.

The analytic frames that accompany this shift—“refusal,” “opacity,” “wake work,” “fugitivity,” “relationality,” “nepantla”—do not destabilize governance. They provide a new semantic regime through which non-alignment is converted into a manageable ontology. These terms do not function primarily to describe alternate modes of life. They function to **substitute the defunct eidolon of prideful legibility with a new eidolon: queer as crisis infrastructure**. The subject of this framework is intelligible only insofar as they occupy and articulate their relation to structural harm. Their perceived removal from liberal inclusion is not an exit from governance. It is the _basis_ of their intelligibility within the new formation.

This is not a paradox. It is the expected progression of ontoeidic machinery under kyriarchal conditions. Infrastructures do not fail when their eidolons disintegrate. They reconfigure. In this case, the reconfiguration is recursive: **the queer subject who cannot perform the prior genre becomes the site through which a new genre is justified**. What was once called deviance or illegibility is now reclassified as volatile but governable instability. This instability is not resolved. It is operationalized.

Attempts to theorize queer life outside the Pride arc therefore do not escape eidic structuring. They instantiate a different genre—one that treats incoherence as legible when it can be situated as symptom, effect, or afterlife. The frameworks of care, refusal, and critical opacity that emerge in this context are not inherently counter-ontoeidic. They are often **key mechanisms** of ontoeidic crisis management, enabling the persistence of liberal governance by distributing the work of stabilization across non-state actors and discursive institutions.

This is not a call for purity or for abstention. It is a clarification of the structural logic. **To be made intelligible as queer—whether through pride or through refusal—is to be formatted by a system that requires queerness to serve as a justification for its own adaptive continuity.** The form may change; the function remains.

The good liberal queer has not disappeared. They have multiplied. Under the Pride regime, they were the subject of visibility. Under the crisis regime, they are the subject of management. In either case, their legibility is conditioned by their utility to governance. What shifts is the axis of intelligibility—from alignment with normativity to proximity to disorder. Neither is outside the arc. Both are **derivatives of ontoeidic coherence**.

The notion of queer life beyond form is, under these conditions, structurally incoherent. Not because such life does not exist, but because its legibility requires its formalization as a _type of disalignment_. This formalization is recursive: each effort to describe what evades recognition produces a new mechanism of recognition. Each effort to refuse the genre affirms that refusal as genre. What emerges is not plurality, but **an expanding crisis infrastructure**, in which disalignment is the medium through which governance justifies its own evolution.

There is no collapse. There is only substitution. No refusal of form, only its displacement. **To understand queerness at all is to participate in its management.**
