> “Where do the quiet gays go? My favorite sound in the world is when you set a teacup down on its little saucer. It is very difficult to flaunt that lifestyle in a parade.” — _Hannah Gadsby_

There is some fear that the current shift in corporate DEI policies, motivated by the Trump administration, is the start of a series of violences that risk destroying the potential for queer liberation in the United States.

This fear is what may be called a *somatic ontoeidic kyriogenizer*: a act of feeling reality into alignment with a projected world.

A **somatic ontoeidic kyriogenizer** is a bodily practice or formation that generates a kyriarchal world by coercing reality into alignment with projected norms of being. It acts through the body (_somatic_), structures reality via recognizable image-forms (_ontoeidic_), and recursively produces stratified domination (_kyriogenizer_). It may be a subject, system, or process that converts bodies and relations into compliant shapes under liberal modernity—_not just enforcing order, but making that order feel real through the flesh_.

Somatics and kyriarchy are established ideas, via Thomas Hanna in 1970 and Elisabeth Schussler Fiorenza in 1992 respectively, but ontoeidy must be defined.

**Ontoeidy** is the practice of shaping reality through the production and enforcement of recognizable forms of being. It refers to the mechanisms by which certain ways of existing become legible, actionable, and legitimate—while others are rendered incoherent, invisible, or unreal. Central to ontoeidy is the concept of the **eidolon**: a stabilized image or template that defines what counts as a valid subject, identity, or reality. The study of **ontoeidics** focuses on how specific configurations of identity, embodiment, and world are made to appear _as real_. It asks: _What kinds of being are made possible, necessary, or prohibited through dominant image-forms?_ And how are these images enforced through institutions, discourse, and embodied life?

Ontoeidics, when used to study the contemporary kyriarchal formation - its eidolon - reveals a narrative arc that is central to all eidolons that formation generates, including the good queer liberal subject: directionality.

The dominant formation of liberal modernity traces its grammar of being to **Platonic formalism**: the belief that reality is organized by idealized, preexistent forms, to which all instances must aspire. Within this framework, coherence is not discovered but imposed—entities are intelligible insofar as they conform to stabilized eidic templates. But under conditions of governance, this eidic logic is not merely spatial or symbolic; it becomes **temporalized**. That is: the real is not only expected to reflect the form, but to **develop toward it**, under threat of correction or abandonment.

Ontoeidics—the study of how recognizable forms of being are produced and enforced—reveals that the dominant kyriarchal formation operates through a specific and reproducible arc. At its core is not stratification, but **directionalization**: the organization of reality through a linear trajectory of form-approximation. What liberalism demands is not simply the existence of compliant entities, but their continual **movement** toward eidic convergence. This is the seed-pattern of **kyriarchal ontoeidizing**.

The arc of kyriarchal ontoeidizing begins with a **felt anticipation**: a preconditioned sense that a certain form of being—whether justice, order, success, or liberation—is imminent, deserved, and natural. This anticipation is not passive; it is an **ontological posture** that infuses the world with a sense of latent coherence waiting to be revealed. From this anticipation arises **entitlement**: a moral and affective claim that the projected eidolon not only should arrive, but already belongs to those aligned with its terms. When the anticipated coherence fails to materialize—when the world resists or exceeds the eidolon—this is experienced not as the failure of the form, but as the **theft** of what was owed. That theft becomes the basis for **possession**: the eidolon is seized, performed, or imposed, regardless of the world’s fit. And when even that fails, the final movement is **justified violence**—violence not as enforcement of rules, but as the restoration of an imagined reality that was believed to have already been promised. The arc is not progress—it is a recursive loop of _feeling the world into shape_, by punishing that which fails to confirm the anticipation.

This is not just a sociocultural pattern—it is the structuring logic of **kyriarchal worldmaking**. What it produces is a universe in which every instance must demonstrate progress toward legible form, or risk being rendered incoherent. Temporality is not neutral; it is **eidically loaded**. Liberal modernity does not merely represent being—it demands **narrativized ontogenesis**, where reality must unfold along a predetermined arc of becoming, or be discarded as aberration.

When applied to queer life, this arc becomes particularly visible. The good queer liberal entity is formed not by essence, but by trajectory: coming out, performing pride, achieving resilience, aligning with normativity. Queerness is made tolerable not when it exists, but when it **moves**—toward legibility, productivity, assimilation. In this sense, liberal queerness is not a static identity but a **managed ontogenic project**, whose validity depends on movement toward an eidolon projected in advance.

This paper examines how that arc has structured the eidolon of queer life across time, and how the eidic technologies developed to manage queer coherence have themselves become generalizable crisis infrastructures. We trace how queerness, under liberalism, has not merely endured crisis but been formed by it—and how it has been positioned as both manager and measure of crisis across multiple domains of governance.

However, this view relies on a narrative of anticipated liberation, where the things now being removed are seen as necessary components of queer being. I.e., DEI risks destroying queer being because queer being cannot be, if not included in liberal programs.

This essay begins in the aftermath—not of queerness, but of its institutional performance.

Recent restrictions on Pride celebrations, drag performance, and queer visibility in the United States are often read as a backlash or reversal.

But this framing presumes that visibility was ever secure or liberatory. Instead, these retractions reveal the contingency of queer representation: it was never a guarantee of freedom, but a strategy of management. Pride, in this view, was not a site of queer sovereignty—it was a ritual apparatus that stabilized a particular **eidolon** of queer life.

By _eidolon_, we mean not a transcendent ideal, but a projected and maintained image-form: a socially recognizable coherence performed, recognized, and enforced through collective rituals. Under liberalism, queer life became legible not in its fullness but as an **eidic structure**, instantiated through repetition and institutional affirmation. The Pride parade was not merely a celebration—it was a seasonal liturgy of **ontoeidic governance**, aligning queer life to a permissible shape through spectacular repetition.

The queerness made visible in Pride was never the total field of queer being. It was an **eidolon**: a synthetic stabilization of difference, curated for legibility within national, capitalist, and neurotypical epistemologies.

This essay tracks how Pride operated as an **ontoeidizer**—a machinic process that bound queer life to a ritualized image.

## Shaping Queerness with Pride

What liberalism offered queer life was not autonomy, but image. Visibility, as Michel Foucault argued in _The History of Sexuality_, is not emancipation—it is the productive operation of power. Modern governance does not repress the subject but manufactures it: through surveillance, discourse, and affective regulation. In the context of queer life, this production did not culminate in freedom, but in the stabilization of a recognizable form. That form was not natural; it was eidic.

By queer **eidolon**, I mean the socially stabilized image of queer coherence—produced not in isolation but through ritual, discourse, and circulation. The eidolon is what queer life became in order to be seen. It does not describe all queer lives, but delineates what can be rendered _legible_, _valid_, _true_. The rituals of Pride—the parade, the archive, the rainbow symbology—functioned not only to reflect queerness but to format it. They offered scripts for disclosure, affect, and kinship that aligned queer life with liberal recognition.

Lauren Berlant’s theory of **genre** (_The Female Complaint_, 2008) helps clarify how these images work. Genre, for Berlant, is an affective expectation of world-shape—a structure that organizes not just narrative but feeling. Pride established a genre of queerness: celebratory, resilient, familial, upwardly mobile. This genre was not merely aesthetic. It imposed an orienting logic, a promise of intelligibility in exchange for coherence. In your orientation, in your trauma, in your pride—you became recognizable to the state.

Jasbir Puar’s concept of **homonationalism** (_Terrorist Assemblages_, 2007) makes this logic geopolitical. Under liberal nationalism, only certain queer subjects—white, cis, patriotic, productive—are permitted entry into the national imaginary. These incorporations do not signal liberation; they are stratified modes of discipline. The queer eidolon is not universal—it is highly selective. Those whose queerness cannot be reconciled with national stability are excluded, rendered incoherent, or pathologized.

We understand this dynamic as a process of **ontoeidizing**: the enforcement of being through eidic form. Ontoeidizing does not merely describe the subject—it shapes them. Judith Butler’s theory of **performativity** (_Gender Trouble_, 1990) is foundational here: identities are constituted through citation of norms. The eidolon of liberal queerness demands performance—of resilience, of pride, of forgiveness. Sara Ahmed’s work on **affective economies** (_The Cultural Politics of Emotion_, 2004) adds that these performances are not neutral; they are affectively regulated. Queer visibility requires the performance of happiness, the management of trauma, the display of gratitude for recognition.

What emerges is not just a visible queer subject, but an **eidic infrastructure**: an entire apparatus of institutions, aesthetics, and rituals that sustain the image of queer life acceptable to the liberal order. Pride became the central site of this infrastructure: a seasonal affirmation of the queer eidolon, distributed across media, markets, and municipalities. Its purpose was not simply to celebrate difference, but to format it—to align queer life with legible, governable form.

This did not eliminate other queer lives, but it rendered them _disqualified_: too disabled, too undocumented, too deviant, too rural, too incoherent. The queer eidolon functioned as both gateway and gate. It offered subjects the possibility of recognition—so long as they were willing to perform its image.

Those who could not or would not perform—whose bodies, speech, affect, or relations exceeded the eidic frame—were not simply excluded. They were **ejected**: marked not as pre-queer, but as threats to queerness itself. Liberal queerness, in its bid for legitimacy, came to define itself against the very forms of queer life it had once gestured toward in solidarity. The queer subject who passed into legibility became the agent of discipline: a nodal point through which the eidolon defended itself against contamination. This is the mechanism **Sara Ahmed** describes as "reorientation": the alignment of subjects not only toward norms, but away from others who disrupt them.

In this process, the liberal queer subject did not merely inherit the state's grammar of order; they became **its mirror**. In striving to sustain access to representation, safety, and social mobility, they learned to distance themselves from those queers who did not or could not sustain the genre. Their own precarious proximity to normativity demanded it. _You make us look bad._ _You set us back._ _You're the reason they hate us._ These are not personal accusations; they are **eidic reactions**—defensive reassertions of the form when its boundary is threatened.

This lateral violence operates under the guise of self-protection, but it is structurally eliminatory. As **Cathy Cohen** argued in “Punks, Bulldaggers, and Welfare Queens” (1997), the politics of respectable queerness have always rested on disavowing those queer and trans people most affected by race, class, ability, and citizenship status. In this disavowal, the liberal queer eidolon is secured not through expansion but through purification. The state need not always repress queer life directly; **the eidolon will do it for them**, through the everyday policing of affect, narrative, and visibility.

This disqualification does not operate at the level of belief or ideology alone. It is enacted materially—through neglect, abandonment, targeted silence. Disqualified queers are not only ungrievable in the public sphere; they are often invisible in queer spaces themselves. Pride floats pass over them. Resources do not reach them. Solidarity stops short. Their refusal or inability to conform is rendered as pathology, their precarity reframed as failure. Their _presence_ becomes framed as a threat to the coherence of queer life itself.

In this way, the queer liberal subject becomes both outcome and instrument of ontoeidic governance. Their own legibility is conditional, and thus perpetually at risk. The cost of that legibility is a vigilance that becomes indistinguishable from surveillance—a compulsive management of who may speak as queer, and in what tone. Those whose queerness cannot be cleanly narrated, or whose existence cannot be made productive, are left not outside the eidolon, but **beneath it**—its structural underside, sacrificed to secure its apparent stability.

What Pride made visible, then, was not queer life in its fullness, but a curated model:  an eidolon of difference that could be commodified within liberalism and weaponized outside it.

## Reshaping Queerness with No-Pride

The recent withdrawal of state and institutional support for Pride—its defunding, restriction, and replacement with punitive policy—has been widely described as backlash. But within an ontoeidic framework, this moment should not be read as reversal, but clarification. The eidolon of liberal queerness was never secure; it was always conditional, always contingent on performance. Its collapse reveals the precarious foundation upon which queer visibility was built. Lauren Berlant’s concept of cruel optimism helps name this structure. The eidolon of queer joy and inclusion operated as a promise: that if you performed right, you could be free. But the promise itself was the trap. The fantasy of recognition kept subjects tethered to the very institutions that extracted their coherence. As those institutions retract support, what remains is not simply repression, but affective suspension—a loss of the structure that once organized intelligibility. Berlant calls this affective condition impasse—a stalled temporality where the genre no longer holds, but nothing else has taken its place. In this impasse, queer life becomes unmoored from the eidic infrastructures that once secured its legibility. The rainbow flag becomes inert. The pride script—coming out, survival, celebration—no longer generates coherence. Queerness becomes once again excessive, undirected, improperly shaped. This moment of eidic collapse has been widely felt as psychic disorientation. For many, especially younger queers formed within the liberal queer genre, the eidolon was not just a public image—it was a scaffolding for being. Its removal is not just a political loss, but an ontological rupture: the withdrawal of a map for how to be a coherent queer subject. Sara Ahmed’s work on orientation clarifies how deeply this mapping operates. The eidolon structured desire, futurity, and action. It offered a horizon—Pride, resilience, chosen family, visibility. Without it, queer orientation loses its trajectory. The subject is not simply repressed, but disoriented: turned away from what previously anchored affective and social life.

This disorientation is not merely private or affective—it is infrastructural. When an eidolon collapses, the normative order that once governed relation to it must reassert coherence. In a liberal regime of governance, this does not result in the abandonment of the queer subject, but in their reclassification: no longer legible as good, patriotic, resilient—now refigured as deviant, unstable, threatening.

This is the ontological function of **ontoeidic violence**: when a dominant eidolon disintegrates, the field of meaning does not become free—it becomes volatile. A vacuum of coherence invites coercion. Where once institutions extracted performance in exchange for recognition, they now impose **punishment in the name of clarity**. What was tolerated becomes targeted. What was performatively legible becomes criminally excessive.

The result is not simply the retraction of rights, but the **reinstatement of punishment as pedagogy**. In the absence of a stable queer eidolon, the state seeks to produce legibility through force: bans on drag, school surveillance, anti-trans legislation, religious exemptions, increased carceral funding. These are not policy shifts alone; they are **eidic interventions**. They aim to reforge a stable image of the real by eliminating that which resists form.

The site of this intervention is often the body. Queer and trans youth, disabled queers, undocumented queers—those least able to perform liberal queerness—are made the bearers of this reimposed legibility. Their lives are not simply unsupported; they are reformatted as **ontological threats**. The queer eidolon has collapsed, and in its place rises a regime of correction, containment, and purification.

Such violence is not incidental—it is **eidic maintenance** by other means. Where performance once secured coherence, now discipline and expulsion do. Visibility becomes surveillance. Representation becomes targeting. The liberal eidolon is not replaced with nothing; it is replaced with its negative image: a vision of queerness as chaotic, dangerous, un-American. And this new image is not diffuse—it is rapidly concretizing into law, curriculum, public discourse.

Understanding this as an ontoeidic phenomenon clarifies what is at stake: not only material rights or cultural inclusion, but the very ability to exist without being violently made into a symbol. It is not the queer subject who collapses, but the eidolon that once shielded them from total exposure. And into this gap flows not emptiness, but a reassertion of normativity—through shame, punishment, and fear.

This is why the collapse cannot be met with nostalgia for the eidolon. The liberal form of queerness was never safe—only conditionally suspended from violence. Its undoing has simply lifted that suspension.

This disorientation is not simply a psychological reaction to political repression—it is the structural consequence of **eidic collapse**. The queer eidolon functioned as a socially stabilized template for legibility: it provided the formal coordinates through which queer life could be recognized as coherent under liberalism. As that eidolon disintegrates, the field of social sense becomes destabilized, not just for the queer subject but for the society that depended on the eidolon to know what queerness is.

This is what **Sylvia Wynter** has described, in her reworking of Fanon, as the moment when a particular "genre of the human" begins to unravel. Liberal queerness was never merely a cultural artifact; it was a mode of humaning that allowed the queer subject to enter into the bio-political calculus of the modern state. Its coherence was epistemically managed—_eidically produced_—as a kind of ontological exception. The subject was allowed to be legible _as queer_, but only through the formalized logics of affective legibility, public representation, and political assimilation.

When that eidic form is withdrawn, it does not create a vacuum. It creates a **crisis of ontoeidic legibility**. Judith Butler’s work on precarity and grievability becomes instructive here: not all lives are equally grievable because not all lives are equally recognizable as lives. In the absence of a governing eidolon, the queer subject becomes once again **pre-subjective**—excessive, incoherent, ungrievable. This is not a return to invisibility; it is a shift into **abjection**.

Julia Kristeva’s theory of abjection frames this precisely: the collapse of form induces a reaction not of mourning, but of **violent expulsion**. Abjection is the psychic process by which that which threatens the boundary of the subject or the social order is cast out, made other, de-realized. The queer subject, once formatted as conditionally coherent through the eidolon of liberal queerness, now becomes the site of _eidic threat_—their indeterminacy renders them not just illegible but contaminating.

This is why the collapse of Pride does not result merely in marginalization but in **institutionalized ontoeidic violence**. The state, as a machinery of eidic production, must restore coherence at the level of social ontology. This is achieved not by tolerating incoherence, but by **re-ontologizing** the queer subject as pathological, criminal, or sacrificial. Hate crimes, anti-trans legislation, school book bans, and family separation policies are not isolated symptoms. They are part of a coordinated attempt to **reinscribe the social field with eidic clarity** through negation.

This is not simply repression. It is what **Achille Mbembe** has described as necropolitics: the right of the sovereign to determine who may live and who must die, reconfigured through modern techniques of classification, regulation, and expulsion. The queer subject, in the wake of eidic collapse, is repositioned not merely as other but as a figure through which the state restores its own ontological boundary. This is a **sacrificial function**, and it is ontoeidic in structure.

In this moment, what had been promised as freedom—the ability to appear, to be seen, to celebrate—becomes weaponized. The rainbow flag becomes a target. The parade becomes a provocation. The eidolon, having collapsed, leaves behind its residue as a **justification for punishment**: “you were given visibility, and you failed.” This failure is projected not onto the institutions that structured the eidolon, but onto the queer subject who could no longer perform its constraints.

This is the perverse fulfillment of **Saidiya Hartman’s** “burdened individuality of freedom.” The promise of liberal queerness was never unconditional; it demanded performance, affective discipline, and narrative coherence. With its collapse, those demands do not disappear—they metastasize into disciplinary violence. The failure to embody the eidolon becomes proof of unfitness for life within the national order.

But for the liberal queer subject—formed and legitimated by their ability to approach that eidolon—the collapse is experienced as **theft**. The social bargains that had once rewarded good queer performance with conditional safety, legibility, and celebration begin to evaporate. In this context, those who never had access to the eidolon—who refused or were structurally barred from its alignment—are not simply seen as unfortunate. They are seen as **culpable**. They become the internal scapegoat onto whom the crisis of coherence is projected.

The logic is cruel but familiar: _We were making progress until you ruined it._ _Your deviance made us illegible._ _You gave them an excuse._ These are not merely rhetorical gestures; they are **eidic defenses**—efforts to restore coherence to the disintegrating form by expelling what it cannot contain. As the state's promises withdraw, the affective labor of loss is redistributed laterally. Those already excluded are made to bear not only their own abandonment, but the grief and rage of those who believed themselves included.

This violent displacement is not accidental. It is an essential function of eidic collapse under kyriarchal conditions: when a dominant form fails, its adherents turn toward those who exceeded or failed to achieve the form as **the reason for its failure**. This mechanism mirrors what **Wendy Brown** has described as ressentiment: the political affect that emerges when promised inclusion falters, and the subject turns punitive to reassert their fantasy of control. In this way, the queer liberal subject—abandoned by the very systems it sought to appease—becomes the hand of discipline, enforcing the eidolon not because it still functions, but because its collapse must be blamed on someone.

This produces a recursive field of harm: lateral violence reasserts the eidic ideal even as it collapses, while queers rendered incoherent must now navigate not only abandonment by the state, but **punishment by their peers**. These punishments are not only social, but infrastructural. From gatekeeping in mutual aid networks to exclusion from healthcare narratives, the disqualified are continually disciplined for the crisis they did not cause.

In this structure, the eidolon of liberal queerness does not die. It becomes spectral: no longer visible, but still governing. Its ruins demand loyalty. Its absence demands explanation. And those queers who never conformed—disabled, undocumented, neurodivergent, poor, gender-nonconforming—become not just failed subjects, but **anti-subjects**: figures blamed for the collapse of a dream they were never allowed to enter.

Crucially, it is not only the unruly, the undocumented, the visibly noncompliant who become targets of this redirected violence. Even those who live quietly—who decline to center their queerness within visibility economies, who refuse the genre of flamboyant resilience or the affective repertoire of sanctioned pride—become suspect. The **quiet gays**, long dismissed as apolitical or regressive, now come to be seen as complicit in collapse. Their refusal to participate in the performative labor of eidolon maintenance is recoded as betrayal.

This response clarifies that the problem was never mere incoherence, nor nonconformity to a general liberal subject. The violence is not just about failing to fit in—it is about failing to **labor** on behalf of the eidolon. The queer liberal subject, having built its identity through iterative citation of the prideful, performative form, now polices not simply behavior but **participation in eidic reproduction**. To live queerness without performing it becomes an **ontoeidic offense**: a refusal to affirm the coherence of the form through one’s own body.

The quiet gay, then, is not merely excluded—they are **discursively abjected**. Their opacity, discretion, or ambivalence becomes a kind of eidic sabotage, threatening to unmake the consensus that once tethered queerness to visibility, legibility, and optimism. As such, they are folded into the scapegoat class—not because they resist, but because they **fail to affirm**. The queer eidolon demands not only adherence but **maintenance**; it must be polished, paraded, defended. In a moment of collapse, anyone who does not contribute to that labor becomes a threat.

This exposes the eidolon’s coercive structure: it offers not belonging, but conscription. What appears as inclusion is in fact assignment—to emotional labor, aesthetic discipline, affective repetition. The quiet gay disrupts this by refusing to stabilize the form, not through defiance, but through attunement to another rhythm. Their very mode of life, unaligned with spectacle, threatens to reveal the eidolon’s artificiality—and for that, they are punished.

We must therefore understand the rise in punitive queer policy not as reactionary alone, but as **eidic re-stabilization** through coercion. Liberal queerness is not replaced by emptiness—it is replaced by a formation of queerness as danger, threat, and degeneration. This new eidic image is rapidly installed across social infrastructures: legal, medical, educational, carceral. It becomes the basis for targeted removal and selective care. And in its logic, the queer subject is no longer called to perform pride, but to **absorb punishment as proof of their deviation**.

In this condition, **returning to the liberal eidolon of queerness is impossible**. The structure that once offered conditional legibility now functions as evidence of betrayal. Nostalgia for the parade, the rainbow, the pride narrative, risks aligning with the same eidic machinery that now demands exclusion.

## Conclusion

This essay has traced the rise, operation, and collapse of the liberal queer **eidolon**—a socially stabilized image of queerness sustained through rituals of visibility such as Pride. We argued that this eidolon, far from being a site of liberation, functioned as an apparatus of **ontoeidic governance**: producing legible queer subjects through affective regulation, performance mandates, and narrative constraints. Drawing on theorists including Berlant, Puar, Ahmed, Cohen, Butler, and Hartman, we demonstrated how queerness under liberalism was not made free but made coherent—at a cost.

That cost was borne disproportionately by those already marked by racialization, disability, poverty, or statelessness. These disqualified subjects—too incoherent, too deviant, too quiet—were not simply excluded from the eidolon but rendered responsible for its instability. As the state and its institutions withdraw their support for Pride and queer inclusion, those who never conformed to the eidic form are scapegoated: blamed for the collapse of a dream they were never permitted to enter.

The collapse of the liberal queer eidolon has not abolished ontological governance. Instead, it has shifted from conditional reward to disciplinary violence. Queerness is no longer policed through the promise of legibility, but through the punishment of incoherence. And still, the eidolon persists—no longer an image of hope, but a template of blame.

This leads us to a final question:  
**If neither prideful visibility nor the closet now qualifies as a legitimate queer subject-position under liberalism, what form of queerness does liberalism actually desire?**  
That is, if both flamboyant participation and quiet refusal are treated as threats to social order, what remains as the acceptable, governable queer form?

The answer may be that liberalism does not seek a new queer eidolon at all—but rather the ongoing reproduction of **queerness as crisis**, a permanent condition of instability against which normativity can be reasserted. In this sense, the eidolon was never an endpoint, but a managerial tool—always temporary, always collapsible, always structured to fail.
