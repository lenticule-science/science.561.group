# Scott 1998 — Seeing Like a State

**Scott, James C. (1998). *Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed*. New Haven: Yale University Press.**

## Annotation

A historical sociology of state legibility — the state's drive to render its subjects and territories *legible* (countable, mappable, taxable, conscriptable, classifiable) and the failures that follow from imposing high-modernist administrative simplifications on locally adapted social practice. Scott's cases run from cadastral mapping in early-modern Europe to Soviet collectivization, Tanzanian villagization, and Brasília.

The argument's relevance to political sociology runs deeper than its specific cases. *Legibility* names a structural feature of the modern state-form: the state must make the social world *visible to itself* through standardized categories, and that visibility-work has political consequences. What does not fit the categories becomes invisible or is forced into categories that distort it.

Scott's later work — *Against the Grain* (2017), *The Art of Not Being Governed* (2009) — extends the analysis backwards (to the agricultural origins of state-formation and the conditions of life that escape it) and laterally (to peoples who have organized themselves to remain illegible to encompassing states, e.g., the upland peoples of Zomia in mainland Southeast Asia).

## For the politics domain

Scott provides the conceptual vocabulary for analyzing how the modern state's categorial and statistical apparatus shapes political life by determining what is visible to the polity's administration. The frame is important for any analysis of how Indigenous polities have been *re-categorized* by settler states — as tribes-without-government, as wards, as enrolled members, as ethnic minorities — and how that re-categorization is itself political work.

The legibility frame also illuminates the politics of refusal (Simpson, Coulthard): refusing to be legible in the settler state's categories is itself a political move. The Lakota refusal to accept the Black Hills compensation refuses the legibility of the relation-to-land as a property relation valued in dollars.
