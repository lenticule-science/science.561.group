## Evidence and interpretation

Reader interrogates the assumption that enslaved people's emotional relationships must have been either freely romantic or entirely coerced and empty of affection. In **“Love and Coercion,” printed p. 484**, Reader analyzes Louisa and Sam Everett's history: their enslaver imposed a relationship, while Louisa later described Sam's kindness and her love for him. Reader's inference is that coercion and later love need not be mutually exclusive. The witness's testimony supports agency within oppressive circumstances without retroactively legitimating the violence of coercion.

Reader's **footnote 116 on p. 484** locates this case in the FWP *Florida Narratives*, p. 128, Library of Congress item \`mesn030\`. This independent citation triangulates the [Everett–Randolph interview](everett-randolph1936.md).

## Limits and counter-reading

The article offers historical interpretation of emotions and a source-critical method, **not** a prevalence estimate of enslaver-directed reproduction. Neither a survivor's later reported love nor Reader's analysis establishes genuine consent at the time of coercion. Read the interview's direct and third-person portions separately. Do not import contemporary psychology categories as if directly measurable from a 1936 recollection of slavery.
