Black feminism is the intellectual tradition that takes the standpoint of Black women as the vantage point from which the interlocking structure of race, class, gender, and sexuality becomes analytically visible. The tradition is not a regional application of feminism to a particular constituency. It is a reconstruction of what feminism, as analysis and as politics, has to be once the standpoint of Black women is taken seriously as analytically primary.

The lineage runs through the practice and writing of Black women in the abolitionist, suffrage, anti-lynching, and labor movements of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries (Sojourner Truth, Ida B. Wells, Anna Julia Cooper, Claudia Jones); through the Black women's club movement; through the foundational period of contemporary Black feminism in the late 1960s and 1970s (the Third World Women's Alliance, the National Black Feminist Organization, the Combahee River Collective); and into the academic and movement work that followed (the Crenshaw–Collins–hooks–Spillers–Davis–Hartman generation and the contemporary writers who extend it).

What unites the tradition is the refusal of single-axis analysis. The mainstream U.S. women's movement of the 1960s and 1970s built a feminism whose paradigm subject was the white middle-class woman; the mainstream Black freedom movement of the same period built a politics whose paradigm subject was the Black man. Neither could describe the experience or analyze the situation of Black women, because neither was structurally equipped to: each was organized around a single axis of oppression and could only treat the other axes as additive. Black feminism's foundational move is the refusal of additivity. The Combahee Collective's 1977 statement names the structure as "interlocking"; Patricia Hill Collins names it the "matrix of domination"; Kimberlé Crenshaw, building from Black feminist legal theory, names the analytic "intersectionality." All three are after the same point: race, class, gender, and sexuality produce conditions together that no single-axis analysis can describe, and the standpoint from which those conditions can be seen is the standpoint of those who live at the intersection.

The tradition is also a methodological reconstruction. Audre Lorde's insistence that "the master's tools will never dismantle the master's house" is not a slogan; it is the argument that the categories and methods inherited from white-dominated theory cannot, on their own, do the analytical work the tradition needs, and that new categories — born of the experience the inherited categories make invisible — have to be developed. Hortense Spillers's "Mama's Baby, Papa's Maybe" is the long-form demonstration of what that reconstruction looks like at the level of grammar itself: the symbolic-political grammar of American kinship is constituted by slavery's ungendering of the captive, and the inherited categories of "woman," "mother," "kinship," and "gender" cannot be applied to Black flesh without remaking what those categories mean. Saidiya Hartman's critical fabulation extends the reconstruction to method: the archive's structural absence of Black women's interiority is not a lack to be filled but a condition to be reckoned with.

And the tradition is a political project. The Combahee Statement frames Black feminist politics not as identity politics in the trivialized sense the term has acquired but as the politics of analyzing one's own conditions of oppression and acting from that analysis — and as the recognition that "if Black women were free, it would mean that everyone else would have to be free since our freedom would necessitate the destruction of all the systems of oppression." bell hooks's framing of "imperialist white-supremacist capitalist patriarchy" is the same move at the level of analytical naming: the integrated system has to be named integrated for the political project to be adequate to it.

## Related subdomains

The subdomain has dense convergences with three siblings under [`critical-theory`](../../../../../../_index.md):

- [`black-radical-tradition`](../../../../../../black-radical-tradition/_index.md). The two subdomains share figures (Davis, Spillers, Hartman) and analytical vocabulary (racial capitalism, the afterlife of slavery, fugitivity). Black feminism and the Black radical tradition are not the same project — Black feminism foregrounds the gendered-sexual constitution of the structure that the Black radical tradition analyzes principally as racial capitalism — but the analytical resources flow in both directions, and several figures sit in both subdomains. The convergence is tracked in the BRT [`questions/`](../../../../../../black-radical-tradition/questions/_index.md) entry on Black feminism.
- [`queer/theories/kyriarchism`](../../../../../../queer/theories/kyriarchism/_index.md). The kyriarchism analysis of interlocking systems of domination is downstream of the Combahee–Collins–hooks framing; this subdomain is the upstream analytical source for that page's central claim.
- [`abolition`](../../../../../../abolition/_index.md). Davis, Gilmore, and the contemporary abolition-feminist literature (Davis, Dent, Meiners, Richie's *Abolition. Feminism. Now.*) sit in both subdomains. The abolitionist horizon and the Black-feminist refusal of single-axis analysis are increasingly articulated together.
