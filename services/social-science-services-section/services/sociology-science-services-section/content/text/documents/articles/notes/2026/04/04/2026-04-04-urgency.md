Urgency is the temporal pressure on action — the quality that makes something need to happen now rather than later. A task is not urgent until its [deadline](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/deadline.md) is near; as the deadline approaches, urgency increases. Urgency is time-dependent: a high-priority task due six months from now has zero urgency today. A low-priority task due tomorrow has high urgency. This is why a [deadline](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/deadline.md) is not just a constraint — it is the engine that converts abstract importance into concrete time-pressure.

Urgency is distinct from importance. Importance is the value or consequence of the outcome; urgency is the time-sensitivity of action. Stephen Covey (1989) formalized this cross in the Eisenhower matrix: do first (urgent + important), schedule (important + not-urgent), delegate (urgent + not-important), drop (neither). The failure mode is treating urgency as a proxy for importance — doing whatever screams loudest, whether or not it matters. A fire alarm is urgent; a house with poor insulation in winter is important. A well-organized dispatch system must separate the two.

Donald Reinertsen (2009) quantifies urgency as cost of delay: the economic cost per unit of time that a task remains incomplete. A task with $1,000 per day cost of delay is more urgent than one with $100 per day, regardless of total importance. Cost of delay makes urgency commensurable — it answers "which of these equally-important tasks should I do first?" by comparing the rate at which value deteriorates. Urgency is the derivative of value with respect to time.

Urgency can be created by four mechanisms: (1) approaching deadlines — the most common. As the deadline nears, the cost of further delay increases exponentially. (2) Decaying opportunities — some options close if not acted on. A seasonal window closes. A negotiation expires. A market moves. (3) Accumulating consequences — problems that worsen over time. Untreated illness becomes critical. Technical debt compounds. Organizational drift hardens. (4) External pressure — imposed urgency from others' timelines. A customer's deadline. A regulator's enforcement action. A political opponent's announcement.

The politics of urgency matter. Paul Virilio (1977) and the anarchist analysis of [speed](../../../../../../../../services/critical-theory-science-services-section/services/anarchism-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/speed.md) show that urgency is not neutral. Imposed urgency compresses deliberation time, which selects for hierarchical decision-making and against consensus-based or democratic processes. Speed is a political structure. When a manager declares "this is urgent," they are not describing a fact — they are imposing a temporal constraint that favors their authority. The ability to resist imposed urgency — to say "this needs more time" — is [temporal autonomy](../../../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/undated/temporal-autonomy.md). Legitimate urgency emerges from real constraints; coerced urgency is a tool of control.

Urgency and [cadence](../../../../../../triage/cadence.md) interact. Periodic [cadences](../../../../../../triage/cadence.md) — sprints, weekly reviews, quarterly planning cycles — create artificial urgency through deadline effects. This is healthy when it prevents drift and forces periodic closure; pathological when it forces premature decisions before sufficient information exists. A weekly review deadline concentrates attention; a sprint deadline that forbids mid-sprint planning changes forces decisions before scope is clear. The question is whether the artificial urgency serves the work or serves the system's need for predictability.

Urgency is often computed as the inverse of days-to-deadline. The dispatch stage uses it to break priority ties — among equally important tasks, the one with the nearest deadline goes first. But urgency should never override the importance distinction: a medium-urgency, high-importance task should not be dispatched before a high-urgency, low-importance task unless the organization has explicitly decided to optimize for deadline-adherence over value-delivery.
