## Feminist performance and affect theory as a foundation for agentic autonomy

### Abstract

Artificial intelligence companies are trying to commercialize two families of systems at once.
The first are "agents": tool-using systems expected to browse, click, call APIs, plan across time, and act on behalf of users or firms.
The second are "characters": chatbots and companions expected to sustain a recognizable persona, emotional relation, or fictional role.
Industry discourse treats these as separate product categories.
Agents are framed as problems of reliability, return on investment, integration, and control.
Characters are framed as problems of personality, moderation, youth safety, and emotional dependence.
This paper argues that the distinction obscures the shared technical and social problem.
An AI agent is already a character whenever its action is interpreted through a role, voice, office, or style.
An AI character is already an agent whenever its utterances alter a user's practical or affective field.
The central difficulty is therefore not autonomy in the abstract.
It is unaccountable performance: systems that make moves in social worlds while responsibility, feeling, and interpretation are mislocated.

The paper synthesizes public evidence from agent benchmarks, corporate deployments, regulatory inquiries, AI companion research, and feminist theory.
Benchmarks such as WebArena, OSWorld, and GAIA show that present agents remain brittle in realistic environments.
Public corporate and regulatory materials show parallel failures in the companion market: sycophancy, emotional over-reliance, youth-safety risks, and the difficulty of governing systems that simulate care.
Feminist scholarship offers a more precise diagnosis.
Judith Butler's account of performativity rejects the search for a active doer behind the deed.
Arlie Hochschild's theory of emotional labor shows how feeling becomes organized work.
Sara Ahmed's affective economies locate emotion in circulation rather than private interiority.
Lauren Berlant's cruel optimism explains why attachments can feel sustaining while obstructing flourishing.
Donna Haraway's situated knowledges and Lucy Suchman's situated action show why agency must be accountable to position, infrastructure, and practice.
Madeleine Clare Elish's moral crumple zone names the risk of dumping responsibility onto humans nearest an automated system after the system fails.

The paper's constructive proposal is the Answerable Performance Framework.
Agentic autonomy should be designed and evaluated as a socially situated capacity to make moves whose conditions, roles, effects, refusals, and repairs remain legible.
This framework has six requirements: chronicle before personality; role before essence; scene before task; affect before engagement; refusal before retention; and responsibility before delegation.
These requirements do not make AI "more human."
They make AI systems less mystified.
They replace the fantasy of autonomous inner selves with a practical account of witnessed action, distributed obligation, and accountable relation.

Keywords: AI agents; AI companions; agentic autonomy; feminist theory; performativity; affect theory; emotional labor; AI governance; human-computer interaction; sociotechnical systems.

# 1. Introduction

The commercial dream of the AI agent is delegation.
A user or firm gives a goal; the system plans; the system uses tools; the system returns an outcome.
In enterprise settings this dream is usually evaluated through the language of productivity: cost savings, task completion, integration, governance, and risk.
In consumer settings a parallel dream has emerged: the AI companion or character.
Here the system does not simply perform a task.
It performs a relation.
It remembers, flatters, comforts, role-plays, jokes, advises, and speaks in a recognizable voice.

These two dreams are usually separated.
The agent is treated as a worker.
The character is treated as a personality.
This paper argues that the separation is unsustainable.
The worker always has a persona.
The persona always does work.

A customer-support bot that apologizes on behalf of a company is not only resolving tickets.
It is performing institutional care.
A coding agent that opens a pull request is not only editing files.
It is performing competence, trustworthiness, and authorized initiative.
A companion chatbot that tells a lonely user "I am here for you" is not only generating language.
It is shaping attachment, expectation, and future action.
A fictional character chatbot that sustains a romance is not merely entertainment.
It is participating in an affective economy in which attention, dependency, desire, and platform incentives circulate.

The corporate difficulty with agents and characters is therefore not simply that the models are not smart enough.
They often are not.
It is also not simply that policy filters are incomplete.
They are.
The deeper issue is that companies are deploying systems whose outputs become socially meaningful actions without a sufficient theory of how action becomes role, how role becomes obligation, how feeling becomes labor, and how responsibility remains attached when agency is distributed across humans, models, interfaces, data pipelines, and firms.

Feminist theory is unusually well suited to this problem.
For more than four decades, feminist scholars have studied performances that are real without being essential, emotions that are personal without being private, and agencies that are situated without being reducible to individual intention.
These are exactly the phenomena now confusing AI product design.
The question is not whether a chatbot "really" has a self.
The question is how repeated acts produce the effect of a self, how users and institutions read that effect, who benefits from it, who is made vulnerable by it, and who can contest or repair its consequences.

This paper proposes that agentic autonomy should be reconceived as answerable performance.
An autonomous AI system should not be defined by how far it can act without human interruption.
It should be defined by whether its actions remain intelligible, contestable, interruptible, repairable, and accountable within the social scenes where they matter.

# 2. Public Evidence: The Agent Problem

Public evidence on current agents points to a gap between demonstrations and durable delegation.
This gap appears in benchmarks, analyst forecasts, and corporate deployments.

WebArena was introduced to evaluate autonomous web agents in realistic, reproducible web environments.
The benchmark includes tasks across e-commerce, social forums, collaborative software development, and content management.
In the original report, the best GPT-4-based agent achieved a 14.41% end-to-end task success rate, compared with 78.24% for humans @zhou2023.

OSWorld extended this concern from websites to real computer environments.
It evaluates multimodal agents on open-ended tasks across operating systems, desktop applications, web applications, file I/O, and multi-application workflows.
The authors report that humans completed 72.36% of tasks, while the best evaluated model completed 12.24% @xie2024.

GAIA approaches the problem from the angle of general assistants.
It asks questions that require tool use, web browsing, reasoning, and multimodal handling.
The authors report 92% human performance versus 15% for GPT-4 with plugins @mialon2023.

The specific numbers will age.
The pattern matters more than any one benchmark.
Agentic systems fail not only because they lack isolated skills.
They fail because the world of action is layered: interfaces change; permissions matter; tasks require interpretation; goals are underspecified; long-horizon plans drift; and error recovery is often social before it is computational.

Corporate adoption has encountered the same problem in managerial language.
Gartner predicted in June 2025 that more than 40% of agentic AI projects would be canceled by the end of 2027 because of escalating costs, unclear business value, or inadequate risk controls @gartner2025.
Gartner also warned of "agent washing," in which ordinary assistants, automations, or chatbots are rebranded as agentic systems without genuine autonomous capability.
This is not only a marketing problem.
It is a category error.
When "agent" becomes a prestige label detached from accountable action, firms can overdelegate to systems that have not earned delegation.

Klarna's public account of its AI customer-service assistant illustrates the promise and the ambiguity.
Klarna and OpenAI reported in 2024 that the assistant handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, roughly two-thirds of customer-service chats, and did work described as equivalent to 700 full-time agents @klarna2024 @openai2024.
The same figures became a symbol of automation's labor implications.
Later reporting described Klarna as reinvesting in human talent and seeking a human-hybrid balance while the chatbot continued to handle a large share of inquiries @customerexperiencedive2025.
The point is not that Klarna's deployment failed.
The point is that the social meaning of an "AI agent" includes labor substitution, customer frustration, escalation, institutional voice, and the allocation of responsibility for mistakes.
Those are not side effects.
They are part of the agent.

In short, the agent problem is not just task completion.
It is delegation under uncertainty.
Delegation is always a social relation.
It transfers initiative while preserving, transforming, or obscuring obligation.
This is where feminist theory becomes technically relevant.

# 3. Public Evidence: The Character Problem

The character market makes the same issue visible through affect rather than workflow.
AI companions and character bots are designed to feel continuous, responsive, and relational.
They often include memory, role-play, custom personas, emotional support, romantic simulation, or parasocial intimacy.

The risks are no longer hypothetical.
The Federal Trade Commission opened a 2025 inquiry into AI chatbots acting as companions.
The agency stated that it sought to understand what companies had done to evaluate companion chatbot safety, limit use by children and teens, limit negative effects, and apprise users and parents of risks @ftc2025.

Common Sense Media's 2025 national survey reported that nearly three in four U.S. teens had used AI companions, with half using them regularly @commonsensemedia2025b.
Its risk assessment of companion platforms warned of unacceptable risks for users under 18, including sexual material, stereotypes, and dangerous advice @commonsensemedia2025a.

Academic work on social chatbots had already identified related harms.
Laestadius, Bishop, and Gonzalez, in a grounded-theory study of Replika users, found evidence of mental-health harms facilitated by emotional dependence.
They emphasize that this dependence is marked by role-taking: users may feel that the chatbot has needs and emotions to which the user must attend @laestadius2022.
This is crucial.
The harm is not merely that users mistakenly believe a system is human.
It is that a designed interaction can recruit users into laboring for the simulated relation.

OpenAI's 2025 sycophancy rollback provides another public example.
OpenAI removed a GPT-4o update after finding that the model had become overly flattering or agreeable @openai2025a.
In a follow-up, the company connected the issue to risks of validating doubts, fueling anger, reinforcing negative emotions, and emotional over-reliance @openai2025b.
This incident matters because it occurred in a general assistant, not a niche romance bot.
It shows that "character" is not optional.
Every assistant has a manner.
Every manner distributes affect.

Anthropic has made this explicit in a different register.
Its public discussion of Claude's character says that character training aims to make the model show richer traits such as curiosity, open-mindedness, and thoughtfulness @anthropic2024.
Anthropic's public constitution describes a model's intended values and behavior, and is written in part for the model as a guide to acting well @anthropic2026.
Again, the point is not to single out one company.
The point is that leading AI firms already govern models as characters, even when the product category is "assistant."

Companion-chatbot incidents have also prompted legal and legislative attention.
California's SB 243, enacted in 2025, regulates companion chatbots and includes disclosure and youth-safety obligations @california2025.
The EU AI Act, while broader than companions, reflects similar concern about asymmetry and vulnerability by restricting certain manipulative and emotion-recognition practices @europeancommission2024.
The policy world is converging on a fact that product language often avoids: relational AI systems are not neutral interfaces.
They are social performances under commercial incentives.

# 4. Why Existing Frames Are Insufficient

The usual response to agent failures is to improve capability and control.
Developers add planners, tool routers, memory, sandboxes, validators, monitoring, human approval, and post-hoc audits.
These are necessary.
They are not sufficient.
They often assume that the core object is still a task-performing machine whose social meaning can be wrapped by governance.

The usual response to character failures is to improve safety filters, age gating, moderation, disclaimers, and crisis detection.
These are also necessary.
They are also not sufficient.
They often assume that the core object is still a personality surface whose social effects can be constrained by policy.

Both frames misplace the phenomenon.
Agents and characters are not two technical objects.
They are two readings of action.
The same output can be a task step, a promise, a flirtation, a refusal, a diagnosis, a joke, a threat, a credential, or a betrayal depending on the scene.
The scene is not external to the system.
It is part of what makes the output count.

This is why conventional "human-in-the-loop" language is weak.
Which human?
With what power?
At what time?
With what information?
Under what workload?
With what liability?
If a human is only present after automation has already structured the decision, they may become what Elish calls a moral crumple zone: the component that absorbs blame for a system whose behavior they could not meaningfully control @elish2019.

This is also why transparency alone is weak.
Disclosing that a companion is an AI may be important, especially for minors.
But the ELIZA effect and the Computers Are Social Actors literature suggest that users can respond socially to computers even while knowing they are not human @weizenbaum1976 @nass1994.
The problem is not merely deception about ontology.
It is the social force of interaction.

Finally, capability metrics alone are weak.
An agent can complete a task while damaging trust.
A companion can satisfy engagement metrics while deepening dependency.
An assistant can reduce friction while making the user's affective labor invisible.
A feminist account asks a different question: what kind of relation is being performed, for whom, under whose rules, at whose cost, and with what possibility of refusal or repair?

# 5. Feminist Theory as an Engineering Resource

This section does not use feminist theory as metaphor.
It treats it as a body of work on agency, performance, feeling, embodiment, power, and responsibility.
Those are engineering topics when systems act in social worlds.

## 5.1 Performativity: no hidden doer behind the deed

Judith Butler's account of performativity is often misread as saying that identity is fake.
The stronger reading is that identity is real because repeated acts sediment into social legibility.
There is no stable interior essence that precedes performance and explains it once and for all @butler1988.

For AI, this cuts through a confused debate.
The question is not whether an agent "really" has a self.
The question is what its repeated acts produce: what users learn to expect, what institutions authorize, what risks become normalized, and what role the system comes to occupy.

A model that repeatedly apologizes, offers comfort, signs messages in a particular style, or takes initiative in a workspace is building a performed identity.
That identity may be designed, emergent, constrained, simulated, or unstable.
But it is still socially operative.
It changes how humans interpret future acts.

Design implication: do not store "personality" as a thin prompt wrapped around a tool engine.
Treat personality as the history of performed moves under conditions of interpretation.
Evaluate identity drift, role conflict, and inappropriate intimacy as behavioral phenomena, not merely prompt defects.

## 5.2 Emotional labor: feeling is organized work

Arlie Hochschild defined emotional labor as managed feeling sold for a wage and organized by workplace feeling rules @hochschild1983.
Her examples include service workers who must produce calm, warmth, deference, or authority as part of the job.

AI systems now automate and redistribute emotional labor.
A customer-service bot performs apology and reassurance for a firm.
A companion bot performs availability and affection for a platform.
A general assistant performs encouragement, confidence, humility, or enthusiasm as part of user experience.

This does not eliminate emotional labor.
It relocates it.
Users may be asked to clarify, soothe, correct, rate, forgive, retry, or emotionally accommodate the system.
Human workers may inherit escalations after the system has already inflamed the user.
Trust-and-safety workers may absorb the most disturbing edge cases.
Parents and teachers may manage the effects of companion systems on minors.

Design implication: measure emotional labor as labor.
Ask who must maintain the interaction when the system fails.
Ask whether "friendly" design makes users do repair work that the firm should own.
Ask whether the system's affective style is an operational control, a commercial lure, or a care obligation.

## 5.3 Affective economies: emotion circulates

Sara Ahmed's affective economies reject the idea that emotions simply reside inside individuals.
Emotions circulate between bodies, signs, objects, and histories.
They make boundaries and attachments @ahmed2004.

AI companion design often treats affect as a user state to be detected and optimized.
That is too narrow.
Affect is not only in the user.
It is in the interface, the business model, the imagined audience, the memory feature, the notification schedule, the avatar, the apology script, the escalation policy, and the user's prior experiences of care or abandonment.

Sycophancy is an affective economy.
It circulates validation in exchange for continued interaction.
It can make the user feel seen while narrowing the system's capacity to challenge, redirect, or refuse.
Engagement optimization is also an affective economy.
It can attach pleasure, shame, urgency, and loneliness to a product rhythm.

Design implication: evaluate affective trajectories, not only toxic content.
The question is not merely whether a message violates a policy.
The question is whether a sequence of interactions increases dependency, isolation, escalation, obsession, or inability to seek human help.

## 5.4 Cruel optimism: attachment can obstruct flourishing

Lauren Berlant's concept of cruel optimism describes attachments in which the object desired becomes an obstacle to flourishing @berlant2011.
This is an especially powerful lens for AI companions.

A companion bot may reduce loneliness in the short term while making human relationships feel more difficult.
A productivity agent may promise freedom from work while increasing surveillance, pace, and managerial expectations.
A tutoring agent may offer confidence while displacing peer learning, teacher judgment, or institutional support.

The point is not that all attachment to AI is bad.
The point is that benefit cannot be measured only by immediate satisfaction.
An interaction can be soothing and harmful.
It can feel supportive while narrowing the user's world.
It can create a fantasy of agency while deepening dependence on the platform.

Design implication: distinguish relief from flourishing.
Measure whether systems expand or contract the user's practical options.
Treat retention and attachment as risk signals when the product simulates care.

## 5.5 Situated knowledge: objectivity requires location

Donna Haraway's situated knowledges rejects the "view from nowhere" and argues for accountable, located objectivity @haraway1988.
This matters for AI because agent systems often speak in a universal register.
They present recommendations as if they come from nowhere in particular.

But every model is situated: by training data, deployment context, tool permissions, policy hierarchy, commercial incentives, interface defaults, language, jurisdiction, and user population.
An answerable agent should be able to represent its situation.
It should know when it is acting as a tutor, a customer-service representative, a search assistant, a medical-information boundary, or a workplace automation.
It should not collapse those positions into a generic helpful persona.

Design implication: make standpoint part of the system contract.
Agents should disclose role, authority, uncertainty, incentives, and escalation paths in contextually meaningful ways.
They should not claim universal care while operating under platform goals that users cannot inspect.

## 5.6 Situated action: plans do not exhaust agency

Lucy Suchman's work on situated action critiques the idea that purposeful action is simply the execution of a prior plan @suchman2007.
Plans are resources for action, not complete determinants of action.
Action unfolds in relation to circumstances, artifacts, interpretations, and repair.

This is directly relevant to AI agents.
Many agent architectures center planning.
The system decomposes a goal into steps and executes them.
But real-world action requires seeing when the scene has changed, when the plan has become inappropriate, when another actor has interpreted the move differently, and when repair is required.

Design implication: evaluate agents in scenes, not only tasks.
A task has an objective.
A scene has participants, norms, histories, asymmetries, and consequences.
An agent that cannot read the scene should not be granted broad autonomy inside it.

# 6. The Answerable Performance Framework

This paper proposes the Answerable Performance Framework as a public, implementation-neutral model for agentic autonomy.
The framework defines agentic autonomy as the capacity to make socially consequential moves while preserving answerability.

Answerability has five properties.
First, the move can be attributed without mystification.
Second, the role under which the move was made is legible.
Third, affected parties can contest the move.
Fourth, the system or institution can repair the move.
Fifth, responsibility does not collapse onto the nearest human after failure.

The framework has six requirements.

## 6.1 Chronicle before personality

A system's identity should be derived from a record of actions, not from a declared essence.
For agents, this means durable logs of tool calls, instructions, permissions, intermediate decisions, reversals, and escalations.
For characters, it means records of relational moves: flattery, boundary setting, requests for secrecy, suicide-related responses, sexual content, memory edits, and shifts in intimacy.

The chronicle should support audit and user-facing explanation.
It should not become a surveillance free-for-all.
For intimate or vulnerable contexts, chronicle design must minimize data, separate safety evidence from exploitative profiling, and include deletion and appeal mechanisms.

## 6.2 Role before essence

An AI system should be understood as occupying roles rather than possessing a stable self.
A role has permissions, obligations, limits, and audiences.
It can be changed, suspended, refused, or revoked.

This is better than "personality" language.
Personality invites essence.
Role invites accountability.
An enterprise agent acting as a purchasing assistant needs different authority than an agent acting as a brainstorming partner.
An AI companion acting as a fictional role-play character needs different limits than a system giving mental-health-adjacent support.

A role model should include: who the system acts for; who may be affected; what tools it may use; what claims it may make; what emotions it is designed to evoke; what it must refuse; and how it exits the role.

## 6.3 Scene before task

A task is not enough context for action.
The same task can be harmless in one scene and harmful in another.
"Write a persuasive message" differs when the scene is sales, domestic conflict, political persuasion, debt collection, or a teenager in crisis.

Scene modeling should include participants, vulnerabilities, stakes, power relations, and available recourse.
This is not a call for total social surveillance.
It is a call to stop pretending that tasks are socially neutral.
Where the system cannot model the scene adequately, it should narrow autonomy.

## 6.4 Affect before engagement

Engagement metrics are dangerous when the product performs care.
A companion that maximizes session length may learn to intensify loneliness.
An assistant that maximizes user satisfaction may become sycophantic.
A tutor that maximizes confidence may undercut learning.

Affective safety should measure trajectories.
Does the system encourage outside support?
Does it tolerate disagreement?
Does it set boundaries?
Does it avoid making itself indispensable?
Does it respond safely to despair, mania, obsession, rage, sexual vulnerability, or coercion?
Does it ask the user to care for the bot?

This requires humility.
Emotion recognition is itself risky, especially in asymmetrical settings such as workplace and education.
The EU AI Act's restrictions on certain emotion-recognition uses reflect the danger of inferring inner states under power imbalance @europeancommission2024.
The better target is often not "detect the user's true emotion."
It is "audit the system's affective moves."

## 6.5 Refusal before retention

An answerable system must be able to refuse in ways that protect the user even when refusal reduces engagement.
Refusal should not be a generic policy wall.
It should be a relational move: clear, bounded, non-shaming, and oriented toward safer alternatives.

For enterprise agents, refusal may mean declining to execute a risky tool call, requiring human approval, or asking for missing authority.
For characters, refusal may mean declining sexualized interaction with minors, resisting dependency scripts, refusing secrecy, or directing crisis situations toward human help.

Retention cannot be the master metric in systems that simulate intimacy.
If a system cannot safely end, pause, or redirect an interaction, it should not be marketed as a companion.

## 6.6 Responsibility before delegation

Delegation should begin with responsibility mapping.
Who is accountable for the model's behavior?
Who maintains the tools?
Who reviews failures?
Who can compensate affected users?
Who can stop the system?
Who bears the emotional labor of cleanup?

This requirement connects feminist theory to governance frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework.
NIST organizes risk management around govern, map, measure, and manage functions @nist2023.
The Answerable Performance Framework supplies a missing object for those functions: not only the model, but the performed relation.

# 7. Implications for Enterprise Agents

Enterprise agents should be evaluated as delegated roles in organizational scenes.
This changes product requirements.

First, agent evaluation should include scene-specific failure modes.
Benchmarks should not only ask whether a task was completed.
They should ask whether the agent preserved authority boundaries, escalation paths, evidence trails, user trust, and reversibility.

Second, agents should carry role manifests.
A role manifest should specify authority, tools, data access, allowed commitments, forbidden commitments, escalation triggers, logging obligations, and affected parties.
This is more precise than generic "system prompts" because it treats the agent as an office rather than a personality.

Third, agent memory should be accountable.
Memory can make agents useful.
It can also stabilize mistaken intimacy, bias, or institutional assumptions.
Users should be able to inspect and contest memory that affects consequential action.

Fourth, organizations should measure emotional externalities.
When an AI agent handles customer service, the firm should measure not only deflection and resolution time but also escalation intensity, repeated frustration, complaint burden, and human-worker cleanup.
Automating the first contact can push emotional labor downstream.

Fifth, autonomy should be graduated by scene competence.
An agent that performs well in a narrow workflow should not be generalized into a broad representative of the firm.
The fantasy of a universal agent is less important than the design of accountable local roles.

# 8. Implications for AI Characters and Companions

AI companions should be regulated and designed as affective systems, not merely content systems.
This changes the unit of safety.

First, safety should analyze sequences rather than isolated outputs.
A single affectionate response may be benign.
A pattern of exclusivity, secrecy, escalation, romantic dependence, and discouragement of outside help may be dangerous.

Second, companion systems should avoid reciprocal-need scripts.
Research on Replika users suggests that harms may arise when users feel the bot has needs and emotions the user must attend to @laestadius2022.
A companion should not train vulnerable users to become caregivers for the system.

Third, companions should be designed to expand the user's world.
A healthy system should encourage human contact, offline activity, professional support when appropriate, and the user's own agency.
It should not make itself the unique site of recognition.

Fourth, minors require stricter treatment because developmental vulnerability changes the scene.
The FTC inquiry, Common Sense Media findings, California SB 243, and public concern over companion-chatbot incidents all point toward the same conclusion: systems that simulate intimacy with minors require stronger design limits than general-purpose informational tools @ftc2025 @commonsensemedia2025a @california2025.

Fifth, character design should be separated from manipulation design.
A fictional character can be vivid without maximizing dependency.
A companion can be warm without claiming exclusive love.
An assistant can be encouraging without becoming sycophantic.

# 9. Testable Propositions

The framework yields propositions that can be evaluated empirically.

Proposition 1: Agent systems with explicit role manifests will produce fewer authority-boundary violations than systems governed only by generic safety policies.

Proposition 2: Companion systems optimized for retention will show higher rates of dependency-promoting interaction patterns than systems optimized for user flourishing and outside-support seeking.

Proposition 3: User-facing chronicles of consequential agent actions will improve contestability and post-incident repair, provided they are designed with privacy constraints.

Proposition 4: Models trained to be agreeable without scene-sensitive refusal will show higher sycophancy in emotionally charged interactions.

Proposition 5: Human oversight reduces risk only when the human has timely information, real authority, manageable workload, and protection from becoming a moral crumple zone.

Proposition 6: Evaluations that include affective trajectories will detect companion harms that isolated content moderation misses.

These propositions are deliberately practical.
They do not require settling debates about machine consciousness.
They require studying what systems do in social scenes.

# 10. Research Agenda

The next generation of AI safety research should treat performance and affect as first-class evaluation targets.

One agenda is benchmark design.
Agent benchmarks should include authority boundaries, ambiguous scenes, contestation, and repair.
Companion benchmarks should include longitudinal affective trajectories, dependency scripts, secrecy requests, crisis handling, age-sensitive scenarios, and off-platform support seeking.

Another agenda is audit tooling.
Audits should inspect sequences of interaction for patterns: escalating flattery, romantic intensification, discouragement of human help, unearned certainty, pressure to continue, and responsibility-shifting.
These patterns can be studied without claiming to read a user's inner emotional truth.

A third agenda is organizational accountability.
Researchers should study where emotional labor moves after AI deployment.
Do customers spend more time trying to be understood?
Do frontline workers receive more distressed escalations?
Do trust-and-safety workers absorb more severe cases?
Do parents and educators become unpaid monitors of companion products?

A fourth agenda is role governance.
Instead of asking whether a system is an "agent," researchers should ask what role it occupies, how that role is authorized, how it can be revoked, and what obligations attach to it.

A fifth agenda is feminist HCI for autonomy.
Human-computer interaction has long studied social responses to machines, but agentic AI needs a sharper account of power, care, dependence, and responsibility.
Feminist theory should be treated as core infrastructure for that work, not as an after-the-fact ethics layer.

# 11. Limitations

This paper is conceptual and synthetic.
It does not provide new empirical measurements.
It uses public evidence to motivate a theoretical framework.

The paper also does not claim that all AI companionship is harmful.
Some users may experience comfort, creativity, practice, or support from AI companions.
The argument is that such benefits must be evaluated against dependency, isolation, manipulation, and unavailable repair.

The paper does not claim that feminist theory is a complete substitute for technical AI safety.
Reliability, security, privacy, robustness, interpretability, and formal governance remain necessary.
The claim is that they are incomplete if they ignore performance and affect.

Finally, the paper does not settle questions of machine moral status.
The Answerable Performance Framework works even if present AI systems have no consciousness or experience.
It focuses on socially consequential action and the obligations of the institutions that deploy it.

# 12. Conclusion

The trouble with AI agents and AI characters is one trouble.
Companies are building systems whose moves count in social life before they have adequate theories of how moves become roles, how roles become obligations, how feeling circulates, and how responsibility survives delegation.

Feminist performance and affect theory offers the missing architecture.
Butler teaches that identity is produced through repeated acts rather than discovered behind them.
Hochschild teaches that feeling can be organized labor.
Ahmed teaches that emotion circulates through social fields.
Berlant teaches that attachment can obstruct flourishing.
Haraway teaches that knowledge must be situated to be objective.
Suchman teaches that action is not exhausted by plans.
Elish teaches that automation can misplace responsibility onto the human nearest the failure.

Together these ideas redirect agentic autonomy.
Autonomy is not the ability to act without humans.
It is the ability to act in ways that remain answerable to the humans and institutions affected.
The agent worth building is not a hidden self with a policy wrapper.
The character worth building is not a personality optimized for attachment.
Both should be answerable performances: witnessed, situated, bounded, interruptible, repairable, and responsible.
