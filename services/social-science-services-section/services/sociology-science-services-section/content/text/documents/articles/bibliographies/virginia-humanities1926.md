# Virginia Humanities / Encyclopedia Virginia (2020) — Racial Integrity Laws (1924–1930)

**Publication:** Encyclopedia Virginia historical synthesis. 
**Source:** https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/racial-integrity-laws-1924-1930/.
**Read status (2026-10-17):** Publisher-authored documentary historical synthesis read, including 1926 bill introduction and House amendment; original 1926 legislative journals not independently consulted.

## Contribution to the commissioned papers

Norris introduced a 1926 revision excluding the 1924 Pocahontas exception; House amended the proposed bill to permit one-eighth Indian ancestry versus 1924 one-sixteenth, a proposed broadening motivated by a desire not to exclude many recognized white Virginians. Evidence for negotiations over the boundaries of socially protected whiteness.

## Provenance and interpretive limit

Do NOT represent the 1926 amendment as a subsequently enacted final law without primary legislative comparison. An enacted 1924 statute, an introduced 1926 bill, and a House-amended bill are distinct legal states.

## Commission-specific source audit (2026-10-17)

**Why this source is essential:** Failed revision as a decisive negative case.

**Recoverable evidence and locators:** Virginia Humanities gives an unusually useful legislative sequence: a January 14, 1926 proposal by Norris to erase the ancestry exception; on February 12 the House amended it to permit up to **one-eighth Indigenous ancestry**; on March 5 the House passed the modified revision **52–18**; on March 12 the Senate voted **20–9 to postpone it indefinitely**. Thus the expanded criterion did **not** become an enacted change under this bill. The resistance reportedly reflected concern that as many as 20,000 whites had some Indigenous ancestry.

**Inferential boundary / competing evidence:** A manuscript stating Virginia *enacted* a broader one-eighth exception in 1926 would be wrong. The case still supports a contest between claimed perfect purity and protecting elite identities, but the actually governing legal standard stayed at the prior statute absent later legislation. Original bill texts, newspapers and Indigenous testimony remain important primary checks.

The commissioning papers cite the [23-claim source crosswalk](bibliography-and-evidence-crosswalk-2026-10-17.md); this audit records a source's contribution without upgrading a limited read to full-text verification.

## Enacted 1930 statute and Pamunkey opposition

A separate original statute was read in full: [Virginia Code §67 (1930)](virginia-code1930.md), printed at page 26 of the 1930 Code. The new law recognized certain Indian identities only under restrictive blood-quantum claims and a **state reservation-domicile** exception while otherwise classifying persons with any ascertainable African ancestry as “colored.” It was actually **enacted on March 4, 1930**, unlike the House's proposed 1926 broadening to 1/8 Indigenous ancestry, **postponed indefinitely by the Senate**. The [scholarly Cook biography](cook-george-major1930.md) records Pamunkey chief George Major Cook's objection that the reservation condition excluded Pamunkey and Mattaponi people who lived elsewhere; his original 1930 legislative testimony remains to be independently recovered. Original law and Indigenous resistance are not the same kind of source: the latter has a biography-mediated quotation.
