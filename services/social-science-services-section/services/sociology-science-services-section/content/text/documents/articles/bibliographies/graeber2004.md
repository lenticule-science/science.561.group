## Annotation

A short, deliberately essayistic argument that anthropology is the discipline best positioned to take anarchism seriously as social theory — because anthropologists have studied at length the many human societies that have organized themselves without a state, and because the ethnographic record refutes the assumption that the state is necessary for social order, complex production, or ordered social life.

Graeber's substantive moves: the state is one historical form, not a universal precondition. The ethnographic record of "stateless" societies (Pierre Clastres's reading of Amazonian groups; the analysis of Madagascar; Graeber's own Madagascar fieldwork) shows societies actively organized to *prevent* the emergence of state-like authority, not societies merely lacking one. Anarchism as political program has been better at practice than at theory; anthropology can help by giving the practice its missing theoretical vocabulary.

The book's later expansion is *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011) and, posthumously, *The Dawn of Everything* (2021, with David Wengrow). All three together form Graeber's reframing of the deep history of human social organization away from the state-as-necessary-development picture.

The pairing with [Kropotkin 1902](kropotkin1902.md) — biological-evolutionary argument — and with the contemporary mutual-aid practice of [`anarchism/theories/disaster-response/`](../research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/social-sciences/sociology/archive/references/theories/critical-theory/theories/anarchism/theories/disaster-response) — present-day organizing — gives the anarchism subdomain an unusually wide temporal range.
