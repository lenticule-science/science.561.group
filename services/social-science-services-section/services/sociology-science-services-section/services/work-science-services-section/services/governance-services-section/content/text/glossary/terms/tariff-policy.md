Tariff policy is a state's choice of which imported goods to tax at the border, at what rates, under which legal authorities and to what ends. The ends differ and are often mixed: revenue; protection of a domestic industry from foreign competitors; leverage in a negotiation, where the tariff is a threat or a concession to trade away; and sanction, where the tariff punishes a practice (forced labor, currency policy, technology transfer) rather than a product.

In the United States the power to lay duties belongs to Congress, which has delegated parts of it to the President by statute, so a US tariff is always read with the authority it was imposed under, because the authority sets how long it can last, what process it needs and whether a court can strike it. As of 2026-09-26 the live authorities on Chinese goods are Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 (the China lists of 2018–2019, at 7.5–100%, and the forced-labor action of 2026-07-24 on 60 economies, 12.5% for China), and Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 (steel, aluminum, copper, autos, some semiconductors, patented pharmaceuticals). The emergency-powers tariffs of 2025 ended when the Supreme Court held on 2026-02-20 that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act authorizes none, and the 150-day Section 122 surcharge that bridged them lapsed on 2026-07-24. Beside the rates sits the question of which shipments pay at all: the United States ended duty-free entry for parcels under $800 for Chinese goods in May 2025 and for all goods in August 2025.

A trading partner's tariff policy is the other half: China's retaliatory tariffs moved Wisconsin ginseng's duty into China from 15% to a peak of 147% in May 2025 and to about 33% by September 2026.
