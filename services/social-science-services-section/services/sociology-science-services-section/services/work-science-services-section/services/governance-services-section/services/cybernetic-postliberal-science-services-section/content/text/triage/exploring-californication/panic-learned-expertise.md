## Abstract

This paper names and analyzes a recurring formation in contemporary platformed knowledge production: panic-learned expertise.
Panic-learned expertise appears when an already-authorized speaker, faced with a crisis that threatens their own conditions of life, rapidly learns enough about a new domain to perform expertise before the slower practices of disciplinary formation, situated accountability, and collective verification can take hold.
The performance is usually sincere.
It is often motivated by real fear, real care, and real intellectual labor.
Its danger lies elsewhere: in the conversion of unfinished learning into affectively satisfying reassurance.

Within the analytic of californication, panic-learned expertise is a downstream effect of the neurotic platformal intellectual.
Californication formats structural crisis as personally manageable affect.
Zen fascism authorizes the calm, regulated subject as ethically and epistemically credible.
Industrial intellectualism converts thought into genre-compliant products.
Together, these mechanisms create a subject who is pressured to process crisis publicly as knowledge, and an audience trained to receive the feeling of understanding as relief from the same crisis.
The result is not simply misinformation.
It is bad knowledge in a stronger sense: knowledge whose social function is to soothe the conditions that would otherwise demand collective reorientation.

## I. The Problem

In periods of acute crisis, audiences often turn to experts.
This is sensible.
Pandemics, ecological disasters, platform collapses, wars, housing shocks, and waves of state repression all generate situations in which ordinary practical judgment is insufficient.
People need concepts, histories, models, causal accounts, and instructions.
They need to know what is happening, what matters, what can be ignored, what must be done now, and what must be prepared for later.

But contemporary platforms do not simply connect audiences to expertise.
They produce expertise as a visible performance under conditions of urgency.
The platform rewards rapid orientation, emotionally legible confidence, and genre-compliant clarity.
The audience rewards the person who can make crisis feel interpretable without making the audience feel abandoned inside the full weight of what interpretation would require.
The speaker is therefore selected not only for knowledge, but for their capacity to make knowledge feel like relief.

This produces a distinct figure: the person who becomes an expert because they are panicked.
They were not formed by the field they now explain.
They were not accountable to the communities whose knowledge they now circulate.
They did not acquire their concepts through long participation in the practices that would teach where the concepts fail, who they harm, and what they cannot license.
Instead, a crisis entered their life.
The crisis threatened their health, class position, professional identity, children, housing, reputation, imagined future, or political self-concept.
They began reading, watching, interviewing, searching, annotating, synthesizing, and posting.
The learning was real.
The panic was real.
The performance of expertise emerged from their effort to survive the panic.

Panic-learned expertise is therefore not reducible to grift, ignorance, or bad faith.
Those exist, but they are not the core formation.
The more important case is the sincere one: the person who learns intensely because they are afraid, and whose fear gives their learning an urgency that audiences mistake for authority.
They do not merely say "I know."
They demonstrate the affective signs by which contemporary platforms recognize knowing: speed, composure, references, explanatory compression, a legible enemy, a practical takeaway, and an invitation to feel steadier after receiving the account.

The central claim of this paper is that panic-learned expertise functions as an affective stabilizer under californication.
It soothes audiences against the same crises that produced the speaker's panic, but it does so through knowledge that has been prematurely closed.
The audience receives orientation before inquiry has ripened into understanding.
The speaker receives authority before accountability has caught up with circulation.
The crisis remains structurally unresolved, but the loop has generated a metabolizable form: analysis as reassurance.

## II. Conceptual Frame: From Californication to Panic-Learned Expertise

[Californication](../../californication.md) names the process by which liberal governance operationalizes structural contradiction as affectively manageable content.
It does not need subjects to deny crisis.
It needs them to experience crisis in forms that return them to participation.
Loss becomes reinvention.
Collapse becomes healing.
Confusion becomes the need to stay informed.
Structural abandonment becomes a personal practice of resilience.

The existing account of [genre calibration under californication](./genre-calibration-under-californication.md) shows how crisis becomes a story whose resolution is internal to the subject.
The healing-era post, the burnout confession, the therapeutic pivot, and the personal-growth arc all convert structural conditions into biographical narrative.
Panic-learned expertise extends that argument from the suffering subject to the knowing subject.
Here, the subject does not only narrate their wound.
They narrate their learning.
Their crisis becomes the scene of their epistemic conversion.

The related lesson on [subjects under californication](../../content/subjects-under-californication/subjects-under-californication.md) gives the immediate subject-form: the neurotic platformal intellectual.
The neurotic platformal intellectual is not merely an intellectual who uses a platform.
They are platformal because the platform structures their intellectual labor.
They think in threads, videos, newsletters, podcasts, explainers, and timely interventions.
They evaluate thought through engagement, legibility, and perceived usefulness.
They are neurotic because legitimacy must be continuously re-demonstrated under conditions where no demonstration is final.

Panic-learned expertise is one recurring output of that subject position.
The neurotic platformal intellectual confronts a crisis that exceeds their settled domain.
The platform still asks them to speak.
Their audience still asks them to make sense.
Silence threatens relevance; uncertainty threatens authority; panic threatens the calm affect by which zen fascism authorizes credibility.
They therefore learn in public, but under a genre that cannot fully admit learning as unfinished.
The content must arrive already shaped as expertise.

This is where zen fascism matters.
Zen fascism is the affective regime in which detachment from crisis functions as ethical credential and distress registers as pathology.
The calm speaker appears more reliable than the panicked one.
The centered explainer appears to have processed what the audience is still struggling to metabolize.
Under this regime, the performance of composure is not decorative.
It is part of the evidence.
The audience reads serenity as epistemic maturity.
The expert seems expert because they can explain the crisis without being visibly destroyed by it.

Industrial intellectualism completes the circuit.
Knowledge is converted into narrative product: the explainer thread, the "what you need to know" video, the rapid-response essay, the podcast episode, the emergency reading list, the course, the webinar, the subscriber-only briefing.
The product is evaluated by genre satisfaction rather than by the slower tests of material correspondence.
Does it answer the felt question?
Does it produce a coherent arc?
Does it leave the audience feeling less alone, less foolish, less afraid, and more correctly oriented?
If so, it circulates.

Panic-learned expertise is thus a californicated knowledge form.
The speaker's structural fear is formatted as a personal epistemic journey.
The audience's structural fear is formatted as relief at receiving the journey's result.
The platform records the relief as engagement.
The engagement authorizes the speaker.
The speaker keeps learning, explaining, and stabilizing the audience, often while remaining caught inside the crisis that made them speak.

## III. What Makes It Panic-Learned

Not all urgent expertise is panic-learned expertise.
Emergency medicine, disaster response, labor organizing, abolitionist defense work, epidemiology, climate science, and many other domains require rapid judgment under pressure.
The difference is not speed alone.
The difference is the relation between fear, formation, and authority.

Panic-learned expertise has five features.

First, the crisis is personally implicating.
The speaker is not learning from detached curiosity.
They are learning because something in their world has become unstable.
The pandemic threatens their body or family.
Wildfire smoke threatens their city.
AI threatens their profession.
State repression threatens their movement.
Transphobic policy threatens their community.
Housing collapse threatens their class position.
The crisis enters as an existential demand.
This matters because panic gives learning its intensity and its distortion.
The learner is searching for usable orientation, not only for truth.

Second, prior authority is transferable.
The speaker already possesses some form of credibility: academic prestige, platform following, professional status, stylistic confidence, moral standing, technical fluency, or proximity to another recognized domain.
This prior authority allows them to cross into a new field faster than accountability can follow.
A person known for cultural criticism can become a pandemic explainer.
A technologist can become a labor theorist.
A therapist can become a geopolitical interpreter.
A climate communicator can become an expert on supply chains, migration, public health, and fascism in the same week.
Sometimes the transposition is productive.
Often it is underdisciplined.

Third, learning occurs under publication pressure.
The platformal intellectual does not learn first and publish later.
They learn while publishing.
The audience's questions shape what is learned.
The platform's metrics shape which parts of the learning appear to matter.
The desire to remain useful generates a cadence in which uncertainty must be continuously processed into content.
This cadence selects for the premature synthesis: the point at which the speaker can make the crisis feel legible enough to post.

Fourth, the product must soothe.
The audience does not only want facts.
It wants relief from epistemic panic.
It wants someone to say: here is the pattern, here is why you feel disoriented, here is what the bad actors are doing, here is what the good people know, here is what you can do today, here is why your fear is meaningful and manageable.
The product can be grim, angry, or catastrophic and still soothe.
Doom can soothe when it gives shape to dread.
Rage can soothe when it gives the audience an object.
Complexity can soothe when it is compressed into a map.

Fifth, the knowledge remains weakly accountable to the people most exposed to the crisis.
This is the most serious feature.
Panic-learned expertise often routes around situated knowledge.
It cites, summarizes, interviews, and translates, but it does not necessarily submit itself to the people whose lives have long been organized by the crisis.
The newly panicked learner may discover a condition that others have endured for decades, then become authorized to explain it because their discovery is legible to the audience.
The crisis becomes real when it reaches the speaker's class, profession, demographic, or affective world.
Their panic becomes the medium through which older knowledge is made publicly important.

These features produce a distinctive epistemic problem.
The speaker may know more than the audience.
They may also know much less than the people whose knowledge they are displacing.
Their authority is therefore relationally unstable.
They are above the audience, beside the archive, and often in front of the communities whose expertise they have only begun to encounter.
The platform collapses these relations into one visible status: expert.

## IV. Performance: The Signs by Which Panic Becomes Expertise

Panic-learned expertise becomes recognizable through performance.
This does not mean it is fake.
Following Erving Goffman's broad insight, performance is not the opposite of reality.
It is the social form through which reality becomes legible in a situation.
Expertise must always be performed in some sense: through method, restraint, vocabulary, citation, uncertainty management, and accountability to standards.
The question is which performance signs a given institution rewards.

Platforms reward signs that are close to expertise but not identical with it.

The first sign is fluency.
The panic-learner acquires names, acronyms, timelines, key studies, canonical phrases, and enemy formations.
Fluency gives the audience the sound of disciplinary competence.
It also allows the speaker to move quickly across the topic, producing the sensation that the field has been surveyed.
But fluency is not judgment.
Knowing the vocabulary of aerosol transmission, settler colonialism, machine learning, counterinsurgency, or monetary policy is not the same as knowing how claims in those domains are tested, bounded, and revised.

The second sign is synthesis.
The platformal intellectual is valued for connecting domains.
They show how the pandemic connects to labor, how climate connects to insurance, how AI connects to enclosure, how policing connects to urban development, how wellness connects to fascism.
This synthetic capacity can be genuinely powerful.
It is also where panic-learned expertise becomes dangerous, because synthesis can outrun warranted relation.
The more satisfying the connection, the easier it is to mistake narrative coherence for explanatory adequacy.

The third sign is affective calibration.
The speaker knows how to feel in public.
They can express alarm without seeming uncontrolled, grief without becoming illegible, anger without losing polish, intimacy without surrendering authority.
They model the appropriate response to crisis.
This is where zen fascism appears inside knowledge production: the regulated affect of the expert becomes part of the lesson.
The audience learns not only what to think but how to inhabit the crisis.

The fourth sign is citation density.
Citations, links, screenshots, footnotes, and reading lists create the visible texture of research.
They may be useful.
They may also become what "citing for containment" names: a way of stabilizing an interpretation rather than submitting the interpretation to the cited work's demands.
The panic-learner cites quickly because the platform asks for receipts.
But receipts are not the same as accountability.
They prove that the speaker touched sources, not that the sources have reorganized the speaker's frame.

The fifth sign is actionable closure.
The content ends with a posture, instruction, purchase, donation, habit, slogan, reading list, or next step.
Closure is essential to reassurance.
The audience must be left with something to do or some reason why doing little is itself understandable.
This closure often arrives before the situation warrants closure.
The crisis is ongoing.
The field is unsettled.
The evidence is partial.
The affected communities disagree.
But the genre requires an ending.

Together these signs produce the feeling of expertise.
The feeling is not trivial.
Audiences use it to regulate their own panic.
They may share the content not because they have evaluated it, but because it made evaluation feel less necessary.
It sounded right.
It named the fear.
It knew the references.
It offered a stance.
It let them breathe.

## V. Audience Relief and the Social Function of Bad Information

The phrase "bad information" can be too narrow.
It suggests factual error: a false claim, a misread chart, a fabricated citation, a misleading statistic.
Those matter.
But panic-learned expertise often works even when many of its claims are individually true.
Its badness lies in its organization.

Bad information, in this formation, is information arranged to perform reassurance before it performs accountability.
It may contain true facts, valid concepts, and useful warnings.
Yet the arrangement teaches the audience to experience relief at the wrong level.
The audience comes away feeling that the crisis has been understood because it has been narrated; that it has been confronted because it has been named; that they are less vulnerable because they are less confused.
The movement from confusion to coherence is treated as a political or practical gain even when no material capacity has been built.

This is coherent confusion in epistemic form.
The audience remains confused about the situation, but coherent about the story of their confusion.
They know which genre they are in.
They know who the villains are.
They know which affects are appropriate.
They know which phrases mark seriousness.
They know which experts to follow.
They know how to recognize themselves as informed.
The crisis persists, but the audience has acquired a stabilized relation to it.

This is soothing.
It is also politically consequential.
An audience that has been soothed by premature expertise may be less likely to seek slower, less gratifying knowledge.
They may be less able to hear affected people whose accounts do not arrive in the polished genre of platform expertise.
They may mistake the newly panicked speaker's discovery for the beginning of the issue itself.
They may reproduce bad strategy because the content taught them what a meaningful response feels like, not what the situation materially requires.

The Stop Cop City analysis in "Citing for containment" offers one version of this pattern.
There, theory-rich narration gives the feeling that repression, arrest, and movement harm have been processed into abolitionist meaning.
The issue is not simply that citations are wrong.
It is that the citational form stabilizes a story in which harm becomes evidence of coherence.
Once this story circulates, it guides future action.
People learn what struggle is supposed to look like by receiving an account that made prior harm feel meaningful.

Panic-learned expertise works similarly in other domains.
The pandemic explainer who discovered public health because their own family became vulnerable may give audiences a map that is more therapeutic than epidemiological.
The AI commentator who discovered labor history because their profession became automatable may give audiences a theory of capital organized around the anxieties of the professional class.
The climate influencer who discovered disability politics through wildfire smoke may explain interdependence in ways that erase disabled organizers who built the concept under conditions of abandonment.
The new authoritarianism expert may discover fascism when institutions they trusted begin to threaten people like them, then explain fascism as the loss of liberal normalcy rather than as a structure long experienced by targeted communities.

In each case, panic opens a door to learning.
The problem is that the platform converts the threshold into a podium.

## VI. Why This Is Not Anti-Expertise

The critique of panic-learned expertise must not collapse into anti-intellectualism.
Crises do require expertise.
Some people really do know more.
Rapid synthesis can save lives.
Public explanation can distribute protective knowledge.
The absence of trusted interpreters can leave audiences vulnerable to conspiracism, state propaganda, grift, and despair.

The problem is not expertise.
The problem is expertise unmoored from the social conditions that make expertise reliable.
Reliable expertise is not merely a property of a person.
It is a relation among training, practice, evidence, peer contestation, institutional memory, situated stakes, and accountability to consequences.
Harry Collins and Robert Evans distinguish between forms of contributory expertise, interactional expertise, and other ways people can speak meaningfully about domains without being full practitioners.
That distinction matters here.
The panic-learner often acquires interactional fluency and performs it as contributory authority.
They can talk the language of the field before they can responsibly intervene in the field.

The Dreyfus model of skill acquisition is also useful.
Novices and advanced beginners rely on explicit rules and visible features.
Experts perceive situations through embodied pattern recognition formed by long practice.
Panic-learned expertise often mistakes advanced-beginner intensity for expert judgment.
The speaker knows enough rules to explain the field attractively, but not enough exceptions to know when the explanation fails.
The audience, lacking even that fluency, experiences the explanation as expertise.

This is why the most dangerous panic-learned expert is not always the least informed.
The most dangerous figure is often the person who is informed enough to be compelling but not formed enough to be careful.
They can defeat simpler errors.
They can cite better sources than obvious grifters.
They can position themselves against misinformation.
They can say "the situation is complex" while still reducing the complexity to the genre their audience needs.
Their authority grows because they are better than the worst alternatives.

The critique therefore asks for more expertise, not less: deeper formation, stronger accountability, clearer domain boundaries, more deference to situated practitioners, and more willingness to leave audiences without closure when closure would be false.

## VII. The Crisis of the Expert Themselves

Panic-learned expertise begins in the expert's own crisis.
This is what gives the formation its force.
The speaker is not simply explaining an external object.
They are trying to restore their own world.

This can be seen in the topics that produce sudden expert performances among professional-class platform subjects.
The crisis often becomes urgent when it threatens the speaker's assumed continuity.
A disease becomes politically real when personal risk cannot be outsourced.
Climate change becomes socially real when insurance, smoke, heat, migration, or supply chains reach the speaker's home.
AI becomes economically real when symbolic labor feels replaceable.
Fascism becomes real when the institutions that once mediated violence begin to look unreliable to those who benefited from mediation.
Housing crisis becomes real when downward mobility reaches people trained to expect stability.

The panic is not necessarily selfish.
Often it is ethically clarifying.
People should learn when crisis reaches them.
But californication turns this learning into an affective drama of personal awakening.
The speaker's fear becomes a conversion story: I did not understand; then the crisis reached me; I researched; now I can help you understand.
The audience receives not only information but a model of subjectivity.
The speaker demonstrates how to be a good person under threat: learn, explain, stay composed, distribute resources, narrate the fear into usefulness.

This is the savior-slave subject as expert.
As savior, the speaker assumes responsibility for making the crisis legible to others.
As slave, they cannot transform the structures producing the crisis.
The gap between responsibility and incapacity becomes intellectual labor.
They cannot stop the pandemic, climate collapse, platform enclosure, state violence, or economic precarity.
But they can explain it.
They can be useful.
They can become the person others trust.

This usefulness is not nothing.
It may help people survive.
But it can also bind the speaker more tightly to the loop.
The audience's relief becomes evidence that the speaker's panic has been redeemed.
The crisis that threatened to make them helpless now gives them a role.
That role is addictive because it converts fear into authority.

## VIII. How Panic-Learned Expertise Displaces Situated Knowledge

The displacement of situated knowledge does not always happen through explicit theft.
It often happens through uptake.
Platforms privilege the speaker whose panic is legible to the target audience.
If a privileged or already-followed intellectual discovers a crisis, their discovery may circulate farther than the long-standing work of people who have been living the crisis.
The platform does not ask who knew first, who paid the cost, or who remains accountable after the content cycle ends.
It asks what travels.

This produces a temporal injustice.
The crisis has a long history for affected communities, but a short history for the newly panicked expert.
The newly panicked expert tells the story from the moment of their own encounter.
Even when they cite prior work, their narrative often centers the conversion point: the moment "we" realized.
The "we" is doing heavy work.
It names the speaker's audience as the relevant public.
It implicitly demotes those who knew before as either sources, examples, or moral witnesses rather than as epistemic authorities.

There is also a genre problem.
Situated knowledge is often messy, local, contradictory, tactical, and bound by obligations that do not scale cleanly.
It may refuse visibility.
It may say "do not go there," "do not post that," "do not make this into a case," "do not generalize this tactic," "do not turn our practice into your framework."
Panic-learned expertise, by contrast, must produce shareable orientation.
The very features that make situated knowledge responsible may make it less platformable.

When the panic-learner translates such knowledge into content, they may strip away its constraints.
A warning becomes a principle.
A local tactic becomes a model.
A relationship becomes an example.
A refusal becomes a lesson.
The audience receives transferable insight.
The people who held the knowledge lose control over the conditions that made it safe or true.

This is why panic-learned expertise must be analyzed as an extraction risk even when no one intends extraction.
The platformal demand for explanation creates pressure to make knowledge portable.
Portability is not neutral.
It often means removing the obligations that made the knowledge more than information.

## IX. Criteria for Diagnosis

Because panic-learned expertise can be sincere and partially useful, it requires careful diagnosis.
The following questions help distinguish responsible emergency explanation from californicated expertise-performance.

Does the speaker clearly mark the difference between their settled domain and their crisis-acquired domain?
Responsible explanation preserves domain boundaries.
It says: I know this part; I am learning this part; here are the people whose judgment should supersede mine.
Panic-learned expertise tends to blur the transition from "I have learned something important" to "I am now a reliable interpreter of the field."

Does the speaker's account become more accountable as it circulates?
Good emergency knowledge thickens its accountability.
It incorporates corrections, names disagreements, changes recommendations, and points away from itself.
Panic-learned expertise often becomes more branded as it circulates.
The speaker becomes the route through which the issue is followed.

Does the content leave room for non-soothing truth?
Some situations do not have a practical takeaway.
Some crises require grief, withdrawal, preparation, or the admission that the available choices are bad.
Responsible expertise can leave audiences with disciplined uncertainty.
Panic-learned expertise tends to convert uncertainty into a consumable stance.

Does the account foreground the people for whom the crisis was not new?
If the speaker's awakening is the center, the account is probably reproducing temporal injustice.
If the account places the speaker's panic inside a longer field of existing knowledge and obligation, it is more likely to be responsible.

Does the knowledge build capacity outside the platform?
This is decisive.
If the audience's main action is to follow, share, subscribe, comment, or feel oriented, the loop remains platformal.
If the knowledge helps people build relationships, protocols, material resources, local plans, protective habits, or collective power that survive the content cycle, then the explanation may be moving beyond californication.

## X. Refusal: What Better Expertise Would Require

The alternative to panic-learned expertise is not silence in crisis.
It is slower authority under urgent conditions.
That sounds paradoxical, but it names a real practice: speaking quickly about what one can responsibly say while slowing down the conversion of learning into status.

Better expertise would require boundary marking.
The speaker must make their limits part of the content's form, not a modest aside.
They must distinguish evidence, inference, experience, speculation, and strategy.
They must refuse the platform's pressure to make all uncertainty feel like insight.

It would require deference that changes circulation.
It is not enough to cite affected people or domain practitioners.
The speaker must route attention, money, and authority toward them.
They must let other people become the path of learning, even when this reduces the speaker's centrality.

It would require unsatisfying endings.
The expert may need to say: this is not resolved; I do not know; the field disagrees; this recommendation may change; the people you most need to hear are not producing platform-ready content; the right action is local and relational rather than shareable.
Such endings perform poorly because they do not soothe.
That is precisely why they matter.

It would require affective honesty without affective domination.
The expert can admit fear without making their fear the organizing center.
They can model composure without making composure the credential.
They can help audiences regulate panic without turning regulation into acceptance.

Finally, it would require building non-platformal knowledge infrastructures.
Reading groups, local preparedness networks, workplace committees, disability justice practices, union education, mutual aid protocols, community science, tenant organizing, and secure channels of coordination all produce knowledge differently than platforms do.
They bind knowledge to people who can contest, revise, and use it.
They make expertise less spectacular and more durable.

## XI. Conclusion

Panic-learned expertise is one of the central knowledge forms of crisis ordinariness.
It emerges because crises are real, because people are afraid, because audiences need orientation, and because platforms reward the performance of calm, useful, well-cited synthesis.
It is not a simple failure of intelligence.
It is a successful adaptation to a damaged knowledge environment.

That is why it is dangerous.
The panic-learned expert often appears precisely where the audience most needs help.
They may provide real facts, genuine care, and better guidance than obvious bad actors.
But the form of the guidance can still soothe the audience into bad relation with the crisis.
It can make premature understanding feel like preparedness.
It can make recognition feel like solidarity.
It can make composure feel like truth.
It can make the speaker's own panic into the credential by which everyone else's panic is managed.

Californication does not require that crisis be hidden.
It requires that crisis be made livable in forms that return subjects to the loop.
Panic-learned expertise is one such form.
It converts the speaker's fear into authority, the audience's fear into relief, and the unresolved crisis into content that feels like knowledge.

The task, then, is not to reject expertise but to refuse its californicated substitute.
We need experts who can say what they know, show how they know it, admit what they cannot know yet, route authority toward those formed by the crisis before it became fashionable, and help audiences build capacities that do not depend on returning to the platform for the next soothing explanation.
