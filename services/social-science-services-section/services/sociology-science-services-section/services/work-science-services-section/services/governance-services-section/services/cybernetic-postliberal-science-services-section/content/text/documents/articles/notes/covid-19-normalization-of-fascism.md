# Operational Denial: COVID-19 and the Normalization of Fascist Practice

The COVID-19 pandemic reveals the fusion of biopolitics, necropolitics, affect theory, and neoliberal governance. Drawing from Foucault, Mbembe, Berlant, Ahmed, and Fraser, among others, this paper argues that pandemic governance exemplifies how market rationality and affective control merge to normalize exposure, disablement, and exhaustion. The normalization of harm operates both as an economic necessity and as an affective pedagogy—a social training that transforms the administration of life and death into everyday habit.

## Abstract

Contemporary “return to normal” responses to COVID-19 have functioned as **operational denial**—a regime that trains populations into fascistic values and thought patterns. Policy shifts such as shortened isolation, data de-intensification, and the rollback of public-health authority reframe infection as acceptable collateral to productivity. The result is a **fascistizing drift**: daily non-mitigation becomes civic duty, and resisting fascism becomes incompatible with normal life. This drift is internalized as **personal moral habits**—beliefs that sickness is inevitable, disability is normal, and care is weakness—constituting a micro-fascism of the self.

## 1. Theoretical and Historical Frame

From Foucault’s *biopolitics* (1979) to Mbembe’s *necropolitics* (2019), modern power governs through life and death—by managing vitality and sanctioning disposability. Esposito’s *immunitas* (2002) reframes protection as exemption: the right to withdraw from mutual care. Ahmed (2004) and Berlant (2011) show how emotions sustain these systems—fear and optimism transform subjection into consent. Fraser’s *Cannibal Capitalism* (2022) demonstrates that such governance depends on consuming the social reproduction it exhausts.

Classical fascism emerged amid twentieth-century crises—its aesthetics theatrical, masculine, and spectacular. **Neoliberal fascism**, by contrast, operates quietly: through metrics, dashboards, and wellness rhetoric. The spectacle has migrated from the square to the spreadsheet. Both forms rely on the same emotional pedagogy of sacrifice: the belief that collective suffering restores moral order. Pandemic governance thus represents a continuation of this trajectory, replacing the authoritarian state with the self-regulating subject while preserving the metaphysics of purity, order, and endurance.

---

## 2. Mythic Core: “Return to Normal” as Palingenesis

Eco’s “Ur-Fascism” (1995) and Esposito’s *immunitas* (2002) together explain the moral logic of rebirth and immunity-as-exemption. Eco describes fascism as an enduring impulse toward purity and action; Esposito shows how immunity grants the privilege of exemption from care. “Return to normal” thus became civic virtue: maturity through exposure, patriotism through indifference. This mythic rebirth through suffering was echoed in Biden’s 2022 State of the Union and corporate reopenings framed as “comebacks.” To live with COVID became to prove one’s belonging. The fascist rebirth myth fused with neoliberal virtue, establishing immunity as the emotional grammar of citizenship.

**Operational mapping (Eco → COVID slogans/practices):**

# *Cult of tradition* → “Back to normal,” “like before,” appeals to pre-2020 life as moral order.

# *Action for action’s sake* → “Open up,” “move on,” RTO as virtue independent of safety outcomes.

# *Fear of difference* → Masked persons framed as anxious, antisocial, or unpatriotic.

# *Newspeak* → “Mild,” “seasonal,” “endemic” as vocabulary that cancels risk perception.

# *Heroism of the crowd* → “We’re stronger together” recoded as together-in-exposure.

**From myth to mechanism.** The palingenetic script underwrites later institutional behaviors: if rebirth requires exposure, then data that would trigger caution must recede (Section 3), disablement must be normalized (Section 4), and throughput must be moralized (Section 5). The myth is not accessory—it is the *schema* that renders ignorance, debility, and logistics emotionally intelligible as civic good.

## 3. Institutional Mediation

### 3.1 Data and Epistemic Governance

Following the end of the Public Health Emergency in 2023, dashboards were retired, reporting halted, and mitigation depoliticized. Proctor and Schiebinger’s *Agnotology* (2008) defines this as **manufactured ignorance**: the deliberate unmaking of knowledge. Star and Bowker’s (1999) *infrastructural inversion* reveals how invisibility becomes control. Agamben’s *state of exception* (2005) mutated into a *state of indifference*—emergency by omission.

**Techniques of de-knowledge:** (i) shift from daily to intermittent reporting; (ii) collapsing multiple respiratory pathogens into a single trendline; (iii) privatizing surveillance to hospitals, insurers, and device platforms; (iv) removing community risk dashboards; (v) converting isolation rules into discretionary guidance.

**Effects on practice:** Risk moves from public dashboards to private guesswork; people time gatherings by vibe, not data. The loss of shared measures dissolves coordination, replacing collective judgement with isolated calculus. Ignorance appears calm because it is *unmeasured*.

### 3.2 Affective Governance

Berlant’s *cruel optimism* (2011) and Ahmed’s *affective economy* (2004) explain how emotional management complements epistemic erasure. Composure replaced knowledge as civic duty. Theweleit’s analysis of fascist masculinity shows how calmness becomes strength.

**Calmness protocols:** HR emails praising resilience; school newsletters framing mitigation as anxiety; editorial styles discouraging “alarm.” These normalize a *tone* that delegitimizes precaution. The result is serenity without safety—a cultivated mood that makes ongoing harm livable.

## 4. Biopolitics of Disposability and Debility

Mbembe’s *necropolitics* (2019) and Puar’s *debility* (2017) describe power’s shift from direct killing to the slow governance of injury. The United States now contains over 20 million people with long COVID, yet media and policy erase its reality. Kafer’s *Feminist, Queer, Crip* (2013) and Eubanks’s *Automating Inequality* (2018) show how data poverty compounds bodily vulnerability. The state no longer kills; it supervises decay. Wellness becomes class privilege, measured in air changes per hour. The ethics of solidarity collapses into the moral of endurance—“we all get sick.”

**Mechanisms of non-recognition:** Diagnostic pathways narrowed to the most acute cases; employer accommodations tied to proof rarely attainable by the poor; media framing long COVID as vague or psychosomatic; clinical focus on “mildness” that excludes cognitive, vascular, and autonomic sequelae.

**Memory work.** The social narrative retrofits the past—“we’ve always been sick this often”—turning present injury into remembered normalcy. This is necro-epistemic success: harm naturalized through induced forgetfulness.

**Diagnostic implication.** Necro-epistemic governance fuses ignorance with endurance, transforming suffering into citizenship.

---

## 5. Economic Re-Ordering

Fraser, Anderson, and Cowen form a throughline revealing how capitalist logistical rationality fuses with fascist aesthetics of vitality. Fraser’s *Cannibal Capitalism* shows capital consuming the care it requires; Anderson’s *Private Government* (2017) reveals the workplace as sovereign; Cowen’s *Deadly Life of Logistics* (2014) exposes global motion as moral order. Together they form a **corporatist necroeconomy**: endurance as belonging, rest as betrayal.

**Infrastructures withheld.** Industrial ventilation and filtration standards exist (e.g., engineering targets for infectious aerosol control), yet adoption is sporadic and stratified. CO₂ monitoring is cheap but rare; portable filtration is privatized to households and elite institutions. The air is governed as a perk, not a right.

**Moral economies of motion.** Return-to-office cycles are justified by culture and innovation while ignoring exposure costs; gig platforms algorithmically reward speed over recovery. In these regimes, throughput is the proof of worth.

## 6. Affective and Behavioral Mechanisms of Self-Fascism

Povinelli’s *Economies of Abandonment* (2011) and Han’s *Burnout Society* (2015) reveal how subjects internalize exhaustion as value. De Certeau (1980) shows how daily gestures—removing a mask, ignoring a cough—become techniques of power. Foucault’s *governmentality* (1979) describes learning to govern oneself within domination; Gramsci and Althusser explain the affective command: “Be reasonable, move on.”

**Affective scripts (everyday vignettes):**

# *The meeting.* A colleague coughs, apologizes—“allergies”—everyone smiles, the agenda proceeds. The script says: smoothness over safety.

# *The classroom.* Windows stay shut to avoid complaints; the teacher tells herself that “kids need normal.” The script says: harmony over harm recognition.

# *The clinic.* A nurse reuses a mask and calls it “good enough.” The script says: thrift over protection.

# *The commute.* The rider lowers a mask between stops “to breathe,” reading this as balance. The script says: comfort over consistency.

# *The home.* Parents label recurring illness “seasonal bugs,” teaching children that feeling unwell is ordinary.

These scenes are not individual failures but cultural choreography. The fascist subject no longer marches—it multitasks. Power reproduces itself through calm, tired bodies who mistake endurance for goodness.

---

## 7. Conclusion: Operational Denial as Political Pedagogy Conclusion: Operational Denial as Political Pedagogy

Operational denial—the collective refusal to perceive preventable harm—serves as moral education. It aligns with Griffin’s rebirth myth, Foucault’s governmentality, and Berlant’s cruel optimism: endurance equals freedom, composure equals citizenship, care equals dissent. Fascism persists through repetition—each act of “moving on” reaffirms obedience.

Reversal begins with material and imaginative interventions. Materially, we must rebuild infrastructures of care: clean air, public data, universal sick leave. Culturally, we must redefine strength through solidarity, not stoicism—art, journalism, and mutual aid must honor interdependence as courage. Philosophically, anti-fascism must reorient affect itself: to feel is to resist.

The refusal to accept preventable suffering is not sentimentality but strategy. Fascism teaches survival through indifference; democracy must teach survival through relation. Resisting operational denial means recovering imagination—the ability to sense and build a world where breath, care, and truth are shared, not sacrificed.
