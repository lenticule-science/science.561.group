## Overview

Three unrelated governance traditions converge on the same structural problem: how do you bind a body of future actors to a goal or principle without writing out every case in advance? Each answers differently — European Union law by separating instruments that bind *ends* from instruments that bind *means*; the Roman Catholic Code of Canon Law by codifying principle into short, sequentially numbered provisions administered under a single legislative authority; and John Carver's Policy Governance model by formalizing, as a named and licensable methodology, the idea that a governing body should write policy at the broadest level that is acceptable and only add detail when that broad statement proves insufficient.

They are not variations on one lineage — legislative treaty law, ecclesiastical codification, and 1970s nonprofit-board consulting practice have no common ancestor — but comparing them surfaces the small set of design decisions any "directive-issuing" governance instrument has to make: who may issue it, what unit it comes in, how it is numbered and cross-referenced, where the rationale for it lives, how it is amended, and whether compliance is monitored by the instrument itself or left to whoever enforces it.

## European Union Law: Directives vs. Regulations

Article 288 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU) [@ref1] names five binding and non-binding instrument types the EU's institutions may adopt. Two matter here:

- A **Regulation** is "binding in its entirety and directly applicable in all Member States" — it becomes law in each member state the moment it enters into force, without any national implementing act. It binds both ends and means.
- A **Directive** is "binding, as to the result to be achieved, upon each Member State to which it is addressed, but shall leave to the national authorities the choice of form and methods." It binds the goal, not the mechanism. Each member state must **transpose** the directive into its own domestic legislation by a stated deadline, choosing whatever legal instrument fits its own system.

This is the distinction the term "directive" is doing real work in: a Regulation is closer to a Rule (specifies the how); a Directive is closer to a Policy or Standing Order (specifies the what, delegates the how). Article 288 also defines **Decisions** (binding only on those specifically addressed) and two non-binding instruments, **Recommendations** and **Opinions** — a five-tier hierarchy of decreasing bindingness and decreasing prescriptiveness that has no exact analogue in the other two frameworks discussed here.

**Structure.** Every Regulation and Directive is drafted in two parts: a preamble of numbered **recitals** ("Whereas (1) ...; (2) ...;") stating the rationale, legal basis, and intent, followed by numbered **Articles** containing the binding text itself. The recitals carry no binding force on their own but are used by courts to interpret ambiguous articles — rationale and rule are structurally separated, not interleaved. This split is governed by an actual formal drafting spec, the *Joint Practical Guide* issued jointly by the Parliament, Council, and Commission [@ref2], which prescribes sentence structure, cross-referencing conventions, and how amendments to earlier instruments must be drafted (textual amendment vs. standalone act).

**Numbering.** Since November 2014, instruments are cited as `Type (EU) year/number` — e.g., *Directive (EU) 2019/790*; before that, the order was reversed, `year/number/EC` — e.g., *Directive 2006/112/EC*.

**Amendment.** Directives and Regulations are amended by later instruments of the same or higher type, adopted through the EU's ordinary legislative procedure (co-decision between Parliament and Council, on a Commission proposal) — authorship is institutionally distributed, not vested in one office.

## The Code of Canon Law: Codification Under a Single Legislator

The 1983 Code of Canon Law (*Codex Iuris Canonici*) governs the Latin Church; a parallel Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches (1990) governs the Eastern Catholic churches [@ref3] [@ref4]. Both are examples of a much older genre — canon law dates to the early councils of the Church — codified into a single, continuously numbered text in the 20th century.

**Structure.** The 1983 Code contains 1,752 **canons**, numbered sequentially straight through the whole work (Canon 1 to Canon 1752) regardless of which of its seven **Books** a canon falls in (General Norms; the People of God; the Teaching Office; the Sanctifying Office; Temporal Goods; Sanctions; Processes). Within a Book, canons are grouped by Part, Title, and Chapter, but the canon number itself never resets — a citation like "c. 204" is unambiguous across the whole Code without needing the Book name attached, the way a citation into the *United States Code* needs its title number. Canons are typically one to a few sentences; longer ones are broken into numbered paragraphs (§1, §2).

**Where the rationale lives.** The canons themselves are terse — closer to Regulation-style rule-text than to recital-annotated Directive-style rationale. The explanatory and interpretive work is done by a separate, non-authoritative literature: centuries of **canonist commentary**, written by scholars and tribunals, that glosses each canon's history, intent, and case application. This is the opposite of the EU's model, where rationale (recitals) is part of the same instrument as the rule; in canon law, rationale is a whole external genre that accretes around a deliberately spare code.

**Authority and amendment.** The Pope holds sole legislative authority over the universal law of the Church; canons are promulgated and amended by apostolic constitution or *motu proprio* — a single point of authorship, unlike the EU's multi-institution co-decision or a corporate board's collective vote. Canon law also recognizes **desuetudo**, obsolescence of a law through long, tolerated disuse — a mechanism by which a canon can lose binding force without formal repeal, which neither EU law nor Policy Governance has an equivalent for.

## Carver Policy Governance: The Board's Job Is to Write Policy

Policy Governance is a named, trademarked governance model developed by John Carver beginning in the 1970s and set out fully in *Boards That Make a Difference* (1990; 3rd ed. 2006) [@ref5], with an implementation guide in *Reinventing Your Board* [@ref6]. Unlike the other two frameworks, it is not a legal system but a consulting methodology — one with formal certification (the International Policy Governance Association trains and credentials practitioners) [@ref7], making it the closest of the three to an explicit, teachable "spec."

**Core move.** A governing board's only proper output is **policy** — written statements that constrain all future decisions within their scope — never individual operational decisions. Carver groups policies into four categories:

1. **Ends** — what results, for which recipients, at what relative cost; the *only* category where the board specifies outcomes ("what") rather than constraining process.
2. **Executive Limitations** — boundaries on the methods the chief executive must not use, deliberately phrased in the negative ("the CEO shall not ...") so that everything not prohibited is delegated, rather than everything not permitted being forbidden.
3. **Governance Process** — how the board defines and disciplines its own conduct and job.
4. **Board–Management Delegation** — how authority passes from board to CEO, and how the board will monitor that its own policies are being honored.

**Specificity levels ("policy circles").** Within each category, the board states its policy at the broadest, most general level first — the outermost circle. It only adds a more specific, narrower statement (a smaller inner circle) if the broader one is judged insufficient to convey the board's intent, and every added level of specificity is itself a distinct policy the board must separately adopt. This gives Policy Governance an explicit, built-in specificity gradient, comparable to Ostrom's nested operational/collective-choice/constitutional policy levels but formalized as a single board's internal drafting discipline rather than a multi-level jurisdictional structure.

**Monitoring is part of the policy itself.** Every Executive Limitations and Ends policy must specify how, how often, and by whom compliance will be monitored (direct board inspection, external report, or internal report from the CEO). Compliance verification is written into the instrument, not left to an external enforcement body — a structural feature neither EU directives nor canons build into their own text.

## Common Patterns Across the Three

- **Goal-binding vs. method-binding is a recurring axis.** EU Directives, Ends policies, and (loosely) canons on doctrine and discipline bind outcomes/principles; EU Regulations, Executive Limitations, and canons on procedure bind methods. All three frameworks find it useful to keep those two kinds of bindingness in visibly separate instrument types rather than blending them.
- **Numbering as a retrieval system, not just an artifact of order.** EU instrument codes, canon numbers, and (in practice) Carver policy codes all function as stable, citable addresses — the number is how the provision gets pointed to from elsewhere, inside and outside the document.
- **Rationale placement is a real design choice, and the three frameworks make three different ones.** EU law keeps rationale in the same instrument (recitals) but structurally separate from the binding text (articles). Canon law keeps rationale almost entirely outside the code, in a secondary commentary literature. Carver policy does not formally require rationale at all — the policy is meant to be a decision-premise for the board's own future decisions, and its justification lives in board deliberation and minutes, not the policy text.
- **A single amending authority makes revision faster but more fragile.** Canon law (one legislator) and a Carver board (one governing body) can amend quickly; EU law's multi-institution co-decision is slower but distributes the risk of any one actor unilaterally rewriting the rules.

## Where They Diverge

| | EU Directive/Regulation | Code of Canon Law | Carver Policy Governance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Issuing authority | Multi-institution (Parliament + Council, on Commission proposal) | Single legislator (the Pope, for universal law) | The governing board, collectively |
| Structural unit | Article, within a numbered instrument | Canon, numbered continuously through the Code | Policy, within one of four categories |
| Goal- vs. method-binding | Split across two instrument types (Directive vs. Regulation) | Not formally split; varies by subject matter | Split across two categories (Ends vs. Executive Limitations) |
| Where rationale lives | Same instrument, separate section (recitals) | External commentary literature | Not formalized; lives in board deliberation |
| Amendment mechanism | New instrument via ordinary legislative procedure | Apostolic constitution / motu proprio | Board vote, reviewed on the model's own cycle |
| Built-in compliance monitoring | No (left to courts, infringement procedure) | No (left to canonical process/tribunals) | Yes (monitoring method specified in the policy itself) |
| Obsolescence without repeal | No | Yes (desuetudo) | No |
| Formal drafting spec | Yes (Joint Practical Guide) | Loosely, via the Code's own internal conventions | Yes (Carver's published model + certification) |
