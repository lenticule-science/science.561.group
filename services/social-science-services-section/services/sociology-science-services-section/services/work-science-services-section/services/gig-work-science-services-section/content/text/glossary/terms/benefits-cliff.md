A benefits cliff is a point where one additional dollar of countable income causes a household to lose an entire means-tested benefit or subsidy at once, rather than the benefit tapering off gradually as income rises. Two current federal cliffs bear directly on a gig worker's finances.

The Supplemental Security Income program's countable-resource limit is $2,000 for an individual and $3,000 for a couple, a hard line unchanged since 1989 under the Social Security Administration's own current policy manual: a recipient whose countable resources exceed the limit on the first day of any month loses SSI eligibility for that month entirely, regardless of how far over the limit they are ([Social Security Administration 2024](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/05/2026-09-05-note-on-american-gig-work-as-a-workforce-and-a-cost-structure.md)). The Affordable Care Act marketplace's premium tax credit carries a second cliff: enhanced subsidies that eliminated the prior 400-percent-of-poverty-line cutoff expired at the end of 2025, so a household crossing 400 percent of the federal poverty level for coverage year 2026 loses marketplace subsidy eligibility outright rather than seeing it phase out ([Congressional Research Service 2026](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/05/2026-09-05-note-on-american-gig-work-as-a-workforce-and-a-cost-structure.md)).

A benefits cliff is distinguished from a taper or phase-out, the design most modern means-tested programs use instead, under which a benefit shrinks gradually as income rises so that no single dollar of earnings costs the household more than it gained. The consequence for a gig worker, especially one who is also disabled or otherwise benefit-dependent, is that an irregular or lump-sum gig payment — a large weekly payout, a tip surge, a bonus — can trigger a total benefit loss that a steadier paycheck of the same annual total would not, making income timing and smoothing a materially different problem for a benefits-cliff-exposed worker than for one who is not.
