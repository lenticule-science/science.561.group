Deadhead miles are the miles a gig driver drives without a paying passenger or order aboard: traveling to reach a pickup, repositioning between assignments, or returning home after a drop-off. They are distinguished from engaged miles, the miles driven with an active paying trip or order underway, which is the only distance most platforms' own per-trip earnings statements show.

The distinction matters because deadhead miles cost the driver exactly as much per mile in fuel, tires, and depreciation as engaged miles — the vehicle does not know whether a fare is active — but earn no fare revenue directly. Every peer-reviewed net-pay study of ride-hailing and delivery driving computes total cost from independently measured total miles (engaged plus deadhead) rather than from a platform's reported trip mileage, precisely because a platform's own statement is structurally blind to deadhead distance ([Zoepf et al. 2018](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/05/2026-09-05-note-on-american-gig-work-as-a-workforce-and-a-cost-structure.md)).

The consequence for a worker is that any mileage deduction or [net hourly rate](./net-hourly-rate.md) computed from a platform statement alone will undercount true vehicle cost by exactly the deadhead share of a shift's driving, which is one of the largest single reconciling items between what a gig platform reports and what a worker's own [standard mileage rate](./standard-mileage-rate.md) deduction is entitled to claim.
