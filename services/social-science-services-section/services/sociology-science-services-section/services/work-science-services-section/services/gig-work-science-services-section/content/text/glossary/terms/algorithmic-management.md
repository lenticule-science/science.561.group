Algorithmic management is the use of software, rather than a human supervisor, to assign, price, monitor, rate, and terminate work, producing an information and power asymmetry between the platform and the worker it manages. Rosenblat and Stark's founding empirical study of Uber's drivers found that the app exercises significant indirect control over how drivers work while marketing them as independent entrepreneurs, and traced this control to structural information asymmetries the app itself creates about fares, ratings, and account status ([Rosenblat and Stark 2016](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/05/2026-09-05-note-on-american-gig-work-as-a-workforce-and-a-cost-structure.md)).

Algorithmic management is distinguished from ordinary scheduling or dispatch software by what decisions it makes autonomously: a calendar tool coordinates human decisions, while an algorithmic-management system itself decides which worker sees which task, what that task pays, how a worker's performance is scored, and whether a worker's account continues to receive work, typically without a visible human decision-maker the worker can identify or appeal to directly.

The consequence of algorithmic management is not uniform across platforms: Griesbach, Reich, Elliott-Negri, and Milkman's study of food-delivery platforms found significant cross-platform variation in how tightly the algorithm constrains a worker's schedule and activity, naming one platform's regime among the most constraining in their sample even though all of the platforms studied used algorithmic management in some form ([Griesbach et al. 2019](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/05/2026-09-05-note-on-american-gig-work-as-a-workforce-and-a-cost-structure.md)). A worker experiencing [platform work](./platform-work.md) is therefore not just adapting to automation in general but to a specific, measurably variable degree of algorithmic control that differs from one company's software to another's.
