*International Journal of Communication* 10, 2016.

## Why this is central

This is the founding peer-reviewed account of algorithmic management in ride-hailing: a nine-month empirical study finding that Uber's app produces significant indirect control over how drivers work while marketing drivers as independent entrepreneurs. The paper's core claim is that information and power asymmetries produced by the app — what fares are available, what a trip will pay before acceptance, why a rating or an account status changed — are structural to that control, not incidental to it. Every later algorithmic-management study cited in the [note on American gig work as a workforce and a cost structure](../notes/2026/09/05/2026-09-05-note-on-american-gig-work-as-a-workforce-and-a-cost-structure.md), section 7, extends this paper's diagnosis to other platforms rather than replacing it.

## What it does not settle

The study is qualitative and single-platform (Uber, pre-2016); it does not quantify the earnings effect of the information asymmetries it documents, and it predates the state and city pay-floor laws and platform fee schedules this note's sections 3 and 6 cover. It also does not address delivery platforms, which the later Griesbach et al. study takes up directly.
