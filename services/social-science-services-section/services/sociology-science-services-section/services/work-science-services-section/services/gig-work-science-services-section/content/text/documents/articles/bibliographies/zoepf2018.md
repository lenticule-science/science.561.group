MIT CEEPR Working Paper 2018-005, February 2018.

## Why this is central

This is the founding quantitative study of ride-hailing driver net pay: a survey of over 1,100 Uber and Lyft drivers paired with per-vehicle cost data, finding a median profit of $3.37 per hour before taxes, 74 percent of drivers earning below their state's minimum wage, and 30 percent losing money once vehicle expenses were counted. Every later net-pay study cited in the [note on American gig work as a workforce and a cost structure](../notes/2026/09/05/2026-09-05-note-on-american-gig-work-as-a-workforce-and-a-cost-structure.md) inherits its basic method: pair independently measured miles and hours against the platform's own reported pay.

## What it does not settle

After Uber publicly disputed the paper's vehicle-cost methodology, lead author Stephen Zoepf acknowledged the criticism had merit and recalculated: the corrected median net profit is $8.55 per hour, with 41 to 54 percent of drivers still below minimum wage. The correction narrows the original headline figure by roughly 2.5x without reversing the paper's qualitative finding; a reader citing the $3.37 figure alone without the correction is citing a retracted number, not the paper's settled position. The paper also predates Proposition 22, the 2022-2024 NYC and Seattle pay-floor rules, and the platforms' current fee schedules, so its dollar figures are historical rather than current.
