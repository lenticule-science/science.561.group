Platform work is [gig work](./gig-work.md) whose tasks are assigned, priced, tracked, and paid through a company's software platform rather than through an informal or offline arrangement. Pew Research Center's 2021 survey measured this specific form directly: 16 percent of U.S. adults had ever earned money through an online gig platform (ride-hailing, delivery, grocery shopping, or errands arranged by an app), and 9 percent had done so in the prior 12 months ([Pew Research Center 2021](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/05/2026-09-05-note-on-american-gig-work-as-a-workforce-and-a-cost-structure.md)).

Platform work is distinguished from gig work in general by who assigns the task and sets its price: on a platform, a dispatch algorithm — not a human customer or a human intermediary — decides which worker sees which task, at what price, and evaluates the worker's performance afterward through in-app ratings and metrics. Gig work arranged through a personal referral, a bulletin board, or a paper flyer is not platform work, even though it is gig work.

The consequence of that algorithmic mediation is the specific research object of the [algorithmic management](./algorithmic-management.md) literature: because the platform, not a visible manager, makes moment-to-moment assignment and pricing decisions, the worker faces an information asymmetry about what a task will actually pay, why an account was rated or restricted, and what alternatives existed at the moment a task was accepted — an asymmetry that does not exist in the same form for gig work arranged directly between two people ([Rosenblat and Stark 2016](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/05/2026-09-05-note-on-american-gig-work-as-a-workforce-and-a-cost-structure.md)).
