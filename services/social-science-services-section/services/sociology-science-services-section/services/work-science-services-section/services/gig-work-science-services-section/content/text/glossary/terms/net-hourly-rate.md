A gig worker's net hourly rate is platform earnings and tips minus vehicle costs, platform fees, and the worker's own share of self-employment tax, divided by total hours worked including unpaid waiting and driving time. It is computed after every deduction a [gig work](./gig-work.md) arrangement pushes onto the worker rather than an employer.

The net hourly rate is distinguished from the gross hourly pay most platform apps display, which typically counts only engaged time (an active trip or order) rather than total time online, and which never subtracts vehicle wear, fuel, or tax. Parrott and Reich's 2020 Seattle study measured both figures for the same drivers and found a large gap between them: gross driver pay of about $21.53 per hour fell to $9.73 per hour once $11.80 per hour in vehicle expenses was subtracted ([Parrott and Reich 2020](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/05/2026-09-05-note-on-american-gig-work-as-a-workforce-and-a-cost-structure.md)).

The consequence of the gap between gross and net is that a worker or a regulator comparing platforms, cities, or years by gross pay alone is comparing the wrong number: every peer-reviewed and institutional study cited in the note on American gig work computes net hourly rate independently, using measured miles and hours rather than a platform's own gross-pay dashboard, precisely because the dashboard figure is not the worker's real return on their time ([Zoepf et al. 2018](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/05/2026-09-05-note-on-american-gig-work-as-a-workforce-and-a-cost-structure.md)).
