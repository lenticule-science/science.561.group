Self-employment tax is the 15.3 percent combined Social Security and Medicare tax an independent contractor owes on net self-employment earnings of $400 or more: 12.4 percent for Social Security, on the first $168,600 of combined wage and self-employment earnings for 2024, and 2.9 percent for Medicare, uncapped ([Internal Revenue Service 2026](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/09/05/2026-09-05-note-on-american-gig-work-as-a-workforce-and-a-cost-structure.md)). It replaces the payroll tax split an employer and employee would otherwise share, because a self-employed worker is legally both parties at once.

Self-employment tax is distinguished from federal income tax: it funds Social Security and Medicare specifically, is computed on Schedule SE from net profit carried over from Schedule C, and is owed in addition to income tax rather than instead of it, though the filer deducts the employer-equivalent half of the SE tax from adjusted gross income when computing income tax.

The consequence for a gig worker is direct: net self-employment earnings — the figure SE tax is computed on — is gross platform income minus every allowable business expense, including the [standard mileage rate](./standard-mileage-rate.md) deduction for [deadhead miles](./deadhead-miles.md) as well as engaged miles. A worker who fails to claim the mileage deduction reports a higher net profit than the true one and overpays both income tax and self-employment tax on the same missed deduction at once.
