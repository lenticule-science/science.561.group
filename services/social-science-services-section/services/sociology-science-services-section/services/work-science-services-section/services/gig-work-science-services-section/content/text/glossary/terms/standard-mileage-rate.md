The standard mileage rate is the IRS's optional per-mile deduction for business use of a personal vehicle, approximating the fully loaded cost of fuel, maintenance, depreciation, and insurance so the taxpayer need not track each cost separately. A gig driver claims it by multiplying business miles driven (engaged miles plus [deadhead miles](./deadhead-miles.md)) by the rate in effect for each mile driven.

The rate is distinguished from the actual-expense method, under which a taxpayer instead deducts the real, receipted cost of gas, repairs, depreciation, and insurance prorated to business use; a taxpayer chooses one method or the other for a given vehicle and year, not both. It is also distinguished from operating-cost-only estimates such as AAA's, which report a lower cents-per-mile figure because they measure only fuel and maintenance and exclude the depreciation and financing cost the IRS rate folds in.

The rate is not fixed for a full year by default: the IRS set the 2026 business rate at 72.5 cents per mile effective 2026-01-01 (Notice 2026-10, IR-2025-128, issued 2025-12-29), then issued a midyear revision, Announcement 2026-11 (2026-29 I.R.B. 49, dated 2026-07-13), raising it to 76 cents per mile effective for miles driven on or after 2026-07-01 to reflect a rise in fuel prices — the first midyear mileage-rate change since 2022. The consequence for a gig worker is that a full 2026 tax year's mileage deduction must be computed in two pieces, at two different per-mile rates, split at the July 1 boundary, rather than as one flat rate times annual miles.
