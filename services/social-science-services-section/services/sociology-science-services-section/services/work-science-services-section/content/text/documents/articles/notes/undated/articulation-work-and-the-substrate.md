# Articulation work and the substrate

Schmidt and Bannon's "Taking CSCW Seriously" (1992) — already a [disciplinary reference](../../bibliographies/schmidt-bannon1992.md) — introduces *articulation work*: the coordination labor that makes primary work possible. Their argument: CSCW systems that optimize for "the task" while ignoring the work of aligning, scheduling, negotiating, and maintaining mutual awareness are optimizing the wrong thing. The articulation work is not overhead around the real work; it is itself real work that the substrate must support.

The predicated-work substrate represents Schmidt and Bannon's distinction precisely — and then, through the lens of non-Western governance traditions, challenges the hierarchy the distinction implies.

## The predicate-typing account

The [operations-base](../../../../objects/operations-base.md) types predicates: action–agent predicates ($\langle E, \texttt{executed-by}, A\rangle$), action–goal predicates ($\langle E, \texttt{produces}, O\rangle$), action–action predicates ($\langle T, \texttt{depends-on}, T'\rangle$, $\langle T, \texttt{decomposes-into}, T_i\rangle$). The predicate-typing makes the distinction substrate-visible:

**Primary work**: cells typed with action–goal predicates. An agent does something that produces an output. These are the cells that task-completion audits look for — they are the settling's "deliverables."

**Articulation work**: cells typed with action–action predicates. An agent coordinates, schedules, aligns, notifies. These cells do not appear in the completion-cell record; they are the infrastructure through which primary work becomes possible.

Schmidt and Bannon's core observation is substrate-algebraically precise: action–action predication is necessary for action–goal predication to occur (dependencies must be managed; coordination must happen), but it is systematically *not counted* as output. The task-cell-system's target valuation is stated over action–goal cells; articulation-work cells are antecedents in the closure rules that fire task-completion, not cells in the target valuation themselves.

## Articulation work enters $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{shared}}$

As articulation work is routinized, it transitions from deliberate individual action to encoded closure rule. A coordination practice that an agent once had to do explicitly — "remind the team to update status before the Monday sync" — becomes a scheduled closure rule in $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{shared}}$: the substrate fires automatically without anyone doing the reminding.

The [shared-closure-rule-vocabulary](../../../../../../../../../social-theory-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/concepts/shared-closure-rule-vocabulary.md) concept and the [closure-rules-and-the-iron-cage](../../../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/teaching-articles/explanations/closure-rules-and-the-iron-cage.md) narrative describe this for work in general. The specific version for articulation work: when coordination overhead becomes a closure rule, the labor of coordination becomes invisible. The rule fires; no agent is credited; the substrate handles it. This is Schmidt and Bannon's worry made precise: CSCW that automates articulation work may be automating the most human and relational part of work — without acknowledging that this labor existed, or that automating it changes something.

## The non-Western challenge: articulation work as primary work

Schmidt and Bannon's framing preserves a hierarchy: primary work is the point; articulation work serves it. Many non-Western work traditions do not recognize this hierarchy.

In **Ubuntu-inflected work communities**, the maintenance of relational fabric — ensuring that all members are in right relationship, that harms are addressed, that collective wellbeing is sustained — is not coordination overhead. It is the community's fundamental work. The facilitator who ensures relationships remain whole and the builder who constructs a wall are not doing primary vs. secondary work; both are doing necessary community work, and in many Ubuntu contexts, the relational maintenance is the more valued labor.

In **Haudenosaunee governance**, clan mothers perform work that Schmidt and Bannon's framework would classify as articulation: they maintain deliberative processes, ensure chiefs fulfill their responsibilities, manage succession, and preserve the conditions under which consensus governance can operate. This is not overhead around "real" governance decisions. It is the central governance function — the work without which the Grand Council's primary deliberation cannot occur. Clan mothers hold articulation work in the traditional substrate and are accorded its highest recognition.

The substrate is neutral on this point: action–action predication is not marked secondary. The hierarchy is imposed by the closure-rule vocabulary that tracks completion — a vocabulary that counts action–goal cells as output and ignores action–action cells. A polity committed to valuing articulation work as primary can write its charter to count coordination cells in the completion record. The substrate admits this; the default deployment does not do it.

## The automation implication

When articulation work enters $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{shared}}$ and becomes automatic, the labor of the humans who used to do it is absorbed into the rule. The credit-assignment problem: the agent who originally built the coordination practice (performing the immaterial labor of encoding it) is not credited when the rule fires automatically. This is the [labor-extraction-as-surplus-cell](../../../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/content/text/glossary/concepts/labor-extraction-as-surplus-cell.md) structure: the coordination practice's value (cells it enables) exceeds the cells the original agent is permitted to claim for having produced the rule.

In settings where articulation work is primarily done by specific groups — in many organizations: administrative staff, coordinators, facilitators; in Indigenous contexts: elders, clan mothers, knowledge-keepers — automating or routinizing it without recognition is not a neutral efficiency improvement. It is an extraction of labor from those groups into $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{shared}}$, where the labor loses its attribution.

The substrate makes this extraction detectable: the history of $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{shared}}$ records what past work installed each rule. Governance that cares about attribution can maintain that record.
