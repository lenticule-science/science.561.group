**Real subsumption** is Marx's term for the second of two historical stages by which capital comes to dominate the [labor process](./labor-process.md). The distinction is developed in *Capital* Volume I [marx1867] and most explicitly in the *Resultate des unmittelbaren Produktionsprozesses* [marx1864] — the unpublished "Chapter Six" recovered and published in 1933 and now standardly printed as an appendix to English-language editions of Capital I [marx1976].

## Formal vs. real subsumption

**Formal subsumption** is the stage in which capital takes over an existing labor process without yet transforming its technical content. The artisan keeps making cabinets the way cabinet-making has always been done; what changes is that the artisan is now a wage-laborer producing for a capitalist who owns the workshop, the materials, and the product. Capital's appropriation here operates on the *outside* of the labor process — through the wage relation, the working day, the disciplinary regime — while the *inside* of the labor process (its sequence of operations, division of tasks, instruments) retains its pre-capitalist form. The corresponding extraction is *absolute surplus value*, achieved primarily through extension of the working day.

**Real subsumption** is the stage in which capital reorganizes the labor process itself. The cabinet-making is no longer done the way it has always been done; it is broken into specialized operations, distributed across an articulated sequence of workers, mediated by machines designed for the new sequence, and disciplined by time-and-motion methods. The labor process now bears capital's organizational stamp throughout. The corresponding extraction is *relative surplus value*, achieved by intensifying the productivity of labor itself — the same hour of work now produces more value because the process has been reorganized.

The transition from formal to real subsumption is, for Marx, the historical core of *industrial* capitalism. The factory and the machine system, analyzed at length in *Capital* I chapters 13–15, are real subsumption's institutional embodiments.

## The mechanisms of real subsumption

Marx and the subsequent labor-process tradition identify five characteristic mechanisms through which real subsumption operates:

1. **The detailed division of labor** — breaking work into specialized partial operations performed by different workers.
2. **Machinery designed for the divided process** — instruments engineered to fit and require the new task structure.
3. **Cooperation in the workplace** — the assembled workforce as a productive force in itself, generating effects no individual worker could produce.
4. **Discipline of time** — the imposition of clock-time, work-rhythms, and pacing structures (extensively treated in E. P. Thompson's *Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism*).
5. **The separation of conception from execution** — the appropriation of the design of work by management, leaving workers only the execution of pre-specified tasks. This last is what Braverman [braverman1974] identifies as the *Taylorist* completion of real subsumption.

## Continuing the distinction

Carlo Vercellone [vercellone2007] supplied an influential extension. His argument: the late-twentieth-century transformations (computerization, knowledge work, the service economy) constitute a third regime — *cognitive capitalism* — in which the [general intellect](./general-intellect.md) of socialized humanity becomes directly productive, and capital's relation to the labor process partially reverts to a *formal-subsumption-like* configuration. Capital appropriates value generated by cooperative-cognitive activity it did not organize and increasingly cannot organize; the productive process exceeds capital's capacity to reorganize it from the inside. The Vercellone reading is contested but is the most developed Marxist framework for what changes, in subsumption-theoretic terms, when productive activity becomes increasingly cognitive.

## Distinguished from

- **Formal subsumption** — see above; the binary distinction is the term's defining contrast.
- **Alienation** — alienation in the Marxian sense names the worker's separation from the means of production, the product, fellow workers, and species-being. Real subsumption is one institutional condition under which alienation deepens, but the categories are not coextensive (alienation operates already under formal subsumption).
- **Deskilling** — [deskilling](./deskilling.md) (Braverman) is a *consequence* of one form real subsumption takes (Taylorism); it is not equivalent to real subsumption as such. Real subsumption can in principle proceed through skilled-labor reorganizations as well as through deskilling.
- **Rationalization** (Weber) — the Weberian rationalization concept covers a broader cultural-historical process; real subsumption is the capital-specific reorganization of the labor process. They overlap empirically but operate in different theoretical registers.
