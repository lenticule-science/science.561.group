*Classical organization theory* is the body of work, developed roughly between 1900 and 1980, that established the formal organization as a distinct object of study and supplied the analytical apparatus subsequent organization theory has either built on, extended, or reacted against. It comprises five recognizable phases: scientific management (Taylor), administrative principles (Fayol), the human relations school (Mayo, Roethlisberger), the decision/behavioral school (Barnard, Simon, March, Cyert), and structural contingency theory (J. D. Thompson, Galbraith, Mintzberg).

## Taylor and scientific management

Frederick Winslow Taylor's *The Principles of Scientific Management* [taylor-1911] supplied the founding manifesto of the scientific-management movement. Taylor's argument, developed across decades of consulting work in U.S. machine shops and steel mills, rests on four principles:

1. **Develop a science** for each element of work, replacing rule-of-thumb methods with systematic study (time-and-motion analysis).
2. **Scientifically select and train workers**, replacing the old practice in which workers chose their own work and trained themselves.
3. **Cooperate with workers** to ensure the scientifically developed methods are followed (wage incentives, functional foremanship).
4. **Divide work and responsibility** between management (planning) and workers (execution): "the managers assume new burdens, new duties, and responsibilities never dreamed of in the past."

The fourth principle — *the separation of conception from execution* — is what later critics (most notably Braverman in [labor-process-theory-survey](./labor-process-theory-survey.md)) identify as Taylorism's defining political move. Taylor presented it as cooperation; Braverman analyzed it as deskilling.

Taylor's influence was global and rapid: by the 1920s, "scientific management" was being applied (often under different names) in Soviet industrial planning, Italian corporatism, German rationalization movements, and U.S. Fordism. Its descendants include time-and-motion study, industrial engineering, work measurement, and contemporary lean production.

## Fayol and administrative principles

Henri Fayol, a French mining engineer and manager, published *Administration Industrielle et Générale* [fayol-1916] independently and contemporaneously with Taylor's work. Where Taylor focused on shop-floor work, Fayol focused on the *administrative function* — the management of the organization as a whole. He distinguished six categories of activity in any industrial undertaking (technical, commercial, financial, security, accounting, administrative) and defined the administrative as comprising five elements:

- **Planning** (*prévoyance*),
- **Organizing**,
- **Commanding**,
- **Coordinating**,
- **Controlling**.

This *POCCC* sequence — later codified as POSDCORB by Gulick (Planning, Organizing, Staffing, Directing, COordinating, Reporting, Budgeting) — became the foundational vocabulary of public administration and management education for half a century.

Fayol additionally proposed *fourteen principles of management* (division of work, authority, discipline, unity of command, unity of direction, subordination of individual to general interest, remuneration, centralization, scalar chain, order, equity, stability of tenure, initiative, esprit de corps). The principles are explicitly *not* universal laws but flexible guidelines whose application requires managerial judgment in each context.

## The Hawthorne studies and human relations

The *Hawthorne studies* — a series of experiments conducted at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works in Chicago between 1924 and 1932 — became the founding empirical reference for the human relations school. The original studies were designed to test the effect of physical conditions (lighting, breaks, hours) on productivity; the unexpected finding was that productivity rose when conditions were *changed in either direction*, including when changes were rolled back. The interpretation, codified by Elton Mayo [mayo-1933] and Roethlisberger and Dickson [roethlisberger-dickson-1939]: workers were responding to *being attended to*, to the *social organization* of the workplace, to the *informal group dynamics* of their work team — not to the physical conditions per se.

The "Hawthorne effect" entered general usage; the human relations school's substantive program was that informal group structure, supervisory style, and worker satisfaction are major determinants of productivity, and that scientific management's view of workers as economic-mechanical units is empirically inadequate. Subsequent re-analyses (notably by Carey and others in the 1960s and 1970s) have raised serious methodological concerns about the original studies; the school's substantive influence on management practice nonetheless was enormous.

## Barnard, Simon, and the behavioral school

Chester Barnard's *The Functions of the Executive* [barnard-1938] reframed organization as a *cooperative system* — a system of consciously coordinated activities of two or more persons. Barnard's executive functions: (1) maintain communication, (2) secure essential services from individuals, (3) formulate purpose. The *acceptance theory of authority* — that authority depends on the willingness of subordinates to accept commands as legitimate — anticipates much later organizational analysis and connects the field to Weber's [weber-bureaucracy-and-legitimacy-survey](theories/governance/texts/weber-bureaucracy-and-legitimacy-survey.md) treatment of legitimate domination.

Herbert Simon's *Administrative Behavior* [simon-1947] turned the focus to *decision-making* as the unit of organizational analysis. Simon's central concept of *bounded rationality* — that real decision-makers operate under cognitive, informational, and time constraints that preclude classical optimization, and that they instead *satisfice* (search until a "good enough" alternative is found) — became the foundation of the behavioral theory of organizations and decision-science generally. Simon won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1978.

March and Simon's [march-simon-1958] *Organizations* and Cyert and March's [cyert-march-1963] *A Behavioral Theory of the Firm* extended the framework into a general theory of organizational decision-making, introducing concepts (programmed vs. non-programmed decisions, organizational slack, dominant coalition, sequential attention to goals, problemistic search) that became standard organizational vocabulary.

## Structural contingency: Thompson, Galbraith, Mintzberg

A second post-war wave addressed *organizational structure* directly. James D. Thompson's *Organizations in Action* [thompson-jd-1967] supplied a foundational typology of *interdependence* — pooled (units share a common resource), sequential (output of one is input of next), and reciprocal (each unit's output is each other's input) — and matched each to characteristic *coordination mechanisms* (standardization, planning, mutual adjustment) of increasing cost. Thompson's argument: organizations group their most reciprocally interdependent activities most tightly and progressively loosen the grouping as interdependence becomes sequential and then pooled.

Jay Galbraith [galbraith-1973] developed an *information-processing* view of organization design, arguing that organizational structure must be matched to the uncertainty of the work it performs (more uncertainty requires more information-processing capacity). The *STAR model* — Strategy, Structure, Processes, Rewards, People — was elaborated in his subsequent design-framework work (notably *Designing Organizations*, 1995, and later editions) and became one of the standard practitioner frameworks for organization design.

Henry Mintzberg's *The Structuring of Organizations* [mintzberg-1979, mintzberg-1983] supplied the most comprehensive synthesis. Mintzberg distinguished:

- **Five basic parts of organization** — strategic apex, middle line, operating core, technostructure, support staff.
- **Five (later six) coordination mechanisms** — mutual adjustment, direct supervision, standardization of work processes, standardization of work outputs, standardization of skills (and later, standardization of norms).
- **Five organizational configurations** — simple structure, machine bureaucracy, professional bureaucracy, divisionalized form, adhocracy.

Each configuration is characterized by a dominant part, a dominant coordination mechanism, and a characteristic environmental fit. The framework remains widely taught and widely used.

## Selznick and institutional analysis

Philip Selznick's *TVA and the Grass Roots* [selznick-1949] inaugurated the *institutional* tradition within organization theory, arguing that formal organizations develop *institutional* features — value-commitments, identity, distinctive competence — beyond their rational instrumental design. Selznick's later work (*Leadership in Administration*, 1957) argued that the executive's task is precisely the institutionalization of the organization: transforming an expendable instrument into a vehicle for distinctive values. The institutional tradition was reanimated in the 1970s and 1980s by Meyer and Rowan, DiMaggio and Powell (treated in the [institutional-logics-survey](theories/governance/texts/institutional-logics-survey.md)).

## Key conceptual distinctions

Four distinctions structure the field:

1. **Conception vs. execution.** Taylor's separation; Braverman's deskilling thesis turns on whether this separation is technically necessary or politically chosen.
2. **Formal vs. informal organization.** The chart vs. the actually-followed practices; the human relations school's central finding.
3. **Bounded vs. unbounded rationality.** Simon's distinction; the foundation of behavioral economics, decision science, and the cognitive turn in organization theory.
4. **Coordination mechanism types.** Mintzberg's typology — mutual adjustment, direct supervision, the four standardizations — supplies the standard analytical vocabulary for "how is work in this organization actually held together?"

## What the tradition supplies

Three contributions:

1. **A vocabulary for the formal organization.** The structural elements (parts, coordination mechanisms, configurations), the administrative functions (Fayol's POCCC, Gulick's POSDCORB), and the decision categories (programmed/non-programmed, satisficing) supply a working analytical apparatus that subsequent theory presupposes.
2. **The reality of the informal.** The Hawthorne studies' lasting contribution is the empirical insistence that the formal chart and procedures are *not* the organization; the actual organization includes the informal social structure that the formal apparatus organizes, ignores, or exploits.
3. **A typology of coordination.** Mintzberg's coordination mechanisms remain the standard analytical apparatus for distinguishing how different organizational forms coordinate their work.
