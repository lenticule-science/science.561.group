The decade roughly 2014–2024 saw the emergence of a substantial empirical and theoretical literature on *platform work* and *algorithmic management* — labor mediated by digital platforms (Uber, TaskRabbit, Amazon Mechanical Turk, Deliveroo, Upwork, Care.com, Instacart) and managed not by human supervisors but by software systems that allocate tasks, set prices, evaluate performance, and discipline workers algorithmically. This literature both reanimates the labor process tradition (treated in the [labor-process-theory-survey](./labor-process-theory-survey.md)) for new conditions and develops a distinctive vocabulary for what is genuinely novel about platform-organized work.

## What is a platform?

Nick Srnicek's *Platform Capitalism* [srnicek-2017] supplied an early and widely-cited typology distinguishing five platform forms: *advertising platforms* (Google, Facebook), *cloud platforms* (AWS), *industrial platforms* (GE Predix), *product platforms* (Spotify), and *lean platforms* (Uber, Airbnb). The labor-platform literature focuses primarily on the lean form — platforms that own minimal physical assets and operate by *intermediating* between independent workers and customers.

Vallas and Schor's [vallas-schor-2020] *Annual Review of Sociology* article supplied the field's standard synthesis. Their definition: a platform is a digital infrastructure that *enables and structures interactions* between two or more types of users (workers, consumers, third parties), with the platform owner extracting value through fees, advertising, or data. Key analytical features: *algorithmic intermediation* (matching, pricing, ranking are software-determined), *worker classification* (typically as independent contractors rather than employees), *rating systems* (consumers evaluate workers, workers evaluate consumers), and *information asymmetry* (the platform knows far more than either workers or consumers).

## Algorithmic management

Alex Rosenblat and Luke Stark's [rosenblat-stark-2016] foundational paper on Uber drivers documented how the platform's algorithmic systems exercise *managerial control* — task allocation, performance evaluation, surge pricing, deactivation — while denying that they constitute employment. Rosenblat's [rosenblat-2018] *Uberland* extended the analysis into a book-length treatment of how the algorithm rewrites the rules of work: drivers experience the platform as a manager, but a manager that is opaque, unaccountable, and immune to the negotiation that human management permits.

Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin [kellogg-valentine-christin-2020] supplied the most systematic theoretical synthesis. Their argument: algorithmic management constitutes a *new contested terrain of control* — explicitly evoking Edwards's [edwards-1979] earlier framework — characterized by what they call the *6 R's* of algorithmic control:

1. **Restricting** — narrowing the worker's choice set.
2. **Recommending** — directing the worker toward platform-preferred actions.
3. **Recording** — capturing detailed behavioral data.
4. **Rating** — evaluating performance in real time.
5. **Replacing** — substituting workers easily through the platform's matching function.
6. **Rewarding** — adjusting compensation through algorithmically determined pricing.

Worker resistance correspondingly takes new forms — *algoactivism*, gaming of the rating system, collective sharing of platform-knowledge through driver forums, regulatory and legal challenges to worker classification — that the paper catalogs in detail.

Wood, Graham, Lehdonvirta, and Hjorth [wood-graham-lehdonvirta-hjorth-2019] documented the *autonomy paradox* of platform labor: workers report high autonomy in some dimensions (when, where, how to work) and intense algorithmic control in others (which tasks they get, what they're paid, whether they remain on the platform), with the paradox itself a defining experience of the work.

## Ghost work

Mary Gray and Siddharth Suri's *Ghost Work* [gray-suri-2019] documented the largely-invisible labor force performing the "AI" tasks — image labeling, content moderation, transcription, data cleaning — that train and supplement machine-learning systems. Workers on Mechanical Turk, Crowdflower, and similar platforms perform millions of micro-tasks for fractions of a cent each, providing the human judgment that ostensibly autonomous systems require. The *ghost* in the title is double: the workers are largely invisible to consumers (who experience the AI as a unified agent), and they are invisible to labor regulation (classified as contractors performing isolated micro-tasks rather than employees performing a job).

The book's foundational argument: a substantial and growing portion of contemporary work is structurally invisible because it has been redefined as *task* rather than *job* — a redefinition that strips it of the legal and social protections that the job category historically carried.

## The gig economy and the worker experience

Alexandrea Ravenelle's *Hustle and Gig* [ravenelle-2019] provided ethnographic depth on the worker experience across Uber, Airbnb, TaskRabbit, and Kitchensurfing. Her central finding: platform workers are categorizable into *Success Stories* (a small minority who genuinely benefit), *Strugglers* (who use platforms as desperate last resorts), and *Strivers* (the largest group, who entered platforms with hopes of entrepreneurial success and discovered themselves precarious). The platform model's promise of flexible entrepreneurship is, for the great majority, a vehicle for under-protected, low-wage labor.

Juliet Schor's [schor-2020] *After the Gig* generalized the analysis, arguing that the original "sharing economy" — peer-to-peer asset sharing among community members — was hijacked by venture-capital-backed platforms that captured the rhetoric while stripping out the redistributive substance. Her book proposes platform cooperatives, regulatory interventions, and worker organizing as paths to reclaim the sharing-economy promise.

Niels van Doorn [van-doorn-2017] supplied the gendered and racialized analysis: platform labor disproportionately recruits women and workers of color into reproductive and care work (housecleaning, child care, elder care, food delivery) under conditions that strip out the limited protections those occupations had historically achieved. The platform's "neutrality" — its ostensibly color-blind, gender-blind matching algorithm — operates against the background of pre-existing racialized and gendered labor markets that the platform reproduces.

Trebor Scholz [scholz-2017] articulated the *platform cooperativism* program — the proposal that platform technologies be retained while platform ownership be transferred to worker-cooperatives, on the grounds that the platforms' coordination capacities are valuable but their extractive ownership structures are not.

## Quantification, surveillance, and the law

Ifeoma Ajunwa's *The Quantified Worker* [ajunwa-2023] is the most comprehensive recent treatment of *workplace surveillance and quantification* — wearable devices, productivity-monitoring software, algorithmic hiring, predictive performance analytics, AI-mediated termination. Her argument: contemporary workplaces are subject to a *quantified worker regime* that gathers behavioral data far in excess of historical norms while operating largely outside the labor and privacy law frameworks that regulated earlier surveillance forms. The book documents both the regime's mechanisms and the legal/regulatory openings for its containment.

Benjamin Shestakofsky's *Behind the Startup* [shestakofsky-2024] supplied the most recent ethnographic investigation of how *venture capital itself* shapes platform-work organization, arguing that the imperatives of VC-funded growth — rapid scaling, market capture, eventual exit — drive characteristic patterns of worker classification, algorithmic management, and labor process design that are not reducible to platform technology as such.

## Key conceptual distinctions

Three distinctions structure the field:

1. **Platform vs. employer.** Platforms claim to be intermediaries (matching workers to consumers) rather than employers (organizing workers' labor); the distinction is legally consequential (different obligations, liabilities, protections) and substantively contested (the platforms exercise managerial functions while denying the responsibilities employment entails).
2. **Algorithmic vs. human management.** Algorithmic management is faster, cheaper, more granular, more opaque, and less negotiable than human management; the substitution is not neutral.
3. **Task vs. job.** The platform model fragments work into discrete tasks, each with its own price, evaluation, and contractual status; the legal and social apparatus built around the *job* category does not extend to the *task* atomization.

## What the tradition supplies

Three contributions:

1. **An empirical record of contemporary algorithmic labor.** A decade of ethnography, survey research, and platform-data analysis documents how platform work is actually organized and experienced — not the marketing rhetoric, but the lived reality.
2. **A reanimation of labor process categories.** Edwards's contested terrain, Burawoy's manufacturing of consent, Friedman's responsible autonomy — all return as analytical tools for understanding algorithmic management, with the algorithm as a new kind of manager that requires extension of the framework.
3. **A vocabulary for what is genuinely novel.** Algorithmic management, ghost work, the autonomy paradox, the 6 R's, platform cooperativism — these are field-developed concepts that name features of contemporary work the older vocabularies cannot capture.
