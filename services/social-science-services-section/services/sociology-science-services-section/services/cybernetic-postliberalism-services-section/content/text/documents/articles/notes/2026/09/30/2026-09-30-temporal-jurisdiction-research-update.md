# Temporal jurisdiction: archive and futurity

This note supports the Substack project on Vine Deloria Jr., place, time, and settler governance. It is research support rather than publishable prose.

## Main correction: archive is not the problem by itself

The working thesis has treated one recurring mechanism as the conversion of continuing relation into record, title, precedent, evidence, or historical past. That remains useful, but recent scholarship makes a necessary correction: recording a relation does not necessarily close it.

Matthew Scobie, Frida Buhre, and Catherine Dussault's 2026 article “Time back! Reclaiming Indigenous temporalities” studies temporal self-determination in Ngāi Tahu, Sámi, and Nunavik contexts. Their cases show historical records, intergenerational narration, and control over research timing being used to assert ongoing authority rather than to consign a relation to a finished past. These cases are not evidence about Lakota temporality and should not be used that way. They clarify a mechanism that can be tested elsewhere.

The sharper concept is **temporal jurisdiction**: authority over when a relation is classified as past, when a record becomes actionable, when a present condition crosses an intervention threshold, and which anticipated future can warrant action now.

This refines “territorialization of time.” The phrase should not name every act of recording, planning, forecasting, or narrating sequence. It names the production of a usable temporal field when authority over that field is detached from the relations being ordered. A past becomes available as title or precedent; a present becomes an input, threshold, or intervention window; a future becomes development, risk, security, adaptation, or salvation.

The empirical question is therefore: **who has jurisdiction over the temporal conversion, and what does that jurisdiction let them do?**

## Future versus futurity

Lindsay Martel Montgomery and Heather Law Pezzarossi's 2026 synthesis “Indigenous Futurities: Theorizing Futurity in the Past and Present” supplies another needed distinction. Futurity is not simply a date after the present. It names practices through which people imagine, calculate, perform, and open fields of possibility now.

This blocks an overstatement in the draft. The target is not future-orientation. A politics of relation necessarily has futures. The target is **future-as-tribunal**: a projected condition treated as authoritative enough to overrule present obligations and foreclose other continuations.

Their article is not an Oceti Sakowin-specific account, so it should not define Lakota or Dakota futurity. Its analytic use is narrower: settler futurity can naturalize settler continuity and Indigenous containment as inevitable, while Indigenous futurities insist on capacities to change and persist on terms not set by that chronology.

## Predictive governance: keep the claim institutional

Sigrid Hartong, Mathias Decuypere, and Steven Lewis's work on digital and predictive governance is a useful constraint. Their rhythmanalytic account does not describe predictive systems as imposing one homogeneous algorithmic time; it tracks interacting chrono-rhythms, kairo-rhythms, and “algorhythms.”

If this project later connects AI or prediction to territorializing time, the claim should therefore be demonstrated at the institutional coupling: a model produces a reference point, score, forecast, classification, or threshold, and an organization gives that output authority over what happens next. Model architecture alone does not establish the political claim.

## Draft consequence

The argument can now proceed more cleanly:

Deloria's place/time distinction → authority over the meaning of change → conversion of continuing relations into governable temporal objects → temporal jurisdiction → temporal sovereignty and futurity → predictive administration as one possible contemporary infrastructure of temporal jurisdiction.

A sentence worth testing as the article's center is:

> The question is not whether a society has history or a future. The question is who gets to decide when a relation has become past, what counts as the actionable present, and which future is allowed to command it.

## Oceti Sakowin anchor: continuity is not priority

The strongest improvement in this pass is to replace an abstract contrast between “past” and “present” with an Oceti Sakowin-specific account of continuity.

Phil Two Eagle’s 2026 message in the *Bulletin of the Ecological Society of America* is useful because it joins treaties, Creation stories, ceremonies, language, land, and future generations in one contemporary Sicangu Lakota statement. He explicitly distinguishes the claim of chronological precedence from a stronger claim of continuing origin and relation to the land. For this project, the analytic consequence is that **being earlier is not the same thing as remaining authoritative**.

This gives “temporal jurisdiction” a more concrete test. A settler chronology can acknowledge that an Indigenous nation was present earlier while still claiming that decisive jurisdiction belongs to the modern state. A continuing treaty relation contests the conversion itself: the nineteenth-century date of a treaty does not by itself make the treaty a finished event. The research question becomes whether the obligations named by the relation remain capable of determining action in the present.

This is a better Oceti Sakowin anchor than importing a generic “Indigenous cyclical time” contrast. It also lets the essay distinguish *priority* from *continuity*: priority is a location in a sequence; continuity is a claim that the relation still operates.

## Medium is not the mechanism

Sarah Hernandez’s *We Are the Stars* supplies an Oceti Sakowin-specific correction to the archive thesis. Her account links literary colonization to the displacement of Oceti Sakowin women’s authority, the appropriation and mistranslation of Dakota/Nakota/Lakota narratives, and settler land narratives. But the second half of the book follows contemporary Oceti Sakowin writers and scholars reclaiming literary traditions through writing and publication.

The research consequence is important: **print, writing, archives, and portability are politically ambivalent infrastructures**. They do not become settler capture merely because relation has been written down. Capture occurs when an institution can detach a record from the people, land, language, and authority that give it force, then administer the remainder as evidence, heritage, data, or completed history. A record can also be reactivated within a living jurisdiction.

This narrows the “conversion of relation into portable temporal object” thesis. Portability is not sufficient for capture. The stronger mechanism is **de-authorized portability**: relation becomes governable at a distance when the authority to interpret, reactivate, refuse, or continue it has been severed from the relation itself.

## Kite: place does not displace time

A close read of Suzanne Kite’s 2021 article supplies an Oceti Sakowin-specific correction that the current draft needs more explicitly. The article should not be used merely for its critique of American apocalyptic futurity. Its Lakota material makes a stronger conceptual point: **place and temporality are not opposing domains**.

Kite describes relations among earth, sky, stars, He SápA, and seasonal ceremonial practice in which spatial orientation and temporal coordination are inseparable. Constellations and places participate in the timing of movement and ceremony. This gives the project a concrete counterexample to the mistaken binary “Western time versus Indigenous place.” A place-based order can have sequence, recurrence, timing, memory, anticipation, and long duration. What distinguishes it is not the absence of temporal organization but where the authority for that organization resides.

This strengthens the concept of temporal jurisdiction in two ways. First, jurisdiction concerns **synchronization** as well as periodization: who may determine when to act, wait, move, gather, research, intervene, or refuse. Second, the key contrast is between *placed temporality* and *portable temporal authority*. A temporal form becomes available for settler-administrative capture when its authority can travel independently of the relations it coordinates and then return as an external command over them.

This suggests a useful refinement of the working mechanism:

1. Relations generate their own temporal coordinations: seasons, obligations, memories, treaty continuities, ceremonial times, ecological rhythms, and anticipated responsibilities.
2. An institution abstracts a temporal trace or rule into a portable representation: date, deadline, period, title, stage, forecast, score, or plan.
3. Portability becomes capture when authority over interpretation and timing is detached from the relations that made the representation meaningful.
4. The detached representation can then synchronize action at a distance: declaring something finished, urgent, premature, inevitable, actionable, or too late.
5. Temporal sovereignty or temporal jurisdiction is therefore not freedom from coordination; it is authority over how coordination remains answerable to relation.

A compact formulation for testing is: **territorialization of time occurs when temporal coordination is detached from relational jurisdiction and returned as an administrable command over the relations from which it was abstracted.**

This is more precise than the previous one-sentence thesis because it handles an important hard case: temporality that is highly structured and directive without being external to place.

## Ramirez: local time makes abstraction the mechanism

This pass closes the highest-priority Deloria-specific gap in the note. Rene Ramirez's 2025 article, “Local Time: A Placed-Based Account of Time in Vine Deloria Jr.'s Various Works,” has now been read closely in full in the Spring 2025 issue of *APA Studies on Native American and Indigenous Philosophy*.

Ramirez's first methodological caution matters for this project: **“local time” is his reconstruction, not a finished concept that Deloria himself explicitly defines.** Ramirez pieces it together from *God Is Red*, *Power and Place*, *The Metaphysics of Modern Existence*, and related essays. He gives the reconstruction three characteristics: time is grounded in a particular location, structured through communal relations, and cyclical in arrangement.

The cyclicality claim is more precise than the familiar “linear West / cyclical Indigenous” binary. Ramirez links cycles to the maturation and continued development of particular beings understood through Deloria and Wildcat's power/place/personality framework. Temporal patterns are therefore relative to distinct beings and relationships rather than instances of one universal Indigenous cycle. In his notes, Ramirez is explicit that maturity is always relative to the being in question and that such cycles cannot be abstracted into a general timetable.

His treatment of history is even more important for the current thesis. Ramirez argues that a spatial account of history remains possible when events remain tied to their place of occurrence and to the relations through which change is experienced. Because places are distinct, histories are multiple. His explicit clarification is that **history itself is not the problem; abstraction and delocalization are**. Linear temporality becomes politically dangerous when time is detached from land and then presented as the proper universal measure of change.

### Consequence for “territorialization of time”

Ramirez reveals a conceptual tension in the project's current term. If place-based time is *localized*, then “territorialization of time” cannot simply mean attaching time to territory. The settler mechanism is closer to a two-step operation:

1. **Delocalization / abstraction** — timing, sequence, or historical meaning is detached from the particular relations, beings, and places that generated it.
2. **Administrative reterritorialization** — the abstracted temporality is reattached to a governing field as title, precedent, periodization, developmental stage, deadline, risk category, intervention window, or forecast.

This is compatible with the Deleuzian debt already noted in the older research dossier: territorialization is not merely localization on land. The useful claim is that a state or institution can *reterritorialize abstract time* as a governable domain after stripping it from the local relations that previously authorized its meaning.

That gives the project a cleaner distinction:

- **placed/local temporality**: temporal order remains generated through and answerable to particular relations;
- **abstracted temporality**: sequence is detached from those relations and made portable;
- **administratively reterritorialized temporality**: the portable sequence is installed as an external jurisdiction over records, property, deadlines, categories, plans, or futures.

This also improves the relation between the article and the existing portability model. Portability is possible only after some degree of abstraction, but abstraction need not erase jurisdiction if governance relations travel with the representation. Capture occurs when abstraction is followed by jurisdiction-stripping reterritorialization.

### Revised compact mechanism

The mechanism can now be stated more tightly:

> **Territorialization of time is a two-step capture of temporality: relations are first delocalized into portable temporal forms, then reterritorialized inside institutions whose categories, schedules, property regimes, and forecasts determine what those forms mean and when they act. Capture occurs when the jurisdiction that generated the timing does not travel with the representation.**

This formulation preserves the project's emphasis on archive, property, prediction, and future-governance while making Deloria and Ramirez central rather than merely introductory. It also gives a principled reason not to describe every calendar, archive, cycle, forecast, or long-term plan as settler temporality.

## September 2026 climate-planning update

Angeline Letourneau and Mark C. J. Stoddart’s September 2026 *Global Environmental Change* article, “Towards a political ecology of temporalities: Indigenous pathways beyond settler time,” develops an adjacent account from research with the Tłı̨chǫ First Nation. It treats temporalities as fields of power in environmental planning and argues that technical and administrative temporalities can marginalize embodied and culturally grounded temporal knowledge. Its conclusion is especially relevant to this project: global climate urgency can itself operate as a temporal tactic that forecloses reconciliation and place-based adaptation.

This is **not** evidence about Lakota or Dakota temporality and should not be cited as such. Its use is comparative and institutional: it gives a current empirical case in which the politics of *when action must occur* affects who can authorize *what action will occur*. That helps operationalize temporal jurisdiction for climate planning without requiring a claim that all forecasting is colonial.

## Organizational tempo: inclusion can still leave the clock elsewhere

Rajiv Maher’s 2026 *Journal of Management Studies* article, “Unbottling Time,” adds an organizational mechanism that the project has only implied so far. Based on qualitative research on the 2021 occupation of a bottled-water plant in Mexico, Maher describes Indigenous Zapatista-influenced activists rejecting efficiency-driven deadlines, prioritizing ecological cycles over productivity metrics, and organizing collective decisions through practices associated with “walking at the pace of the slowest.” He theorizes these practices as “time-less organizing” and links them to temporal sovereignty.

The case is Mexican and Zapatista-influenced; it is **not** evidence about Lakota/Dakota temporality. Its value is institutional. It shows that self-determination can concern **tempo** as well as substantive outcomes. A decision process may include a community’s voice while retaining external authority over how quickly that voice must become legible, when deliberation ends, and when a decision becomes actionable.

This exposes a form of jurisdictional downgrading that content-focused models can miss. Consultation may preserve representation while stripping control over pace. The resulting pattern is:

`recognized voice + externally imposed timetable -> participation without temporal jurisdiction`

That pattern should be tested in environmental review, emergency declarations, transition planning, research protocols, and predictive administration. A deadline may be materially justified and still distribute authority asymmetrically. The relevant questions are: who sets the deadline, what relations were used to justify it, who can slow or suspend the process, and whose claims become “delay” once the clock is running?

This suggests a refinement to the project’s practical test: temporal jurisdiction includes not only **when an output becomes actionable**, but also **who controls the pace and stopping rule of the decision process that makes it actionable**. A government can consult a people and still govern their time if it alone decides when consultation has taken long enough.

## Oceti Sakowin counter-archive: history can remain joined to authority

Walaa Alqaisiya’s 2026 article “Uncis’ Life-worlds the decolonial ecologies of the matriarchs of the Oceti Sakowin” gives the project a current Oceti Sakowin-specific hard case for the archive thesis. The published abstract describes ethnographic fieldwork and a counter-archival methodology centered on Oceti Sakowin *uncis*, including Madonna Thunder Hawk, and organizes the analysis around “righting history” and “reviving sovereignty.” It connects that work to child welfare, traditional food systems, water protection, and ceremonial practice.

The research consequence is stronger than “counter-archives tell better histories.” Here historical work is described as part of the reproduction of authority and relation in the present. That means a temporal representation can remain **authorized by the relation it represents**. This is exactly the hard case the current mechanism needed: representation and portability do not entail de-authorization.

Because this pass had access to the published abstract and metadata rather than the full article, the draft should use only claims established there until the article is read closely. The source is nevertheless especially valuable because it is contemporary and Oceti Sakowin-specific rather than a comparative case imported from another Indigenous context.

## Option-form hard case: sovereignty is not consumer choice

Gavin Meyer Furrey’s 2026 “Native Voice, Settler Choice” supplies a second Oceti Sakowin-specific mechanism. In the publisher abstract, Furrey argues that proposed Oceti Sakowin charter schools in South Dakota are better understood as instruments of tribal sovereignty and community empowerment than as additional consumer options for individual families. He frames the conflict as one over tribal control of tribal education.

This connects directly to the existing emsenn argument in *A Different System is Not an Alternative*. A jurisdictional claim can be transformed without being censored: it can be admitted as an **option** inside an already-authorized decision space. The transformation is:

`authority to constitute the field -> selectable alternative inside the field`

That is a useful specification of **jurisdictional downgrading**. It is adjacent to de-authorized portability but analytically distinct. Portability asks whether a representation can travel away from the relations that produced it. Jurisdictional downgrading asks what status the receiving system gives the representation when it arrives. A treaty can become “historical evidence”; a sovereign educational project can become “school choice”; a land relation can become an “interest” to balance; a different system can become an “alternative.” In each case the content may remain visible while its capacity to authorize the decision is reduced.

This improves the territorialization-of-time mechanism because temporal conversion often works through precisely such status changes. A continuing relation is not necessarily declared dead. It may instead be assigned a bounded role in the present—input, option, heritage, precedent, stakeholder view—while another institution keeps jurisdiction over when and how it can act.

Methodological caution: this pass reviewed Furrey’s publisher abstract and bibliographic record, not the full article. The claim about why particular South Dakota officials acted as they did is Furrey’s interpretation and should remain attributed if used. The narrower point about the distinction between tribal control and consumer choice is well supported by the abstract and is the part relevant here.

## Property is a temporal conversion, not merely a spatial enclosure

R. Ben Fawcett and Ryan Walker’s 2026 *Urban Studies* commentary helps specify the property mechanism. Drawing on Heather Dorries and Robert Nichols, they treat settler property not as a pre-existing legal form that conquest merely reallocates but as something *generated through dispossession*. Nichols’s formulation, quoted by Fawcett and Walker, is especially useful for this project: those dispossessed can appear only retrospectively as “original owners” once land has already been translated into the property form.

That is close to the temporal-jurisdiction thesis but more exact than the current draft. The conversion is not:

`Indigenous property -> settler property`

It is closer to:

`ongoing land relation -> settler property regime -> retroactive description of the prior relation as an earlier ownership claim`

The final term looks backward and makes the new legal ontology seem continuous with what preceded it. This is a powerful example of de-authorized portability: relation becomes representable inside the settler system only after the system has changed what kind of thing the relation is.

For the article, this supports replacing the loose sentence “land becomes territory, then property” with a mechanism: **property manufactures a settled past whose categories make the present jurisdiction appear already decided.**

Fawcett and Walker are writing across Canadian urban cases, not Oceti Sakowin contexts. Their value here is analytic and institutional rather than ethnographic.

## Infrastructure sediments temporal jurisdiction

Andrew Curley’s 2021 study of the Central Arizona Project gives “territorialization of time” a material referent. Curley describes national infrastructures as “colonial beachheads” and, in the article’s abstract, as *temporal encroachments* on Indigenous lands and livelihoods that accumulate material and political difference over time.

This matters because the current project has mostly described temporal jurisdiction as an authority to classify, interpret, forecast, or trigger action. Curley shows another layer: **prior acts of jurisdiction can become material conditions for later decisions**. A canal, dam, grid, road, settlement, service territory, or regulatory regime persists; later actors confront it not as a fresh political choice but as an inherited fact with maintenance costs, constituencies, dependencies, and legal expectations.

That suggests a distinction between:

- **temporal representation** — record, title, precedent, deadline, forecast, stage;
- **temporal sedimentation** — the embedding of earlier decisions into durable material and institutional arrangements that constrain the next decision.

The second explains how temporal jurisdiction can persist without being continuously asserted in language. The system’s past decisions become part of the environment in which future options are evaluated.

Curley’s case is Diné and should be identified as such. It is not evidence for Lakota temporality. It is strong evidence for the settler-state mechanism the article is trying to describe.

## Hard case: anticipation can coexist with self-determination

Carla Alexandra, Carina Wyborn, Erin O’Donnell, and colleagues’ 2026 preprint on 2050 governance scenarios for the Coorong, Lower Lakes, and Murray Mouth is useful precisely because it prevents the thesis from becoming “future planning is colonial.” Their scenario framework treats **Indigenous self-determination** as one of six independent dimensions of governance architecture alongside anticipatory capacity, adaptive capacity, decentralization, multilevel coordination, and technological innovation.

The paper is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed, so it should not carry a major argumentative burden. Its value is as a hard case. It explicitly imagines anticipatory governance in which Indigenous authority is not merely an input to a state forecast but a constitutive dimension of who governs.

For this project the implication is:

> anticipation is not territorialization of time merely because it brings possible futures into present decisions.

The question is whether the governance architecture preserves or displaces jurisdiction over those decisions. A scenario process can still be colonial if Indigenous participation is consultative while the settler state retains the final authority to define objectives, thresholds, and acceptable futures. But anticipation can also be organized through self-determining authority.

This sharpens the test for predictive governance: **not “does the system forecast?” but “whose jurisdiction does the forecast operationalize?”**

## Counter-mechanism: jurisdiction-bearing portability

The newest research supplies a hard case that changes the mechanism again. The September 2026 *Guidance for Indigenous Collections and Indigenous Data*, produced by Creative Commons and Local Contexts, is designed for cultural institutions that hold Indigenous cultural materials and associated data. It explicitly rejects the assumption that copyright status or the public domain exhausts the governance question. Depending on the material and community, institutions may need to restrict access or reuse, seek free, prior, and informed consent, and involve source communities in decisions about digitization, exhibition, transfer, and repatriation.

The technical detail matters. Local Contexts Traditional Knowledge (TK) and Biocultural (BC) Labels can accompany digital objects in collection-management metadata alongside Creative Commons licensing information. The Labels communicate community-defined provenance, protocols, access conditions, and expectations for future use. They are not themselves a new state-enforceable property right, and the guidance cautions that they are not a legal mandate. Their value for this project is analytic: **portability can be engineered so that a representation continues to carry evidence of, and practical hooks for, the authority of the relation from which it came.**

This gives us a necessary distinction:

- **jurisdiction-bearing portability**: a representation travels while source-community protocols, permissions, provenance, and governance claims remain attached and continue to constrain interpretation, access, reuse, or future circulation;
- **jurisdiction-stripping portability**: a representation travels after the receiving system has severed, downgraded, or ignored those governance relations, leaving the representation available to be interpreted and acted upon under rules set elsewhere.

The distinction is not equivalent to “good metadata versus bad metadata.” It concerns whether metadata and institutional procedure merely describe the source or preserve a route through which the source community continues to exercise authority. A record can be richly described and still be de-authorized. Conversely, digital circulation need not by itself transform living relation into dead evidence.

This is a direct correction to the earlier phrase **de-authorized portability**. Portability should now be treated as a branching point rather than as a step that naturally tends toward capture. The empirical question is what travels with the representation: content alone, or content together with continuing authority over its interpretation and future use.

### AI governance makes the same distinction at another scale

Jacob Prehn, Bronwyn Carlson, Maggie Walter, Ray Lovett, and Bobby Maher's 2026 article on Indigenous Data Sovereignty and AI provides a parallel contemporary governance case. Their argument is Australian and comparative, not evidence about Oceti Sakowin temporality. Its importance here is that it blocks technological determinism. They define Indigenous data sovereignty in terms of authority over the creation, interpretation, and use of data concerning Indigenous peoples, communities, and lands. They also argue that AI's relation to Indigenous self-determination depends on who defines system purpose, curates data, and governs outputs, not on architecture alone.

That supports a tighter version of the predictive-governance claim. A forecast, risk score, classification, or generated continuation does not territorialize time merely because it represents a possible future. The political mechanism appears when an institution gives that output authority over present action while the people and relations being acted upon lack corresponding authority over the system's purpose, categories, data, thresholds, interpretation, or downstream use.

This suggests a practical test for future case studies:

1. Who authorizes the data or record to travel?
2. What provenance, protocols, permissions, or jurisdictional claims travel with it?
3. Who defines the category, score, forecast, or continuation produced from it?
4. Who decides when the output becomes actionable?
5. Can the originating relation refuse, reinterpret, delay, or redirect the action?

The first two questions concern **jurisdiction-bearing portability**. The last three test whether predictive administration converts portability into external temporal command.

## Legal hard case: temporal ratification in *City of Sherrill*

This pass adds a concrete U.S. legal mechanism that the current draft had not yet isolated. In *City of Sherrill v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York*, 544 U.S. 197 (2005), the Supreme Court rejected the Oneida Nation’s attempt to treat reacquired parcels within its historic reservation as immediately restored to sovereign tax status. The majority emphasized the long lapse of time, generations of state and local governance, changed conditions, and settled expectations, invoking laches, acquiescence, and impossibility.

The doctrinal caution matters. *Sherrill* did not simply hold that “old Indigenous claims expire,” nor did it adjudicate the validity of the Oneida Nation’s underlying historic land claim in the abstract. The case concerned a particular present remedy and jurisdictional consequence. The analytic point for this project is narrower: **elapsed time and institutions built during that time were made relevant to what jurisdiction could be exercised now.**

That suggests a useful sub-mechanism of temporal sedimentation: **temporal ratification**. A contested arrangement persists; institutions, property expectations, residence, budgets, and governance accumulate around it; the accumulated consequences are then treated as reasons that changing jurisdiction would be disruptive; and that anticipated disruption becomes a reason to preserve the arrangement. Persistence is converted into normative weight.

Kathryn E. Fort’s 2009 and 2011 analyses call the doctrine that grew from *Sherrill* the “new laches.” Fort argues that the Second Circuit’s use of *Sherrill* transformed equitable considerations into a defense broad enough to defeat Indian land claims even where the underlying legal right might otherwise survive. Her phrase “creating title where none existed” is especially useful for the present project because it names a temporal transformation without requiring the stronger claim that time literally legalizes the original dispossession. The safer formulation is: **duration can help convert an established settler jurisdiction into the legally protected baseline against which restoration is judged disruptive.**

This sharpens the six-stage mechanism already developed here. Between sedimentation and governance-at-a-distance, add a ratification feedback:

5a. Earlier decisions are sedimented into property, infrastructure, administrative routines, and expectations.

5b. The persistence of those sedimented arrangements is redescribed as settled reality, reliance, repose, or the avoidance of disruption.

5c. Those temporal facts are then allowed to weigh against restoring the jurisdiction displaced by the original arrangement.

The loop is recursive: prior exercise of jurisdiction produces the conditions that later count as evidence for continuing that jurisdiction.

### Current comparative legal scholarship

Rawnak Miraj Ul Azam, Ashiqur Rahman, Ishtiak Abdullah, and Kazi Tamrin Rashed’s 2026 *Time & Society* article, “Beyond the horizon of finality,” independently argues that procedural devices such as statutes of limitation and causation rules do not merely operate inside neutral time but construct what counts as an actionable legal event and when a claim is considered closed. Their proposed “polytemporal legality” is much broader than this project and synthesizes multiple Indigenous traditions with chronobiology and complexity theory. It should therefore be used only as comparative legal-temporality scholarship, not as evidence about Oceti Sakowin thought. Its value is that it makes procedural timing itself visible as legal infrastructure.

## Revised mechanism

The working mechanism is now more specific, and portability is a branching point rather than a one-way path toward capture:

1. A relation continues to generate obligations, capacities, timing, or jurisdiction.
2. Some aspect of the relation is **abstracted or delocalized** into a portable temporal form: record, title, precedent, category, forecast, score, stage, or plan. The abstraction can preserve jurisdiction, but it creates the possibility that meaning will travel separately from the relations that generated it.
3. Portability can remain **jurisdiction-bearing** when provenance, protocols, permissions, and governance claims travel with the representation and preserve a practical route for the originating relation to govern interpretation or future use.
4. Territorializing capture begins when portability becomes **jurisdiction-stripping**. A receiving institution can perform **jurisdictional downgrading**, preserving the representation while recoding an authority as an input, option, interest, heritage object, precedent, or stakeholder position inside a field whose rules are set elsewhere.
5. The decisive step is **de-authorization**: the receiving institution claims the right to determine what the representation means, when it becomes actionable, which future it may authorize, and the pace or stopping rule through which competing claims must be resolved.
6. The resulting decision may then be **sedimented** into durable arrangements—property, infrastructure, budgets, service territories, administrative routines, legal dependencies—that make the prior decision part of the conditions for later decisions.
7. Sedimentation can produce **temporal ratification**: the persistence of those arrangements is redescribed as settled reality, reliance, repose, or avoidance of disruption, and those temporal facts acquire weight against restoring displaced jurisdiction.
8. The system can now govern at a distance and across time: the relation is assigned to a settled past, an actionable present, or a constrained field of futures while the relation's own authority over continuation is displaced.

This suggests a sharper one-sentence thesis:

> **Territorialization of time is a two-step capture of temporality: relations are first delocalized into portable temporal forms, then reterritorialized inside institutions whose categories, schedules, property regimes, and forecasts determine what those forms mean, when they act, and how quickly competing claims must become legible. Capture occurs when the jurisdiction that generated the timing does not travel with the representation; the resulting decisions can then sediment into durable arrangements and later ratify the authority that produced them.**

That sentence remains a hypothesis, not a definition imposed on every case. The strongest counterexamples are now part of the model rather than exceptions to be explained away: treaty enforcement, community-controlled archives, Local Contexts-style governance metadata, Indigenous data sovereignty, and anticipatory systems operating under Indigenous jurisdiction all show that records, portability, planning, and prediction can remain coupled to relational authority.
