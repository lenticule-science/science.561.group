The [fascist grammar](../../../glossary/terms/fascist-grammar.md) entry now concedes that technocratic crisis management is a genuine exception and predicts that a technocratic grammar "will acquire the fascist middle at the moment its accounting fails to legitimate." That prediction is not yet falsifiable, because "fails to legitimate" is read off the outcome: if the middle appears, the accounting must have failed; if not, it must still be working. This note replaces the outcome-word with two conditions checkable before the speech is given, and so bounds the grammar to a kind of legitimacy crisis rather than a set of steps.

The thesis: the middle four steps are installed only when two conditions hold together. First, the people who bear the cost hold an instrument that can stop the policy — an election in which the policy is the question, a referendum, a parliamentary vote that must be won. Second, the ledger has visibly failed, so that the cost can no longer be presented as a price that buys the promised outcome. Under the first alone, cost still legitimates, because the cost-bearers can see it buying something. Under the second alone, no legitimation is needed, because the cost-bearers cannot stop anything. Only when a public that must consent is asked to pay for a ledger that does not balance does cost become useless as a currency, and standing — suffering converted into desert — the only currency left. Technocratic grammar is therefore not a disguise but the grammar of crises resolved without the consent of those who pay, which is exactly the arrangement [Slobodian](../bibliographies/slobodian2018.md) describes as encasement.

The strongest objection is the entry's own Federal Reserve case, read against this thesis rather than for it. Powell's 2022 Jackson Hole speech was addressed to a mass public, not to peers. That public bore the cost in rents, layoffs, and mortgage rates, could not audit a Taylor rule, and disapproved of the Fed at record levels through 2023. If mass address to an unauditing, cost-bearing public were the trigger, the middle steps should have appeared, and they did not — nor did any rival voice install them: no candidate won on inflation as the work of a corrupt element sacrificed for by a pure community. The exception looks like a refutation of the bounding, not a confirmation.

The answer is that the objection conflates addressing a public with requiring its consent. Powell forecast pain; he did not ask permission for it, and no instrument existed by which permission could be withheld. The chair's term was secure, the rate was on no ballot, and the mandate ran to Congress, which had delegated it precisely so it would not have to be re-won. The first condition was absent. So was the second: inflation fell, monthly, in a number anyone could read, so the cost visibly bought the outcome. The thesis predicts what occurred, and something the entry's outcome-reading cannot: that the Fed's grammar would have stayed technocratic however far approval fell, so long as neither condition turned.

The eurozone case, which the entry uses as its confirmation, is better read as a test of the two conditions separately. In 2010 the ledger had not yet visibly failed and the Greek public held no instrument the troika needed; cost-accounting held. By 2015 both conditions had turned, but for a different public. Greek GDP had fallen by a quarter and debt-to-GDP had risen despite austerity, so the ledger had visibly failed for everyone; and the German Bundestag had to vote a third bailout in August, with a party founded on refusing exactly that vote already polling. The public whose consent was now required was the German taxpayer, and the moralized rhetoric — *Schuld* as debt and guilt, Greeks who had not done their homework, a union whose future justified the pain — was aimed at them. The middle was installed by the voice that had to win the instrument, and it named as corrupt the party that had none.

This corrects the entry's prediction in one respect. It is not "the same institutional voice" that acquires the middle but whichever voice competes for the instrument the cost-bearers hold. Where the technocratic speaker is also the candidate, as Schäuble was, the voices coincide. Where the technocratic speaker is insulated and a rival is not, the middle is supplied by the rival: Brüning's deflation decrees of 1930 to 1932 stayed in the grammar of necessity, and German suffering was converted into standing by the party that took the Reichstag elections his cabinet could not avoid. The grammar does not migrate into the technocrat; it fills the vacancy the technocrat leaves, which is what [Hall](../bibliographies/hall1979.md) means by consent being won rather than assumed.

The claim is refuted by a case in which both conditions hold and no voice competing for the instrument installs the middle within one electoral cycle, or by a case in which the middle is installed while either condition is absent — a central bank with secure independence and a falling inflation print that nevertheless moralizes the suffering it causes, or a mass electorate held to a visibly failed austerity by accounting alone. Ireland, 2010 to 2013, is where the second refutation should be sought, using the entry's own test: strike the suffering from the government's speech and ask whether the argument for continuing weakens. If it does not, the grammar has a second exception and this note is wrong about why the first holds.
