## Why this is central

[Describing Californication](../notes/describing-californication.md) names "Nikolas Rose" as the sole attribution for the "responsibilization process: reframes systemic problems as personal self-management," and [Responsibilization](../../../glossary/terms/responsibilization.md) states the concept "originates in Michel Foucault's account of neoliberal governmentality, developed further by Nikolas Rose" — until now, no entry in this corpus supported that attribution. *Governing the Soul* is the book where Rose makes that case at length: it traces how the "psy" disciplines — psychology, psychiatry, psychotherapy, social work, personnel management — became technologies of government that constitute the modern subject as a private, interior self one is obligated to know, monitor, and improve. Rose's governing image, given in the title, is that liberal rule increasingly operates by "governing the soul" rather than commanding the body: expertise multiplies at a distance from the state (in the clinic, the workplace, the school, the therapist's office) and recruits each person into working on themselves as the object of government. That is the mechanism the note's responsibilization process names — systemic conditions displaced onto techniques of self-management — applied here specifically to the californicated subject's crisis-coping.

## What it does not settle

Rose's book is a historical account of Anglophone (chiefly British) psy-expertise from the late nineteenth century through the 1980s; it says nothing about digital platforms, affect theory, genre, or California as a place, so the note's extension of "responsibilization" into spectacle, platform algorithms, and the savior-slave subject is the note's own synthesis, not a claim this book makes. I should also flag directly that I am not fully certain the word "responsibilization" itself is a term Rose foregrounds by name in this particular book, as opposed to in his later essays on advanced liberalism (with Peter Miller and elsewhere) — the mechanism is unmistakably Rose's argument here, but the exact terminology's first appearance in his corpus is a detail I cannot verify precisely and would rather flag than misstate. Separately, this is Rose's revised edition (Free Association Books, 1999); the book was first published earlier (commonly cited as 1985 or 1989, by Routledge), and I cannot confirm the exact original year or what was changed between editions without inventing a publication history I don't actually know.
