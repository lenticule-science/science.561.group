## Why this is central

The term states that "spectacular diffusion (Guy Debord)" is one of the six processes through which californication operates, glossed as "spreading a place-fantasy through images that organize social relations" such that "California-as-image produces desire for a form of life that displaces the structural conditions it depends on" — but until this entry the corpus never cited the work that mechanism is drawn from. The Society of the Spectacle supplies it directly: Debord's argument is that under advanced capitalism, lived social relations are progressively displaced by relations to and between images, stated in the book's most quoted formulation as something close to "the spectacle is not a collection of images, but a social relation between people that is mediated by images." Read onto California, this is exactly the operation the term needs — the Gold Rush myth, Hollywood, and Silicon Valley are diffused as image rather than argued as fact, and the image does the work of producing desire for a form of life while the material conditions (extraction, land theft, labor) that produce it recede from view. This grounds, rather than merely names, the mechanism californication.md has relied on since it first listed Debord.

## What it does not settle

Debord wrote a general theory of the image-mediated commodity society in France in 1967 — originally published as *La Société du spectacle* by Buchet-Chastel — and says nothing about California, the Spanish missions, or Silicon Valley; the application of "spectacular diffusion" to that specific American lineage is the corpus's own synthesis, not a case Debord analyzes. The book also predates networked and algorithmic media entirely — its "spectacle" is broadcast, cinematic, and print — so it cannot by itself supply the mechanism californication.md separately and correctly attributes to the platformal process (Wendy Chun); the term is right to keep these as distinct processes rather than collapsing platform selection into Debord's diffusion. Existing English translations of the book (Black & Red, Nicholson-Smith, Knabb, among others) also render Debord's numbered theses differently enough that any specific phrase should be read as translation-dependent rather than as one fixed, citable quotation, which is why no page number is given here.
