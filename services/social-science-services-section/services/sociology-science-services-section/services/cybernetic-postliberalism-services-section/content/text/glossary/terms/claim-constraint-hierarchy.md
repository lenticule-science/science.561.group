A system publishes claims about itself — jobs it will bring, standards it will meet, laws it will follow. Most analysis treats these as statements of intent and argues about whether the intent is sincere. The claim-constraint hierarchy asks a different, and answerable, question instead: does this claim constrain any operation the system actually performs, and if the claim is violated, does the system behave differently?

emsenn's [2025-12-25 log on Mathy Monday](../../../../../../../../../../../public-services-section/services/personal-services-section/emsenn-services-section/content/text/logs/personal-logs/2025/12/25/2025-12-25-emsenn-personal-log-about-mathy-monday.md) develops this against a proposed data-center development, treating the approving city not as an agent but as a system performing a finite, listable set of operations (zoning approvals, environmental review classification, permit issuance, enforcement, appeals, court review) and sorting the project's claims by whether they touch those operations:

- **Decorative claims** ("this project will bring jobs," "this will be good for the local economy") constrain no operation. Nothing in the system checks them; nothing breaks if they fail. They frame the project narratively without steering the machine.
- **Aspirational claims** ("the project will meet environmental standards") are sometimes consulted — they appear in reports and meetings — but without thresholds wired to automatic consequences, a violation is absorbed and the system continues unchanged.
- **Structural claims** (which environmental review pathway applies, whether the review was properly scoped, whether a legal requirement was met) directly constrain operations. A violation pauses a permit, forces a study to be redone, or draws a court in — and a resolution changes what the system does next time, not just this once. These claims create feedback; the other two do not.

The log's further move is to refuse the tempting conclusion that a system could simply choose to enforce more of its aspirational claims. Enforcing material outcomes (job creation) is expensive — it requires measurement, attribution, monitoring, and punishment — while enforcing procedural correctness is cheap, legible, and court-compatible. Under strain, a system does not decide to neglect outcomes; it sheds the expensive constraint and hardens the cheap one, because the system is structurally incapable of absorbing outcome-level enforcement without destabilizing itself. In control-theoretic terms, outcome variables go under-regulated while internal state transitions go over-regulated: "compliance replaces performance," "high-cost feedback loops are cut, and low-cost invariants are preserved." A claim's place in the hierarchy is not a judgment about honesty; it is a measurement of which feedback loop the claim is wired into, if any.

The same test applies to the [choke-point governance](./choke-point-governance.md) reading of sanctions and export controls: an insurance requirement, a benchmark reference price, or a certification standard is a structural claim (violating it changes what a shipment, a chip, or a transaction can do next), while a stated policy goal ("reduce dependence," "protect national security") sits at the decorative or aspirational level above it, doing no governing work of its own.
