Both traditions agree that liberalism is finished. They disagree about what killed it, and the disagreement is not a matter of emphasis — it determines whether the diagnosis needs a villain.

## Corruption versus condition

**Postliberalism**, in its dominant right-wing form, names a moral failure with an identifiable cause. Patrick Deneen's argument in [*Why Liberalism Failed*](../bibliographies/deneen2018.md) (2018) is that liberalism did not fall short of its promises but delivered on them, and delivery was the catastrophe: individual autonomy, procedural neutrality, and market rationality dissolved the communal, familial, and religious bonds that made a shared life legible as good. Adrian Vermeule's [common-good constitutionalism](../bibliographies/vermeule2022.md) (2022) supplies the remedy Deneen's diagnosis leaves open: judges and administrators should read positive law toward a substantive account of the good — peace, justice, abundance — rather than toward procedural neutrality, which means the administrative state is not liberalism's overreach but its most useful unclaimed inheritance. **Rod Dreher**'s answer runs the opposite direction, toward strategic withdrawal into intentional, tradition-bound community rather than capture of existing institutions, but the diagnosis underneath is the same one Deneen states: a prior order existed, something corrupted it, and the corruption is reversible by the right agents acting on the right target.

Cybernetic postliberalism needs no corrupting agent, because it locates the instability inside the system's own operation rather than in something done to it from outside. [Norbert Wiener's](../bibliographies/wiener1950.md) cybernetics and [Stafford Beer's](../bibliographies/beer1972.md) viable system model describe regulation achieved through nested feedback rather than command — a structure that maintains itself by continuously generating and absorbing disturbance, not by achieving a stable prior state and then defending it. [Niklas Luhmann's](../bibliographies/luhmann1984.md) account of social systems as self-referential and operationally closed removes the corrupting outsider entirely: a system reproduces itself through its own communications, indifferent to the intentions of the people caught inside it. What Deneen reads as secularism's assault on community, [Elizabeth Povinelli's](../bibliographies/povinelli2011.md) *Economies of Abandonment* (2011) reads as late liberalism's ordinary operation — governance withdrawing obligation from populations it no longer needs to legitimate itself to, which produces the same felt abandonment without requiring an enemy who did it. Where restoration needs a villain, [structural contradiction](../../../glossary/terms/structural-contradiction.md) needs none.

## No remedy, only grammar

Cybernetic postliberalism is deliberately not a program; it prescribes no return and no capture, because it treats "prescribe a return" as itself the object of analysis rather than a live option. Its target is [fascist grammar](../../../glossary/terms/fascist-grammar.md): the six-step sequence — crisis, pure community, corrupt enemy, suffering as sacrifice, destiny, inevitability — that generates statements regardless of the politics filling them in. Deneen's crisis-and-return argument runs the sequence with liberal ideology as the corrupt enemy; **the friend/enemy distinction** that **Carl Schmitt** formalized supplies its antagonistic core, letting Vermeule's institutional program and Dreher's withdrawal both stand as competing answers to the same "who did this to us" that the grammar requires. **Producerism**'s division of the social body into builders and parasites does the same load-bearing work inside common-good rhetoric that it did in Reagan's makers-versus-takers, sorting the restored community from what it excludes — a sorting [Georges Bataille's](../bibliographies/bataille1949.md) general economy already refutes on its own terms, since *The Accursed Share* (1949) argues that every economy must expend surplus without productive return as a condition of its functioning, not a corruption of it; producerism mistakes a general economy for a restricted one and calls the mistake virtue. [Stuart Hall's](../bibliographies/hall1979.md) account of authoritarian populism gives the missing mechanism for how a restoration narrative recruits mass consent without recruiting mass understanding: crisis rhetoric manufactures the common sense that makes redirecting state power toward a substantive good feel like liberation rather than capture.

Liberalism's own [harm governance](../../../glossary/terms/harm-governance.md) — managing the gap between promise and performance by administering harm rather than resolving it — is the same grammar run without a villain: crisis without an enemy, sacrifice without destiny, inevitability asserted through procedure rather than prophecy. Cybernetic postliberalism does not adjudicate between liberalism and its postliberal critics; it shows both reaching for the identical rhetorical scaffolding when their legitimacy is under stress, one filling the scaffolding with an enemy and the other refusing to. The two postliberalisms agree an ending has arrived; they disagree about whether an ending requires someone to blame, and cybernetic postliberalism's wager is that it does not.
