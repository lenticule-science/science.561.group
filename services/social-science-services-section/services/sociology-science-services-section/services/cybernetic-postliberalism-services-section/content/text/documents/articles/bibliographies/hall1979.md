## Why this is central

[Describing californication](../notes/describing-californication.md) lists Stuart Hall among the theorists the framework "draws on," alongside Jameson, Debord, and others, but nothing in the bibliography actually cites him — this entry grounds that debt. Written months before Margaret Thatcher's May 1979 election, "The Great Moving Right Show" reads the era's swing toward law-and-order politics — moral panic over crime, "permissiveness," and national decline — not as old Toryism reasserting itself but as a new hegemonic project that wins popular consent for an authoritarian state response while continuing to speak in the register of populism, family, and "common sense." This is the essay in which Hall begins working out what he and his collaborators would soon name **authoritarian populism**: a formation that secures mass consent for stronger discipline and stronger state power without requiring fascism's classical apparatus of a single mass party or the abolition of parliamentary rule. Read against [fascist grammar](../../../glossary/terms/fascist-grammar.md)'s six-step sequence, Hall's Thatcherism runs several steps almost exactly — crisis is declared (the crime wave, the "enemy within"), a pure community is named (the nation, the hardworking family), enemies are denounced as parasitic or permissive, and national renewal is promised — while conspicuously not carrying the sequence through to steps five and six's full closure, since the promised destiny is delivered through, not against, electoral consent.

## What it does not settle

Hall wrote this essay for a specific object, British politics on the eve of a single election, and does not present his reading as a transhistorical rhetorical grammar; the six-step sequence emsenn abstracts is emsenn's own generalization from material Hall analyzes historically, not a set of "steps" Hall himself lays out. I am not certain whether the exact phrase "authoritarian populism" already appears in this essay or was coined in Hall's subsequent pieces — he kept developing and defending the term through the early 1980s, including in a well-known exchange with Bob Jessop and others in *New Left Review* in 1985 — and rather than invent a precise coinage date I flag that uncertainty plainly. Most importantly, Hall's polemical point in that later exchange was that authoritarian populism is not fascism: it strengthens and works through the very representative institutions fascism abolishes, so whether a formation that runs fascist grammar's crisis-to-renewal steps while stopping short of the sequence's full inevitability-closure counts as a variant of that grammar or a categorically different modern formation is exactly the question this essay does not, on its own, resolve.
