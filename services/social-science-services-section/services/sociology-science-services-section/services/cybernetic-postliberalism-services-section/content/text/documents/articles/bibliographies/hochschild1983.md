## Why this is central

This is the unlisted source behind a term the corpus's own [californication](../../../glossary/terms/californication.md) entry names outright: "Affective normalization (Arlie Russell Hochschild): standardizing authentic feeling through feeling rules that prescribe which affects count as appropriate responses to structural crisis." Hochschild's actual argument, built from fieldwork with Delta flight attendants (and, as a contrast case, bill collectors trained to project the opposite affect), is that feeling itself is organized by socially and institutionally administered "feeling rules" — norms that specify not just how a feeling should be displayed but which feeling one ought to have — and that employers can demand, train, and extract this "emotion work" as a condition of the job, whether through "surface acting" (managing the outward display) or "deep acting" (inducing the feeling itself). The book's subtitle is its thesis in miniature: the private capacity for authentic feeling becomes a commercialized resource. Every downstream use of "feeling rules" across this section's terms — californication, responsibilization, coherent confusion, genre calibration, industrial intellectualism, the neurotic platformal intellectual — is drawing on this specific finding: that which affects count as legitimate is administered, not natural, and that the administration is itself a form of labor.

## What it does not settle

Hochschild's case studies are paid, waged service work inside a commercial employment relation — flight attendants selling warmth, debt collectors selling threat — not digital platforms, activist genre expectations, or scholarly production. The corpus's extensions of "feeling rules" to platform algorithms, abolitionist narrative arcs, or the affective demands on public intellectuals are applications the corpus builds on top of her framework, not cases the book itself analyzes. Nor does Hochschild use the compound term "affective normalization"; that is the corpus's own coinage grafted onto her "feeling rules" and "emotion management." Whether a wage-labor account of commercialized feeling transfers cleanly to settings with no employer and no wage — a platform's algorithm, a genre's audience — is a substitution the corpus makes and Hochschild's book does not itself argue for.

## Bridges

- [Californication](../../../glossary/terms/californication.md) — where "affective normalization" is defined by explicit appeal to this book, without a citation to it.
- [Responsibilization](../../../glossary/terms/responsibilization.md) — names Hochschild's feeling rules as "the affective prescriptions that enforce responsibilized conduct."
- [Industrial intellectualism](../../../glossary/terms/industrial-intellectualism.md) — extends the flight-attendant case directly: "Just as Hochschild showed how flight attendants are required not merely to perform cheerfulness but to cultivate genuine warmth as a condition of employment, emsenn argues that scholars, journalists, and public intellectuals are required to produce analysis that satisfies the affective expectations of their genre."
