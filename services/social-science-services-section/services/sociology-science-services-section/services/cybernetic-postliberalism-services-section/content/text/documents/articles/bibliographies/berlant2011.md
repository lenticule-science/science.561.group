## Why this is central

Berlant supplies the affective mechanism the note needs to explain why the institutional worker stays attached to a day job whose systemic violence they can plainly see: cruel optimism names an attachment to an object — upward mobility, legal protection, moral innocence — that is itself what blocks the flourishing it promises. Without this concept the note's account of institutional complicity would have to fall back on false consciousness or simple self-interest; Berlant's framing lets the note treat the attachment as structurally produced and genuinely felt, not merely mistaken.

## What it does not settle

*Cruel Optimism* is a study of affect and genre — the "impasse," "slow death," the situation-comedy of ordinary crisis — under conditions of late-liberal capitalism generally; it is not a book about settler colonialism, artificial intelligence, or endangered-language preservation, and it offers no criterion for distinguishing a "cruelly optimistic" attachment from one that is merely disappointing or difficult. The application to the specific asymmetry this note describes (day-job compliance versus AI-bootstrapped language work) is the note's own synthesis, not a case Berlant analyzes or a test her framework is built to adjudicate.
