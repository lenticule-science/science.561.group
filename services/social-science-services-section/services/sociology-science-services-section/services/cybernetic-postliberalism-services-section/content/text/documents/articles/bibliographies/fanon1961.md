## Why this is central

Fanon's account of the horizontal violence the colonized direct at one another — aggression generated by domination, discharged sideways against reachable peers rather than upward against an colonizer too dangerous to confront directly — supplies the mechanism the note needs for why the institutional worker's moral outrage lands on the bootstrapper rather than on the institution that employs them.

## What it does not settle

Fanon does not use the term "lateral violence" himself; that label belongs to later Indigenous and community-health scholarship that built on his account and applied it specifically to violence within Indigenous communities living under ongoing settler-colonial conditions — the bibliography carrier and glossary term for [lateral violence](../../../../../../critical-theory-science-services-section/services/povinellian-services-section/services/settler-colonialism-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/lateral-violence.md) mark this lineage explicitly. Fanon's own discussion is embedded in the context of anticolonial war and the psychology of colonized subjects under direct colonial violence; it is not a theory of institutional employment, digital platforms, or AI-era peer policing, and applying it to those domains is this note's extension rather than a case Fanon treats.
