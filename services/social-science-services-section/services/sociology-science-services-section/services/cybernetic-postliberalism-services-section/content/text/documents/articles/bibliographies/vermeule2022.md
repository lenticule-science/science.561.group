## Why this is central

Adrian Vermeule, a Harvard Law professor, expanded his controversial 2020 essay "Beyond Originalism" into this book-length argument that American constitutional law should be read, in the classical tradition of *ius naturale*, as inherently ordered toward a substantive common good rather than toward neutral procedure or individual autonomy. This is the fullest jurisprudential statement of the strand the corpus's own notes gesture at when they name Vermeule as proof that fascist grammar can generate "postliberal Catholic integralism" rather than only fascism proper: crisis is located in liberal proceduralism's moral vacancy, the corrupt enemy is legal positivism and originalism themselves, and the promised destiny is a restored, morally competent state empowered to legislate for order, health, and public morals. It gives the corpus's illiberal-legalism strand an actual legal mechanism — a "common good" test licensing the state to override individual liberty claims — where the corpus's own terms had so far only asserted that such a mechanism exists.

## What it does not settle

Vermeule does not specify who counts as a "competent," virtuous ruler entitled to determine the common good, nor how a judge's substantive moral judgment differs in practice from the symbolic declaration the corpus's law-as-control names as one governance style among others rather than a neutral legal method. The book was contested on publication by originalists and legal positivists on the right as much as by liberals on the left, so "common good constitutionalism" names a live, unsettled argument inside conservative legal thought, not an established doctrine the corpus can treat as settled fact. Vermeule also does not engage cybernetic or feedback models of governance at all; reading his book as one instance of a generative grammar that also produces fascism and left populism is the corpus's own analytic move, not a comparison Vermeule draws himself. I am not certain whether the book's central arguments first appeared only in the 2020 Atlantic essay or were substantially reworked for this edition, and say so here rather than asserting a precise publication history.
