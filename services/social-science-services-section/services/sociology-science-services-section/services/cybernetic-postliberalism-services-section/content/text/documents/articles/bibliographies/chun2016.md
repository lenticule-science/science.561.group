## Why this is central

Chun's book argues that networked media are experienced less as rupture than as habit: we do not adopt new media once and stay changed, we *update* — software, profiles, platforms, selves — over and over, and it is exactly this repetition, not any single transformation, that lets a "you" remain recognizable to a network across constant small changes. That is the uncited engine behind [californication.md](../../../glossary/terms/californication.md)'s claim that "programmability — users updating themselves to remain visible — becomes the dominant mode of self-regulation" under **platformal process**: Chun's title phrase, "update to remain the same," names precisely that loop before the corpus names it as platform self-regulation. It also grounds [telemetricism.md](../../../glossary/terms/telemetricism.md)'s uncited **media synchronization theory** ("coherence is preserved through rhythm, not meaning"), since Chun's chapters on habit and crisis describe networked time as a chronic cycle of outage-and-patch whose coherence is sustained by the rhythm of updating itself, independent of what any given update means or fixes.

## What it does not settle

Chun does not use the terms "californication," "platformal process," "telemetricism," or "media synchronization theory" — these are the corpus's own coinages applied on top of her habit/crisis framework, not claims she makes about platforms or telemetric labor specifically. Her argument is pitched at networked media broadly (memory, search, the "imagined network" as a descendant of imagined communities) in 2016, before the recommendation-algorithm platforms that californication.md is centrally about had taken their current shape, so the extension to algorithmic selection for "platform-compatible values" is the corpus's own move, not one she makes explicitly. I'm also not certain which exact chapter title best matches the corpus's paraphrase of "rhythm not meaning," so I have not attributed a specific chapter or page to that phrase.
