Fascist grammar is a term emsenn introduces in "A storm is a storm is a storm" (2025-09-22) to describe the rhetorical sequence that fascist political speech follows regardless of its surface vocabulary. The grammar consists of six steps:

1. **Declare crisis** — assert that the present moment is one of existential threat.
2. **Name a pure community** — identify the group whose survival is at stake.
3. **Denounce enemies as sterile or corrupt** — characterize opponents as agents of decay who contaminate the community from within or without.
4. **Reframe suffering as sacrifice** — convert the community's pain into evidence of its moral standing.
5. **Promise destiny** — assert that the community's future greatness is assured if it acts now.
6. **Assert inevitability** — collapse the distinction between what should happen and what will happen.

emsenn's central argument is that this grammar persists across vocabularies. Joseph Goebbels built it around Volk and national humiliation. Postliberal intellectuals build it around "the West" and martyrdom. The same sequence can appear in queer-progressive idioms, as emsenn demonstrates by tracing it through COVID-era public health [discourse](../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/discourse.md).

The grammar survived 1945 through what emsenn calls "strategic forgetting" — the process by which fascist categories were laundered into post-war conservative [discourse](../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/discourse.md), stripped of their explicit associations while retaining their structural function. [Carl Schmitt](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/knowledge-services-section/services/research-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/carl-schmitt.md) and his [friend/enemy distinction](../../../../../critical-theory-science-services-section/services/postliberalism-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/friend-enemy-distinction.md) provided much of the conceptual infrastructure for this laundering: the grammar needs an enemy, and [Schmitt](../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/knowledge-services-section/services/research-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/carl-schmitt.md)'s framework made enmity foundational to politics without requiring racial vocabulary.

emsenn contrasts fascist grammar with what they call "[anarchist](../../anarchism/index.md) grammar," which follows a different sequence: condition, [commons](../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/commons.md), relation, solidarity, desire, immanence. Where fascist grammar moves from crisis to inevitability, [anarchist](../../anarchism/index.md) grammar moves from present conditions to present possibility.

## Falsifiability and boundary conditions

The claim that the grammar "persists across vocabularies" is stated as a universal, and a universal needs a condition under which it would be false. As written, the term has none: any speech that declares a crisis and ends in inevitability can be read as fascist grammar with the middle four steps "laundered," and a claim that survives every case by reinterpretation is not a finding about fascism but a lens that produces fascism wherever it is pointed. The strongest counter-cases are therefore not the ones that lack the grammar entirely, which would be trivial, but the ones that share its bookends. Technocratic crisis management is the sharpest: a central bank raising rates into a recession, a regulator harmonizing capital requirements after a banking failure. The United States Federal Reserve's 2022 position on inflation declared an existential threat to price stability (step 1), acknowledged that "households and businesses" would experience "pain," and asserted that the tightening would continue "until the job is done" (step 6). There is no pure community, only "the economy"; no enemy, since inflation is treated as a condition rather than an agent; no destiny, since the promised end-state is a return to a two-percent trend, not greatness. If the grammar is universal, this speech must be a disguised instance. If it is not, the grammar's scope must be bounded.

The disguise reading is available, and it has to be taken seriously because it is what the term's own logic of "strategic forgetting" invites. The argument runs: "the economy" is a pure community whose membership is defined by contribution; "structural rigidities" and "wage-price spirals" are sterile agents of internal decay; "necessary pain" is sacrifice by another name; "stability" is a destiny modest enough to pass as neutral; and "no alternative" is the sixth step said out loud. On this reading the technocratic case is more fascist grammar, not less, because it has laundered even the moral vocabulary, which is exactly what [Fisher's](../../documents/articles/bibliographies/fisher2009.md) capitalist realism describes: inevitability asserted without any community to be inevitable for. [Slobodian's](../../documents/articles/bibliographies/slobodian2018.md) account of ordoliberal encasement, in which the economy is deliberately placed beyond the reach of democratic contest, supplies the historical mechanism by which such a laundering could have happened.

The disguise reading fails, and it fails at step 4. The distinction between sacrifice and cost is not one of vocabulary but of what the speech would lose if the suffering disappeared. In fascist grammar, suffering is evidence: it is the proof of the community's moral standing, and a community that had not suffered would have a weaker claim to destiny, not a stronger one. In the technocratic case, suffering is a price, and a lower price is strictly preferred; the central banker who could achieve disinflation with no unemployment would take that outcome without hesitation and would lose nothing rhetorically by doing so. This is an operational test that can be run on any text: strike the suffering and ask whether the argument for inevitability is weakened. Where it is, the speech converts pain into standing and the middle steps will be found around it. Where it is not, the speech is doing accounting, and the middle steps are absent not because they are hidden but because the slot they would occupy is filled by something incompatible with them. A community is a subject that can deserve; a system, in [Luhmann's](../../documents/articles/bibliographies/luhmann1984.md) sense, is not, and no amount of laundering makes a systemic variable into a bearer of desert.

The conclusion is that technocratic crisis management is a genuine exception, and the universal must be bounded to survive it. The bounded claim is: any speech that declares crisis, asserts inevitability, and converts suffering into moral standing will exhibit the remaining three steps regardless of vocabulary. This is falsifiable in two directions. It is refuted by a speech that has steps 1, 4, and 6 but genuinely lacks a pure community, a corrupt enemy, or a destiny; the COVID-era case already in this entry passes rather than refutes, since lockdown-as-solidarity did convert suffering into standing and did produce a contaminating enemy in the unvaccinated. And the bounding itself makes a further prediction that the unbounded claim could not: that a technocratic grammar which shares steps 1 and 6 with fascist grammar, but runs crisis, system, dysfunction, cost, stability, necessity through the middle, will acquire the fascist middle at the moment its accounting fails to legitimate. The eurozone crisis is the test case. The same institutional voice that in 2010 spoke of fiscal adjustment as a cost had by 2015 re-moralized it, with German finance-ministry rhetoric leaning on the identity of debt and guilt in *Schuld*, Greeks who had not "done their homework" as the corrupt element, and the union's future as the destiny that justified the pain. The middle four steps did not emerge from hiding; they were installed when cost stopped working, which is what [Cooper's](../../documents/articles/bibliographies/cooper2017.md) account of the co-production of neoliberal and moral-conservative rationality would lead one to expect.

What this bounding costs the original formulation is the comfort of a single grammar of closure. There are at least two, and they are not the same thing: fascist grammar and technocratic grammar share the move from crisis to inevitability, and it is that shared move, not the moral middle, that anarchist grammar's terminal step of immanence is built to refuse. The correction is that fascism is not the only way to foreclose the political, and that the technocratic foreclosure is not fascism in disguise but a distinct grammar whose failure mode is fascism. That is a stronger claim about fascism, because it says where to look for the grammar's next appearance: not in the technocratic speech itself, but in the speech that follows the moment the technocratic speech stops being believed.
