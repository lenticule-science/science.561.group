Responsibilization names a specific displacement: the reframing of a systemic problem as a task of individual self-management, so that the subject inherits the burden of a condition they did not cause and cannot alone resolve. [Californication](../../../glossary/terms/californication.md) performs this displacement at the level of genre — a job loss becomes a "pivot," a housing crisis becomes a gratitude practice — and asks nothing of the subject beyond a correctly told story. The wellness and resilience industry performs the identical displacement, but it does not stop at narrative. It prices it, packages it, and sells it back — to the subject, and to the institutions that employ them, at margin, on a recurring billing cycle. This is the distinction the corpus has not yet drawn cleanly: californication's genre-calibration can happen for free, in a single unmonetized caption; the wellness/resilience economy is what results when that same calibration is vertically integrated into a sector with suppliers, subscriptions, and a return owed to capital.

The apparatus predates its monetization. **Nikolas Rose**'s [*Governing the Soul*](../bibliographies/rose1999.md) (Free Association Books, 1999) traces how psy-expertise — clinical psychology, personnel management, social work — made the interior self into an object each person is obligated to know, track, and correct, decades before a wristband could sample heart-rate variability. Rose's psy-experts worked mainly inside institutions: the clinic, the personnel office, the therapist's hour. What the resilience industry adds is not the obligation to self-monitor — Rose has already named that obligation as governance — but a scalable, direct-to-consumer product form for discharging it: an app that scores your sleep, a device that grades your vagal tone, a seminar billed by the hour. The soul Rose describes as governed is, in the wellness economy, additionally metered.

Metering requires a reason to pay, and here **Eva Illouz** supplies the missing half. [*Cold Intimacies*](../bibliographies/illouz2007.md) (Polity, 2007) names emotional capitalism: the fusion of economic rationality with therapeutic idiom, under which an inner state stops being merely felt and becomes a *competency* — assessed, trained, listed. Resilience is emotional capitalism's most fully monetized affect because it clears two markets at once. The individual buys resilience as human capital: a breathwork subscription, a wearable, a retreat, each pitched as an investment against the next shock. The employer buys resilience as a substitute for remediation: a "resilience training" seat is a real line item, invoiced and budgeted, purchased instead of raising wages, fixing scheduling, or reducing caseloads. Responsibilization usually names unpaid affective labor extracted from a subject; the resilience industry is responsibilization with an invoice attached, running in both directions — down to the worker as a purchase, back up to the vendor as revenue.

None of this is speculative extrapolation. [A timeline of californication](./history-of-californication.md) has already dated the sequence without naming its economics: 2006–2009's quantified self, 2020's "Pelotonized resilience," 2021's mass adoption of breathwork and polyvagal discourse, 2022–2024's institutional pivot from DEI toward "resilience." Each entry names a product category, a device, a subscription, a vendor contract — not merely a mood. The wearable that scores vagal tone is also, following [surveillance intimacy](../../../glossary/terms/surveillance-intimacy.md), a device that monetizes the self-knowledge it sells twice over: once as a subscription fee, once as the biometric telemetry harvested in the act of caring for oneself. [Resilience discourse](../../../glossary/terms/resilience-discourse.md) is the correct name for the genre this sells in. What needs adding to it is that the genre is a storefront.

This is also, following **Boltanski and Chiapello**'s account of [the new spirit of capitalism](../../../glossary/terms/new-spirit-of-capitalism.md), a metabolization rather than a resolution: burnout was a critique of overwork before it was a market. The resilience industry did not answer that critique. It incorporated its vocabulary — nervous system, boundaries, bandwidth — into a new line of billable expertise, so that the language in which exhaustion first named its own cause returned as the product sold as its cure. A mood can be refused by declining to feel it. An industry has to be defunded.
