## Why this is central

Césaire's boomerang argument — that colonization decivilizes the colonizer, that administering colonial violence brutalizes the one who administers it — is the theoretical basis for the note's claim that the institutional worker's daily bureaucratic complicity coarsens them in a domesticated way, "sanitized ... into simply 'doing one's job.'" Without Césaire's claim about what practicing domination does to the practitioner, the note would have no basis for treating institutional participation as more than passively unfortunate.

## What it does not settle

Césaire's argument is addressed to the colonizer and the European metropole circa 1950, arguing that Nazism was colonial violence returned home; it is not an argument about a marginalized worker's own complicity in institutions that also dominate people like them, nor about AI, language preservation, or risk. Applying decivilization to the institutional worker rather than to a colonizer proper is this note's own transposition — the note states this explicitly rather than presenting the application as Césaire's claim — and Césaire's text does not itself license or test that transposition.
