## Why this is central

Mirowski's puzzle is that the 2008 financial meltdown should have discredited neoliberal doctrine — deregulated markets caused the crisis — yet neoliberalism emerged stronger. His answer is organizational, not ideological: neoliberalism persists as a "neoliberal thought collective," a coordinated, self-reproducing network of economists, think tanks, foundations, and pundits (descending from the Mont Pelerin Society) that survives any single crisis of legitimacy because no individual thinker or claim carries the doctrine — the collective does, shifting its "everyday" public arguments while a more esoteric doctrine circulates among insiders. That distinction between what a formation says to outsiders and what it holds internally is what grounds this corpus's new term, thought-collective, as a direct extension of [industrial intellectualism](../../../glossary/terms/industrial-intellectualism.md): where industrial intellectualism names thought formatted for a genre's market of circulation, thought-collective names the coordinated apparatus — funding lines, journals, conferences, personnel moving between institutions — that manufactures which formatted products get credited as legitimate in the first place. Mirowski himself borrows "thought collective" from Ludwik Fleck's sociology of science, and this corpus's application of the same organizational logic to postliberal intellectual production — how postliberal argument, too, circulates through a network of shifting venues, foundations, and public-facing versus internal registers — is currently uncited despite being the clearest available model for it.

## What it does not settle

Mirowski's book is a study of one specific formation (the Hayek–Friedman–Mont Pelerin lineage and its post-2008 mutations), not a general theory of intellectual collectives as such; extending "thought collective" to postliberal intellectual production is this corpus's analogical move, not a claim Mirowski makes or would necessarily endorse. The book is also pitched at economics and policy discourse specifically — think tanks, university economics departments, financial press — and says nothing about platforms, algorithmic governance, or the californication apparatus this corpus otherwise develops. Beyond the well-known phrase itself, the exact essays and interviews across the book's chapters where Mirowski elaborates the "everyday" versus esoteric "double truths" of neoliberal doctrine are not something this entry attempts to cite more precisely; a reader wanting to check will need to work from the book's own table of contents rather than any page number given here.
