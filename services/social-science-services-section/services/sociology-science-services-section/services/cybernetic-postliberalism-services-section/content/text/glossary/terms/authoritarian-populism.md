Authoritarian populism names a mechanism, not a mood: the production of mass electoral consent for a disciplinary, exclusionary state, secured through the vocabulary of common sense, nation, and family rather than through suspension of the vote that produces it. It is what [fascist grammar](./fascist-grammar.md)'s six-step sequence — crisis, pure community, corrupt enemy, suffering-as-sacrifice, destiny, inevitability — looks like when it runs inside representative institutions instead of against them. The grammar supplies the narrative; the ballot supplies the mandate; the state that results is more centralized and more disciplinary, and it was voted for, on schedule, by people who would reject it if it arrived any other way.

**Stuart Hall** names the mechanism in "The Great Moving Right Show," published in *Marxism Today* in January 1979, months before Margaret Thatcher's election that May. Adapting Antonio Gramsci's theory of hegemony — consent to a worldview, not coercion alone — Hall reads the period's swing toward law-and-order politics (the "crime wave," moral panic over "permissiveness," talk of national decline) not as old Toryism resurfacing but as a new hegemonic project: one that wins popular backing for a harder, more centralized state while continuing to speak in the register of ordinary people, family, and common sense. Whether the exact phrase "authoritarian populism" already appears in this essay or crystallizes across the pieces Hall wrote defending and refining it through the early 1980s is not a detail I can confirm with certainty; what is certain is that this essay is where he begins working the concept out.

Mapped onto fascist grammar, the referent stops being abstract. Crisis is the crime wave and the "enemy within." The pure community is the nation and the hardworking family. The corrupt enemy is the permissive, the parasitic, the "scrounger." Suffering-as-sacrifice is ordinary forbearance under decline, recast as virtue. Destiny is national renewal. The sequence runs cleanly through its first five steps — and then the sixth step changes shape: inevitability is not delivered by seizing the state against the vote but by winning it through the vote, repeatedly. This is the substance of Hall's later, polemical point, developed against Bob Jessop and others in a 1985 *New Left Review* exchange, that authoritarian populism is not fascism: it strengthens, rather than abolishes, the parliamentary and representative machinery that classical fascism's single-party form requires eliminating.

Authoritarian populism is fascist grammar's first five steps wearing an election result as its sixth.
