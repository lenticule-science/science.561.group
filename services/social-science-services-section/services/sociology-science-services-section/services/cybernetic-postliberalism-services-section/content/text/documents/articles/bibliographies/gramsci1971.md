## Why this is central

The overview names Gramsci's theory of hegemony as one of the four pillars the framework "draws on" but is "not reducible to," without citing him anywhere else — this entry grounds that debt. Gramsci's hegemony argues that a ruling group secures consent primarily through cultural and ideological leadership over civil society, not through coercion alone: its worldview comes to occupy the position of "common sense," experienced by the ruled as simply how things are rather than as one contestable position among others. That is exactly the operation [postliberal grammar](../notes/postliberal-grammar.md) performs on [fascist grammar](../../../glossary/terms/fascist-grammar.md)'s six-step sequence: the sequence recurs across positions that claim to oppose each other because it has stopped registering as a rhetorical choice and become the ambient, common-sense shape crisis is expected to take. Hegemony names the mechanism by which a grammar naturalizes into something that no longer looks like grammar at all.

## What it does not settle

The *Prison Notebooks* were written in fragmentary, aphoristic form between roughly 1929 and 1935 while Gramsci was imprisoned by the fascist state, and the 1971 English volume is a translated selection (edited by Quintin Hoare and Geoffrey Nowell-Smith) rather than a text Gramsci himself organized for publication; I am not certain of the exact original Italian publication history and will not invent one. Gramsci's hegemony describes a strategic relation between historical blocs contesting leadership of a specific class-structured civil society — it has no built-in account of a grammar that recurs identically across blocs that are supposed to be contesting each other, which is the framework's own extension, not Gramsci's claim. Nor does he settle whether his "war of position" — the slow contest for common sense within civil society — has any analogue in a framework that, per its own overview, declines to prescribe what should replace the system it describes.
