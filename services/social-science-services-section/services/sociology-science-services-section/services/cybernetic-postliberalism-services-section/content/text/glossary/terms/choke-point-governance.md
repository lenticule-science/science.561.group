A sanctions regime does not stop oil from existing, and an export control does not make advanced chips harder to design. Choke-point governance is what both actually do: they govern not the thing itself but the small number of intermediary layers every instance of that thing has to pass through to become usable, sellable, or financeable. Control the choke point and you control the flow, without having to touch the wells, the fabs, or the ships.

emsenn's [2025-12-25 log on the oil sanctions](../../../../../../../../../../../public-services-section/services/personal-services-section/emsenn-services-section/content/text/logs/personal-logs/2025/12/25/2025-12-25-emsenn-personal-log-about-latest-oil-sanctions.md) names the mechanism directly: "If you want to see how energy sanctions really work, don't look at wells or pipelines. Look at insurance pools, price benchmarks, and clearing systems." Three choke points recur in that log's reading of the 2025 oil sanctions: **maritime insurance** (a small number of Western protection-and-indemnity clubs decide which shipments are "legible" to the formal economy), **benchmarking** (pricing crude against Brent or WTI encodes which flows count as normal and financeable, pricing everything else as a discounted deviation), and **clearing and settlement** (dollar-denominated trade routes even non-Western transactions through Western legal and financial infrastructure somewhere in the stack). The same log reads export controls on advanced semiconductors through the identical structure: **fabrication** (a handful of plants, tooling suppliers, and process licensors define what counts as a modern chip), **tooling and updates** (design software and maintenance contracts sit upstream of any single device and degrade it over time rather than bricking it outright), and **standards and compliance** (certification regimes and legal definitions of "dual use" quietly determine what can be sold, where).

The log's claim is not that these mechanisms fail to work as advertised, but that they work by doing something other than what they are advertised as doing: "sanctions shift governance away from states and producers toward insurers, traders, and financial intermediaries. That's not an accident or a side effect; it's the mechanism." Authority relocates to gatekeepers — insurers, benchmark administrators, clearing banks, fabrication-tooling licensors, standards bodies — who are structurally insulated from the downstream accountability that would attach to a state or a producer making the same decision. Finance is what makes the relocation governable at all: "price spreads, discounting, insurance premia, futures, compliance costs, and capital access convert real shortages and declining returns into legible, deferrable risks" — finance is the buffer that converts a material constraint (declining energy return on energy invested, a scarce fabrication process) into something that can be carried, priced, and deferred rather than resolved.

The [Who Owns What?](../../documents/articles/notes/expanded-profile-on-bernd-bergmair.md) series applies the same reading to corporate ownership structures rather than commodity flows: a Colbeck Capital financing chain, a BVI-to-Luxembourg shell structure, an Ethical Capital Partners acquisition are each an intermediary layer — private credit, jurisdictional registration, deal structuring — that a beneficial owner's control has to pass through, and that layer's operators (the lender, the registered agent, the acquirer) hold governance-relevant authority over the underlying asset while bearing none of the downstream accountability for what it is used for.
