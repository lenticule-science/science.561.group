## Why this is central

Brown's thesis is that neoliberalism is not only an economic policy program but a form of governing reason that remakes every domain it touches — law, education, the state, the self — in an economic image, displacing *homo politicus* (the citizen, a bearer of political rights and democratic sovereignty) with *homo oeconomicus* (a unit of "human capital" responsible for its own investment, competitive positioning, and enhancement). That is the mechanism directly underneath [responsibilization](../../../glossary/terms/responsibilization.md)'s claim that governance "operates not by commanding behavior but by formatting subjects who govern themselves": Brown supplies the account of *why* a subject formatted this way experiences wage stagnation, privatized risk, or civic decay as personal shortfalls rather than structural facts. This is the "stealth revolution" of the subtitle — democratic vocabulary and institutions persist in name while their content is hollowed out and reissued in market terms. Brown's book is the widely cited source for this specific claim, and it is currently uncited in the corpus despite grounding one of responsibilization's core moves.

## What it does not settle

Brown's argument is pitched at the level of political theory and political ontology — what neoliberal reason does to democracy, sovereignty, and citizenship as such — not at the level of platforms, infrastructure, or algorithmic administration; she does not discuss social media, telemetric governance, or anything resembling this corpus's californication apparatus. She is also careful to distinguish neoliberalism as a *rationality of governing* from neoliberalism as a narrower economic policy bundle (deregulation, privatization, austerity), a distinction the corpus's own use of "responsibilization" tends to compress. Where the book draws most heavily on Michel Foucault's account of governmentality (developed in his late-1970s lecture courses rather than in *The History of Sexuality, Volume 1*, the Foucault text already catalogued in this bibliography), Brown extends and sharpens that account for the post-2008 political moment rather than simply restating it — a distinct contribution this entry does not want to flatten.
