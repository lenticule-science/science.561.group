The new spirit of capitalism names the mechanism by which capitalism survives a crisis of legitimacy by metabolizing the critique addressed to it, rather than by conceding to that critique or being destroyed by it. A "spirit," in this sense, is not an economic structure but the set of moral justifications — the reasons work is worth doing, engagement is worth giving, inequality is worth tolerating — that must accompany capital accumulation to make it bearable and desirable to the people who staff it. When a spirit stops persuading, capitalism does not stand undefended; it produces a successor spirit built substantially from the terms of the objection that discredited the last one. Critique is not capitalism's limit condition. It is an input.

**Luc Boltanski** and **Ève Chiapello** develop this in *The New Spirit of Capitalism* (Verso, English translation 2005; originally Gallimard, 1999), tracking the shift from the mid-century "second spirit" — bureaucratic, hierarchical, organized around the salaried career and the large firm — to a "third spirit" consolidated after 1968: network- and project-based, organized around flexibility, mobility, and "passionate" self-directed work. Their explanation for the transition turns on distinguishing two kinds of objection that had both been leveled at the second spirit. The *social critique* indicted it for inequality, exploitation, and insecurity — a critique aimed at capitalism's distribution of harm. The *artistic critique* indicted it for stifling authenticity, autonomy, and creativity — a critique aimed at capitalism's imposition of conformity, voiced by the counterculture and by currents within May '68. Management theory's response, over the following decades, incorporated the artistic critique almost wholesale — flexibility, networks, self-realization, "doing what you love" — while leaving the social critique substantially unanswered. The demand for authenticity became the vocabulary of contemporary management; the demand for equality did not.

This is the incorporation/valorization cycle: the other's objection, once metabolized, returns as a new form of the same system's productivity. It is worth stating plainly where the corpus's own evidence runs out. Boltanski and Chiapello are reading French management and business literature from roughly the 1960s through the 1990s; they make no claim about platformized or algorithmically-mediated production, and their framework does not assert that the cycle is total — whether a critique could be formulated that the apparatus of justification cannot absorb is a question they leave open rather than settle.

The direct parallel is [californication](./californication.md), which performs the identical operation one register down: where the new spirit of capitalism absorbs *critique* into management's language, californication absorbs the *affect* that critique would otherwise mobilize into personal narrative. Both name structures that do not defeat opposition — they metabolize it, and profit from the metabolizing.
