## Why this is central

Capitalist realism, per Fisher, names not a belief that capitalism is *good* but the narrower and more totalizing sense that no alternative to it is even imaginable — he opens the book with a line usually credited to Fredric Jameson (Slavoj Žižek uses it too), that it is "easier to imagine the end of the world than to imagine the end of capitalism." This is the closest published concept to [zen-fascism.md](../../../glossary/terms/zen-fascism.md)'s claim that "liberalism becomes legitimate precisely because it produces crisis": Fisher's account of capitalism naturalizing its own crises as the horizon of the thinkable is the political-economic register of what zen fascism names at the affective register, where calm, adaptive self-regulation under crisis is treated as evidence of ethical and epistemic superiority. His chapter on mental health — arguing that the "reflexive impotence" and depression he observed in students and claimants is produced structurally but privatized as individual, chemical malfunction — is the uncited premise under zen-fascism's mechanism of "pathologization of affect," where structural harm is recoded as personal failure. Fisher already has a profile note elsewhere in this repository's biographical materials, but capitalist realism itself has had no bibliographic presence in this section despite being arguably the nearest existing published concept to both zen-fascism and [coherent-confusion.md](../../../glossary/terms/coherent-confusion.md).

## What it does not settle

*Capitalist Realism* is a short, polemical diagnostic essay, not a worked-out institutional or historical account; it does not trace anything like zen-fascism's specific California genealogy (Palo Alto cybernetics, Esalen, the wellness industry) or coherent-confusion's account of ambiguity itself, rather than foreclosure, as a governance mechanism. Fisher's "realism" describes alternatives becoming unimaginable outright; it does not anticipate the platform-specific condition coherent-confusion.md describes, in which interpretation proliferates endlessly without resolving — confusion rather than closure sustaining participation. Fisher died in January 2017 having only sketched, in an unfinished project he called "acid communism," a possible exit from capitalist realism, so the book itself does not resolve whether the condition it names is escapable, or how.
