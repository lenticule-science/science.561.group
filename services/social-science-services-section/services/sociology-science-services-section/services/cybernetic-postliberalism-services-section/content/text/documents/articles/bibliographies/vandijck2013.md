## Why this is central

Van Dijck's book argues that platforms do not merely host sociality but engineer it: through algorithms, protocols, interfaces, default settings, and ownership structures, platforms such as Facebook, Twitter, YouTube, and Wikipedia code spontaneous "connectedness" into a manageable, monetizable "connectivity," building popularity and sharing into the technical architecture itself as the values a platform selects for. This is the earlier, more direct source for [californication.md](../../../glossary/terms/californication.md)'s **platformal process** ("platform algorithms select for platform-compatible values") than the corpus's currently-cited chun2016: van Dijck and Shoshana Zuboff are the pair named together in the corpus's own working notes as platformal process's source, yet before this entry neither had a bibliography entry anywhere in the corpus. This entry closes that gap on the theorist actually named for the claim, rather than through Wendy Chun's adjacent argument about habitual updating.

## What it does not settle

Van Dijck never uses "californication," "platformal process," or any cybernetic-postliberalism vocabulary; her history covers five platforms through roughly 2012, before the opaque, AI-curated recommendation feeds that the corpus's account of platformal process is often really describing, so extending her argument about coded architecture to today's black-box ranking systems is the corpus's own move. The book also does not itself explain how her account of platform engineering relates to Zuboff's surveillance-capitalism argument, with whom the corpus's own notes pair her; that division of labor between the two theorists is asserted by the notes, not derived from either book. I have not located specific page numbers for the phrases above and am paraphrasing van Dijck's well-known thesis rather than quoting her directly.
