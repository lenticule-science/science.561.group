## Why this is central

Deneen's argument is not that liberalism fell short of its promises but that it succeeded, and the success is the problem: liberal individualism dissolved the communal, familial, and religious bonds that gave life meaning, and liberal procedural neutrality — far from leaving prior traditions undisturbed — actively corroded them. overview.md's genealogy names Deneen, by this book, as the source of the framework's "postliberal component," glossing his position as the claim that "liberalism's promises (freedom, equality, progress) are structurally incapable of delivering what they promise." Until this entry, that attribution existed only as a one-line inline citation in the separate postliberalism-services-section; here it is annotated on the cybernetic-postliberalism side specifically, so the two frameworks' actual relationship to the book — one advocating from inside its critique, the other analyzing the critique from outside — can be drawn out in a dedicated comparison note.

## What it does not settle

The book is a sustained diagnosis of liberalism's failure but is comparatively thin on institutional remedy: Deneen calls for cultivating local, "countercultural" practices and communities of resistance rather than specifying a replacement constitutional order, leaving that gap for later postliberal writers (Vermeule's common-good constitutionalism, Ahmari, and others) to fill in mutually incompatible ways. Deneen also does not, and does not attempt to, examine whether his own argumentative sequence — name the crisis liberalism has produced, indict the corrupting force, call for a return to a truer prior order — is structurally shared with movements he would disavow; that is the reading postliberal-grammar.md supplies, not a claim the book makes about itself.
