The Californian Ideology names a fusion of two historically opposed political vocabularies into a single technologically-determinist article of faith: New Left communalism — decentralization, self-expression, distrust of institutions — and New Right economics — deregulated markets, entrepreneurial individualism, hostility to the state — converge on the shared conviction that networked computing dissolves the need to choose between them. Political and economic disagreement gets recoded as a technical one: is the network open or closed, is information flowing freely or blocked. Government becomes an obstacle to an egalitarian, self-organizing order that will emerge on its own once digital tools clear away the old bottlenecks of geography, hierarchy, and industrial scale. Hippie and yuppie, communard and financier, meet inside this single conviction as the Bay Area's regional common sense, not as a program either camp had to install and run.

Richard Barbrook and Andy Cameron name and diagnose this "bizarre hybrid" in their 1996 essay "The Californian Ideology" ([barbrook1996](../../documents/articles/bibliographies/barbrook1996.md)), arguing that its anti-statist self-image sits uneasily atop the state-funded infrastructure — defense research, the university system — that the ideology cannot acknowledge without undoing its own premise. Fred Turner's history of the same Peninsula ([turner2006](../../documents/articles/bibliographies/turner2006.md)) supplies the documented institutional route by which Cold War cybernetics research and Stewart Brand's New Communalist counterculture merged into this belief-system. Malcolm Harris's material history of Palo Alto ([harris2023](../../documents/articles/bibliographies/harris2023.md)) reaches back further still, tracing what he calls the "Palo Alto System" — Leland Stanford's own name for his method of engineering better outcomes by controlling breeding and environment — from stock-farm eugenics through Frederick Terman's university-military-capital triangle into the region that would later narrate itself as spontaneously self-organizing.

This must be held apart from [californication](./californication.md). The Californian Ideology is a set of propositions — claims about what computers and markets prove — that can be argued for or refuted point by point, as Barbrook and Cameron do. Californication is emsenn's term for the mechanism by which liberal governance actually metabolizes [structural contradiction](./structural-contradiction.md) into affectively manageable content, and it operates whether or not the subject believes any of the Californian Ideology's claims. Someone who rejects every proposition Barbrook and Cameron catalogue can still be fully californicated — still locate coherence in responsiveness rather than resistance, still narrativize collapse as personal growth — because californication works on affect and genre, not doctrine. The Californian Ideology is a story about what California's machines prove; californication is what those machines, and the crises they produce, do to people regardless of what anyone believes the machines prove. One is a claim to be checked against a history like Harris's; the other is a process that does not need the claim to be true to keep running.
