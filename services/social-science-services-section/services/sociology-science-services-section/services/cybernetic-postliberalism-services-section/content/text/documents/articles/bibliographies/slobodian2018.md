## Why this is central

Slobodian's history of the "Geneva School" of neoliberal thought (Ludwig von Mises, Friedrich Hayek, Wilhelm Röpke) argues that the movement's real project was never simply to shrink the state, but to build law and institutions — GATT, the European Communities, investor-state arbitration — that would wall the market order off from what these thinkers explicitly named as the danger: democratic majorities voting to redistribute, nationalize, or otherwise re-embed the economy in social demands. Slobodian's own word for this is "encasement": constitutionalizing markets in international law, above and beyond the reach of ordinary electoral politics. That is a real, citable ground for the corpus's law-as-control claim that governance can operate through symbolic/legal declaration which treats deviation — a demos voting to expropriate, tariff, or nationalize — as noncompliance to be formally overridden rather than a legitimate political outcome to be answered. It also gives choke-point governance a genealogy: the Geneva School's strategy of insulating flows from majority rule by relocating authority to formally neutral, legally protected bodies (trade tribunals, standards-setters, treaty organizations) is an early version of the same structure that later note finds in insurers, clearing systems, and fabrication-tooling licensors.

## What it does not settle

Slobodian's narrative runs from the interwar collapse of the Habsburg and British empires through to the WTO's founding in the 1990s, and says little about the digital- and platform-era mechanisms — algorithmic pricing, cloud dependency, chip export controls — that later choke-point analysis in this corpus develops from a 2025 sanctions case; his own examples are trade law, currency regimes, and international organizations, not digital infrastructure, and it would be an extrapolation, not a claim of his, to say he anticipated the latter. Nor does the book offer a normative alternative: it is a history of how encasement was built and by whom, not a proposed cybernetic or postliberal remedy to it, so it should not be read as arguing either for or against this corpus's own claims about recursive, participatory planning.
