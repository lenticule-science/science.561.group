Lateral violence names violence that colonized or otherwise oppressed people direct at one another rather than at the colonial or oppressive structure itself. Frantz Fanon, in *Les damnés de la terre* (1961), describes this horizontal violence among the colonized as a release valve produced by the colonial situation: aggression generated by domination is discharged sideways, against the people closest and most reachable, rather than upward against the colonizer who generated it and who remains, for the colonized, dangerous to confront directly. Later Indigenous and community-health scholarship takes up the term "lateral violence" specifically for this pattern within Indigenous communities living under ongoing settler-colonial conditions.

Lateral violence is distinguished from horizontal hostility as treated in general workplace or organizational-behavior literature, which typically explains peer conflict through role competition, scarce resources, or interpersonal friction internal to an organization. Lateral violence instead locates the cause outside the group in a structure of domination that has made the dominating party unsafe or unreachable to confront, so that the violence produced by that domination is redirected among its targets.

The consequence is that lateral violence, however real the harm it does between individuals, is not resolved by treating it as a private or interpersonal failing alone: because the structure that produces the aggression persists untouched by violence turned inward, addressing lateral violence requires addressing the colonial or oppressive structure that generates it, alongside any repair done between those it has set against one another.
