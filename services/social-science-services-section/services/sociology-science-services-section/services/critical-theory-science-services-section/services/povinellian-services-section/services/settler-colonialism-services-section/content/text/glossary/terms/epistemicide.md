Epistemicide names the destruction of a knowledge system together with the social groups that produce, hold, and transmit it. Boaventura de Sousa Santos develops the term in *Epistemologies of the South: Justice against Epistemicide* (2014) to describe how colonial and capitalist domination has proceeded not only by extracting land, labor, and resources but by killing off the ways of knowing that organized life before and outside that domination — so that the knowledge that survives the encounter is the conqueror's knowledge, now presented as the only rigorous or universal knowledge available.

Epistemicide is distinguished from mere loss or forgetting: knowledge is not simply misplaced or allowed to lapse, it is actively destroyed as part of the same operation that destroys or subordinates the people who hold it — the epistemic and the social casualty are one event, not two. It is likewise distinguished from [knowledge sovereignty](./knowledge-sovereignty.md), which names the community's assertion of authority over its own knowledge; epistemicide names the destructive operation that knowledge sovereignty is asserted against.

The consequence Santos draws is a call for an ecology of knowledges — a plurality of knowledge systems recognized as valid on their own terms and in relation to one another, without a single epistemology (Western science included) sitting in judgment over the rest as the arbiter of what counts as rigorous.
