Decivilization is Aimé Césaire's claim, in *Discours sur le colonialisme* (first published 1950, this edition 1955; translated by Joan Pinkham as *Discourse on Colonialism*, Monthly Review Press), that colonization does not only brutalize the colonized — it decivilizes the colonizer. The colonizer who tortures, who accepts and administers colonial violence as ordinary practice, is brutalized by the practice of that violence, however civilized the metropole otherwise considers itself. Césaire names this the boomerang effect: the violence Europe exported to its colonies did not stay there, it returned to strike Europe itself, and Césaire argues that Nazism was in large part this returned violence — colonial method turned back onto a European population.

Decivilization is distinguished from, and complements, Fanon's account of the dehumanization the colonized suffer under colonialism: Fanon describes what colonial violence does to the colonized subject's being; Césaire describes what administering that same violence does to the colonizer, who is not spared but corrupted by the practice of domination. Neither claim substitutes for the other — the colonized are dehumanized and the colonizer is decivilized by the same colonial relation, from its two ends.

The consequence is that complicity in institutionalized colonial violence cannot be normalized as a merely external or contained practice: Césaire's argument is that a society that tolerates and administers such violence abroad has already produced, within itself, the capacities and permissions that later reappear as domestic fascism.
