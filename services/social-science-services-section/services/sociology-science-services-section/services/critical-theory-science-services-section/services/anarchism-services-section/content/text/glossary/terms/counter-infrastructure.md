Counter-infrastructure is the anarchist practice of building institutions and material systems — communication networks, food distribution, health care, education, dispute resolution — that meet a community's real needs outside state and market provision, undertaken as an ongoing, permanent practice rather than a temporary tactic.

Counter-infrastructure is distinguished from dual power, the term already ruled in this section, by scope and claim: dual power names a strategy of building parallel institutions specifically to make existing power arrangements unnecessary and to reduce dependency on them, while counter-infrastructure is the broader material practice dual power depends on — the actual servers, kitchens, clinics, and networks built — without dual power's additional strategic claim that this building displaces state and capitalist power specifically. Gordon's ethnography of contemporary anarchist movements documents this building practice as continuous with anarchism's traditional suspicion of centralized organization, arguing that anti-authoritarian politics is worked out through practice, including the practice of building and running shared infrastructure, rather than derived from theory first (Gordon 2008); Graeber's anthropological argument for treating existing mutual-aid and consensus-based institutions as evidence that non-state social coordination is materially possible, not merely a utopian aspiration, supplies counter-infrastructure's underlying warrant (Graeber 2004).

The consequence of treating counter-infrastructure as a permanent practice rather than a temporary tactic is that its maintenance becomes an ordinary, recurring commitment rather than a one-time achievement — the same maintenance-labor and burnout considerations documented for tech collectives generally apply to any counter-infrastructure project, since building the alternative is not itself sufficient; someone must keep it running.
