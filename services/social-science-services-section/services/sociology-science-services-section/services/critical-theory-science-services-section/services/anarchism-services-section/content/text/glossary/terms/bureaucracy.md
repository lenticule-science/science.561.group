A **bureaucracy**, in Max Weber's sense, is an administrative form whose ideal-type combines a small number of structural features that together distinguish it from the patrimonial, charismatic, and household-based administrative forms it historically displaces.
Bureaucracy is, on Weber's analysis, the *characteristic carrier* of [rational-legal authority](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/authority.md) — the administrative apparatus that fits and reproduces the rational-legal type of legitimacy.

## The seven structural features

Weber's [Economy and Society](../../../../../../../../content/text/documents/articles/bibliographies/weber1922.md) specifies bureaucracy through seven features that the ideal-type combines:

1. **Fixed jurisdictional areas** ordered by rules.
2. **Office hierarchy** — a graded system of superordination and subordination.
3. **Written documents** (the files) as the basis of administration; the office is separated from the person.
4. **Expert training** required for office management; appointment (not election) on the basis of demonstrated qualifications.
5. **The office as a full-time vocation** demanding the official's whole working capacity.
6. **General rules** — stable, exhaustive, learnable — applied impersonally (*sine ira et studio*: without anger or favor).
7. **Personal freedom of officials**: subject to authority only with respect to impersonal official obligations; fixed salary, career advancement, pension rights; they hold the office, do not own it.

The conjunction of these features — not any one alone — distinguishes bureaucracy from earlier administrative forms.
Patrimonial administrators may be expert and full-time but do not separate office from person; charismatic followings may have written records but do not generalize rules; household administration may be hierarchical but is bound by personal loyalty rather than impersonal rule.

## The technical-superiority claim

Weber's claim is that bureaucracy is *technically superior* to other administrative forms — faster, more precise, more predictable, more impersonal, more economical at scale.
Once the modern mass state, the modern industrial firm, the modern political party, and the modern army require the capacities bureaucracy provides, the bureaucratization process becomes largely irreversible.
Bureaucratization is, for Weber, the most unambiguous trend of modernity.

The claim is technical, not normative.
Bureaucracy's technical superiority is consistent with its substantive disadvantages — the [iron cage](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/iron-cage.md) it imposes, its tendency to displace substantive value-rationality with formal procedural rationality, its capacity to be turned to monstrous ends.

## Distinguished from

- **[Office](../../../../../../../../../../../services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/business-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/office.md)** — the bureaucratic position is *one form* office takes; offices exist in non-bureaucratic forms (patrimonial vassal, charismatic disciple, ritual priesthood).
- **Hierarchy as such** — many administrative forms are hierarchical without being bureaucratic; bureaucratic hierarchy is distinguished by its rule-bound, impersonal, document-based character.
- **Rationality as such** — bureaucracy embodies a specific *Zweckrationalität* (instrumental rationality) of administrative form; other rationalities (substantive, value-rational, communicative) operate by different criteria.
- **The actual organization** — Meyer and Rowan's [institutionalized organizations](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/meyer-rowan-1977) thesis observes that real organizations often adopt bureaucratic *appearances* without bureaucratic *substance*, decoupling formal structure from operational reality.
  The ideal-type is not a description of any particular organization.

## Reception

The bureaucracy concept became the founding object of organizational sociology through Talcott Parsons's mid-twentieth-century reception.
Reinhard Bendix [bendix1960] supplied the standard intellectual portrait.
Critics from the human-relations school onward have documented bureaucracy's empirical departures from the ideal-type; the [labor-process tradition](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/texts/labor-process-theory-survey.md) (Braverman, Burawoy) has read bureaucratic rule as a particular form of capitalist control; the [neo-institutional tradition](../../../../../../../work-science-services-section/services/governance-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/institutional-logics-survey.md) has analyzed how bureaucratic forms spread through legitimacy-conferring isomorphism rather than functional necessity.
Paul du Gay's [In Praise of Bureaucracy](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/du-gay-2000) defends the Weberian impersonal office against late-twentieth-century anti-bureaucratic management discourse, arguing the bureaucratic ethic carries important capacities that "post-bureaucratic" alternatives sacrifice.

A bureaucracy is an organization run by permanent officials following fixed rules and procedures.
It is [hierarchy](hierarchy.md) made administrative: positions are arranged in a chain of command, roles are defined by the institution rather than by the people in them, and decisions follow established procedures rather than emerging from the people affected by them.
Government agencies, corporate management, university administrations, and large nonprofit organizations are all bureaucracies.

[Anarchism](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/index.md) opposes bureaucracy not because paperwork is annoying but because bureaucracy is a mechanism for concentrating [power](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/power.md) while disguising it as neutral procedure.
A bureaucratic process appears impersonal — "we're just following the rules" — but the rules were written by someone, they serve someone's interests, and the people who administer them exercise real [authority](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/authority.md) over the people subject to them.
The welfare office that denies benefits because a form was filled out incorrectly is exercising power; the bureaucratic frame makes this exercise appear as neutral administration rather than as what it is — a [hierarchy](hierarchy.md) deciding who eats.

Bureaucracy tends to reproduce itself.
Once an organization creates administrative positions, those positions develop their own interests — budgets to protect, authority to maintain, procedures to expand.
[David Graeber](/general/people/david-graeber.md) documented this in *The Utopia of Rules* (2015): bureaucracy does not shrink when given the opportunity; it grows, because the people within it benefit from its growth.
This is why [insurrectionary anarchism](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/insurrectionary-anarchism.md) insists on [informal organization](informal-organization.md): permanent structures accumulate bureaucracy regardless of their original purpose.

Bureaucracy also enforces [obedience](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/obedience.md) through abstraction.
When a person in [authority](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/authority.md) gives you an order, you can argue with them, refuse, or resist.
When "the system" requires you to fill out forms, meet deadlines, follow procedures, and comply with regulations, the domination is harder to identify and harder to resist — who do you argue with?
The form?
The procedure?
The office?
Bureaucracy diffuses [authority](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/authority.md) so widely that no one appears responsible for the [coercion](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/coercion.md) the system produces.

The alternative is not disorganization.
[Consensus](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/consensus.md), [federation](../../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/federation.md), rotating roles, revocable mandates, and temporary task-specific coordination are all forms of organization that do not require permanent officials, fixed rules, or chains of command.
They are harder to maintain than bureaucracy — they require potential participation rather than passive compliance — but that difficulty is the price of [self-organization](self-organization.md).
