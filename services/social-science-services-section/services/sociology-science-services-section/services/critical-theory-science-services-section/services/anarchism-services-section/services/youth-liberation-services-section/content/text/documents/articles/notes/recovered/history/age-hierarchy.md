# age-hierarchy

Age-hierarchy is domination organized by age: the systematic subordination of the young to the authority of adults, naturalized as care, as protection, or as the mere fact of immaturity. It names the arrangement by which adults hold near-unquestioned power over children and young people — over where they live, what they learn, how they spend their time, whether their words are believed — and by which that power is treated not as a political arrangement open to challenge but as the natural order of human development. Generated by: domination, ranking by age. It is the parent structure under which this library groups the more specific concepts of adultism, childism, adult-supremacy, and misopedy.

What makes age-hierarchy distinctive among forms of domination is its near-total naturalization. Every adult was once a child and passed through subordination to adult authority; having survived it, adults tend to regard it as inevitable rather than as something done to them. The hierarchy is also presented to its subordinates as being for their own good — the language of care, safety, and "the best interest of the child" does real work, and some of what it names is genuine dependence, since young children do require sustained care. The critical question the concept raises is where genuine care ends and unjustified command begins: whether the adult's authority over a young person's life requires any justification beyond the adult's age, and whether children's testimony, preferences, and judgments deserve the dismissal they routinely receive.

The component concepts divide the analysis by level. Adultism names the discriminatory practices and institutional arrangements through which adult power operates — the concrete rules and policies that constrain young lives, from the school's command-and-compliance structure to legal exclusion from voting and contracting. Childism names the underlying prejudice, the cognitive and affective orientation, developed by Elisabeth Young-Bruehl, that makes those practices seem natural and reasonable. Adult-supremacy names the whole political system — the interlocking institutions, norms, and ideologies that concentrate power in adults — by deliberate analogy to white supremacy and male supremacy. Misopedy names the antipathy: the ideological treatment of childhood itself as a defective condition to be overcome, corrected, or governed.

Age-hierarchy matters within the wider analysis of domination because of a claim several theorists in this lineage advance: that it is the first hierarchy most people meet, and so the one that trains the rest. The family, on this reading, is the primary site where obedience to authority is learned and made to feel ordinary, before it is generalized to boss, state, and law. Toby Rollo presses the argument further toward colonialism, contending that the figure of the colonized-as-child — the "savage" requiring tutelage and civilization — borrows the template already laid down by the subordination of actual children. On that account age-hierarchy is not one oppression beside the others but something closer to a template for domination as such, which is why this library treats it as a hierarchy in its own right rather than a mere extension of the adult forms.
