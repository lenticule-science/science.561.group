# the-state

The state is the set of institutions that claim the exclusive right to use force within a territory —
legislatures that make laws, courts that interpret them, police that enforce them, militaries that
defend the borders, prisons that punish, and bureaucracies that administer the rest. Generated by:
the state is authority in its monopolizing form, authority composed with a claimed monopoly on
legitimate coercion over a bounded territory. Max Weber's canonical definition makes exactly this
monopoly the defining feature: a state is the human community that successfully claims the monopoly
of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory. What makes a cluster of armed and
administrative institutions a *state* rather than merely a powerful gang is the claim to *legitimacy*
— the assertion that its force is rightful, lawful, and necessary, while anyone else's is crime or
terror.

That claim is precisely what the anarchist tradition refuses. Its argument is not that the present
government is bad and should be swapped for a better one, but that the structure itself — an
institution concentrating decision-making in few hands and enforcing that concentration through
violence — is a form of domination regardless of who runs it. A democratically elected government, on
this view, remains a hierarchy: it still compels obedience, still punishes disobedience, still claims
authority over people who never consented. Elections let you choose who dominates you; they do not let
you choose not to be dominated. Marxist analysis reaches a related conclusion by a different route,
treating the state as an instrument bound up with class rule — the apparatus that secures the
conditions of accumulation and contains the conflicts capitalism generates.

The state and capitalism reinforce one another. The state enforces the property rights without which
the claim that one person "owns" a factory and everyone who works there must obey them would be
unenforceable; capitalism funds the state through taxation and furnishes the economic base of its
military and administrative power. This mutual constitution is why so much radical thought holds that
neither can be abolished while leaving the other intact: a revolution that seizes state power leaves
the hierarchical structure standing, and one that redistributes property while preserving the state
simply installs a new ruling stratum — the party, the bureaucracy — in control of the redistributed
wealth.

It is worth stressing that the state is not the only possible form of large-scale social
organization. For most of human history most people lived without states. Indigenous governance
systems across the world coordinated complex societies through councils, consensus, kinship
obligation, and seasonal assembly — without police, prisons, or a monopoly on force — and these were
not primitive precursors to the state but sophisticated alternatives to it, which colonialism worked
to destroy precisely because they demonstrated the state's contingency. Contemporary experiments such
as the Zapatista autonomous municipalities and the cantons of Rojava show that such alternatives
continue to be built. The anarchist proposal is not chaos but self-organization: horizontal assemblies
and federations in which decisions are made by those affected and delegates carry revocable mandates.

Within this cluster the state is authority that has claimed both supremacy and exclusivity — sovereign
authority joined to a monopoly on coercion over a territory. It is the institution that issues law,
that decides the exception, and that, in fusing with the idea of a single people, becomes the
nation-state, with statelessness and displacement as its constitutive remainders.
