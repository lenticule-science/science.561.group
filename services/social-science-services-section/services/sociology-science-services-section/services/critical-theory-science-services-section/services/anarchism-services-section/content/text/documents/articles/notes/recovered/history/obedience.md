# obedience

Obedience is the habit of complying with authority. This library, following the anarchist analysis, identifies it as the primary mechanism through which hierarchy reproduces itself — not coercion, which is expensive and unstable, but the internalization of subordination as natural, necessary, or virtuous. A system of domination is secure not when it can punish every act of disobedience but when most people never consider disobedience in the first place. Generated by: domination — obedience is the compliance domination induces and requires, for a domination that had to be forced at every moment would not be domination but a permanent standoff.

Obedience is taught. Families teach children to obey parents; schools teach students to obey teachers and schedules; workplaces teach workers to obey managers and deadlines; legal systems teach citizens to obey laws; religious institutions teach believers to obey doctrine and clergy. Each context dresses obedience in a different name — respect, discipline, responsibility, faith, civic duty — but the structural function is the same: the person in the subordinate position learns to treat the authority above them as legitimate, and to experience disobedience as failure, transgression, or pathology rather than as a reasonable response to an unreasonable demand. This training is why domination so rarely needs to show its force: the force has already been anticipated and absorbed.

The analysis turns the common-sense objection to anti-authoritarian politics inside out. Anarchism is often called impractical on the grounds that people cannot be trusted without authority over them. But that objection reveals its own premise — that obedience is the normal condition and autonomy the exception that must be justified. The anarchist move is to reverse the order: autonomy is the default human capacity, and obedience is the trained behavior that needs explaining. The question is not "why would anyone disobey?" but "why does anyone obey?" — and the answer is that they were taught to, in every institution from the cradle onward.

Étienne de La Boétie posed the question early, in his *Discourse on Voluntary Servitude* (written around 1553): tyranny persists not because the tyrant is strong but because the people consent to serve. Withdraw obedience and the tyrant has nothing, for all his power was borrowed from those who obeyed him. This is why refusal — the deliberate withdrawal of compliance — is in this analysis the most basic act against domination, and why visible disobedience matters beyond its immediate effect: it demonstrates that the authority which appeared invulnerable in fact rests on a compliance that can be taken back. Obedience is thus the quiet hinge on which the whole apparatus of domination turns, and its reciprocal counterpart is the freely given cooperation of people who can also freely refuse.
