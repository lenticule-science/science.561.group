# indigenous-sovereignty

Indigenous sovereignty is the inherent right of Indigenous peoples to self-determination and governance over their lands, waters, and communities. It is not a right granted by settler states through treaty or legislation. It precedes colonization, and it persists regardless of whether settler governments recognize it. Generated by: autonomy — indigenous sovereignty is a people's collective self-governance held in constitutive relation to its land and kin, the form autonomy takes when it is asserted by a nation that the settler state has tried to absorb.

The concept is sharpened by contrast with state sovereignty, which the modern political tradition defines through a monopoly on violence over a bounded territory, secured by international recognition from other states. Indigenous sovereignty rests on entirely different ground. It derives from ongoing relationships with land, from governance traditions that predate the settler state, and from the continuity of peoples who never consented to the authority claimed over them. On Indigenous terms, treaties between Indigenous nations and colonial powers are nation-to-nation agreements — covenants between sovereigns — not concessions handed down from a superior power to a subordinate one. The settler state's habit of reading those treaties as grants of limited self-rule inverts what they actually were.

Settler-colonial states have worked systematically to undermine Indigenous sovereignty, and the means are worth naming because they recur. There are the legal doctrines — the Doctrine of Discovery, which declared "discovered" lands available to Christian European powers; the plenary-power doctrine, which asserts unilateral congressional authority over Indigenous nations; blood quantum, which makes Indigenous identity into a fraction that dilutes across generations toward administrative extinction. There are the material assaults — forced removal, and the allotment policies that broke collectively held territory into individual parcels precisely to dissolve communal governance. And there is administrative absorption, the folding of Indigenous governance into the bureaucracy of the settler state until self-government becomes a department of the colonizer. The Land Back movement reasserts sovereignty against all of this — not as a symbolic demand but as a material one: the return of land and of jurisdictional authority to Indigenous nations.

Indigenous sovereignty is not confined to land and law. It encompasses governance over knowledge systems, languages, and cultural practices — dimensions that Western sovereignty frameworks, fixated on territory and force, routinely ignore. Knowledge sovereignty, a people's authority over how its knowledge is held, represented, and transmitted, is one expression of this wider claim, inseparable from sovereignty over land because for Indigenous peoples land, governance, and knowledge are not separate domains but aspects of one self-determination.

In the tree of reciprocal relations, indigenous sovereignty is autonomy asserted at the scale of a nation and grounded in grounded normativity — the place-based ethic supplies the content of self-governance, and sovereignty is the political assertion of authority to govern by it. Its companion in the order of practice is indigenous resurgence: where sovereignty names the standing authority a people holds, resurgence is the daily work of regenerating the relationships and practices through which that authority is lived, without waiting for the settler state to grant the recognition it has no right to withhold.
