# coloniality

Coloniality is the decolonial term for the way colonial domination persists even after formal colonial rule has ended. Where colonialism names direct conquest and administration — the period of flags, governors, and occupying armies — coloniality names the enduring patterns of power that outlive that period: racial hierarchy, economic extraction, legal regimes, and epistemic authority that continue to organize the formerly colonized world long after independence was declared. The concept was developed primarily by the Peruvian sociologist Aníbal Quijano, who named the "coloniality of power" as the durable matrix of racial and economic classification that colonialism installed and that survived decolonization intact. Generated by: colonialism — coloniality is its persistence as a structure rather than its conclusion as an event.

Quijano's argument is that the conquest of the Americas inaugurated not only a political order but a global model of power organized around the idea of race. Race became the basic principle for classifying the world's populations and for distributing labor, land, and authority among them — a classification that did not disappear when colonies became nations but was carried forward into the modern world-system. Coloniality, on this account, is the hidden underside of modernity itself: the Enlightenment's universal "Man," its science, and its standards of rationality were defined against the colonized, who were positioned as lacking. Sylvia Wynter's work on the "coloniality of being" extends the analysis to the level of what counts as human, arguing that a particular, racially marked figure of Man was overrepresented as humanity as such, leaving the colonized as its constitutive outside.

The concept does important work as a corrective to two other framings. Against post-colonial discourse, which can suggest that colonialism is over, coloniality insists that the colonial order is reproduced in the present — in the governance of land, in who is made available for exploitation, and in which forms of knowledge are permitted to count as universal. Against accounts that treat colonial dispossession as merely a prehistory of capitalism, a violent founding moment now superseded, coloniality insists that the colonial relation is ongoing and structuring, not finished. This is why decolonial thinkers treat the project of decolonization as irreducible to reform or redistribution: what must be undone is not a past wrong but a present arrangement of power.

In settler contexts, coloniality typically appears through settler-colonialism — a structure oriented toward the elimination of Indigenous political presence and the permanent occupation of land — which is why decolonial critique in those contexts converges with the Indigenous insistence that decolonization means the return of land and life, not the management of attitudes. Within this library coloniality is the concept that keeps colonialism from being filed under "history." It names the residue, the long shadow, the way the lines colonialism drew through the world continue to govern even where its formal institutions have been dismantled — and it thereby sets the stakes for every concept of refusal and resurgence that answers it.
