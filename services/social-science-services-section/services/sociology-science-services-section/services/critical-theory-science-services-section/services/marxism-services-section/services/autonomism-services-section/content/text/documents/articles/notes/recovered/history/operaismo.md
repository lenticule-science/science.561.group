# operaismo

Operaismo — Italian for "workerism" — is the political and intellectual tendency that emerged in Italy in the early 1960s, associated with the journals *Quaderni Rossi* (1961–65) and *Classe Operaia* (1964–67) and with figures including Raniero Panzieri, Mario Tronti, Romano Alquati, and Antonio Negri. Its foundational move is a deliberate inversion of the relationship between capital and labor: rather than studying capital's laws of motion and deriving the working class as a consequence, operaismo starts from working-class *struggle* and reads capital's development as a *response* to it. Generated by: **class-struggle** read from labor's own side — operaismo is the standpoint that takes the worker, not capital, as the active subject of history.

This inversion gives the tradition its distinctive method, *conricerca* (co-research, or workers' inquiry), in which intellectuals and workers collaboratively investigate the conditions, practices, and spontaneous forms of resistance within the workplace. The aim is not to produce academic knowledge *about* workers but to produce knowledge *with* workers that serves their own autonomous organization. Alquati's investigations at the Fiat plants in Turin are the canonical example: close, militant study of how workers actually behaved on the line, what they sabotaged and slowed and refused, treated as primary political data rather than as raw material for a theory imposed from outside.

Operaismo's central theoretical contribution is the concept of *class composition*. The specific technical arrangement of labor — the machinery, the process, the distribution of skill — shapes the forms of resistance available to workers, so that different organizations of work produce different kinds of worker with different capacities for struggle. The tradition spoke of the "mass worker" of the Fordist assembly line, deskilled and interchangeable, whose very homogeneity made possible new forms of collective militancy. And it argued that capital, confronted with the resistance a given composition enables, *recomposes* labor through technological and organizational restructuring — so that automation, in this reading, is not progress but capital's answer to workers' refusal to cooperate. The history of the labor process becomes a running dialectic of workers' struggle and capital's restructuring response.

From operaismo grew the broader politics of autonomism and the strategy of the refusal of work — Tronti's claim that the most radical struggle is not the seizure of the means of production but the refusal to produce on capital's terms, the withdrawal of the labor on which the whole edifice depends. In its later, post-operaismo phase (Negri, Paolo Virno, Maurizio Lazzarato), the tradition turned to the analysis of immaterial labor, the general intellect, and the multitude, carrying the original standpoint — labor as the active subject, capital as reactive — into the analysis of cognitive capitalism.

As a node, operaismo is the methodological and political heart of the labor-centered reading of the cluster. It supplies the angle of vision from which surplus-value appears as something workers might withhold rather than something capital simply takes, from which the labor process appears as a contest capital is forced to keep restructuring, and from which refusal-of-work follows as the characteristic politics. To adopt the operaismo standpoint is to insist that wherever there is capital reorganizing work, there is, prior to it, a struggle it is reorganizing work to answer.
