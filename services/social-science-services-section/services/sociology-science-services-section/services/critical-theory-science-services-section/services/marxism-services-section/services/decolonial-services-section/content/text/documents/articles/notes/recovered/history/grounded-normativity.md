# grounded-normativity

Grounded normativity is the name Glen Coulthard (Yellowknives Dene) gives, in *Red Skin, White Masks* (2014), to the ethical and political frameworks that arise out of Indigenous peoples' relationships to specific places and the practices those places sustain. It is land-based knowledge that teaches how to live, how to govern, and how to relate to other beings — human and other-than-human alike — drawn not from abstract reasoning but from the accumulated, transmitted understanding of how a particular community lives well on a particular land. Generated by: autonomy — grounded normativity is the place-rooted ethics through which a people governs itself, the source from which its self-determination draws its content rather than receiving it from outside.

The concept is positioned against the abstract universal norms of liberal political theory, which claim to derive moral principles from reason alone, independent of place, culture, and embodied practice. Coulthard's argument is that this abstraction is not the neutral, view-from-nowhere standpoint it presents itself as. It is a specific European intellectual tradition, and one that serves settler-colonial interests by delegitimizing knowledge systems rooted in particular lands. When the standard for legitimate political thought is universality, anything grounded in a specific place can be dismissed as merely local, merely traditional, merely pre-modern — and a people whose ethics and governance are bound to a territory can be told that their claims are parochial while the colonizer's claims are rational. The demand for universality is, in this reading, one of the mechanisms by which dispossession is made to look like progress.

What grounded normativity asserts in its place is that the land itself is a source of ethical knowledge. This is not mysticism or a claim that the land speaks in words. It is the recognition that the relationships between a human community and the ecosystem it inhabits — knowledge of when to harvest and when to let the land rest, of obligations to the waters and the animals, of how to resolve conflict and how to raise children — constitute a genuine body of ethical understanding, transmitted across generations through practice, story, ceremony, and governance. The norms are not deduced from first principles; they are learned by living rightly in a place over a very long time. Coulthard draws this from the Dene relationship to their territory, but the structure recurs across Indigenous traditions: ethics as something the land teaches those who attend to it.

This makes grounded normativity, in plain terms, relational thinking practiced on the land. If a community's life is constituted by its relations — to its territory, to its kin, to the other beings it shares a place with — then the specific relations it sustains with its land are not secondary to some abstract principle that could be stated anywhere. They are the very substance of its ethical life. The obligations are co-equal kinship extended beyond the human: each relation carries inherent standing and generates real, place-specific obligations, and what one owes follows from the particular tie rather than from a universal rule that treats all situations alike.

Grounded normativity is the ethical ground beneath Indigenous resurgence and indigenous sovereignty. Resurgence is the regeneration of the very practices through which grounded normativity is lived and transmitted; sovereignty is the political assertion of a people's authority to govern by it. Set against the abstract, transposable norm of liberal and extractive frameworks, grounded normativity is its reciprocal, land-bound counterpart — and, on Coulthard's account, the prior one, since peoples were governing themselves through place-grounded ethics long before the universal norm arrived to declare them illegitimate.
