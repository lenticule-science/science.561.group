# immaterial-labor

Immaterial labor is the term Maurizio Lazzarato introduced in his 1996 essay of the same name for "the labor that produces the informational and cultural content of the commodity." It anchors the post-operaismo tradition's analysis of contemporary work and is the field's most-debated category. Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri extended it across the ambitious macro-political synthesis of *Empire* (2000) and *Multitude* (2004); Carlo Vercellone developed it within heterodox economics as cognitive capitalism; and the feminist tradition of affective-labor analysis (Arlie Hochschild, Kathi Weeks) carried it toward care and emotion. Generated by: **labor-process** in its post-Fordist form — immaterial labor names what the labor process becomes when its characteristic product is no longer a physical thing but information, communication, and feeling.

Lazzarato divides immaterial labor into two dimensions whose combination characterizes much contemporary work. The *informational* dimension covers the way industrial labor has been transformed by computing and process control, so that the worker's relevant activity is increasingly the manipulation of symbols and information rather than the transformation of physical materials — the factory worker who once tightened bolts now watches process-control screens, the clerk who once filed papers now manages databases. The *cultural* dimension covers the production of meaning: the activities that determine fashions, tastes, consumer norms, and public opinion, performed by advertising creatives, designers, social-media managers, brand consultants. Most jobs combine the two: a barista produces a drink (material) but also a customer experience and a contribution to the café's brand atmosphere (cultural); a developer produces code (informational) embodying aesthetic and usability judgments (cultural).

The post-operaismo tradition identifies several features that distinguish immaterial labor from the industrial labor on which Marx built his analysis. It tends to *produce subjectivity*: its characteristic output is the consumer, the user, the public — a disposition or a habit — alongside or instead of any material product. It *mobilizes communicative and affective capacities* that workers develop largely outside the wage relation, in the broader social and cultural environment: a competence in language, social cognition, emotional regulation, and taste that no employer trained and that the wage compensates only indirectly. Its *value is indeterminate* — the worth of an hour of design or scholarly writing cannot be read off the time it took, which puts strain on any attempt to measure it in labor-time. And it *spills across the boundary between work and non-work*: the worker who builds a personal brand on social media after hours, the academic who reads in the evening, the consultant whose dinner conversation is reconnaissance — all illustrate a boundary the wage relation can no longer hold.

The concept generated a large literature and a vigorous debate, and an honest account names the objections as well as the claims. Hochschild's earlier work on emotional labor (the airline attendant's managed smile) and the feminist analysis of care work entered productive conversation with the post-operaismo framework, with Kathi Weeks supplying an influential synthesis. But many critics argue that the thesis overestimates novelty and neglects the continuing material base of contemporary economies — mining, manufacturing, agriculture, logistics, care — and that it treats the knowledge-worker's experience as paradigmatic when it is empirically a minority, and provincial to the post-Fordist economies of the Global North, where industrial and pre-industrial forms remain dominant elsewhere. The concept is now standard apparatus in critical theory of work, cultural studies, and platform studies, but usually carried with these qualifications attached.

As a node, immaterial labor is the post-operaismo extension of the labor-process tradition into the contemporary, informational economy. It draws on the broader social-cooperative substrate the tradition calls the general intellect, and it is the theoretical antecedent of the analyses of platform and algorithmic work that observe the same informational and affective labor now organized through apps and ratings.
