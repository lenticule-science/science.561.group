# indigenous-resurgence

Indigenous resurgence is the regeneration of Indigenous practices, governance, knowledge systems, and relationships to land as acts of freedom — not as performances staged for settler audiences, and not as applications submitted to the settler state for approval. The framework is most closely associated with Leanne Betasamosake Simpson (Michi Saagiig Nishnaabeg) and Glen Coulthard (Yellowknives Dene). Generated by: grounded-normativity — resurgence is grounded normativity regenerating itself, the land-based ethic renewed in practice as a people rebuilds the relationships through which it lives.

Resurgence is defined in the first instance against the politics of recognition. Recognition asks the settler-colonial state to acknowledge Indigenous rights, title, and identity; resurgence practices Indigenous life without waiting for that acknowledgment. Coulthard's central argument in *Red Skin, White Masks* (2014) is that recognition, when it is granted by the colonizer, reproduces the colonial relationship rather than ending it: it makes Indigenous freedom conditional on settler approval, draws Indigenous people into seeking validation from the very power that dispossessed them, and shapes them to want what that power is willing to give. The subject who waits to be recognized has already accepted the recognizer's authority. Resurgence refuses this conditionality by refusing to make settler acknowledgment the measure of Indigenous life.

In practice, resurgence is a positive, generative program rather than a list of demands. It includes the revitalization of Indigenous languages, the renewal of land-based practices — harvesting, ceremony, and governance carried out on the land itself — the transmission of knowledge through Indigenous pedagogical forms rather than colonial schooling, and the rebuilding of governance structures that operate outside, alongside, and sometimes against the settler state. Simpson describes resurgence as "generative refusal": its refusal of colonial recognition is not its primary content but its precondition. The point is not mainly to oppose the colonial state but to generate Indigenous life on Indigenous terms — to do the things, raise the children, speak the languages, and renew the relationships that make a people itself, whether or not the state is watching or approving.

In the terms of relational thinking, resurgence is the reconstitution of relations that settler colonialism set out to sever. Colonialism worked by cutting the ties through which Indigenous peoples were constituted — to land, to kin, to language, to ceremony, to the knowledge carried in all of these — and replacing them with dependence on colonial institutions. Resurgence reweaves those ties. It is the active rebuilding of the relational field, and because it works by regenerating practice rather than by petitioning for rights, it sits downstream of grounded normativity: it is the land-based ethic put back into living motion, the renewal of the very practices through which that ethic is held and passed on. Where indigenous sovereignty asserts the political authority a people holds, resurgence is the everyday work of living that authority out.
