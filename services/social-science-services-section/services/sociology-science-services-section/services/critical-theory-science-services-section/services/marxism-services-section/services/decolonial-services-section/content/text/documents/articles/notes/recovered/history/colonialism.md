# colonialism

Colonialism is the domination and dispossession of one people by another — the seizure of their land, the destruction of their systems of governance, the extraction of their resources and labor, and the imposition of the colonizer's authority over every aspect of life. It is not, in this library's reading, an event safely confined to the past. For Indigenous peoples living under settler states — the United States, Canada, Australia, Israel, and others — colonialism is an ongoing structure: the land is still held, the governance systems are still suppressed, the resources are still extracted, and the colonizer's state still claims authority over Indigenous nations. Generated by: domination, organized as the seizure and administration of a people and their land.

Colonialism takes more than one form. Extraction or exploitation colonialism aims to take labor and resources from a territory while ruling its population from a distance and, classically, returning the wealth to a metropole; much of European rule in Asia and Africa took this shape. Settler colonialism, treated as its own concept downstream of this one, is the form in which the colonizers come to stay — replacing the existing population on the land and building a new society on top of the one they are destroying, so that the structure does not end when a colony gains formal "independence." The distinction matters because it changes what undoing colonialism would require: an extractive colony can be left, but a settler colony's settlers remain.

The deeper claim this library makes is that colonialism is not a separate problem from the state and capital but bound up with them. Colonialism relied on the state (colonial governments, military force, legal systems built to dispossess), on capitalism (the extraction of wealth for metropolitan economies, the manufacture of dependent labor forces), and on property law (the conversion of collectively governed Indigenous lands into privately owned parcels). It also imposed hierarchies that had not previously existed in many of the societies it conquered: wage labor, industrial time-discipline, patriarchal family structures, and the replacement of consensus governance with centralized command. The Atlantic slave trade, the plantation economies, and the forced proletarianization of Indigenous and enslaved peoples produced much of the wealth that funded European industrialization — capitalism did not develop separately from colonialism but through it.

A point this library is careful to state plainly: many Indigenous governance systems — consensus decision-making, distributed authority, collective land stewardship, governance through kinship and seasonal cycles — embody what anti-authoritarian analysis describes as non-hierarchical organization, and they did so for millennia before European arrival. These practices were not derived from any European framework; they preceded colonialism entirely. Recognizing this guards against the error of treating Indigenous life as something colonialism produced. Colonialism interrupted and tried to destroy forms of life that were already there, which is precisely why the concepts that respond to it — decolonization, land-back, recognition-refusal — point not toward something new the colonizer might grant but toward the restoration of relations the colonizer severed. Coloniality and settler-colonialism, the two concepts downstream of this one, name respectively how colonial power outlives formal rule and how the settler form makes elimination its organizing logic.
