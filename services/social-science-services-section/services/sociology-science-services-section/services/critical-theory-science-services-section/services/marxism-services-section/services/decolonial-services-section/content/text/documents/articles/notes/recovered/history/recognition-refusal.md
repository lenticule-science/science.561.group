# recognition-refusal

Recognition-refusal is the rejection of the demand that Indigenous peoples legitimate their governance, knowledge, and sovereignty by translating them into terms the settler-colonial state can recognize. Glen Coulthard (Yellowknives Dene) develops the concept in *Red Skin, White Masks* (2014), drawing on and inverting Frantz Fanon's analysis of colonial recognition in *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952). Generated by: refusing recognition — recognition was the dominant power's offer to acknowledge the subordinate on the dominant's own terms, and its refusal is the decolonial move. The crucial qualification: recognition-refusal is defined against the settler offer of recognition, but the positive ground it clears space for — Indigenous resurgence, grounded normativity, the relationships to land and kin that sustain Indigenous political life — precedes recognition and is in no way produced by it. Indigenous sovereignty is not downstream of the settler state; it is what the settler state interrupted.

Fanon argued that the colonized subject who seeks recognition from the colonizer enters a trap. The terms of recognition are set by the colonizer, and what is recognized is not the colonized as they are but a version of them the colonial framework can accommodate. Recognition does not end domination; it reformats it. The colonized subject becomes legible to the colonial order, and legibility is the precondition for administration. Coulthard applies this to the Canadian settler-colonial context: liberal multiculturalism offers Indigenous peoples recognition — land-claims processes, self-government agreements, heritage protections — but only on terms that presuppose the legitimacy of the Canadian state and the sovereignty of the Crown. What cannot survive translation into the language of rights and constitutional procedure — governance grounded in specific relationships to land, in ceremony, in oral tradition and kinship obligation — is excluded or reduced to cultural decoration.

Recognition-refusal answers: we do not need your recognition. Indigenous governance does not derive its legitimacy from the settler state's acknowledgment, and grounded normativity — the ethical and political knowledge that arises from Indigenous relationships to specific places and practices — is self-legitimating. It does not require translation into the dominant power's universal terms in order to be real. The refusal is not withdrawal or sulk; it is the assertion of a sovereignty that was never the settler's to grant. Audra Simpson's *Mohawk Interruptus* (2014) documents the practice concretely: the Kahnawake Mohawks carry Haudenosaunee passports, govern their own territory, and decline the recognition framework altogether — not asking Canada to acknowledge their sovereignty but simply exercising it.

The constructive dimension is what Leanne Betasamosake Simpson, in *As We Have Always Done* (2017), calls resurgence: if recognition is refused, what replaces it is not a void but the active practice of Indigenous life, governance, and knowledge on Indigenous terms. Resurgence does not seek a place within the settler framework; it operates from its own relational ground, rebuilding the nations whose continuity colonialism tried to interrupt. Within this library recognition-refusal is the direct refusal-counterpart of recognition and sits alongside decolonization and land-back among the moves against settler-colonial domination. All three are defined in opposition to that structure, but each rests on a positive ground — Indigenous sovereignty and grounded normativity — that the structure did not create and cannot revoke.
