# affect-governance

Affect governance names the management of feeling — emotional response, affective orientation, mood — as
a mode of governance: the claim that a social order can be held together and steered not primarily through
ideology or coercion but by formatting how people feel. Where older accounts located the durability of
domination in belief (people are persuaded) or in force (people are compelled), affect governance locates
it in feeling (people are emotionally oriented so that the order reproduces itself through their own
self-management). Generated by: **liberalism** — affect governance is the mode by which a late-liberal
formation rules, formatting feeling where it can no longer rely on shared belief or open coercion.

The concept synthesizes several lines of work. From Arlie Hochschild it takes *feeling rules*: the
institutional prescriptions for which emotions are appropriate, legitimate, and expected — the
infrastructure through which feeling is normatively governed in the first place. From Lauren Berlant it
takes *cruel optimism* and *affective infrastructure*: the analysis of how attachment to a fraying present
is cultivated and sustained, and how the ordinary labor of metabolizing crisis becomes the means by which
a system persists without changing. Behind both stands a broader claim, developed in affect theory
(Massumi, Ahmed, Stewart) and in the analysis of late liberalism, that social order is maintained through
the organization of affect as much as through argument or law — that a rally moves people by rhythm and
intensity, a workplace secures compliance by atmosphere, and a political order holds its subjects by
shaping the felt sense of what is possible before any belief is formed.

What distinguishes affect governance from feeling rules considered alone is its *governing* function: it
is a mechanism of rule because it is self-reproducing. The defining move is the conversion of structural
conditions into personal feeling. A structural crisis — in housing, employment, health, climate — is, by
the system's formatting, made to register first as a personal emotional matter: something to be felt,
managed, recovered from, optimized around, rather than something to be analyzed structurally or acted on
collectively. The disposition to "route the crisis through personal affect" arrives faster than, and tends
to preempt, the disposition toward collective response — partly because the affective channels are
ambient and immediate while the collective channels require infrastructure most people lack. The subject
who processes structural contradiction as private feeling keeps participating; their participation
generates further feeling to be managed; and the order is governed without any central enforcer, because
the work of governance is performed by subjects on themselves.

A second mechanism is the policing of which feelings may be expressed at all. Affect governance does not
only prescribe positive feeling; it delegitimates feeling that threatens the order. Public expressions of
structural distress — anger, grief, refusal directed at the system rather than the self — tend to be
recoded as signs that the speaker has failed to manage themselves, costing them standing and authority,
and thereby redirecting them back toward private affective self-work. The demand becomes serenity,
resilience, equanimity: the well-governed subject is the one who has metabolized the contradiction into
calm. This is the dynamic the late-liberal analysis sometimes calls *zen fascism* — the conversion of a
demand for collective change into a demand for individual composure, where the appearance of distress is
itself the disqualifying offense.

For a graduate student, affect governance is best understood as a thesis about *where* late-liberal power
operates. Its claim is that contemporary rule increasingly works upstream of belief, at the level of mood,
attachment, and emotional orientation — formatting feeling so that structural contradiction is absorbed
personally rather than contested collectively. It gathers feeling rules, emotional labor, cruel optimism,
and affective infrastructure into a single account of governance-by-feeling, and it is the apparatus that
lets a fraying formation *hold* its subjects: keeping them attached to what harms them, busy managing the
crisis privately, and emotionally composed enough to keep the present going without it ever resolving.
