Trust in infrastructure is the affective disposition of relying on a sociotechnical system to function as expected without personally verifying its operation — a disposition Giddens generalizes as trust in "abstract systems": modern social life depends on disembedding mechanisms, expert systems and technical infrastructures that lift social relations out of local, face-to-face contexts, and trust in those mechanisms substitutes for the face-to-face trust that anchored earlier social forms (Giddens 1990).

Trust in infrastructure run by comrades differs from trust in infrastructure run by a corporation not in its underlying psychological mechanism but in its object and its available repair: Giddens's account of trust in abstract systems does not distinguish who operates the system, but a user of a movement-run tech collective can appeal to shared political commitment and direct relationship as grounds for trust, where a user of a commercial platform has only the platform's stated terms of service and its market incentive not to lose customers — grounds that do not hold when a state actor, rather than a competitor, is the threat.

The consequence is that trust in infrastructure is not simply higher or lower for movement-run systems than for commercial ones; it is differently grounded, and the empirical study of why activists do and do not adopt security tools found that the practical consequence of ungrounded trust — not understanding what a given tool's guarantees actually cover — was a larger driver of tool-adoption failure than either excessive trust or excessive suspicion (Abu-Salma et al. 2017).
