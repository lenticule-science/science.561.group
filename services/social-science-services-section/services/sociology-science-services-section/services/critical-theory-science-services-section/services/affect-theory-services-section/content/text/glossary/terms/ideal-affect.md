Ideal affect is the set of feeling states a person wants to feel, as distinct from their actual affect, the states they do feel. The term belongs to Jeanne Tsai's affect valuation theory. It holds that culture shapes ideal affect more than actual affect, and that ideal affect in turn shapes what people choose, admire and consume.

The best-measured difference is arousal. European Americans value high-arousal positive states (excitement, enthusiasm, elation) more than Hong Kong Chinese do. Hong Kong Chinese value low-arousal positive states (calm, peacefulness, relaxation) more. Asian Americans value both. The differences hold after controlling for actual affect, and they appear in children's storybooks and in magazine advertising, not only in self-report (Tsai, Knutson and Fung, "Cultural Variation in Affect Valuation", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 90(2), 2006, [PubMed 16536652](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16536652/)).

Ideal affect is a companion to [feeling rules](./feeling-rules.md). Feeling rules say which feeling is owed in a situation; ideal affect says which feeling a person hopes to be in. A genre answers to both. It prescribes what should move its readers, and it offers them the state they want to reach.
