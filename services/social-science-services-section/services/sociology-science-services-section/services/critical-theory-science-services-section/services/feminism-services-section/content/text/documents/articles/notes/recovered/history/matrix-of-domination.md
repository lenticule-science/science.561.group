# matrix-of-domination

The matrix of domination is Patricia Hill Collins's name, developed in *Black Feminist Thought* (1990), for the structure the Combahee River Collective called interlocking. Domination, in this account, is organized through the intersection of multiple systems — race, class, gender, sexuality, nation, age — operating together as a single matrix rather than as a stack of separate axes. The framing refuses both the additive reading of oppressions (race plus class plus gender) and the reduction of the whole to any single primary axis. Generated by: interlocking-systems — the matrix is Collins's sociological systematization of the Combahee insight.

Collins's most worked-out version holds that the matrix operates through four domains of power. The structural domain comprises the macro-level institutions — economy, polity, family, education, law, healthcare — where the major distributive arrangements are made and categorical inequalities are reproduced at scale. The disciplinary domain comprises bureaucratic rule, surveillance, and the institutional protocols that govern movement through the structural domain, translating structural inequality into individual experience through procedures that present themselves as neutral. The hegemonic domain comprises the cultural production — media, schooling, religion, common sense — that legitimates the structural and disciplinary arrangements and makes them appear natural rather than produced. The interpersonal domain comprises the everyday encounters in which individuals, occupying their positions in the matrix, act upon one another from those positions.

The point of the four-domain scheme is that they operate together, simultaneously, on any given case. Take a Black woman worker who experiences harassment at her job: she is positioned at all four levels at once — by the structural arrangements that placed her in the job she holds, by the disciplinary apparatus that will process or fail to process her complaint, by the hegemonic frames that determine whether her account is heard as credible, and by the interpersonal encounters in which the harassment actually occurs. A single-axis analysis cannot describe her situation; a multi-domain analysis can. The matrix is thus not only a claim about which systems interlock but a method for reading where and how they do their work.

The matrix of domination converges with intersectionality — categories produced through their intersection rather than summed from their separation — and the two describe the same object from different points of entry, Collins's from Black-feminist sociology and Crenshaw's from Black-feminist legal theory. Within this library the matrix is one of the central analytical tools for the whole interlocking branch of domination; it is also the heuristic the analysis of the carceral system and of organized abandonment relies on, since reading those structures requires moving across all four domains at once. It is, in short, the sociologist's instrument for the claim the interlocking-systems entry states and intersectionality formalizes: that domination is a single weave, and must be read as one.
