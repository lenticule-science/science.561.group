## Abstract

Organizations routinely present their formal rules — hiring criteria, promotion timelines, compensation formulas — as gender-neutral: the same standard, applied the same way, to everyone. This paper argues that this neutrality is not evidence of fairness but a specific kind of accounting artifact. Formal rules are neutral only with respect to what an organization chooses to measure, and what organizations measure has been built, historically and structurally, around a worker who is fed, rested, and free of caregiving obligations by virtue of someone else's unpaid labor. Because that labor is overwhelmingly performed by women, a rule that never mentions gender can still impose systematically unequal real costs by gender — and because the labor is excluded from the organization's own accounting, the inequality is invisible from inside the very system that produced it. Drawing on feminist political economy, the sociology of invisible work, status-characteristics research, and audit-study and archival evidence on hiring, pay, and promotion, this paper traces the mechanism from its theoretical origins @acker1990 @federici2004 through its empirical documentation @correllbenardpaik2007 @castilla2008 to its legal analogue in disparate-impact doctrine. It closes by arguing that reforms which leave organizational accounting practices untouched — removing explicit bias, adding representation — cannot resolve a problem that is located in what gets counted, not in what gets said.

## 1. Introduction

A hiring rubric that scores every candidate on the same criteria. A tenure clock that gives every assistant professor the same six years. A pay-for-performance system that ties raises to the same performance rating scale for everyone. Each of these is neutral in the ordinary sense of the word: no clause mentions gender, no evaluator is instructed to treat men and women differently, and if audited for explicit bias, each would likely pass.

And yet each of these mechanisms has been shown, empirically and repeatedly, to produce outcomes that differ systematically by gender @correllbenardpaik2007 @masongoulden2002 @castilla2008. This is not primarily a story about individual prejudice, though prejudice plays a role. It is a story about what an organization's formal apparatus is built to see. This paper's central claim, developed across the sections that follow, is that formal organizational neutrality is compatible with — and in specific, well-documented conditions, produces — structural dependence on unpaid, gendered labor that the organization's own accounting does not recognize as labor at all. The neutrality is real, in the narrow sense that the rule genuinely contains no gender term. What is not real is the inference organizations invite from that fact: that because the rule is blind to gender, its effects must be too.

The argument proceeds in three stages. First, it establishes the theoretical basis for treating organizations as built around an implicitly gendered "ideal worker" @acker1990 @williams2000, and situates this within the broader feminist critique of formal equality as a legal and political strategy @mackinnon1987 @fredman2016. Second, it traces the specific mechanism by which unpaid reproductive labor is externalized from formal economic and organizational accounting @dallacostajames1972 @federici2004 @waring1988, and how that externalization is compounded by a second, independent mechanism operating through evaluators' status beliefs rather than through accounting at all @ridgeway2011 @castillabenard2010. Third, it reviews the empirical record — audit studies, archival personnel data, and time-use surveys — that has, over roughly three decades, converted this argument from theoretical claim to documented pattern.

## 2. The Gendered Organization: Theoretical Foundations

### 2.1 Acker's abstract worker

Joan Acker's "Hierarchies, Jobs, Bodies: A Theory of Gendered Organizations" @acker1990 remains the foundational statement of the claim this paper develops. Acker's argument is that organizational structures — job descriptions, career ladders, definitions of commitment and availability — are built around an image of "the worker" that has no body: no menstrual cycle, no pregnancy, no obligation to leave in the middle of the day for a sick child, no one at home who needs anything from him. This worker is available on demand. Acker's point is not that organizations discriminate against women who fail to resemble this figure — although they do — but that the figure itself is not neutral. It is a description of a person whose reproductive and care needs are being met, invisibly, by someone else. The organization did not have to specify "the ideal worker is male" for the job description to nonetheless describe, overwhelmingly, a life that only a particular arrangement of unpaid domestic labor makes possible.

Acker's later work @acker2006 extends this into what she calls "inequality regimes" — the observation that the practices generating gender inequality inside organizations are the same practices generating class and racial inequality, operating through ostensibly neutral mechanisms like job evaluation, hiring networks, and informal mentorship access. The throughline across both papers is that neutrality is a property of the rule's language, not of its effects, and the gap between the two is not an oversight to be corrected but a structural feature of how the rule was built.

### 2.2 The ideal-worker norm

Joan Williams's *Unbending Gender* @williams2000 gives this figure a name that has stuck in the organizational literature: the "ideal worker" norm. Williams's argument is doctrinal as much as sociological — she traces how U.S. employment law, in the name of formal equality, has consistently protected employers' right to demand full, uninterrupted availability, and has treated the resulting exclusion of caregivers (disproportionately mothers) as a neutral consequence of job requirements rather than as gender discrimination. Mary Blair-Loy's *Competing Devotions* @blairloy2003 develops the same idea from the inside, documenting how women executives in finance describe internalizing two competing "devotion schemas" — total devotion to career, total devotion to family — that the ideal-worker norm treats as incompatible by design, since the norm was never built to accommodate someone holding both.

### 2.3 MacKinnon and the limits of formal equality

Catharine MacKinnon's *Feminism Unmodified* @mackinnon1987 supplies the jurisprudential vocabulary for why "neutral treatment" is an inadequate response to this structure. MacKinnon identifies what she calls the sameness/difference trap: anti-discrimination law offers women either sameness (identical treatment to men) or difference (special accommodation), and both options leave the baseline against which sameness or difference is measured untouched — a baseline built, as Acker's abstract worker shows, around the unencumbered male life. Sandra Fredman's more recent synthesis @fredman2016 systematizes the alternative as "substantive equality" along four dimensions — redressing disadvantage, addressing stigma, enabling participation, and accommodating difference — each of which a formally neutral rule is, by definition, indifferent to. Formal neutrality is not a weaker version of substantive equality; on Fredman's account, it is close to its opposite, because it defines fairness precisely as not looking at the facts substantive equality asks an institution to track.

## 3. The Externalization of Reproductive Labor

### 3.1 The Marxist-feminist political economy

The theoretical foundation for why unpaid care labor ends up structurally necessary to, yet excluded from, organizational accounting comes from the wages-for-housework tradition of the 1970s. Mariarosa Dalla Costa and Selma James's *The Power of Women and the Subversion of the Community* @dallacostajames1972 made the argument that reproductive labor — cooking, cleaning, childrearing, caring for the sick and elderly — is not peripheral to capitalist production but a precondition for it: a worker can be paid a wage covering only his own subsistence precisely because someone else's unpaid labor reproduces him day to day and reproduces the next generation of workers at no cost to the employer. Leopoldina Fortunati's *The Arcane of Reproduction* @fortunati1995 formalizes the same claim, arguing that the household labor process is structurally identical to the workplace labor process, with gender substituting for the wage as the mechanism of extraction.

Silvia Federici's *Caliban and the Witch* @federici2004 extends this analysis historically, arguing that the transition to capitalism required not only the enclosure of common land but a parallel enclosure of women's reproductive capacity — the witch trials, on her account, were in part a political campaign against women who retained autonomous control over reproduction and healing knowledge, clearing the way for a gendered division of labor in which men's labor was waged and women's was not. In *Revolution at Point Zero* (2012), Federici draws the organizational-level implication directly: workplace scheduling, expectations of availability, and career-progression timelines all presuppose that someone, somewhere, is doing the reproductive work that makes an employee's participation possible, and the presupposition is nowhere written into policy because it does not need to be — it is simply built into what counts as a normal working life.

### 3.2 Feminist economics and the accounting problem

A parallel literature in feminist economics makes the same point at the level of measurement rather than production. Marilyn Waring's *If Women Counted* @waring1988 is the founding text here: Waring shows that the System of National Accounts underlying GDP explicitly excludes unpaid household production from what counts as economic output, so that a woman cooking for her own family contributes nothing to measured output, while the same activity performed for pay does. Antonella Picchio's *Social Reproduction* @picchio1992 develops the theoretical case that this is not a measurement oversight correctable by better statistics but a structural feature of how the "supply price" of labor is calculated: wages are set as though the worker's day-to-day reproduction were free, because a substantial share of it, priced at market rates, actually is being performed for nothing.

Nancy Folbre's *The Invisible Heart* @folbre2001 brings this into a more directly organizational register, examining how care work — for children, the elderly, the ill — generates value that markets systematically undercount, and identifying a specific complication: care work often depends for its quality on not being priced or instrumentalized in the first place, meaning that even attempts to formally recognize and compensate it can change its character. Nancy Fraser's more recent essay "Contradictions of Capital and Care" @fraser2016 names the resulting situation a "crisis of care": contemporary capitalism, Fraser argues, has intensified its reliance on women's unpaid and underpaid labor for social reproduction at precisely the moment that the same economic pressures have pulled more women into paid work, producing a structural squeeze with no institutional mechanism designed to resolve it.

## 4. Two Mechanisms That Make the Externalization Invisible

The externalization described in Section 3 would not be self-sustaining if it were simply visible and unaddressed — organizations would face constant, legible pressure to account for it. Two further, more sociological mechanisms explain why it instead remains largely invisible from inside the organizations that depend on it.

### 4.1 Invisible work and the politics of what counts

Anselm Strauss's concept of "articulation work" @strauss1985 — the scheduling, repairing, translating, and aligning labor that makes interdependent tasks cohere into a working whole — set off a research program showing that this coordinating labor is systematically uncredited. Susan Leigh Star's "The Sociology of the Invisible" @star1991 and the subsequent collaboration with Strauss @starstrauss1999 establish that this invisibility is not incidental: recognition as "work" tracks status, gender, and organizational position, so that the identical activity is counted as labor when performed by some workers and as natural disposition, helpfulness, or unpaid extra effort when performed by others. Lucy Suchman's "Making Work Visible" @suchman1995 adds the crucial point that what counts as work is itself politically contested and actively produced through organizational accounting practices, not a neutral description waiting to be recorded accurately. Arlene Kaplan Daniels's "Invisible Work" @daniels1987 extends the category explicitly to domestic labor, emotional labor, and community-maintenance work performed largely by women and left out of formal labor statistics entirely.

Arlie Hochschild's *The Managed Heart* @hochschild1983 identifies a specific subtype directly relevant to organizational life: emotional labor, the management of one's own feelings to produce a desired feeling in another (the flight attendant's practiced calm, the nurse's manufactured warmth), which is disproportionately demanded of and performed by women in service and caregiving roles, typically without being named in a job description or compensated as a distinct skill. Hochschild and Anne Machung's *The Second Shift* @hochschildmachung1989 documents the household-level counterpart: employed women, the study found, return home to a second full workload of domestic labor that their male partners, even in dual-earner households, largely do not share equally — meaning the externalized labor discussed in Section 3 does not disappear when a woman also holds paid employment; it is simply performed twice.

### 4.2 Status beliefs and the paradox of meritocracy

A second, independent mechanism operates not through what organizations fail to count, but through how evaluators apply the criteria they do count. Cecilia Ridgeway's *Framed by Gender* @ridgeway2011 synthesizes several decades of status-characteristics research to argue that gender functions as a diffuse status cue that shapes evaluators' judgments of competence even under formally identical criteria — evaluators are not applying different rules to men and women; they are applying the same rule while unconsciously supplying different priors about who is likely to satisfy it.

Emilio Castilla's archival study "Gender, Race, and Meritocracy in Organizational Careers" @castilla2008 provides direct evidence of this mechanism inside a single large service-sector firm that used a formally standardized, transparent, performance-based compensation system explicitly designed to eliminate bias. Castilla found that women and racial minorities received smaller salary increases than white men who had received *identical* performance ratings from the same evaluators under the same formal rule — the "neutral" system was not being applied unevenly through a hidden rule change; it was producing unequal outcomes from apparently equal formal inputs. Castilla and Stephen Benard's follow-up experimental work, "The Paradox of Meritocracy in Organizations" (2010), goes further: they find that explicitly labeling an organizational system as merit-based can paradoxically *increase* managers' willingness to favor male employees over equally qualified women, because the belief that the system is already fair licenses evaluators to trust their own judgment rather than scrutinize it. Formal neutrality, on this evidence, does not merely fail to prevent the disparity — under specific conditions it actively enables it, by converting a system's stated fairness into evaluators' confidence that bias-checking is unnecessary.

These two mechanisms — accounting invisibility and status-belief perturbation — are independent of one another. The first concerns what labor an organization's ledger recognizes as having occurred; the second concerns how evaluators judge a candidate or employee even when the ledger's inputs are held constant. Because they are independent, they compound rather than substitute, a point the empirical literature bears out directly, discussed next.

## 5. The Empirical Record

### 5.1 The motherhood penalty and fatherhood bonus

Shelley Correll, Stephen Benard, and In Paik's "Getting a Job: Is There a Motherhood Penalty?" @correllbenardpaik2007 is the most direct experimental demonstration of the status-belief mechanism. The study sent matched pairs of fictitious resumes, identical except for a parenthood cue (membership in a parent-teacher association versus a neutral community organization), to real job openings, and additionally tested employer evaluations of the same resumes in a controlled lab setting. Mothers received lower competence ratings, fewer callbacks, and lower recommended starting salaries than otherwise identical non-mothers; fathers, by contrast, received a "fatherhood bonus" — higher competence ratings and higher recommended salaries than non-fathers with identical resumes. Because the resumes were identical apart from the parenthood cue, and the hiring criteria applied were formally the same for every applicant, the study isolates the status-belief channel directly, independent of any actual difference in productivity or availability.

Michelle Budig and Paula England's "The Wage Penalty for Motherhood" @budigengland2001, using longitudinal U.S. panel data, find a persistent wage penalty of roughly five to seven percent per child for mothers, even after controlling for experience, education, hours worked, and occupation. Importantly, the penalty is only partially explained by these labor-supply-side factors — a residual gap remains after they are accounted for, consistent with an additional discrimination-based mechanism operating on top of any real reduction in continuous experience. This is the compounding predicted in Section 4: reduced measurable labor-market inputs (partially reflecting the externalized caregiving burden discussed in Section 3) combine with a separate evaluator-side penalty that the input data alone does not explain.

### 5.2 Academic careers and the tenure clock

Mary Ann Mason and Marc Goulden's research on the academic tenure clock @masongoulden2002 documents that a rule containing no reference to gender — publish sufficiently and secure a positive recommendation within a fixed number of years, typically six — interacts with the biological and social timing of childbearing and early childcare to produce systematically lower tenure-track retention and promotion rates for women who have children during that window, compared to men in equivalent positions and to childless women. Lauren Rivera's "When Two Bodies Are (Not) a Problem" @rivera2017 extends this into hiring itself, finding that in academic search committees, a candidate's apparent relationship and parental status interacts with gender in evaluators' assessments in ways that formal search criteria do not register or authorize.

### 5.3 Overwork norms and the widening wage gap

Youngjoo Cha and Kim Weeden's "Overwork and the Slow Convergence in the Gender Gap in Wages" (2010, published 2014) identifies a specific, contemporary mechanism reproducing Acker's ideal-worker norm in the wage structure itself: as more occupations began rewarding long hours (50+ per week) with disproportionately higher pay, and as men remained more able to supply those hours given the persistent unequal division of unpaid care work documented by Hochschild and Machung, the reward for overwork became a substantial and growing driver of the aggregate gender wage gap — again, through a formally neutral mechanism (pay scales with hours worked) rather than an explicit rule differentiating by gender.

### 5.4 Global time-use data

The International Labour Organization's global synthesis, *Care Work and Care Jobs for the Future of Decent Work* (2018), aggregates time-use survey data across dozens of national economies and finds that women perform unpaid care work at a rate on the order of three times that of men worldwide, a ratio that holds, with variation, across income levels, regions, and labor-force-participation rates. This is the empirical scale of the externalization described theoretically in Section 3: the imbalance Waring identified in 1988 as a measurement exclusion remains, thirty years later, a measured and substantial real-world asymmetry.

## 6. A Structural Parallel in Employment Law

The pattern this paper describes — a facially neutral rule producing systematically unequal effects because of an unequally distributed precondition the rule presupposes — was recognized independently, decades earlier, in a different institutional and doctrinal context. In *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.* (401 U.S. 424, 1971), the U.S. Supreme Court held that an employer's requirement of a high school diploma and passing scores on general aptitude tests, applied identically to all applicants and containing no reference to race, nonetheless violated Title VII of the Civil Rights Act because it produced a substantially disproportionate exclusionary effect on Black applicants, given the unequal educational access those applicants had historically been afforded, and because the employer could not demonstrate the requirements were meaningfully related to job performance. *Griggs* established the disparate-impact doctrine: intent to discriminate is not required where a neutral practice produces a discriminatory effect that is not justified by business necessity.

This is not offered here as a claim that gender and care-labor dependence are legally identical to race and educational credentialing — they are distinct histories with distinct mechanisms. But the doctrine's core inferential structure is the same one this paper traces: a rule's neutral wording does not settle the question of its effects, and an unequally distributed precondition can convert a neutral rule into a systematically unequal one without any discriminatory intent on the part of whoever wrote the rule. That U.S. employment law arrived at this structure independently, in a different domain, is a form of corroboration that the mechanism is general rather than specific to gender and care work alone.

## 7. Discussion: Why Formal Reform Falls Short

The evidence reviewed above supports a specific, somewhat uncomfortable conclusion: interventions that operate on the wording of the rule — removing an implicit bias from a job description, adding a nondiscrimination clause, running a one-time unconscious-bias training — do not, on their own, address either of the two mechanisms identified in Section 4. Accounting invisibility is not fixed by better language in the rule; it is fixed, if at all, by changing what the organization's ledger records as labor — Waring's proposed satellite accounts for unpaid work being the macroeconomic analogue, and organizational practices like accounting for caregiving leave as legitimate career-continuous time (rather than a gap) the organizational one. Status-belief perturbation is, if anything, made worse by declarations of formal fairness, per Castilla and Benard's @castillabenard2010 finding that labeling a system "meritocratic" can increase rather than decrease evaluator bias by lowering evaluators' guard against their own judgment.

bell hooks's *Feminist Theory: From Margin to Center* @hooks1984 anticipated this outcome from a different angle, arguing that a liberal feminism focused on access — adding women to existing leadership structures without changing what those structures require or reward — tends to improve outcomes for women already closest to the existing "ideal worker" norm (typically white, professional, and without primary caregiving responsibility) while leaving the structure, and the women furthest from that norm, untouched. Fredman's @fredman2016 substantive-equality framework gives this the same diagnosis in doctrinal terms: formal equality is not a weaker or partial version of substantive equality; it is a different aim entirely, one that is satisfied by sameness of treatment regardless of whether that sameness redresses disadvantage, and the empirical record surveyed in Section 5 shows organizations reliably achieving formal equality while substantive disparity persists or, per the overwork findings of Cha and Weeden @chaweeden2014, widens.

What the literature suggests instead, taken together, falls into two broad categories. The first targets the accounting boundary directly: parental leave designed with non-transferable, use-it-or-lose-it segments for each parent (rather than a shared, mother-defaulting pool), tenure-clock extensions triggered automatically rather than requiring a formal, often career-marking request, and internal cost-accounting that treats predictable care-related absence as a planned organizational cost rather than an individual deviation from a norm. The second targets the status-belief channel directly: structured, criteria-anchored evaluation applied before rather than after candidates are known to be parents or caregivers (a "blind audition" logic, applied to hiring and review rather than only to orchestral seating), and — per Castilla and Benard's @castillabenard2010 finding — active, ongoing bias-auditing of outcomes precisely because a system's formal meritocratic label cannot be trusted to indicate its actual fairness.

## 8. Conclusion

Formal organizational neutrality is real in a narrow and defensible sense: a rule containing no reference to gender genuinely contains no reference to gender. What this paper has argued, following and consolidating a substantial and largely independent body of theory and evidence, is that this narrow fact does not license the broader inference organizations routinely draw from it — that a gender-blind rule must therefore produce gender-blind outcomes. It does not, when the rule presupposes a precondition (availability, continuous experience, an unencumbered evaluation period) that is unequally supplied because of who performs the unpaid reproductive labor that makes that precondition possible, and when the organization's own accounting does not recognize that labor as having occurred at all. It does not, independently, when the evaluators applying the rule carry status beliefs about who typically satisfies it, beliefs that operate on the rule's application rather than its content. The two mechanisms are well documented separately and shown, in cases like the motherhood penalty, to compound. The employment-discrimination law's own disparate-impact doctrine reached the same structural conclusion, in a different domain, decades before most of this organizational literature existed. Taken together, this body of work supports treating formal neutrality not as evidence that an organizational rule is fair, but as the specific condition under which unfairness becomes hardest to see from inside the system that produces it.
