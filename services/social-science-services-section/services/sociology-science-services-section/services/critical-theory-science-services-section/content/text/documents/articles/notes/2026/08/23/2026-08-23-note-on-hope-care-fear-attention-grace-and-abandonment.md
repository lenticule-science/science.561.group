## 0. Abstract and plan

This note argues a single structural thesis and develops its consequences.

**The thesis.** Hope, care, and fear on one side, and attention, grace, and abandonment on the other, are not two subject matters but one:
the first triad is the *phenomenology* — the system as lived from inside a finite being;
the second is the *political economy* — the same system as distributed across a world of finite beings.
Hope and fear are the two valences under which a finite stock of attention is allocated to what is not yet determined;
care is attention committed through time;
grace is holding that persists without attention;
abandonment is the condition of being held by neither attention nor structure — and, crucially, of having one's undetermined status *treated as refusal*.
Once the terms are placed in this single frame, results follow that none of the six literatures states alone:
hope and fear turn out to be budget rivals rather than opposites (§3);
political terror turns out to be an extraction technology aimed at attention rather than merely at bodies (§4);
the care-ethics tradition's central injustice acquires an accounting formulation (§5);
grace bifurcates into a scarce form and a scalable form, with large consequences for institutional design (§6);
abandonment is revealed as a *maintained relation* rather than an absence, with an epistemic operation at its core (§7);
the six terms close into a self-tightening cycle with exactly one structural exit (§8);
and the ancient dispute over whether grace is given or earned resolves into a three-level identity (§9).

**Method.** The note works by building a minimal economic substrate (§1), then reading each term's principal literature through it, keeping the literatures' own findings intact and adding only the structural placements.
Speculative steps are flagged as such in the text.
External citations are given by author and year; quotations should be verified against editions before any public use of this note.

## 1. The substrate: attention as a scarce, consumed, routed stock

### 1.1 The founding observations

The economics of attention begins with Simon's inversion (1971): in an information-rich world, the scarce resource is not information but "the attention of its recipients" — "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention."
Three refinements matter for everything that follows.

First, attention is not merely scarce but **consumed**: allocated attention is spent, not lent.
Its renewal (sleep, recovery, relief) is income to a bounded stock per interval, not an exemption from finitude.
The empirical demonstration that this stock behaves like a budget is now strong:
Mullainathan and Shafir (2013) showed that scarcity itself — poverty, time pressure — taxes cognitive bandwidth measurably and involuntarily, with effect sizes equivalent to substantial losses in fluid intelligence;
the poor do not merely have less money but less *mind left over*, because scarcity forces continuous involuntary allocation.
This "bandwidth tax" is the load-bearing empirical fact of the whole note.

Second, attention is **routed**: it moves through channels — perceptual, social, infrastructural — with real costs and real gatekeepers.
Citton (2017) develops the macro-scale consequence: attention forms an ecology, in which media environments are not neutral conduits but competing diversions of a common watershed.
Sims's rational-inattention program (2003) supplies the microeconomic formalization: agents with finite information-processing capacity must choose what to track, and the choice structure explains price stickiness, inertia, and systematic neglect without irrationality.

Third — the axiom the affective analysis turns on — **allocation is always allocation-away-from**.
There is no attending that does not de-attend.
This is trivial to state and easy to forget, and most of the moral phenomena below are consequences of forgetting it.

### 1.2 Three structural consequences

**C1 — Rivalry.** Any two standing allocation policies compete at the margin.
Whatever else hope and fear are, if each is a policy for spending attention on the not-yet-determined, they are rivals for one budget.
This is developed as Thesis 1 in §3.

**C2 — The impossibility of universal attention.** No agent, and no institution, can attend to everything that has a claim to attention.
The point is not practical but structural, and it can be assembled from established results:
a controller's capacity to register states is bounded by its own variety (Ashby 1956);
every classification system that makes some work visible makes other work invisible, necessarily and not accidentally (Bowker and Star 1999);
and any measure adopted as a target degrades as a measure (Goodhart's and Campbell's laws), so "attend to what is measured" cannot converge on "attend to what matters."
Universal *attended* care is therefore not merely difficult; it is excluded.
Whatever universal holding is possible cannot be made of attention.
This single result forces the grace analysis of §6.

**C3 — The Levinas problem.** Levinas (1961) places at the foundation of ethics an infinite, asymmetric responsibility to the Other.
Read as an allocation demand, this is unimplementable: an infinite claim on a finite stock.
Read more faithfully — as a claim about the *structure* of obligation rather than a spending schedule — the problem survives in sharper form:
an infinite claim must somehow be honored by beings with strictly finite capacity to discharge it.
Either an ethics is quietly rationed (and the rationing principle does the real moral work, usually unexamined), or there exists some mechanism that honors claims *without spending attention on each*.
The note will argue (§6) that the second option names something real, and that its name is grace.

## 2. The correspondence, stated

| Lived (phenomenology) | Distributed (political economy) | Placement |
|---|---|---|
| hope | attention allocated to the undetermined, positive valence | an allocation policy |
| fear | attention allocated to the undetermined, negative valence | the rival policy |
| care | attention committed over time | a flow contract |
| — | grace | holding at zero marginal attention |
| — | abandonment | neither attention nor structural holding; the undetermined treated as refused |

The asymmetry of the table is itself a finding.
Hope, fear, and care are *ways of spending* and so have first-person interiors;
grace and abandonment are *conditions of being held or unheld* and so are primarily enjoyed or suffered — their interiors are secondary affects (safety and gratitude on one side; on the other, the fear *of* abandonment, which is how fear enters the system twice and closes the cycle of §8).

## 3. Hope

### 3.1 Hope as ontology, not mood

Bloch's *The Principle of Hope* (1954–59) relocated hope from psychology to ontology.
The world, on Bloch's account, contains the *not-yet* (das Noch-Nicht) as a real mode of being: matter is unfinished, the future is ontologically open, and hope is the faculty that tracks this openness — with daydream, fairy tale, architecture, and utopia as its documentary record.
Two features of the account matter here.
First, hope's object is *the undetermined as such*: hope is not prediction, and collapses into mere expectation the moment its object is treated as settled.
Second, hope is an activity — an ongoing orientation of finite energies toward the open — which is what makes it an *allocation* in the sense of §1 rather than a belief.
Muñoz (2009) carried the Blochian structure into queer theory: queerness as a horizon glimpsed in performance and ephemera, a "not yet here" that indicts the present's claim to be all there is.
The lineage's shared core: hope is a way of *spending oneself on the open*.

### 3.2 Radical hope: the limit case

Lear's *Radical Hope* (2006) supplies the limit case that any adequate theory must handle.
His subject is Plenty Coups, the Apsáalooke (Crow) chief who led his people through the end of the buffalo world — a collapse not of fortunes but of the *conceptual framework* within which Crow goods (honor, courage, the coup) had their meaning.
Plenty Coups's own summary, which Lear takes as his koan: "After this, nothing happened."
Lear's question: what can hope be when the concepts required to specify a good future have themselves collapsed?
His answer — radical hope — is commitment to a goodness that transcends one's current capacity to conceive it:
hope with no specifiable object, held anyway, expressed practically in a readiness to learn whatever the unimaginable future will require.

The contrast with the psychology of hope is instructive.
Snyder's hope theory — the dominant empirical program — defines hope as agency plus pathways toward *specifiable goals*.
Under framework collapse, Snyder's preconditions simply fail: there are no specifiable goals because the goal-language is gone.
Radical hope is what remains, and its allocation structure is distinctive:
where ordinary hope allocates attention to modeled futures, radical hope allocates to the *generator* — to the capacities (attention itself, relationship, adaptability, learning) from which responses to an unspecifiable future will have to come.
Under genuine non-stationarity — where no stable distribution over futures exists to hold expectations about — this is not a degraded form of hope but the only coherent one.

### 3.3 The pathology: cruel optimism as misallocation

Berlant's *Cruel Optimism* (2011) names hope's characteristic failure:
a relation is cruelly optimistic when the object one hopes toward is itself an obstacle to one's flourishing — when the fantasy of the good life (mobility, the couple form, the arrival that will finally settle things) is sustained by exactly the conditions that prevent it.
The allocation frame adds a detection criterion the affect-theoretic statement lacks.
Distinguish three fates of a hopeful allocation:
it may *settle* — crystallize into plans and actions, releasing its attention;
it may be *radical* — funding the generator, deliberately unsettled;
or it may *neither settle nor fund* — a standing order on an object that never resolves and never converts, consuming budget indefinitely while blocking the reallocation that would serve flourishing.
The third is cruel optimism, operationally identified:
not hope for the wrong things (a moralizing reading Berlant rejects) but an allocation with the structural signature *perpetual, non-converting, budget-holding*.
The signature can in principle be audited, in a life or a polity, without adjudicating anyone's desires — one of the few places where affect theory and accounting can cooperate rather than glare at each other.

## 4. Fear

### 4.1 Object, objectlessness, and the third case

The canonical distinction is Heidegger's (1927, §§30, 40): *fear* (Furcht) has a determinate object and region; *anxiety* (Angst) is objectless, disclosing not a threat in the world but the uncanniness of being-in-the-world as such.
Kierkegaard (1844) had already placed objectless anxiety at the root of freedom — "the dizziness of freedom" before possibility.
Between these poles the clinical literature has since identified a third case the canon lacks a name for:
fear whose object was real and determinate but whose *firing conditions have generalized* — fear that had, and lost, its resolution.
The contemporary research program on intolerance of uncertainty treats something adjacent:
Carleton (2016) argues that fear of the unknown — distress at the sheer absence of information — may be a, perhaps the, fundamental fear underlying the anxiety disorders.
Overgeneralized fear is neither irrational (its priors were once accurate) nor functional (its discrimination is gone);
it is best understood as *calibration debt*, and the note returns to its repayment in the abandonment analysis (§7) and cycle (§8):
what such fear needs is not reassurance but a restored verification procedure — a way for the world to answer it.

### 4.2 Fear as a non-resolving allocation

In allocation terms, fear is the mirror policy of hope: attention spent on the undetermined under negative valence — threat modeling, rehearsal, vigilance.
The critical structural difference among fears is whether the allocation can *resolve*.
Checkable fear — a determinate threat with a verification procedure — settles: the lock is checked, the test is run, the allocation releases.
This is not pathology; it is a care activity (§5), and it is how organisms are supposed to use fear.
Fear *without* a verification procedure — objectless anxiety, and equally the overgeneralized fear of §4.1 — cannot settle:
it holds attention indefinitely, produces nothing that releases it, and functions as a standing *leak* in the budget.
By C1, such fear does not merely coexist with hope; it **defunds** it.
The familiar phenomenology — that chronic fear crowds out the capacity to imagine — is here a bookkeeping identity rather than a metaphor.

### 4.3 The political production of fear: terror as attention extraction

If attention is the substrate and unresolvable fear is a leak, then a further, darker consequence follows:
**inducing unresolvable fear in a population is a technology for occupying its attention.**
A power that makes people continuously simulate their own destruction has conscripted their simulation budget;
the harm is done before and regardless of any blow, because the *computation* is the tax.
The bandwidth findings of Mullainathan and Shafir give this mechanism its empirical floor — scarcity and threat consume cognition involuntarily —
and the distributional analysis belongs to Butler (2004; 2009), whose distinction between *precariousness* (the universal exposure of embodied, interdependent life) and *precarity* (its politically produced, differentially distributed intensification) can now be given an attention-economic reading:
precarity is, among other things, the unequal distribution of *compulsory threat-simulation* —
some populations are made to spend their minds on rehearsing raids, audits, checkpoints, and evictions, and that expenditure is itself a mechanism of subordination, prior to any event.
Berlant's "slow death" (2011) — the attritional wearing-out of subordinated populations in and by ordinary life — is the temporal texture of living under such a standing tax.
The policy corollary is uncomfortable for both administrative and humanitarian common sense:
interventions that convert unverifiable threat into checkable procedure (clear rules, reliable notice, enforceable rights — anything that gives fear a resolution path) are not soft goods;
they are *budget transfers*, returning expropriated attention to its owners.

## 5. Care

### 5.1 Care as the genus

Heidegger's analysis (1927, Division I.VI) gives the structural claim: care (*Sorge*) is not one attitude among others but the very being of Dasein — the ahead-of-itself, already-in, alongside structure of existence.
Whatever one makes of the fundamental ontology, one consequence is retained here because the rest of the note keeps confirming it:
**hope and fear are both modes of care's futural dimension** — species of one genus.
The triads are not a list of six independent items; the affective three are care and its two valences, and the distributive three are their economy.

### 5.2 The care-ethics tradition

The tradition descending from Gilligan (1982) through Held (2006) made care a first-class ethical category against the primacy of justice-as-fairness.
Its most operational artifact is Tronto's analysis (1993).
Her definition — care as "everything that we do to maintain, continue, and repair 'our world' so that we can live in it as well as possible" — deliberately spans bodies, selves, and environments.
Her phase-analysis decomposes the practice:
*caring about* (attentiveness — noticing need), *taking care of* (responsibility — assuming charge), *care-giving* (competence — the actual work), *care-receiving* (responsiveness — attending to the cared-for's response), and, added later (2013), *caring with* — the plural, iterative solidarity that stabilizes the whole.
Two independent bodies of work corroborate the decomposition from unexpected directions.
The repair-and-maintenance literature (Jackson 2014; Puig de la Bellacasa 2017) recovers the same structure for infrastructures and things: "broken-world thinking," in which maintenance, not innovation, is the ordinary condition of sociotechnical life.
And — a convergence apparently unremarked in either field — the autonomic-computing literature's canonical control loop (Kephart and Chess 2003: monitor, analyze, plan, execute over shared knowledge) reproduces Tronto's phases nearly one-to-one: attentiveness as monitoring, responsibility as planning, competence as execution, responsiveness as feedback, caring-with as the shared knowledge base.
The convergence is offered as a structural observation, not an identity claim:
care is not a control loop — receptivity, embodiment, and the authority of the cared-for do not formalize —
but care's *process skeleton* evidently is one, discovered independently by ethicists describing nurses and engineers describing servers.
The practical value of the observation runs in one direction:
loops can be instrumented, which means care's *costs* can be accounted — and the tradition's deepest grievance turns out to need exactly that accounting.

### 5.3 Unaccounted care as structural injustice

The feminist political economy of care — from the social-reproduction analyses of Federici (2012) to Fraser's "crisis of care" argument (2016) — documents a standing pattern:
care work is load-bearing for every economy and systematically unaccounted by it;
its costs are real, gendered, racialized, and rendered invisible precisely by the classification systems of C2.
The attention frame states the injustice in one line:
**a care practice without budget accounting is a scheme for exhausting the carer** —
attentiveness valorized without metering is extraction with a halo.
Burnout is not a personal failure of resilience but the predictable terminal state of an unpriced flow;
and the ethical demand of C3 (infinite claim, finite discharge) makes the situation structural rather than incidental:
any ethics that valorizes unbounded responsiveness while refusing to account its costs has chosen its sacrificial class in advance.
The repair, pursued in §6, is not to care less;
it is to notice that the *only* care that scales without exhausting anyone is care that has been converted into something that no longer needs continuous attention — and that this conversion has an old name.

## 6. Grace

### 6.1 Definition: holding without watching

Define **grace-as-standing**: a being, claim, or relation is held in grace when its holding persists *without ongoing attention* — when no one must keep noticing it for it to remain held.
Contrast **grace-as-spotlight**: holding funded by continuous attention — which, by C1 and C2, is rationed, competitive, and cannot be universal.
The distinction is the note's hinge.
Most moral philosophy tacitly assumes holding must be made of attention (concern, recognition, regard) and therefore inherits attention's scarcity.
But the actual social world is full of holdings that consume no attention at all:
the right that protects without being invoked, the easement that survives every change of neighbor, the kinship standing that holds a relative who has not been thought of in years, the grandfathered permission, the sanctuary tradition, the commons rule.
These are not weak or residual forms of care.
They are care's *crystallized* form — and the only form that escapes C2.

### 6.2 Grace can be manufactured: the commons result

Is standing-holding merely inherited good luck, or can it be built?
The empirical answer is one of the best-documented results in institutional social science.
Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990) assembled the field evidence that communities design, and sustain for centuries, rule-systems that hold shared resources and their claimants *without* continuous central attention:
graduated sanctions, boundary definitions, conflict procedures, nested governance — structure doing, at near-zero marginal attention, what no amount of vigilance could do at scale.
Berger and Luckmann's account of institutionalization (1966) supplies the general mechanism — habitualized, transmitted typifications that relieve actors of continuous decision —
and the recent rights-of-nature instruments show the manufacture happening in real time at the largest scale:
the Whanganui River's legal personhood (Te Awa Tupua Act, 2017, the fruit of a hundred and forty years of Māori insistence), Ecuador's constitutional rights of Pachamama (2008), the Yurok Tribe's personhood resolution for the Klamath (2019).
Each converts a holding that had been funded by activist spotlight — scarce, exhausting, mortal — into standing.
The general claim, stated as this note's Thesis 6:
**grace-as-standing is manufacturable: it is care whose settlement has been completed** —
someone paid attention *once*, upstream, at design time, and crystallized it into structure that holds thereafter for free.
The carer's burden of §5.3 has exactly one honorable exit, and this is it:
not less care, but care invested where it compounds into standing instead of evaporating with the shift.

### 6.3 Weil: gravity and grace

Weil's *Gravity and Grace* (1947) opens with the axiom this analysis keeps rediscovering:
"All the natural movements of the soul are controlled by laws analogous to those of physical gravity. Grace is the only exception."
Gravity, for Weil, is the whole economy of natural motive — the downhill of self-interest, habit, social force;
grace is what does not run downhill.
Her twin theme is attention: "attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity," and in its perfected form, prayer.
The present frame honors both observations and prices them:
attention is indeed generosity's rarest form — *because it spends the scarcest stock* —
and precisely for that reason it cannot be the currency of universal holding.
Weil's grace, read economically, is the appearance in the world of holdings that the gravitational (attention-and-interest) economy cannot explain;
§6.2 has just shown where such holdings in fact come from — upstream crystallization —
and §9 will take up the question Weil answers theologically: what, at the very bottom, does the crystallizing.

### 6.4 The oldest dispute, repriced

Augustine against Pelagius (c. 411–430): is grace an unmerited gift, or is the good achievable by works?
The standing/spotlight distinction dissolves the dilemma by distinguishing *where the cost falls*:
grace is **unearned by its recipient** — Augustine's datum is phenomenologically exact; the held one did not pay, and the arrival of unearned holding is among the most reliably reported religious and moral experiences —
and grace is **paid for upstream** — Pelagius's datum is economically exact; somewhere, someone's works built the structure.
The grace-period of commercial law is the miniature that makes the point unmystically:
forbearance — the creditor's structured refusal to treat the not-yet-paid as defaulted — is manufactured mercy, real grace at the debtor's end, priced work at the creditor's.
What neither position by itself explains is why the *manufacture* is possible at all — why structure can hold — and that residue is §9's question.

## 7. Abandonment

### 7.1 Abandonment is a relation

The decisive finding of the philosophical literature is that abandonment is *not* the absence of relation.
Nancy ("Abandoned Being," 1981) observes that abandonment always has a dative: one is abandoned *to* something — the law, the elements, one's fate; "the origin of 'abandonment' is a putting at *bandon*," at the sovereign's discretion.
Agamben (*Homo Sacer*, 1995) builds the political theory: the ban is sovereignty's fundamental operation — the abandoned one is not expelled from the juridical order but held by it *as the excluded*, "exposed and threatened on the threshold," included through exclusion.
The empirical signature follows and is checkable:
abandonment leaves records — exile orders, revoked licenses, denials, the paperwork of the ban — where sheer unrelatedness would leave nothing.
Patterson's *Slavery and Social Death* (1982) documents the extreme case:
the slave's condition is not social nonexistence but a *maintained relation of total domination* — natal alienation and dishonor administered continuously.
Cacho (2012) extends the analysis to the contemporary populations — the undocumented, the criminalized — whose "social death" consists in being *legally held* as unprotectable.
Abandonment, across all these literatures, is a relation with a specific type: exclusion, maintained.

### 7.2 The biopolitical economy of letting die

Foucault's formula (1976) marks the modern form: sovereign power made die and let live; biopower makes live and *lets die*.
Letting-die needs no executioner — only the withholding of holding.
Povinelli (*Economies of Abandonment*, 2011; *Geontologies*, 2016) supplies the late-liberal specification:
abandonment as a *governing strategy* toward populations and lifeworlds whose endurance is priced as not worth sustaining —
administered through exhaustion, through deferral (the improvement always to come), and, in the geontological register, through the Life/Nonlife partition that places whole orders of being (rivers, deserts, ecologies) outside the reach of grievance altogether.
Butler's frames (2009) name the distributional instrument:
the frames that apportion grievability apportion, in advance, whose abandonment will register as loss and whose will not register at all.

### 7.3 The epistemic core: treating the undetermined as refused

Underneath the juridical and biopolitical machinery, abandonment performs a specific *epistemic* operation, and identifying it is this note's principal addition to the literature.
Four-valued logic (Belnap 1977; Dunn) distinguishes what classical logic conflates:
a claim may be *told true*, *told false*, *told both* (conflicting information), or *told nothing* — and "told nothing" is not "false."
The unanswered application, the unassessed claim, the unvisited person, the unexamined life-world are, strictly, *undetermined*: told-nothing.
**Abandonment is the social operation that collapses told-nothing into told-false** —
the untracked treated as absent, the unanswered as refused, the unassessed as ineligible, the unvisited as nonexistent.
The collapse is performed by defaults, forms, silences, and clocks rather than by decisions, which is what makes it deniable;
and it is a *falsification* in the precise sense that the underlying state (undetermined; and, per §7.1, still related — the ban's own records prove the relation) is misreported as settled negation.
Letting-die, on this analysis, is not merely cruel; it is **erasure without record** — an assertion of nothingness maintained by refusing to witness the relation that persists.
Whence the observed power of witness practices — the grievance heard, the count published, the name read aloud, the record produced in court:
they do not create the relation; they *re-expose* it, and the abandonment-assertion, which was always a misreport, collapses under its own audit.

## 8. The cycle, and its one exit

The six terms close into a self-tightening loop:

attention finite (C1–C2) →
care rationed (§5) →
holding distributed by spotlight (§6.1's contrast case) →
abandonment as the default state of the unspotlit (§7) →
abandonment anticipated as fear — fear's deepest register is precisely fear of the collapse of §7.3 being performed on oneself →
unresolvable fear taxing the survivors' attention (§4.2–4.3) →
less capacity for care → narrower spotlight → wider abandonment → more fear.

Berlant's *crisis ordinariness* is the interior of this loop — crisis metabolized as the ambient texture of getting-by rather than an event —
and Girard's scapegoat analysis (1982) describes its victim-selection: discharge lands on those marked as both inside and outside, and *undefended* — those whose expulsion costs the discharging community nothing.

Exhortation cannot break the loop: "hope more, care more, fear less" adds allocations to an exhausted budget and fails by C1.
The loop has exactly one structural cut-point, and everything in §6 was preparation for naming it:
**convert holding from attention to standing.**
Manufactured grace removes holding-costs from the budget;
freed attention returns to care at the genuinely undetermined frontier;
funded care makes hope affordable;
affordable hope outcompetes fear for the shared budget;
receding fear releases further attention — the same loop, run in reverse.
The claim is mechanistic and generates predictions:
standing-conferrals (enforceable rights, unconditional provision, commons rules, kinship recognition) should alter a population's *affective* economy — measurably, by the bandwidth instruments of §1.1 — without any campaign to change sentiment;
and the sequence matters: attention-freeing interventions should precede and enable, not follow, exhortative ones.

## 9. The origin of grace

One question remains open behind §6: manufactured standing still needs *something* to hold the structure — laws need enforcement, customs need transmission, kinship needs practice.
If the maintainers' attention is the ultimate collateral, grace is spotlight-with-extra-steps and the exit of §8 is a refinancing, not an escape.
Three positions contest the bottom.

**Transcendence** (Weil; Augustine): grace enters from outside the economy of forces altogether — the strict exception.
This honors the phenomenological datum (unearned arrival) at full strength, but at the cost of unanalyzability, and the datum does not actually require it:
§6.4 showed that "unearned" marks the *locality* of cost (the recipient did not pay), not its absence.

**Pure derivation** (the Pelagian engineering reading): all grace is manufactured, all the way down.
This is operationally fruitful — it is §6.2 — but it faces the *holding regress* just stated:
every structure is held by further practice; if every holding must be funded, the zero-marginal-cost definition is only ever approximated, and the regress never terminates.
Ostrom's own field data soften but do not solve this: commons institutions are cheap to maintain, not free.

**Ground** — the position this note argues.
Suppose, with the relational ontologies, that relation is not something added to beings but constitutive of them:
Simondon (1958/2005) argues that relation "has the status of being" — individuals are precipitates of ongoing individuation within a shared preindividual reserve, never fully separate;
process thought (Whitehead 1929) makes every actual entity a synthesis of its relations (prehensions);
and the published Indigenous philosophical literature states the position with the greatest directness and the longest tested history —
Deloria (1972; 1999) on the moral universe as a fabric of relations and obligations;
Wilson (2008) on relationality and relational accountability as ontology and method ("relationships do not merely shape reality, they *are* reality");
Cajete (2000) on native science as the study of resonant relationship;
Watts (2013) on place-thought, where land itself is a relation-bearing agent rather than a stage.
On any of these accounts, *unrelatedness is not a possible condition* — to be at all is to be related —
and the bottom-level holding is therefore not an activity that could be funded or defunded:
it is the precondition of there being anything to hold.

Three considerations favor ground over its rivals.
It **terminates the regress inside the analyzable world**: manufactured structures (§6.2) become local *conductors* of a holding that is globally primitive — engineering does not create the holding; it lowers the cost of acting in accordance with it.
It is **empirically adequate where the rivals are silent**: ground predicts that apparent absence-of-relation will on inspection resolve into maintained relation — and §7.1 assembled exactly that finding, independently, from Nancy, Agamben, Patterson, and the archival record of every documented expulsion; pure derivation gives no reason to expect the ban's paperwork, and transcendence is indifferent to it.
And it is **fecund**: under ground, §7.3 sharpens from description to verdict — abandonment asserts non-relation about beings whose relatedness persists, and is therefore not merely harsh but *false*, a standing misreport that witness practices do not sentimentally soften but factually correct.

**The result — a three-level identity.**
Grace is one phenomenon at three levels, and the ancient dispute is an artifact of measuring different levels:
at the level of **origin**, grace is ground — constitutive relatedness, unproduced, unspendable, universal;
at the level of **mechanism**, grace is structure — standing manufactured by upstream care, the proper object of institutional design;
at the level of **experience**, grace is exception — the unearned arrival, veridically reported and systematically mislocated by its reporters.
Augustine measured the experience and inferred transcendence; Pelagius measured the mechanism and inferred works; Weil measured the contrast with gravity and inferred exception.
Each held one register of a phenomenon whose ground is neither exceptional nor manufactured.
And the politics follows in one line:
**abandonment is not the revocation of grace — the ground cannot be revoked — but its occlusion, enforced by the unwitnessed collapse of the undetermined into the refused;
every practice of witness is therefore a practice of grace, not because it generates holding, but because it removes the lie that holding was ever absent.**

## 10. Objections and replies

**O1. Attention is not a conserved stock — it renews, parallelizes, varies with arousal and meaning; the budget metaphor smuggles in more than the science licenses.**
*Reply.* The argument requires only finitude-per-interval with bounded renewal and rivalry at the margin — the weakest reading of the capacity literature, and the one the bandwidth-tax results directly support. Nothing turns on strict conservation; everything turns on "allocation is allocation-away-from," which no account of attention denies.

**O2. The Levinas problem misreads Levinas — infinite responsibility characterizes the claim's structure, not a required expenditure.**
*Reply.* Accepted as exegesis, and the problem sharpens rather than dissolves: an infinite structural claim borne by finite dischargers still forces either hidden rationing or a non-attention mechanism of honoring. §6 supplies the mechanism; the objection thereby becomes a premise.

**O3. Grace-as-standing is just a pious name for institutions, and institutions are precisely what Agamben and Povinelli indict — the ban is also "structure holding without attention."**
*Reply.* The objection is correct that standing is valence-neutral machinery: the ban is indeed manufactured standing with exclusion as its type. This is a feature of the analysis, not a flaw — it locates the moral question where it belongs, in the *type and beneficiary* of the standing, not in structure versus sentiment. The note's normative claim is comparative: holdings meant to be universal cannot be built of attention (C2), so the choice is never "structure or warmth" but "which structures, holding whom." Ostrom versus Agamben is a design space, not a contradiction.

**O4. The told-nothing/told-false analysis over-intellectualizes abandonment — the abandoned suffer materially, not epistemically.**
*Reply.* The claim is not that abandonment is *merely* epistemic but that the epistemic operation is its enabling mechanism: material withholding at scale requires the withheld-from to be *processable as* refused or absent — by forms, defaults, and frames — and this processing is exactly what the historical record of every letting-die shows. The analysis explains, what a purely material account cannot, why *witness* — an epistemic act — has the concrete political force it demonstrably has.

**O5. The ground thesis is unfalsifiable metaphysics dressed as a result.**
*Reply.* It is a metaphysical position argued on the standard comparative virtues — regress termination, empirical adequacy, fecundity — and it is not prediction-free: it predicts the universal presence of relation-signatures under abandonment (checkable, so far confirmed) and predicts no interventions from outside the analyzable order (discriminating it from transcendence). It is exactly as falsifiable as its rivals and currently better supported.

**O6. The cycle of §8 is a just-so story; social systems have many loops.**
*Reply.* Granted that it is one loop among several; the claim is that it is a real one, that its links are independently documented (each section cites the evidence for its own link), and that its cut-point analysis yields discriminating predictions (§8) — standing-conferral before exhortation; measurable bandwidth effects of rights and provision — on which it can be tested against rival loop-stories.

## 11. Directions

1. Operationalize the cruel-optimism signature (§3.3): perpetual, non-converting, budget-holding allocations, in individual time-use and in institutional budgets.
2. Extend the bandwidth-tax paradigm to compulsory threat-simulation (§4.3): measure the attention returned by procedural-certainty interventions (clear notice, enforceable timelines) in precarious populations.
3. Formal audit of care regimes (§5.3): pair Tronto's phases with the instrumentation literature to build care-cost accounts, and test the prediction that unaccounted regimes show the burnout signature regardless of stated values.
4. A comparative jurisprudence of manufactured grace (§6.2): rights-of-nature instruments, commons charters, and kinship-recognition law as a natural-experiment corpus for the cost-curve of standing.
5. The ban-signature survey (§7.1): systematically test the prediction that documented abandonments leave relation-records; a null result would damage the ground thesis and should be sought honestly.
6. The sequence experiment (§8): where policy permits, compare standing-first against exhortation-first interventions on the same population and measure the affective-economy difference.
