Scarcity priming is an experimental paradigm in which participants are cued — typically by an imagined scenario, a survey framing, or a task involving hypothetical shortfall — to think about lacking money, time, or another resource, after which their performance on an unrelated cognitive or behavioral measure is compared to a non-primed control group. The paradigm was introduced by Shah, Mullainathan & Shafir (2012) to argue that scarcity produces attentional "tunneling" or "focusing" on the scarce resource, with knock-on effects including neglect of other concerns, over-borrowing, and cognitive fatigue. It became one of the two empirical pillars (alongside the harvest-cycle field paradigm) supporting the broader bandwidth-tax hypothesis.

The paradigm's evidentiary status is the clearest case of fragility in the whole scarcity-cognition literature, and the audit result is load-bearing for how the concept should now be used. O'Donnell, Shah, Budiman, Goldstein and colleagues (PNAS, 2021) conducted preregistered, high-powered replications of 20 studies sampled from the paradigm's citation network, covering domains from pain sensitivity to materialism to resource allocation. The result: replication effect sizes were smaller than the originals in 80% of the 20 studies, directionally opposite in 30%, and only 4 of the 20 reproduced a statistically significant effect in the original direction. The one effect that replicated most cleanly — recalling scarcity elevates self-reported back pain — has no evident mechanistic connection to attention or cognition at all, which undercuts rather than supports the paradigm's interpretive framework. The original authors published a rebuttal (Shah, Mullainathan & Shafir, PNAS, 2022) disputing the sampling and characterization of several of the 20 studies; the auditors defended their methodology in reply (PNAS, 2023). The dispute remains open, but it concerns which studies should have been sampled, not whether the paradigm's original effect sizes have been restored.

Scarcity priming should therefore be treated, going forward, as a historically important but empirically weak paradigm: valuable for having generated a testable hypothesis and a productive dispute about replication methodology in psychology and economics, but not citable as demonstrating that momentary scarcity cues reliably move cognition or behavior. Any argument in the estate's notes that depends on an "imagining lack degrades thinking" mechanism should be re-grounded in the chronic-stress and mental-health literatures (see bandwidth-tax) rather than in scarcity-priming results specifically.
