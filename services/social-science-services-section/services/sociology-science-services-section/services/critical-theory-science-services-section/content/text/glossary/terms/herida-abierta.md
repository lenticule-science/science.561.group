Gloria Anzaldúa opens *Borderlands/La Frontera: The New Mestiza* (1987) with the line the whole book, and much subsequent borderlands writing, keeps returning to: "The U.S.-Mexican border es una herida abierta where the Third World grates against the first and bleeds. And before a scab forms it hemorrhages again, the lifeblood of two worlds merging to form a third country — a border culture." The figure does two things at once. It names the border as an injury rather than a line — something inflicted on a body, ongoing, unhealed — and it names that injury as productive: the wound is also where a third thing, a border culture, a mestiza consciousness, is being made. Anzaldúa does not resolve the wound into either pure damage or pure becoming. It is both, at once, permanently.

The embodiment claim is the concept's load-bearing element. "Herida abierta" is not a metaphor Anzaldúa deploys and then sets aside for cartographic language; it is a claim that the border is first and most truly a bodily fact, felt in the flesh of the people who live astride it — "I am a border woman," she writes, situating her own body as the terrain the wound crosses. This inverts the settler-cartographic frame this repository tracks elsewhere: where a survey line is an abstraction imposed on land it does not touch, a wound is felt precisely because it does touch — skin, kinship, watershed, grove. The border, on this reading, is not adjacent to bodies; it runs through them.

The figure is not archival. On August 12, 2026, CrimethInc. published a dispatch titled "The Border is an Open Wound: A Tree Sit in Lochiel, Arizona Halts Border Wall Construction," opening with Anzaldúa's line verbatim and then extending it: the wall itself becomes "the knife of Gloria Anzaldúa's metaphor," cutting through the valley's "living tissue" and leaving "a ragged wound across the precious and diverse ecosystems." A reportback from a Phoenix solidarity action folded into the same piece opens, in the dispatch's paraphrase, "The border is like an open wound," explicitly recalling Anzaldúa; the CrimethInc author's gloss — "There is no truer statement. Everywhere that the border is developed, maintained, and expanded, there is blood" — treats the 1987 figure as live description of 2026 events, not homage. The [Lochiel research note](../../../../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-lochiel-history.md) supplies the events the dispatch is describing: three of the four Fremont cottonwoods at Lochiel, estimated 200–250 years old, uprooted by Fisher Sand & Gravel crews on July 27–28, 2026, under a waiver that set aside 34 federal laws; a lone tree-sitter occupying the surviving "Grandmother" tree since July 29; a binational camp still holding as of the most recent verified reporting.

At Lochiel the figure fits with unusual precision, because the site supplies both halves of Anzaldúa's claim in the same acre. The grove germinated, if the age estimates hold, in the 1820s — a flood-recession archive predating the Gadsden line by roughly thirty years — and it sits on a river, the Santa Cruz, that crosses the international boundary twice, indifferent to the survey. The 1855 Emory survey then placed Monument 127 near this spot 4.41 miles off its own treaty coordinates, an error the line has carried, uncorrected, into the present. Anzaldúa's wound "before a scab forms it hemorrhages again" describes this history almost literally: the cottonwoods die and the grove does not become quiet event but recurring injury — 2020 blasting at Monument Hill, 2020 arrests at Quitobaquito, 2026 felling at Lochiel — because the line that keeps reopening it is itself an ongoing act, not a settled fact, requiring fresh legal waivers and armed contractors each time it needs re-cutting.

That is the concept's connection to this knowledge base's larger argument. Settlerism-as-filing-system depends on treating land, water, and people as separable categories — infrastructure here, environment there, security elsewhere — so that a single waiver notice can set aside dozens of statutes at once without appearing to touch a single, continuous thing. "Herida abierta" refuses the filing. It insists the wall, the felled grove, the river's two crossings, and the bodies that grate against the line are one wound, not several files, and that the exclusion-powers built into property-as-disposition — the state's standing capacity to decide who and what may cross, to declare a monument line where a treaty coordinate was missed, to hold ~152,899 acres illegally and call it conservation — operate by insisting on separations the wound does not honor. A wound does not distinguish infrastructure from ecology from kinship; it just keeps bleeding across all three until something stops the cutting.
