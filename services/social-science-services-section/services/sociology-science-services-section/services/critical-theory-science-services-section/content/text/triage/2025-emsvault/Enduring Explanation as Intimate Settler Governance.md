### **I. The Situation: A Relational Breakdown and Its Patterns**
With a specific household, a persistent relational rupture emerges in moments where harm is named—often by the colonized partner—whether that harm is material, emotional, or existential in nature. The settler partner, when confronted with this naming, typically responds not by tending to the rupture itself, but by offering explanations: “why” they acted as they did, what they were feeling, what they intended. These explanations function as both defense and justification, offered in place of acknowledgment or reparation.

What becomes visible over time is that these explanatory gestures are not merely individual habits or communication misfires. They are **repetitions of a relational formation grounded in settler colonial governance**—a governance that operates not through overt force, but through **endurance**, the reproduction of patterns that stabilize the dominant’s emotional and epistemic coherence, while requiring the colonized to continually navigate, accommodate, and explain themselves to remain legible.

The settler partner’s “why” is presented as a stabilizing gesture: as if articulating intention or emotional state could resolve the rupture. But for the colonized partner, this explanation displaces the original concern. The harm named is not tended to—it is overwritten by a framework in which **the settler’s interiority is made the central site of truth**.

This structure requires that the colonized partner continue explaining: not simply to be understood, but **to survive the logic of the relation**, to maintain partial coherence in a space where their own frameworks of feeling, interpretation, and response are prefigured as harms.

### **II. Emotion as Structure, Not Just Feeling**

**(Sara Ahmed, Lauren Berlant)**

In settler liberal modernity, emotion is often imagined as interior, immediate, and individually owned—a spontaneous state arising _within_ a person and expressing their authentic truth. But affect theorists like **Sara Ahmed** and **Lauren Berlant** challenge this view. They show how emotions are not merely private experiences, but **social structures that organize relational space**. Emotions bind bodies to narratives, values, and others, shaping what becomes possible within any given encounter.

In this sense, emotion is not just a state of being—it is a **mode of world-making**. Ahmed writes that emotions “stick” to certain bodies, histories, and identities, creating habitual orientations. For example, the figure of the white woman in settler societies becomes associated with **grievability, innocence, and the right to comfort**. Her feelings are treated as morally legible and politically urgent. Meanwhile, racialized, Indigenous, or otherwise non-dominant subjects are positioned as either emotionally excessive or emotionally illegible—too angry, too withdrawn, too irrational, too cold.

In the context of this household, these broader emotional logics are recapitulated. The settler partner’s emotional states—particularly confusion, hurt, guilt, or overwhelm in response to being told they caused harm—are not experienced as one possible perspective among many. Rather, they appear to the partner as **real, primary, and authoritative**. These emotions arrive with **affective legitimacy already attached**: they are felt as _true_, not just personally but socially.

The problem is not that they feel emotion—it’s that the emotion is **taken as a grounding structure**, one that defines the field of available action and relation. The partner may say, for example, “I can’t talk about this right now,” not as a strategic pause for reflection, but as a closing off of the relational field altogether. Or they may feel that their hurt feelings _must_ be addressed first, because they register their pain as more pressing than the harm that prompted the conversation. Their emotional state becomes a **sovereign terrain**, a border the relation is not allowed to cross unless the terrain permits entry.

What Ahmed calls _affective economies_—the ways emotions circulate and consolidate around particular figures—come fully into view here. The settler partner’s emotional coherence is the relational default. It has been supported by decades of subtle reinforcements in their person: being asked what’s wrong, being given the benefit of the doubt, being allowed to narrate their feelings as self-justifying. The colonized partner, by contrast, enters the relation from a very different affective position—often one where emotion has been punished, pathologized, or made unintelligible. Their hurt is routinely deferred, reframed, or explained away by dominant narratives.

Thus, when the colonized partner raises a harm, they do so within **an asymmetrical emotional field**. The emotional reality of the settler partner already shapes the terrain, determining what kind of emotional expression is acceptable, whose discomfort can be voiced, and what kind of speech acts will be heard as legitimate. This asymmetry is not just a power imbalance—it is a **structure of feeling** (to use Raymond Williams’ term, extended by Berlant), a patterned organization of affect that _governs relational possibility before anything is said_.

This structure requires the colonized partner to continually adapt, interpret, and regulate their own emotions in order to remain legible within a field already bent toward settler emotional norms. This adaptation is the emotional form of what **Elizabeth Povinelli** names _governance by endurance_—a structure in which the dominated must perpetually manage the slow, repeated violence of being made marginal, unintelligible, or excessive within the affective order of the dominant.

In this way, emotion is not just what each partner feels. It is **how the relation is scaffolded**. It is how harm is filtered, how care is allocated, and how authority is distributed. The settler partner’s emotions are not the origin of harm, but they become the **terrain through which harm is deflected, managed, or denied**—not through malice, but through a deeply conditioned inability to imagine their emotional state as _one_ reality among others, rather than _the_ reality through which relation must pass.

### **III. The Colonizing Function of Explanation**

**(Povinelli, Fanon, Butler)**

When the settler partner, in response to being told they have caused harm, begins to explain their behavior—why they acted a certain way, what they were feeling, how they didn’t mean harm—this is not a neutral act of communication. Nor is it merely a coping mechanism or a rational appeal. It is a _performative gesture_ that recasts the terms of the interaction. In explaining, the partner does not respond to the rupture—they **reframe** it. They shift the ontological ground of the interaction away from the harm’s effects and onto the interiority of their own intent and experience.

This is a classically **colonial move**, though often misrecognized as innocuous. Drawing from **Judith Butler’s** theory of _performativity_, we see that the act of speaking—particularly within power-laden contexts—_does_ something. When the settler partner explains themselves, they are not simply stating a truth. They are _doing_ a relational act: attempting to stabilize their moral position, reassert their intelligibility, and reorganize the scene of accountability. The speech act of explanation functions to **produce legitimacy**, not just reveal it.

**Elizabeth Povinelli** helps us understand how this functions as **a form of governance**. In _The Empire of Love_ and _Economies of Abandonment_, she identifies the mechanisms through which liberal settler societies manage the otherwise—those lives and epistemologies that don’t conform to settler temporalities, logics, and forms of care. Explanation, in this sense, becomes a **technology of governance**: it enforces coherence, regulates discomfort, and reinscribes settler normativity by insisting that all disorder can be managed through reasoned narrative.

The settler’s explanatory behavior is thus not just about soothing themselves—it’s about reasserting control over the **field of meaning**. When the colonized partner raises harm, they invoke an alternate logic of relation: one in which effect matters more than intent, in which harm disrupts coherence, and in which discomfort is not something to be explained away but _tended to_. But this alternate logic cannot stabilize within the field because the settler partner immediately pulls the frame back to their own interiority—what they were trying to do, what they thought was happening, what they felt.

This return to self-narration **prevents the rupture from becoming transformative**. It forecloses relational movement by redirecting the energy of repair back into the settler subject’s own coherence.

And so, **the colonized partner is made to explain again**—not instead of the settler, but _because of_ the settler’s explanation. The settler’s narrative of self must be understood, their innocence assumed until disproven, their discomfort legitimized as an object of care. So the colonized partner is drawn into another round of translation: explaining again why the impact matters, recontextualizing their perspective, softening their clarity to prevent further escalation. The structure **tightens**, requiring the colonized to keep speaking while the settler remains the sovereign narrator of the real.

**Frantz Fanon**, in _Black Skin, White Masks_, described the colonized’s enforced reflex to explain themselves into being—to anticipate misrecognition, to counter pre-imposed frameworks, to work through the dominant's logic just to become conditionally visible. That dynamic remains intact here, even as the settler speaks more. The settler’s explanation doesn’t relieve the colonized of that burden; it **extends it**, requiring them to do even more interpretive, affective, and linguistic labor to navigate the meaning-space the settler has re-secured through their explanatory act.

Explanation, then, is not merely a practice of reflection. In settler-colonial contexts, it is a **form of soft control**, a reiteration of epistemic primacy, a reterritorialization of the relational field around settler affect, settler cognition, settler temporality. It allows the settler partner to _appear responsive_ while actually preventing the relation from undergoing any real reconfiguration.

This is why the repeated offering of “why” feels not only insufficient but actively harmful. It is not dialogue—it is **relational containment disguised as dialogue**.

### **IV. Selfhood as Infrastructure**

**(Psychoanalysis, Anzaldúa, Fanon, decolonial theory)**

To fully grasp the dynamic at play, we must examine how the settler partner’s sense of **self** operates—not as a neutral subjectivity but as **an infrastructural formation**, one historically and affectively composed through colonial modernity. This self is not simply a private interior; it is a _social machine_, engineered to prioritize coherence, mastery, and innocence, even under conditions of relational rupture.

In psychoanalytic terms, we might say the settler self is structured around a need for **ego-integrity**: a coherent, bounded identity that must maintain its self-image as “good,” “reasonable,” or “misunderstood.” This coherence is fragile. When challenged—especially by being told they caused harm—the settler self begins to fray. But rather than metabolize that fraying, it deploys self-narration as a repair mechanism: explaining motives, contextualizing feelings, invoking intention. These gestures shore up the ego. They do not expand the self—they **fortify its perimeter**.

This is the defensive architecture that **Gloria Anzaldúa** described as the _bordered self_: a self that defends against relational and epistemic permeability. When something from outside threatens its stability—say, the reality of someone else’s pain, or a cosmology that doesn’t center intention—the bordered self activates containment. It translates the rupture back into terms it can survive. Explanation becomes the **wall**, not the window.

And this is not only a psychic process—it is a **colonial one**. The settler self was built through centuries of structural insulation from the consequences of harm. Its formation relies on the presumption that it can act upon the world without having to fully _be in relation to_ that world. This presumption becomes embodied. The partner feels their emotions as **totalizing** because they’ve been shaped to experience emotion as ontologically real, as **what is** rather than as one possible affective truth among many.

This is what **Frantz Fanon** identified as a kind of **epistemological closure**: the colonizer does not have to imagine another's interiority, only their own. The colonized must live in the tension of multiple frameworks simultaneously—their own, and the settler’s. The colonizer, by contrast, has the luxury of a singular, self-authorizing world.

This structure is maintained recursively. When harm is named, the settler self feels destabilized, and to restabilize it reaches not for shared meaning-making, but for **narrative insulation**. That insulation is achieved through affectively charged explanation. But to function, it must be accepted—and so the colonized partner is once again called in: to reassure, to understand, to clarify their critique so it doesn’t threaten the fragile infrastructure of settler selfhood.

In this way, the settler self becomes not just an actor in the relation but **the terrain** through which relation must pass. Everything becomes routed through their interpretive and emotional capacities. If those capacities are limited—if they are only able to tolerate truth that confirms their goodness—then the relation itself becomes circumscribed. It cannot transform, only repeat. The colonized partner’s reality is made **contingent** on how much the settler’s self can bear, and how much the colonized is willing to endure in translating themselves into that limited field.

This asymmetry is the expression of **a larger ontological order**—one in which settler coherence is treated as a precondition for legitimacy, while colonized coherence must be continually negotiated, often at the cost of further fragmentation.

To engage with this dynamic requires more than better communication. It requires the **undoing of the settler self as the central infrastructure of relation**.

### **V. Emotional Reflexes and the Limits of Choice**

**(Affect theory, colonial liberalism, relational praxis)**

In dialog, the settler partner may at times feel themselves as pausing and **choosing** how to respond: they pause, reflect, and then offer an explanation. But this appearance of deliberation obscures a more important reality. The field from which they are choosing—what seems sayable, feelable, or doable—is **structured and constrained by their emotional position at the time**, which they experience as both self-evident and unchangeable.

This is not just a lack of skill or maturity. It is an inherited ontology. In **liberal settler frameworks**, emotion is treated as sovereign, interior, and inviolable. A person is not responsible for how they feel; they are responsible only for whether they act on it “inappropriately.” This divorces feeling from its social context and **naturalizes emotional states as real and morally valid** simply because they are felt.

When the settler partner feels confusion, hurt, or shame in response to critique, these feelings are experienced not as relational responses, but as **existential truths**. The intensity of these states—their _viscerality_—is taken as proof that they cannot be set aside or worked through in the moment. As such, the partner often treats their emotion as a kind of boundary condition: “I can’t engage with this until I feel differently,” or more subtly, “I can’t engage with this without first explaining how I feel.”

But this is not neutral. It is **a form of relational withdrawal**, disguised as self-regulation. In practice, it puts the entire relational process **on hold** until the settler’s internal world has been stabilized—often by the very person who named the harm in the first place. The colonized partner is thus again conscripted—not just into the act of explanation, but into the work of waiting, softening, and managing the settler’s emotional terms of engagement.

What appears as a limit (“I can’t feel differently”) is upheld by **a belief system that refuses relational accountability at the level of affect**. The settler partner does not believe that they can change their emotional orientation _because they do not believe they should have to_. Emotion is treated as sacrosanct—outside the sphere of shared governance. But in a relational or decolonial framework, feelings are not just private—they are **relational actions**. To feel shame and turn inward is a choice. To feel hurt and insist on being understood is a choice. These are **affective gestures** with real consequences.

The refusal to see these emotional reflexes as choices allows the settler partner to remain morally centered, even when relationally absent. Their belief in their own emotional truth becomes the alibi for non-reciprocity. And because the emotional state cannot be interrupted—only obeyed—the settler’s internal condition becomes the final arbiter of what the relation can hold. The colonized partner is once again displaced, their truths suspended in a holding pattern until the settler partner “feels ready” to engage.

This dynamic—harm, emotional destabilization, explanation, recentering—relies on **a profound misrecognition of emotion as pre-relational**. But emotions are never pre-relational. They arise from, circulate through, and reshape the field of relation itself. To act as though they do not is to participate in a colonial logic of emotional sovereignty: a logic in which one’s own internal coherence must be preserved at all costs, even if it means violating the relational space that gives rise to it.

To break this pattern would require not the repression of feeling, but **the cultivation of emotional reflexivity**—an understanding that emotion is not only what is felt, but _what is done_; that it is not only personal but _positional_; and that to feel something does not make it good, true, or just. Only then can the settler partner begin to participate in the relation not as the emotional sovereign, but as one actor among others, capable of being shaped by others and responsible for the shaping they do in return.

### **VI. The Outcome: Recursive Harm**

**(Epistemic violence, endurance, relational closure)**

When taken together, the patterns described in the previous sections form a **self-reinforcing structure**: a recursive circuit of harm that operates not through explosive acts of violence, but through the slow, patterned reassertion of settler epistemology at the site of emotional relation. The settler partner’s feelings become the horizon of the encounter; their explanations the medium of legitimacy; their selfhood the infrastructure through which everything must pass. The colonized partner is thus not only disbelieved or dismissed—they are **positioned as out-of-phase** with the emotional logic of the relation.

This produces a unique form of violence—what **Gayatri Spivak** and others have called **epistemic violence**—in which the colonized partner’s frameworks for making sense of harm are made unhearable, not by direct contradiction but by being constantly **reabsorbed into the settler’s system of meaning**. When the colonized says, “This hurt me,” and the response is, “Let me explain why I did that,” the initial wound is not healed. It is **reframed**, and the wound becomes illegible unless it can be restated in the settler’s terms.

This is what **Elizabeth Povinelli** means by _governance by endurance_: the settler does not need to win every argument or deny every harm. The system merely needs to **hold**—to keep functioning in a way that requires the colonized partner to explain themselves, wait, absorb, re-narrate, and persist. Over time, this labor of adaptation becomes the very condition of relation. The colonized is made not just to survive within this structure, but to provide the affective glue that holds it together.

The cycle looks like this:

1. **A harm is named**—an act of relational rupture is brought to light.
    
2. **The settler partner becomes emotionally destabilized**—experiencing discomfort, guilt, or confusion.
    
3. **They offer an explanation**—not to tend to the harm, but to stabilize their self-image.
    
4. **The explanation re-centers the settler's perspective**, making their internal state the organizing truth of the moment.
    
5. **The colonized partner is required to explain again**—to reframe their hurt in terms the settler can understand or accept.
    
6. **The initial harm is never resolved**, but displaced—held in suspension, marked by delay, or diluted into mutual misunderstanding.
    
7. **The relation resumes**, with the settler’s coherence preserved and the colonized’s slowly worn down.
    

The violence here is not simply that harm goes unacknowledged. It is that harm becomes **a site of relational deferral**: the colonized must continue to show up, to perform coherence in the face of incoherence, to explain into a void where explanation does not produce change, only further explanation. Beyond a failure of empathy, it is **a structural foreclosure of relational transformation**.

Nothing here is overtly catastrophic. The settler does not need to shout or strike or flee. They feel. They explain. They wait to feel differently. But in this waiting, the colonized is left **to endure the gap between harm and recognition**, often while continuing to support, soften, or stabilize the relation. This is how soft colonial governance functions in the intimate sphere: not by destroying relation, but by making relation contingent on the affective thresholds of the dominant.

And so the harm recurs—not because it is repeated identically, but because it is **never metabolized**. The explanatory logic ensures that the rupture becomes narratively closed _before_ it becomes relationally addressed. The settler moves on, believing that their intentions were clarified. The colonized moves on, **carrying the residue of being made unintelligible**. Over time, this process produces a secondary harm: the erosion of faith that relation can ever be mutual, reciprocal, or transformative.

To interrupt this cycle would require not better communication, but a **different ontology of relation**—one in which emotional experience is not sovereign, where harm is not reframed but felt across difference, and where coherence is not precondition but **co-constructed across rupture**.
