**Subjectivation** (also *subjectification*) is the production of subjects: a subject is a person as produced by the institutions, practices, and [discourses](../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/discourse.md) that act on them.
The word *subject* carries a deliberate double meaning here: a subject is both the one who acts (the thinking subject of philosophy) and the one who is acted upon (the subject of a king, the subject of an experiment).
Foucault used the term to name this double bind: people are constituted as agents through the same processes that constitute them as objects of power.
In "The Subject and Power" (1982), he described his work's central concern as "not power, but the subject" — understanding how human beings are made into subjects through specific historical practices.

Subjectivation proceeds through practices of classification, examination, and normalization.
The school does not receive a "student" — it produces one through enrollment, placement testing, grading, and daily routine.
The student's self-understanding ("I'm good at math," "I'm a troublemaker") is shaped by the categories the institution uses to classify them.
The prison does not receive a "criminal" — it produces a "delinquent," a type of person with a profile, a case file, and a predicted trajectory.
The hospital does not receive "a sick person" — it produces a patient, a diagnostic case, a data point in an epidemiological pattern.

Subjectivation operates on two axes simultaneously.
The first is categorization by others — the institutional classifications (delinquent, gifted, normal, pathological) that position a person within a field of knowledge and governance.
The second is self-formation — the ways people come to understand themselves through the categories institutions provide.
The person who internalizes a psychiatric diagnosis, who lives through the lens of their credit score, who understands their own desires through the framework of sexual identity categories — each is participating in their own subjectivation.
This is not false consciousness.
The subject produced through these practices is real.
But it is produced, not given — contingent, not necessary.

Subjectivation is not the same as oppression, though it can involve it.
A person can be subjectified as "capable," "healthy," "normal" — categories that carry privilege.
The point is that the categories through which people are known and know themselves are products of institutional practices, not descriptions of what people naturally are.
Power does not just say "you can't do that."
It says "you are this kind of person," and it produces the [power/knowledge](../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/power-knowledge.md) that makes that claim stick, under regimes of [biopower](../../../../services/foucauldian-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/biopower.md) and [governmentality](../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/governmentality.md).

The relationality framework uses *subject* in another sense — a position in a directed relating — kept apart from this one: see [subject](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/subject.md).
