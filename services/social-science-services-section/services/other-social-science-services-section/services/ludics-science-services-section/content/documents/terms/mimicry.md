Mimicry is Caillois's category of play built on illusion and
impersonation: the player either becomes an illusory character (a child
playing pirate, an actor in a role, a masked dancer) or accepts an
illusion as if it were real for the play's duration. Its Latin root,
shared with "mime," points at the core mechanism — the temporary
suspension of the ordinary rule that one is only oneself, in favor of a
fiction both the player and, often, an audience agree to treat as
governing.

What distinguishes mimicry from simple pretending is that Caillois
locates it as a fundamental, universal category of play rather than a
special case: children's make-believe, carnival masking, ritual
possession, theater, and role-taking games are, on his account, the
same underlying activity appearing across very different cultural
settings. The illusion is bounded — Caillois's French term *illusion*
carries the etymological sense of "in-play" (from Latin *in-ludere*) —
the mimicry-space is set apart from the ordinary demand that claims be
literally true, without the participants ceasing to know the
difference.

Caillois treats mimicry as paired, in his fourfold scheme, opposite
ilinx rather than agon or alea: where ilinx pursues the destabilization
of ordinary perception through vertiginous physical sensation, mimicry
pursues it through the fictional displacement of identity — both
categories offer an escape from the ordinary self, by different routes.
