Search-and-matching theory is the branch of economics that models markets where finding a counterparty is itself costly and time-consuming — a buyer and a seller who would agree to trade if they ever met do not automatically meet, because locating each other, verifying the other party's terms, and negotiating all take real time and effort.
This is a departure from the frictionless [supply-and-demand](./supply-and-demand.md) model, where price alone clears the market instantly; in a search-and-matching model, unfilled vacancies and unemployed searchers can coexist in equilibrium even at a market-clearing price, because the friction is in *finding*, not in *pricing*.
The formal apparatus — most associated with the Diamond-Mortensen-Pissarides framework, for which Peter Diamond, Dale Mortensen, and Christopher Pissarides shared the 2010 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (**[UNVERIFIED]**: the prize citation itself was not independently read in this pass; the attribution is carried forward from general economics literature, not a primary source) — treats a "matching function" as a production process: it takes the number of searchers on each side of the market as inputs and produces a rate of successful matches as output, the same way a Cobb-Douglas production function takes capital and labor and produces output.

The labor market is the canonical application: job seekers search for openings, employers search for candidates, and the matching function's shape determines how quickly vacancies get filled and how long unemployment spells last, independent of whether wages are "right."
The same structure applies wherever a match must be found before a transaction can happen: real estate (a buyer wants *a* house, not *any* house at the market price), dating and marriage markets (the original application some of this literature borrows vocabulary from), and any two-sided platform matching buyers to sellers, employers to candidates, or shippers to carriers.

**This is the theoretical ground [intermediation](./intermediation.md) and [brokerage](./brokerage.md) stand on.**
A paid intermediary is, in the vocabulary of this theory, a technology for improving the matching function's efficiency — reducing the search time or search cost each side would otherwise bear alone, and capturing part of the resulting surplus as a fee.
Whether that fee is economically justified in a given market is exactly the question of how large the search friction actually is: a market with near-zero search cost (a commodity traded on an open exchange) has little room for a paid matcher to add value, while a market with high search cost and high stakes per match — filling an executive role, finding a buyer for a distressed property fast, finding a truck for a time-sensitive load — is precisely where [contingency-fee](./contingency-fee.md) brokerage income is largest, because the fee is a share of a search cost the client would otherwise have paid in lost time, lost deals, or a worse match.
