Price dispersion is the persistence of different prices for what is, by any reasonable measure, the same good — same quality, same specification — sold by different sellers, in different markets, or at different moments in time.
In a frictionless market with perfect information, the law of one price should eliminate this: if two sellers offer an identical good at different prices, buyers move to the cheaper one until the gap closes.
That price dispersion nonetheless persists, and persists widely, is one of the durable empirical findings of price theory — a classical framing traces this to buyer search cost (a buyer who does not check every seller's price will sometimes pay more than necessary, and sellers who know this have less pressure to match the lowest price in the market), a framing associated with George Stigler's 1961 "The Economics of Information" (general domain knowledge carried forward from the discipline's standard history; the 1961 paper itself was not read for this term — **[UNVERIFIED]** as a direct citation).

Price dispersion is [arbitrage](./arbitrage.md)'s raw material: an arbitrageur's entire function is finding a good priced low in one place before that gap is discovered and closed by other buyers or by the seller's own repricing.
The five goods-arbitrage variants surveyed in [the note on goods arbitrage and the capital gate](../../documents/articles/notes/2026/08/28/2026-08-28-note-on-goods-arbitrage-and-the-capital-gate.md) each rest on a distinct source of price dispersion: liquidation pallets are underpriced because the seller (a retailer clearing returns and overstock) values speed and certainty over maximizing per-item recovery, and sells in bulk at "20 to 30 cents on the retail dollar" specifically because sorting and repricing every item individually is not worth the retailer's own time — search synthesis, per [the goods-arbitrage-mechanism survey](../../../../../../../../../../data/records/2026-08-28-goods-arbitrage-mechanism/goods-arbitrage-survey.md); expired domains are underpriced when a domain's accumulated backlink equity is not visible to, or not valued by, the party letting it lapse; retail arbitrage exploits price differences between a physical store's clearance price and an online marketplace's prevailing price for the identical item; and error fares are a pricing-system malfunction (a human or technical input error) rather than a market-structure gap at all, which is part of why they resist becoming a reliable business rather than a one-off event.

Price dispersion is not evidence of market failure on its own — some dispersion is efficient, reflecting real differences in seller convenience, search cost, or risk that a single "true price" would not capture.
The distinction that matters for an arbitrageur is whether a given instance of dispersion is durable enough to act on before it closes, which is a timing and information question, not merely a pricing one.
