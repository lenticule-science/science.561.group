A placement fee is the [contingency fee](./contingency-fee.md) a recruiting intermediary earns specifically when a sourced candidate is hired by the client employer — the labor-market instance of [brokerage](./brokerage.md)'s general fee-for-match structure, paid by the employer rather than the candidate in the standard US model (charging a job seeker a placement fee is illegal or against industry norms across the US, UK, and EU for permanent placement — search synthesis, see [the 2026-08-28 brokerage-mechanism record](../../../../../../../../../../data/records/2026-08-28-brokerage-mechanism/brokerage-survey.md) for the sourcing).

**Pricing.** As of a 2026-08-28 search-synthesis survey, placement fees for contingency (as opposed to retained) recruiting run 15-30% of the placed candidate's first-year salary, with a quoted 2026 median around 20-22%, and fees scaling with role seniority: roughly 15-18% for entry-level roles, 20-22% for mid-level roles, and 25-31% for executive placements.
Because the fee is a percentage of salary rather than a flat rate, the absolute size of a placement fee scales directly with how senior and well-compensated the placed role is — a $200,000-salary executive placement at 20% nets $40,000 in a single transaction, while a $50,000-salary entry-level placement at 15% nets $7,500; the percentage does the same work in both cases, but the base salary sets the ceiling on what any individual placement can pay.
This pricing pattern follows directly from [search-and-matching theory](./search-and-matching.md)'s general prediction: the harder and higher-stakes the search — an executive role is scarcer, harder to fill correctly, and more costly to mis-hire than an entry-level one — the larger the contingency fee a client will accept, because the client's own cost of a failed or bad-fit search rises with the role's stakes.

**Licensing note, carried from the source record rather than restated in full**: most US jurisdictions do not require a special license for the standard employer-fee-paid placement-fee model, because state and city "employment agency" licensing regimes are aimed at agencies that charge job seekers, not employers; a minority of jurisdictions (New York State and New York City are the clearest documented examples) still require a lighter compliance filing even for an employer-fee-paid firm, short of a full employment-agency license.
