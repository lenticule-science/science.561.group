Saidiya V. Hartman (b. 1961) is the figure through whom the [black-radical-tradition](../_index.md) holds its analysis of how slavery did not end — how its grammar, its categories, its distributions of life and death persist as the structuring conditions of Black life in the post-emancipation present. University Professor at Columbia and a 2019 MacArthur Fellow.

## What this subdomain takes from her

- **The afterlife of slavery.** Hartman's framing in *Lose Your Mother* (2007): "If slavery persists as an issue in the political life of black America, it is not because of an antiquarian obsession with bygone days or the burden of a too-long memory, but because black lives are still imperiled and devalued by a racial calculus and a political arithmetic that were entrenched centuries ago. This is the afterlife of slavery — skewed life chances, limited access to health and education, premature death, incarceration, and impoverishment." The phrase has become one of the subdomain's central organizing claims.
- **The scenes of subjection.** *Scenes of Subjection* (1997) refuses to reproduce the spectacular scenes of slave violence that abolitionist texts and contemporary historiography both circulate — Frederick Douglass's account of his aunt's beating becomes the case in point. Hartman argues that recirculation of those scenes does not produce empathy; it produces consumption, and reinscribes the position of the captive as object. The book's methodological move is to read instead the everyday, mundane scenes — the coffle, the marketplace, the stage — where subjection is enacted in less legible registers.
- **Critical fabulation.** The methodological response to the structural absence of enslaved people's interiority from the archive. The records that survive were produced by the slave system; they record what mattered to slaveholders and what could be made legible to property law. Hartman's method (developed across *Lose Your Mother*, "Venus in Two Acts" [2008], *Wayward Lives, Beautiful Experiments* [2019]) is to write at the limit of the archive — to fabulate without falsifying, to imagine the inner lives the records cannot supply while marking the speculation as such. The method is the subdomain's central methodological inheritance.
- **The wayward.** *Wayward Lives, Beautiful Experiments* (2019) reads the lives of young Black women in early-twentieth-century Philadelphia and New York as practitioners of a freedom that historiography has not learned to recognize — refusal of work, refusal of family, refusal of the narratives prepared for them, the invention of forms of life from inside conditions that should have made them impossible. The waywardness is not failure; it is method.
- **Refusal of recovery.** Across Hartman's work, a refusal of the redemptive narrative — the move that takes a life destroyed by slavery and tries to rescue it through historiographic recovery. The refusal insists that the loss is the loss, that no narrative work undoes it, and that the analytical task is to live with the unredeemability.

## Key works

- *Scenes of Subjection: Terror, Slavery, and Self-Making in Nineteenth-Century America* (1997)
- *Lose Your Mother: A Journey Along the Atlantic Slave Route* (2007)
- "Venus in Two Acts" (*Small Axe*, 2008)
- *Wayward Lives, Beautiful Experiments: Intimate Histories of Social Upheaval* (2019)

## Where her work figures elsewhere

Hartman is one of the load-bearing figures for the subdomain's analysis of slavery as a continuing structure rather than a past event, and for its methodological grappling with the limits of the archive. ## What this subdomain takes from her

- **The archive's structural absence of Black women's interiority.** Hartman's central methodological insight is also the central Black-feminist analytical fact: the archive of slavery and its aftermath was produced by the slave system and by the institutions that succeeded it, and what it records is what mattered to slaveholders, planters, courts, censuses, and police. Black women's interior lives — what they thought, wanted, refused, hoped — are not in the archive in any straightforward way. They have to be inferred at the limit, against the grain of the documents that purport to describe them. The analytical fact is the precondition of the methodological response.
- **Critical fabulation.** From "Venus in Two Acts" (*Small Axe*, 2008) and developed across *Lose Your Mother* (2007) and *Wayward Lives, Beautiful Experiments* (2019): the methodological response to the structural absence of Black women's interiority from the archive. Critical fabulation is to write at the limit of the archive — to fabulate without falsifying, to imagine the inner lives the records cannot supply while marking the speculation as such, to refuse both the recovery narrative (which would falsify) and the silence (which would let the archive's violence stand unchallenged). The method is one of the central methodological inheritances of contemporary Black feminist scholarship.
- **The wayward.** *Wayward Lives, Beautiful Experiments* (2019) reads the lives of young Black women in early-twentieth-century Philadelphia and New York as practitioners of a freedom that historiography has not learned to recognize — refusal of work, refusal of family, refusal of the narratives prepared for them, the invention of forms of life from inside conditions that should have made them impossible. The waywardness is not failure; it is method. The book is a sustained Black-feminist counter-historiography.
- **Refusal of recovery.** Across Hartman's work, a refusal of the redemptive narrative — the move that takes a life destroyed by slavery and tries to rescue it through historiographic recovery. The refusal insists that the loss is the loss, that no narrative work undoes it, and that the analytical task is to live with the unredeemability. The refusal is constitutive of the kind of Black-feminist thought that does not console.
