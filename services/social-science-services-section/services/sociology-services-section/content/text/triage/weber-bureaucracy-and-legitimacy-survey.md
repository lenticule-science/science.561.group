Max Weber's (1864–1920) analysis of *legitimate domination* (*legitime Herrschaft*) and the *bureaucratic ideal-type* are foundational for the sociology of organizations, the political sociology of the modern state, and the study of authority in any institutional setting. Both treatments appear principally in *Wirtschaft und Gesellschaft* [weber-1922, weber-1968] — Weber's posthumous master-work, edited and assembled from manuscripts by Marianne Weber after his death — and in the lecture *Politik als Beruf* (Politics as a Vocation) [weber-1919].

## Domination, power, and legitimacy

Weber's basic distinction is between *Macht* (power: the probability that an actor will be able to carry out their will despite resistance) and *Herrschaft* (domination, often translated as "authority": the probability that a command of definite content will be obeyed by a given group of persons). Domination is power that is *recognized as legitimate* by those subject to it; the obeyers comply not (only) because of force or interest but because they *take the command as binding*.

The question Weber pursues: on what *grounds* do those subject to domination accept it as legitimate? His answer is the celebrated *three pure types of legitimate domination*.

## The three pure types

Weber distinguishes three ideal-typical bases on which domination claims and receives legitimacy:

| Type | Basis of legitimacy | Carrier | Administrative apparatus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Traditional** | "It has always been so" — the sanctity of immemorial traditions | The lord/patriarch | Personal household, retainers, vassals |
| **Charismatic** | The exceptional gift (*charisma*) of the leader — heroism, sanctity, exemplary character | The charismatic leader | Personal disciples, comrades-in-arms |
| **Rational-legal** | A belief in the legality of enacted rules and the right of those elevated to authority under them to issue commands | The office (*Amt*) | Bureaucracy |

These are *ideal types* in Weber's specific methodological sense: analytical constructs that abstract and accentuate features for purposes of comparison; no historical regime is purely any of them. Real political orders combine the types in characteristic mixtures, and the types undergo historical transformations into one another — Weber's celebrated *routinization of charisma* (*Veralltäglichung des Charisma*) describes the process by which charismatic movements, in the succession crisis after the founder's death, transform into traditional or rational-legal forms.

## The bureaucratic ideal-type

Bureaucracy is, for Weber, the *characteristic carrier* of rational-legal authority — the administrative form that fits and reproduces the rational-legal type of legitimacy. The bureaucratic ideal-type comprises a small number of structural features that, taken together, distinguish it from traditional and charismatic administrative apparatuses:

1. **Jurisdictional areas** are fixed and ordered by rules.
2. **Office hierarchy** organizes the bureau into a graded system of superordination and subordination.
3. **Written documents** (the files) are the basis of administration; the office is separated from the person.
4. **Office-management requires expert training**; officials are appointed (not elected) on the basis of demonstrated technical qualifications.
5. **The office is a full-time vocation** demanding the official's whole working capacity.
6. **Office management follows general rules** — stable, exhaustive, learnable rules — applied impersonally (*sine ira et studio*: without anger or favor).
7. **Officials are personally free**, subject to authority only with respect to their impersonal official obligations; they have a fixed salary, career advancement by seniority and merit, and pension rights; they hold the office, do not own it.

Weber's claim is technical, not normative: the bureaucratic apparatus is *technically superior* to other administrative forms — faster, more precise, more predictable, more impersonal, more economical — and its progress is largely irreversible because the modern mass state, the modern firm, the modern political party, the modern army all require its capacities. Bureaucratization is, for Weber, the most unambiguous trend of modernity.

## The iron cage

Weber's concern about bureaucracy is captured in the celebrated metaphor of the *stahlhartes Gehäuse* — Talcott Parsons translated this as "iron cage" in *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* [weber-1905], though more recent scholarship favors "shell as hard as steel" or "steel-hard housing." The image: the rationalization process that produced modern capitalism and modern bureaucracy creates an order that imprisons even those it serves, with no clear path out and no clear meaning beyond its own efficient functioning. Weber's diagnosis is melancholic: the rationalized world is one in which "specialists without spirit, sensualists without heart" come to occupy positions of nominal mastery within a structure that overwhelms them.

## The monopoly of legitimate violence

In *Politics as a Vocation* [weber-1919], Weber gives the famous definition of the modern state: an "*human community that (successfully) claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory.*" Two qualifications are central. First, the monopoly is *claimed*; whether the claim is empirically successful is a contingent matter. Second, the monopoly is over the *legitimate* use of force; private violence may exist, but it is recognized as illegitimate within the state's territory. The definition is descriptive-historical, not normative; Weber is identifying the differentia specifica of the modern state, not endorsing it.

The same lecture distinguishes the *ethic of conviction* (*Gesinnungsethik*) from the *ethic of responsibility* (*Verantwortungsethik*), arguing that political action requires the latter — the willingness to take responsibility for the foreseeable consequences of action — while recognizing that the ethic of conviction has its own integrity in domains where consequences cannot or should not govern.

## Reception and critique

Weber's bureaucracy analysis became, through Talcott Parsons and the mid-twentieth-century American organization theorists, the founding text of organizational sociology. The reception has been ambivalent:

- Reinhard Bendix [bendix-1960] supplied the standard intellectual portrait, situating the bureaucracy and legitimacy analyses in the broader corpus.
- Wolfgang Mommsen [mommsen-1974] developed the political-sociology reception, attending especially to Weber's anxieties about bureaucratic dominance of democracy.
- David Beetham [beetham-1985] argued that Weber's account of legitimacy is conceptually narrower than it could be, distinguishing legitimacy-as-belief (Weber's frame) from legitimacy-as-justification-by-shared-norms (a richer alternative).
- Paul du Gay's [du-gay-2000] *In Praise of Bureaucracy* defends the Weberian bureaucratic ethic against late-twentieth-century anti-bureaucratic management discourse, arguing that the impersonal, rule-bound office holds important ethical capacities that "post-bureaucratic" alternatives sacrifice.

Critics across the twentieth century — including the labor-process tradition (Braverman, Burawoy), the human-relations school (Mayo, Roethlisberger), the institutional-sociology tradition (Selznick, Meyer-Rowan), and the post-Foucauldian tradition (Power, Strathern, Shore-Wright on audit cultures) — have variously challenged, supplemented, or radically reformulated Weber's claims. None has displaced him as the field's reference point.

## Key conceptual distinctions

Three distinctions structure the subsequent literature:

1. **Power vs. domination.** *Macht* operates without legitimacy; *Herrschaft* requires belief in legitimacy. The distinction grounds the field's later question of how legitimacy is produced, contested, and lost.
2. **The three pure types.** Traditional, charismatic, and rational-legal are not historical stages but ideal-typical bases of legitimacy that combine in real regimes. Most historical regimes are mixtures; the analytical question is which mixture and with what dynamics.
3. **Office vs. person.** The bureaucratic separation of office from officeholder — the impersonal office that survives any particular incumbent — is the analytical novelty Weber names; pre-bureaucratic administration treats office as personal property of its holder.

## What the tradition supplies

Three contributions of enduring use:

1. **A typology of legitimate authority.** The three pure types remain the standard analytical apparatus for any question about why people accept commands as binding.
2. **The bureaucratic ideal-type.** A precise structural specification of the modern impersonal-rule-bound administrative apparatus, against which empirical organizations can be measured for proximity, deviation, and hybridization.
3. **The vocabulary of rationalization.** The diagnosis that modernity is characterized by an expanding *Zweckrationalität* (instrumental rationality) at the expense of *Wertrationalität* (value rationality), with the iron-cage thesis as its melancholic end-point, frames a great deal of subsequent critical theory.
