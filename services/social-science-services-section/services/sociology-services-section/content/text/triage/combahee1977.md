# Combahee River Collective 1977 — *A Black Feminist Statement*

Combahee River Collective. "A Black Feminist Statement." 1977. Reprinted in Zillah Eisenstein, ed., *Capitalist Patriarchy and the Case for Socialist Feminism* (Monthly Review Press, 1978), and in Barbara Smith, ed., *Home Girls: A Black Feminist Anthology* (Kitchen Table: Women of Color Press, 1983). Drafted principally by Barbara Smith, Beverly Smith, and Demita Frazier.

## Status

Read locally; foundational reference for the [black-feminism](theories/critical-theory/theories/feminism/theories/black-feminism/_index.md) subdomain. The statement is short (roughly ten pages in most reprintings) and openly available online.

## Annotation

The statement is one of the founding documents of contemporary U.S. Black feminism. It does four things in close sequence:

1. **Names the lineage.** "We are a collective of Black feminists who have been meeting together since 1974." The collective situates itself in a long Black women's tradition (Sojourner Truth, Frances E.W. Harper, Ida B. Wells, Mary Church Terrell) and in the contemporary moment of Black women's organizing in the late-1960s civil rights and Black-power movements and the late-1960s women's movement, neither of which could accommodate Black women's full situation.

2. **Names the analytic.** "The most general statement of our politics at the present time would be that we are actively committed to struggling against racial, sexual, heterosexual, and class oppression, and see as our particular task the development of integrated analysis and practice based upon the fact that the major systems of oppression are interlocking. The synthesis of these oppressions creates the conditions of our lives." The word "interlocking" is the analytical move from which everything downstream — Patricia Hill Collins's matrix of domination, Crenshaw's intersectionality, the kyriarchism analysis — extends.

3. **Names identity politics.** "This focusing upon our own oppression is embodied in the concept of identity politics. We believe that the most profound and potentially most radical politics come directly out of our own identity, as opposed to working to end somebody else's oppression." The phrase "identity politics" enters U.S. political vocabulary here. The later reduction of the phrase to a slur for narrow group interest is a mis-citation of the statement's argument.

4. **Names the universal claim.** "If Black women were free, it would mean that everyone else would have to be free since our freedom would necessitate the destruction of all the systems of oppression." Black women's situation is not a special case of more general oppressions; it is the case where the structure is most fully exposed.

The statement is also explicitly socialist ("We realize that the liberation of all oppressed peoples necessitates the destruction of the political-economic systems of capitalism and imperialism as well as patriarchy") and explicitly lesbian, and treats both commitments as analytical rather than additive.

The reception document for the statement at its fortieth anniversary is Keeanga-Yamahtta Taylor, ed., *How We Get Free: Black Feminism and the Combahee River Collective* (Haymarket, 2017), which collects interviews with the surviving founders.
