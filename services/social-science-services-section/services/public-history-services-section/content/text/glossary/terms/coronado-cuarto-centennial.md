The Coronado Cuarto Centennial was the commemorative machinery the United States built in 1939–40 to mark the 400th anniversary of the Coronado and de Niza entradas into the Southwest. Congress supplied the legal chassis: the Coronado Exposition Commission Act of 1939 (76th Congress) authorized federal participation in a joint observance across six states — Arizona, Colorado, Kansas, New Mexico, Oklahoma, Texas — and directed a monument near the point where the expedition was understood to have first crossed into what is now the United States, a provision that fed directly into the later establishment of Coronado National Memorial (1941). Around that federal core, states and localities built out a full public-history apparatus: outdoor pageantry staged for thousands of spectators, souvenir publications, and a 3-cent commemorative postage stamp issued September 7, 1940. In Arizona, the labor arm was the National Youth Administration, the Depression-era jobs program that put local teenagers to work building monuments across the state — among them the Fray Marcos de Niza monument at Lochiel, dedicated April 15, 1939, with the governors of both Arizona and Sonora in attendance.

The apparatus is worth holding as one object because of what it did structurally: it converted a single contested historical claim into permanent, government-funded, cross-border infrastructure. A congressional act, a paid federal workforce, statewide pageantry, and a postage stamp are not incidental to the commemoration — they are the commemoration, in the specific sense that public history operates through institutions and appropriations rather than through belief alone. The Lochiel monument's inscription declares Fray Marcos de Niza "the first European west of the Rockies," entering at this exact spot on April 12, 1539. That sentence, cut in stone and dedicated by two governors, is treated by the apparatus as settled; the underlying history is not.

Both layers of the Lochiel monument's claim are, in fact, live scholarly disputes, and they compound. The route through the San Rafael Valley that the monument commemorates is the minority reconstruction (Wilson 1987); the majority scholarly position, tracing de Niza along the San Pedro River well to the east, goes back to Carl Sauer's *The Road to Cíbola* (1932), and Hadley and Sheridan's 1995 land-use history of the valley — the most careful modern treatment of the ground the monument stands on — side with Sauer, not with the route the monument asserts. And the report the whole expedition is commemorating is itself doubted at its source: Cleve Hallenbeck's *The Journey of Fray Marcos de Niza* (1949) calls the friar "a lying monk," arguing that the golden cities of Cíbola his account promised dissolved on Coronado's actual arrival into ordinary stone-and-mud pueblos, and that the report may have covered for a much shorter journey — one cut off after Zuni fighters killed de Niza's enslaved guide, Estevanico, at Hawikuh, well short of any claimed sighting. So the monument sits at the intersection of a minority route claim and a report a serious biographer calls a fabrication: contested geography built on a contested source, and neither dispute is footnoted at the site.

None of this made the apparatus provisional. The commission was chartered, the youths were paid, the governors attended, the stamp was printed, and the monument still stands at Lochiel — permanent infrastructure built on a doubly uncertain foundation, exactly because public commemoration does not require the underlying history to be settled, only for the institutions producing it to agree to proceed. That is the pattern this repository tracks as settlerism-as-filing-system: the state's capacity to convert a claim into a category — "historic," "commemorated," "first" — and fund permanent structure on that category regardless of the evidentiary ground beneath it. The same valley now sits inside a different filing act, a 2025 border-wall waiver that set aside 34 federal laws to clear the same acre the Cuarto Centennial once monumentalized; the flood-recession cottonwood grove nearby, a two-century archive with no comparable doubt attached to its age or its record, received no monument and got a wall. The settler apparatus reliably funds and defends the doubtful commemorated claim while it bulldozes the demonstrable, undocumented one.
