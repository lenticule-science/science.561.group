Romantic nationalism is the political movement that fuses Romantic aesthetics — the valorization of feeling, tradition, organic community, and the particular over the universal — with national identity. Its founding premise, articulated by Johann Gottfried Herder in the 1780s, is that each people (*Volk*) possesses a unique spirit ([*Volksgeist*](../../philosophy/terms/volksgeist.md)) expressed through its language, songs, myths, and customs. This spirit is what makes a people a people, and it must be made visible, collected, preserved, and defended.

The movement emerged against Enlightenment universalism — the claim that there is one human nature, one reason, one set of rights applicable to all. Romantic nationalists countered that universalism was a mask for French cultural hegemony, and that authentic human life is particular: rooted in a specific language, a specific landscape, a specific tradition. Herder's intention was pluralist (all peoples are equally dignified in their particularity), but the framework he created was immediately weaponizable. If each people has a spirit, someone must curate it — decide which songs, which tales, which costumes count as authentic. The curation is where [genring](../theories/cybernetic-postliberalism/terms/genring.md) begins.

The Brothers Grimm, Fichte's *Addresses to the German Nation* (1808), the Finnish *Kalevala*, the Czech National Revival, the Irish Literary Revival, the Pahlavi regime's deployment of the *Shahnameh* — all are instances of Romantic nationalism producing the cultural materials that prove the nation exists. Benedict Anderson (1983) called nations "imagined communities": not fictions, but communities that exist because enough people imagine them the same way. Romantic nationalism is the machinery that produces the shared imagination — the songs, stories, costumes, and festivals that make the nation feel real.

The movement's legacy is ambivalent. It gave colonized and peripheral peoples a framework for asserting their cultural dignity against imperial homogenization. It also gave dominant nations a framework for [genring](../theories/cybernetic-postliberalism/terms/genring.md) their own populations and their neighbors into consumable folk types, producing the genre figures that [fascism](../theories/cybernetic-postliberalism/texts/describing-fascism.md) would later recruit as its cast.
