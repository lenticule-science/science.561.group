A tree-sit is bodily interposition escalated vertically. The base tactic — a person's body placed between a tool and the thing it would destroy — becomes an occupation of the canopy itself: a platform built into the tree that is scheduled to fall, with someone living on it. The escalation changes what removal means. A chainsaw through an empty trunk is property destruction, insurable and administratively routine. A chainsaw through a trunk with a person in the branches above is a killing in progress. The tactic does not stop the saw with force; it stops it by relocating the act across a legal and moral line that almost no institution — logging company, agency, or court — is willing to cross in public.

The lineage runs nearly the length of the modern environmental movement. The word itself comes from Chipko ("to cling" or "to embrace"), the Himalayan movement that began organizing against contract logging at Mandal village, Chamoli district, in April 1973, and became internationally known when Gaura Devi led twenty-seven women of Reni village in physically embracing trees to stop a logging crew in March 1974 — bodily interposition at ground level, the tactic's founding form (Guha 1989). The Pacific Northwest and Northern California timber wars of the 1980s–90s took the same principle into the canopy, where Earth First! organizers built the first sustained tree-sit platforms against old-growth logging accelerated by Charles Hurwitz's 1985 leveraged buyout of Pacific Lumber. Judi Bari, an organizer of the 1990 Redwood Summer campaign, was permanently disabled by a car bomb in Oakland on May 24, 1990, in an attack the FBI blamed on her and Earth First! before a 2002 civil judgment established the agency's own investigation had fabricated evidence against them — an early instance of the state answering canopy occupation with the same domestic-terrorism framing that recurs at every later site in this lineage.

Julia Butterfly Hill's occupation of the redwood she named Luna, near Stafford, California, is the tactic's proof of concept: 738 days on a platform roughly 180 feet up, December 10, 1997 to December 18, 1999 — more than eight times the previous world tree-sit record — ending not in eviction but in a negotiated agreement with Pacific Lumber that protected Luna and a 200-foot buffer around it, funded by a $50,000 payment Hill and supporters raised and Pacific Lumber redirected to sustainable-forestry research at Humboldt State (Zinn Education Project 2023; Wikipedia, "Redwood Summer" 2026). The tactic can end in a negotiated win, not only in removal or arrest — the sit itself is the leverage, sustained by the same fact that makes it dangerous: someone is up there.

Fairy Creek, on unceded Pacheedaht territory on Vancouver Island (2020–21), scaled the tactic to a blockade generating more than 1,100 arrests under an RCMP-enforced injunction — Canada's largest single act of civil disobedience. What ended it was not the settler court, which had sided with the logging company throughout, but Indigenous jurisdiction: the Pacheedaht, Ditidaht, and Huu-ay-aht Nations requested a provincial deferral on old-growth logging in the watershed and began their own forest stewardship planning (The Narwhal 2021) — resolution by the authority the land already answers to, not by attrition of the occupiers. The pattern echoes, partially, in the Tohono O'odham Nation's own trespass notices to contractors at Lochiel, issued alongside — not through — the tree-sit there.

Weelaunee, the Atlanta forest occupied against the Atlanta Public Safety Training Center ("Cop City"), shows what happens when the state answers presence with force rather than negotiation. On January 18, 2023, Georgia State Patrol troopers shot and killed Manuel "Tortuguita" Paez Terán during a clearing operation — reporting has called it the first killing of an environmental protester by police in modern U.S. history (Wikipedia, "Killing of Tortuguita" 2026). No trooper was charged. The state instead turned its force on the movement: dozens charged with domestic terrorism, sixty-one people indicted under Georgia's RICO statute in September 2023 (ACLU 2023) — charges a Fulton County judge dismissed in late 2025 for want of prosecutorial authority, with five domestic-terrorism charges still pending appeal into 2026. That charging strategy, not abstract caution, is why Lochiel's tree-sitters rotate anonymously.

The Center for Biological Diversity calls the platform installed 60 feet up the surviving Grandmother cottonwood at Lochiel, Arizona — raised July 29, 2026, one day after Fisher Sand & Gravel crews uprooted three of its four sibling trees under a border-wall contract issued through a waiver of 34 federal statutes — the first tree-sit aimed at stopping U.S. border wall construction (CBD 2026). It inherits every part of this lineage at once: Chipko's bodily logic, Luna's proof that presence can out-negotiate a company, Fairy Creek's reminder that Indigenous jurisdiction can resolve what protest alone cannot, and Weelaunee's warning about what the state does when it chooses escalation anyway. It also sits inside this glossary's own argument about the filing system that authorizes the wall: the §102 waiver treats "border security" as a category that outranks environmental, religious, and historical law in one administrative motion, exactly the kind of exclusion-power this glossary tracks elsewhere as selectively energized against everything but itself. A waiver can file away a statute. It cannot file away a body in a tree — which is also why the tactic reads, honestly, as a contrast with proceduralism rather than a version of it: the Center for Biological Diversity's own lawsuit against the waiver power had already died on summary judgment four months before the sit began, and the Tohono O'odham Nation's injunction was denied two weeks into it. The sit does not ask a court to recognize a claim it has already refused twice. It makes the claim physically unignorable instead, for as long as someone stays up the tree.
