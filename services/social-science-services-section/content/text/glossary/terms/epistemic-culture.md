An epistemic culture is the entire apparatus through which a knowledge community produces and validates what it knows.
Karin Knorr-Cetina introduced the concept in *Epistemic Cultures: How the Sciences Make Knowledge* (1999), based on ethnographic comparison of two sciences: high-energy physics at CERN and molecular biology in a leading laboratory.
Her central claim: domains are defined by **how** they know, not **what** they know.

The two fields she studied differ on nearly every dimension of knowledge production.
High-energy physics works through collaborations of thousands of researchers, operates massive shared detectors, reasons from theoretical predictions to experimental confirmation, treats the individual researcher as interchangeable, and produces papers with author lists longer than the text.
Molecular biology works through small laboratory groups, employs benchtop instruments manipulated by individual researchers, reasons from experimental observation toward theoretical explanation, and publishes under a few named authors.
These are not superficial differences in style.
They are different **machineries of knowing** --- different ways of constituting objects, different relationships between theory and experiment, different social structures for producing consensus.

Knorr-Cetina's concept does related but distinct work from Kuhn's [disciplinary matrix](disciplinary-matrix.md).
Where Kuhn focuses on shared commitments within a single discipline, Knorr-Cetina focuses on the structural differences between disciplines.
And where [domain analysis](../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/knowledge-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/domain-analysis.md) in information science treats domains as discourse communities, epistemic cultures emphasizes the material and procedural infrastructure --- the laboratory equipment, the data pipelines, the publication venues, the career structures --- that makes a domain's knowledge possible.

The concept matters for knowledge organization because it explains why universal classification schemes always strain at the boundaries between sciences.
If domains differ in their fundamental epistemic machinery, a single vocabulary for describing "method" or "evidence" or "theory" will systematically distort at least some of them.
