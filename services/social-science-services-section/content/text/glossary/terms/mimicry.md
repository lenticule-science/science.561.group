Mimicry is one of Roger Caillois's four fundamental categories of play: play structured around becoming someone or something else — taking on a role, inhabiting a fiction, simulating a situation.
Theater, [role-playing games](../../../topics/role-playing-games/index.md), children's pretend play, cosplay, and simulation games all involve mimicry.
The [player](../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/player.md) temporarily sets aside their ordinary identity and operates as a fictional persona, and the interest comes not from competition or chance but from the experience of transformation itself — seeing through different eyes, making choices you wouldn't otherwise make, inhabiting a world that doesn't exist.

Mimicry depends on the [magic circle](magic-circle.md) in a specific way.
The player must simultaneously be themselves and not-themselves: aware enough to maintain the fiction, committed enough to invest it with meaning.
A child playing house knows they aren't really a parent, but the play only works if they act as though they are.
An RPG player knows their character isn't real, but the game's emotional and strategic depth requires genuine engagement with the character's situation.
This double consciousness — knowing it's pretend while taking the pretense seriously — is what distinguishes mimicry from delusion on one side and mere description on the other.

What makes mimicry distinctive among Caillois's categories is that it doesn't require a determinate outcome.
[Agon](agon.md) produces winners and losers, [alea](alea.md) produces results, but mimicry can sustain itself indefinitely as long as the fiction holds.
This is why mimetic [play](../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/play.md) often resists the [win-conditions](../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/win-condition.md) and scoring systems that structure other game types — and why role-playing games, which are built on mimicry, have historically struggled with the question of whether "winning" even applies to them.
