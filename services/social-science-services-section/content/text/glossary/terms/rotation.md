Rotation is the [recurrence](recurrence.md) of assignment: different people take the same role in sequence, and the pattern repeats across time.
The duty officer rotates weekly.
The on-call engineer rotates nightly.
The committee chair rotates annually.
The Roman consul's term rotated every year, guaranteed by law.
What makes these rotations is that the role persists (officer, engineer, chair, consul) while the occupant changes according to a fixed schedule.

Rotation serves three distinct structural purposes.
First, it distributes burden: no single person bears a difficult or consuming duty permanently.
A nurse on permanent night shift faces documented physiological and social costs; nurses who rotate through shifts keep their wellbeing longer.
Second, it builds skill breadth: everyone learns every role.
An organization where all engineers learn on-call eventually has a robust capability; an organization where one person monopolizes on-call knowledge becomes brittle when that person leaves.
Third, and most politically crucial, it prevents power accumulation.
A person in the same [office](../../../../../services/economics-and-business-science-services-section/services/business-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/office.md) for decades can build networks, leverage, and constituencies that become personal rather than institutional.
The Athenian democracy understood this: most offices were filled by lottery (sortition) and rotated out after one year.
Mogens Herman Hansen (2005) demonstrates that term limits combined with rotation made it rational for Athenian officials to honor the constitution—they knew they would soon be ordinary citizens subject to the laws they had made.

Rotation has four dimensions that must be jointly designed. **Sequence** is the order of rotation: fixed (A→B→C→A→B→C), randomized (select from eligible pool), competency-based (rotate among the qualified), or preference-based (accommodate personal constraints).
Fixed sequence is most predictable but least flexible; randomized is fairest but harder to plan around. **Speed** is rotation frequency: fast rotation (daily shifts) maximizes exposure but increases handoff cost and knowledge loss at each transition; slow rotation (annual committees) allows deep learning but risks burnout and power drift.
Folkard and Tucker (2003) showed that 12-hour shifts are often optimal for operational roles because they balance fatigue against handoff cost. **Scope** is what gets rotated: a single role, a set of roles, or entire offices. **Continuity** is whether people exit entirely or rotate back to previous roles—a fixed cycle (A→B→C→A) creates predictable long-term patterns, while one-time assignment creates definitive separation.

Agricultural rotation operates by the same structural logic.
[Crop rotation](../../../../../domestic-science-services-section/services/domestic-production-services-section/services/gardening-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/crop-rotation.md) cycles different crops through the same fields to manage soil fertility and break pest cycles.
The field is the fixed position; the crop is what rotates.
Grass-clover one year restores nitrogen; grain the next consumes it; fallow returns land to recovery.
The pattern is recurrent—the sequence repeats across years—and the mechanism is identical to organizational rotation: distributing demand across a resource to prevent depletion and allow recovery.

Rotation is foundational to egalitarian governance.
Lewis Henry Morgan (1877), writing of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, documented that clan councils rotated leadership responsibility, preventing any single person from accumulating unchecked power.
The practice was not accidental: it was encoded in the constitutional structure itself.
Woodburn (1982) shows the same pattern in East African pastoralist societies where formal offices don't exist but rotation of hunting leadership and resource access prevents the emergence of permanent hierarchy.
Rotation is how you maintain [recurrence](recurrence.md) of power-holding—the pattern of governance repeats, but the occupant changes.
