Emergent obligation is obligation that arises from situated relations rather than from pre-defined rules, and that shifts in real time as conditions change.
It is not assigned by a framework, ratified by a procedure, or derived from a universal principle.
It is generated by the relation itself — by what the relation requires in this moment, under these conditions, given what is at stake.

A grandmother falls on ice.
The obligation to help her is not produced by a social contract, a deliberative assembly, or a moral calculus.
It is produced by the relation: she is your grandmother, you are here, she is down.
The obligation is immediate, specific, and non-transferable.
It does not wait for justification.
It does not require that helping her be universalizable.
It does not ask whether everyone in comparable situations would be helped.
The relation generates the obligation and the obligation generates the action.

This is the temporal structure that distinguishes emergent obligation from rule-based systems.
[Proceduralism](../../../../services/sociology-science-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/proceduralism.md) produces obligation through process: obligations exist because they were collectively established, transparently documented, and ratifiable by all affected parties.
This works when there is time to establish them.
Under collapse — when conditions shift faster than procedures can track, when the people affected change from hour to hour, when the resources available transform between morning and night — procedural obligation cannot keep up.
By the time the assembly ratifies the obligation, the situation it addressed no longer exists.

Emergent obligation operates at the speed of relation.
When a kin network faces flooding, the obligations shift in real time: this elder needs evacuation now, these children need shelter, this family holds seed stock that cannot be replaced.
No one convened a meeting to establish these priorities.
The priorities emerged from the relational structure — who is connected to whom, who carries what responsibility, who is most vulnerable, who holds knowledge the community cannot afford to lose.
The relational field generates the obligation and the obligation generates the response.

Leanne Betasamosake Simpson's grounded normativity and Kyle Powys Whyte's Indigenous climate ethics both describe governance systems in which obligation operates this way: not through universal frameworks applied to particular cases, but through particular relations generating particular responsibilities that universal frameworks could not have predicted.
The strength of this model under collapse is that it does not depend on institutional infrastructure — on the meeting space, the communication channel, the shared evaluative framework — that collapse destroys.
It depends on relations, and relations persist where infrastructure does not.
