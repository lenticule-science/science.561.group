The **ordinal society** is the term Marion Fourcade and Kieran Healy develop across their [2017 paper](fourcade-healy-2017) and [2024 book](fourcade-healy-2024) of that name for a society whose distinctive feature is the proliferation of algorithmic *ranking and scoring* systems that distribute life chances along continuous ordinal scales.
The credit score, the school ranking, the criminal-risk score, the employability rating, the platform reputation score, the social-media engagement metric, and the dating-app desirability rank all share the same structural form: they take heterogeneous information about a person, institution, or transaction; collapse it into a position on a single ordered scale; and use the resulting rank to allocate access to opportunity, capital, attention, or partnership.

The argument is not that ordinal ranking is new — credit scores date to the 1950s, school rankings to the 1980s.
The argument is that the *generalization* of ordinal ranking across previously unranked domains, the *granularity* of contemporary scoring (real-time, individual-level, multi-dimensional), and the *consequentiality* of the resulting positions amount to a qualitative transformation in how social standing is produced.

## Three features of ordinal sorting

Fourcade and Healy identify three structural features that together distinguish the ordinal-society's ranking apparatus from earlier modes of social classification:

1. **Continuous scale rather than categorical class.** Earlier modes of social classification (the census-defined occupational group, the legal class of citizen / resident / non-citizen, the medical diagnosis as present / absent) sorted people into a small number of categorical bins.
  Ordinal sorting positions each person at a specific location on a continuous scale.
  A credit score of 712 is not in a different category from a credit score of 711; it is one ordinal position higher.
  The distinction has consequences: categorical classification supports group-level political mobilization (the working class, women, citizens) that continuous-scale ranking does not, since each individual occupies a unique position rather than sharing membership with others.
2. **Individual-level granularity.** The unit of ranking is the individual person (or institution), not the demographic category.
  The credit score is *yours*; the platform reputation rating is *yours*; the predictive-policing risk assessment is *yours*.
  Group membership influences the score (through statistical inheritance from training data) but the score is presented to its bearer as an individual property they personally own and can personally improve.
3. **Continuous re-ranking.** The ordinal position is not assigned once and held; it is recomputed continuously as new behavioral data arrives.
  The driver's platform rating updates with every trip; the credit score updates monthly; the social-media engagement rank updates with every post.
  The rank is a moving target, with the implication that the ranked subject must continuously perform to maintain or improve their position.

## Ranks as governance

The ordinal society is a particular *governance regime*: it allocates opportunity, capital, partnership, and access through ranking rather than through categorical eligibility, rule-bound entitlement, or direct political assignment.
The credit score determines access to mortgages and rental housing; the employability rating shapes hiring; the school ranking allocates students to schools and shapes school operations; the platform reputation determines whether the gig worker gets the next assignment.

The mechanism is structurally parallel to [governance-indicator](governance-indicator.md) regimes operating at the institutional level: in both cases, a numerical rank exerts governance effects through *making things matter* rather than through commanding compliance.
The ordinal society applies the indicator-governance logic to the individual scale, with all the reactivity (Espeland-Sauder [espeland-sauder-2007]) the indicator literature documented at the institutional scale: ranked subjects reorient their behavior to optimize the rank, with effects on the substantive activity the rank was originally meant to describe.

## Distinguished from

- **Class society** — a class society organizes life chances through categorical position in the relations of production (capitalist / worker / petty bourgeois).
  The ordinal society overlays ordinal ranking on top of class structure: class continues to operate, but its expression is increasingly mediated through individual scores and ranks rather than through direct categorical assignment.
- **Status society** — a status society (Weberian) organizes life chances through categorical position in honor hierarchies (estate, caste, recognized social rank).
  The ordinal society's continuous individual ranks differ from status society's categorical group memberships in not generating the collective identifications status memberships did.
- **[Governance indicator](governance-indicator.md)** regimes — the institutional-level analog.
  Governance indicators rank countries, firms, schools, hospitals; the ordinal society ranks individuals.
  The mechanisms are parallel but operate at different scales and produce different politics (collective resistance to country-level Doing Business is more available than collective resistance to individual-level credit scoring).
- **[Surveillance capitalism](../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/undated/surveillance-capitalism.md)** ([Zuboff](../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/undated/surveillance-capitalism.md)) — Zuboff's frame emphasizes the *accumulation logic* (behavioral surplus, prediction products, behavioral futures markets); the ordinal society emphasizes the *distributional logic* (ranking-based allocation of opportunity).
  The two analyses overlap in their empirical referents (the same platforms and scoring systems) but answer different analytical questions.
