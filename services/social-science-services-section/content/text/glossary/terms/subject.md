In the [relationality](../../../../../../../lib/legacy/2026-07-31-lenticule-science-corpus/animating/content/becoming/doing/working/governing/content/individual/content/agent/actuating/content/actuator/embodying/content/body/integrating/content/drone/swarming/content/swarm/content/social/_archive/relationality-repo/relationality/_index.md) framework, **subject** names a position in a directed act of [relating](relating.md): the relatum *of which* the predicate is asserted.
The subject and the [object](object.md) are the two sides of any directed relating; what makes them subject and object — rather than two interchangeable poles — is the directionality of the relating itself.

The subject is not a substance, a thing-with-properties, or an agent in the philosophy-of-mind sense.
A subject in this framework is constituted by the relating in which it stands — it is what the predicate is asserted *of*, and it would not be that subject (or any subject at all) outside the relating.
There is no Cartesian *res cogitans* underneath, no grammatical-subject as a property the world has independently of being read; the subject is a *position*, and the position is constituted by the directed relating that gives it content.

## Subject and object are not symmetric

In substance metaphysics, the subject is the bearer of properties; the object is what is acted upon.
In relational ontology, the directionality is more austere: subject and object are the two coordinates of a directed [predication cell](../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/math-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/predication-cell.md), structurally distinct, with the predicate running *from* the subject *toward* the object.
Swapping the two does not give the same relating with the poles relabeled; it gives a different relating, related to the original by [predicate converse](../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/math-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/predicate-category.md).

This asymmetry is what allows relating to constitute its terms in the first place.
A symmetric primitive could not produce two distinct positions; the directed primitive does, and the *subject* position is one of the two positions it produces.

## Distinction from grammatical and Cartesian subject

Three readings of *subject* should be kept apart:

- **Grammatical subject** — the noun phrase in subject position of a sentence.
  A surface feature of language, not the ontological category the framework names.
- **Cartesian subject** — the *res cogitans*, the thinking thing as substrate of mental properties.
  Substance-metaphysical; the relationality framework refuses the substrate while keeping the term *subject* for the position.
- **Relationality subject** — a position in a directed relating, constituted by the relating itself. *This* reading.

The three coincide in particular cases (a sentence whose grammatical subject names a Cartesian subject who is occupying a relationality subject position) but the framework's term means the third.

## Subjects are entities

A subject is always a [relational entity](relational-entity.md) — a stable pattern occupying the subject coordinate of one or more predication cells.
The same entity can occupy the subject position of one relating and the object position of another; *being a subject* is not a property of the entity but of the position the entity is in within a particular relating.
The framework has no inherent subjects, only entities that are subjects within specified relatings.

A subject is a person as produced by the institutions, practices, and discourses that act on them.
The word carries a deliberate double meaning: a subject is both the one who acts (the thinking subject of philosophy) and the one who is acted upon (the subject of a king, the subject of an experiment).
Foucault used the term to name this double bind: people are constituted as agents through the same processes that constitute them as objects of power.
In "The Subject and Power" (1982), he described his work's central concern as "not power, but the subject" — understanding how human beings are made into subjects through specific historical practices.

Subjectification names the process: the production of subjects through practices of classification, examination, and normalization.
The school does not receive a "student" — it produces one through enrollment, placement testing, grading, and daily routine.
The student's self-understanding ("I'm good at math," "I'm a troublemaker") is shaped by the categories the institution uses to classify them.
The prison does not receive a "criminal" — it produces a "delinquent," a type of person with a profile, a case file, and a predicted trajectory.
The hospital does not receive "a sick person" — it produces a patient, a diagnostic case, a data point in an epidemiological pattern.

Subjectification operates on two axes simultaneously.
The first is categorization by others — the institutional classifications (delinquent, gifted, normal, pathological) that position a person within a field of knowledge and governance.
The second is self-formation — the ways people come to understand themselves through the categories institutions provide.
The person who internalizes a psychiatric diagnosis, who lives through the lens of their credit score, who understands their own desires through the framework of sexual identity categories — each is participating in their own subjectification.
This is not false consciousness.
The subject produced through these practices is real.
But it is produced, not given — contingent, not necessary.

Subjectification is not the same as oppression, though it can involve it.
A person can be subjectified as "capable," "healthy," "normal" — categories that carry privilege.
The point is that the categories through which people are known and know themselves are products of institutional practices, not descriptions of what people naturally are.
Power does not just say "you can't do that."
It says "you are this kind of person," and it produces the power/knowledge that makes that claim stick.
