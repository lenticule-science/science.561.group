Democratic confederalism is Abdullah Ocalan's political philosophy for stateless self-governance, developed during his imprisonment on Imrali Island (from 1999 onward) through a synthesis of the PKK's Marxist-Leninist heritage with Murray Bookchin's libertarian municipalism.
It represents one of the most dramatic ideological transformations of any modern political movement: the PKK's shift from seeking an independent Kurdish nation-state to rejecting the nation-state as a political form altogether (Jongerden and Akkaya 2012).

The model organizes political life through nested communes — neighborhood, district, canton — rather than through state institutions.
Decision-making flows from the bottom up.
Each level of governance operates through councils with mandatory gender co-chairship: every leadership position is held jointly by one man and one woman.
This is not representational politics (electing individuals to speak for constituencies) but direct governance (communities making decisions about their own lives through assembly).

Three pillars structure the model: democratic autonomy (self-governance without state permission), gender liberation (not as an addition to politics but as its precondition — Ocalan argues that the domination of women is the template for all other domination), and ecological communalism (economic life organized around communal needs rather than capital accumulation).
The associated feminist theory, [jineoloji](jineoloji.md), is not a Western feminist import but an indigenous intellectual production of the Kurdish women's movement.

Democratic confederalism is practiced in Rojava (the Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria), where it governs a multi-ethnic territory through the structures described above.
It is also the political framework of the KCK (Group of Communities in Kurdistan), the umbrella organization connecting Kurdish movements across Turkey, Syria, Iraq, and Iran.
Western media coverage of Rojava during the anti-ISIS campaign (2014–2019) celebrated Kurdish fighters while systematically stripping away the political philosophy that animated them — presenting "feminist warriors" without mentioning Bookchin, Ocalan, or the abolition of the state form.
