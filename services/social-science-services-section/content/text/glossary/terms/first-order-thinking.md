***First-order thinking*** is generative, associative, and uncritical — it produces material. ***Second-order thinking*** is analytical, editorial, and critical — it evaluates and shapes material. [Peter Elbow](../../general/people/peter-elbow.md) introduced this distinction in *Writing with Power* (1981) to explain why most writers produce neither fluency nor quality: they try to generate and evaluate simultaneously [@elbow1981].

The problem is familiar to any writer who stares at a blank page, composing and deleting the same sentence. The internal editor (second-order thinking) intervenes before the generator (first-order thinking) has produced anything to edit. Elbow's solution is to separate the two: write freely first ([freewriting](./freewriting.md)), then revise critically later ([revision](./revision.md)).

This isn't a claim that first-order thinking produces good prose — it usually produces [writer-based prose](./writer-based-prose.md), disorganized and associative. The claim is that this raw material is the necessary input for productive revision. Without first-order thinking, there's nothing to revise. Without second-order thinking, there's no shaping.

[Linda Flower](../../general/people/linda-flower.md) and [John Hayes](../../general/people/john-hayes.md)'s cognitive process model provides the empirical framework: composing involves recursive interaction between planning, translating, and reviewing [@flowerhayes1981]. Elbow's distinction maps onto this: first-order thinking corresponds to generating and translating; second-order thinking corresponds to reviewing and restructuring.

The vault's approach reflects both: [freewriting](./freewriting.md) and [brainstorming](./freewriting.md) support first-order thinking; the [style guide](style-guide.md), [plain language specification](../topics/plain-language/index.md), and [Paramedic Method](./paramedic-method.md) support second-order thinking.

- [revision](./revision.md) — the second-order process that shapes first-order material
