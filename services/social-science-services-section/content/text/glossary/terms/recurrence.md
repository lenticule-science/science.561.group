Recurrence is the temporal pattern of events that return.
A ceremony that happens yearly is a recurrence.
A term-limited office whose holder cycles through the community is a recurrence.
An obligation that passes to descendants is a recurrence.
What makes these recurrences is that the same pattern — the ceremony, the office, the obligation — appears again and again across time.

But recurrence is not repetition.
Repetition is the return of identical copies: the same event, unchanged, redundant.
Recurrence is the return of the same pattern in changed circumstances.
This distinction matters fundamentally.
Ilya Prigogine (1997) argued that time has an irreversible arrow — that forward in time means genuinely new states become possible.
A spring returns to the same position in the year, but the spring that returns is spring in a world that has aged, where climate has shifted, where seeds are different.
The [cycle](../../../../../philosophy-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/time.md) is real; the novelty is also real.
The pattern spirals, not circles.

Recurrence operates at multiple temporal scales.
Periodic recurrence returns at fixed intervals: daily standups, weekly [reviews](./review.md), annual audits, term-limited elections.
Conditional recurrence triggers on events rather than time: a milestone might trigger a retrospective, an error might trigger an investigation, a crisis might trigger a convening.
Generational recurrence binds people across lifetimes: a name carries obligation to ancestors; a tradition transmits across generations; the seven-generations principle of Haudenosaunee governance asks whether decisions serve those seven generations forward.

Recurrence is the temporal foundation of [governance](./governance.md).
Accountability requires recurrence — a review that happens once is an event; a review that recurs produces a pattern of assessment.
Term-limited offices, elections that recur, audits that recur — these create the periodic supervision that prevents power from drifting unchecked.
The rhythm of return is what governance builds on.
H.-G.
Gadamer (1960) showed that all understanding occurs within [tradition](tradition.md), and tradition is itself a form of recurrence — practices and meanings that return across generations, adapted to new circumstances but continuous with what came before.

The biological world runs on recurrence.
[Crop rotation](../../../../../domestic-science-services-section/services/domestic-production-services-section/services/gardening-services-section/content/glossary/terms/crop-rotation.md) works because different crops return in sequence, managing soil and pest cycles.
Seeds germinate across multiple years as a bet-hedging strategy against drought or flood.
The circadian rhythm returns daily.
The menstrual cycle returns monthly.
Life itself depends on recurrence — on patterns that return.

[Cadence](./cadence.md) is the operational instantiation of recurrence.
Rhythm (in music) is recurrence applied to sound.
[Tradition](tradition.md) is recurrence applied to culture.
The concept is general; its domain instances are distinct.
