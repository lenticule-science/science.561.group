Free verse is poetry written without a fixed [meter](meter.md) or [rhyme](rhyme.md) scheme. The term translates the French *vers libre*, which named the movement that emerged in late-nineteenth-century French poetry and was taken up in English by Pound, Eliot, Williams, and others in the early twentieth century.

Free verse is not formless. It replaces external constraints (a predetermined metrical pattern, a rhyme scheme) with internal ones: the poet must find the form that fits this particular poem. The [line break](line-break.md) becomes the primary formal tool — without meter to dictate where lines end, every break is a decision about pacing, emphasis, and meaning.

As William Carlos Williams argued, free verse is not "free" in the sense of unconstrained but free in the sense of self-governing: each poem discovers its own measure. The challenge of free verse is that without inherited structure, the poet must create structure from scratch — through image patterning, syntactic rhythm, repetition, spacing, and the shape of the poem on the page.

Most contemporary English-language poetry is written in free verse, though formal and metrical poetry continue to be practiced.
