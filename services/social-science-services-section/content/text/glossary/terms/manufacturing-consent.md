**Manufacturing consent** is Michael Burawoy's thesis, formulated in his ethnographic study of a Chicago machine shop [burawoy1979], that the labor process itself organizes workers' consent to participate in their own exploitation.
The question Braverman's *Labor and Monopoly Capital* [braverman1974] had bracketed — *why do workers, knowing themselves exploited, consent to participate in their own exploitation?* — Burawoy answered through ethnography: not because workers are deceived, and not because workers are coerced, but because the structure of the labor process generates *games* that absorb workers' creative energies into the productive process while obscuring the underlying exploitation.

## The shopfloor games

The Chicago-area plant Burawoy studied — Allied Corporation's "Allis" engine division, the same plant Donald Roy had studied as a participant-observer thirty years earlier under the pseudonym "Geer Company" — was a machine shop producing engine parts under a piece-rate incentive system.
Workers were paid a base rate for hitting an assigned production target; output above target was paid as bonus.
The system seemed designed to elicit work effort through monetary incentive — a textbook capitalist arrangement.

What Burawoy observed: the workers had transformed the system into a game. *Making out* — hitting the bonus rate — became an end in itself, a source of pride, status among peers, and absorbing engagement that occupied the workers' attention through the eight-hour shift.
The game had its own internal rules (which jobs were "good jobs," how to set up the machine, how to negotiate with the inspector), its own forms of skill and craft, its own emotional structure of victory and defeat.
Workers who could make out were respected; those who could not were pitied or scorned.

The game was not management's design.
Management permitted and tacitly encouraged it but did not invent it; the workers had constructed it, generation by generation, in the same way industrial workers everywhere construct workplace cultures.
The *political* effect of the game, however, served capital: workers' creative energies were channeled into hitting the production target rather than into questioning whether the target should be hit at all, why the rate was set where it was, who benefited from their effort, or what alternatives to wage labor might exist.

## How consent is manufactured

Burawoy's argument generalizes from the case to a structural claim.
Three features of the capitalist labor process tend to manufacture consent:

1. **Constituted choice**.
  The labor process presents workers with apparently real choices — which job to bid on, how to set up the work, how hard to push — that absorb attention and confer a sense of agency.
  The choices are real *within* the labor process; the labor process *as a whole* is not chosen.
2. **Coordinated activity that produces visible results**.
  Workers see what they have produced, attribute the production to their own activity, and experience that production as their own achievement.
  The achievement is real; what is invisible is the surplus-value extraction the achievement underwrites.
3. **The internal-state apparatus of the firm**.
  Personnel systems, job classifications, grievance procedures, and seniority rights install a *firm-level citizenship* with its own rights and procedures, which workers experience as governance they have a stake in defending.
  Defending the internal state of the firm is, structurally, defending the firm's continued operation as an extracting apparatus.

The point is not that workers are duped or false-conscious.
Burawoy explicitly rejects false-consciousness explanations.
The argument is that the labor process is structured in ways that *channel real activity* (with real cognitive engagement, real choices, real solidarity) into outcomes that *also* serve capital's accumulation needs.
The game is real and the consent is real; they are the labor process's own products.

## Factory regimes

Burawoy's later [Politics of Production](burawoy1985) generalized the analysis into a comparative framework distinguishing *factory regimes* — the joint configuration of labor process, state intervention, and labor market institutions — across capitalist and state-socialist settings.
His central typology distinguished *despotic* regimes (where workers' livelihood depends entirely on the firm and labor markets are unregulated) from *hegemonic* regimes (where state-provided social wage and labor regulations cushion workers and the firm must therefore manufacture consent rather than coerce).
The Chicago shop was a hegemonic regime; the despotic regimes of nineteenth-century capitalism, contemporary export-processing zones, and post-Soviet privatization-regimes manufactured consent by different means or did not need to manufacture it at all.

## Distinguished from

- **Herman and Chomsky's "manufacturing consent"** (their 1988 *Manufacturing Consent: The Political Economy of the Mass Media*) — same phrase, very different referent.
  Herman and Chomsky's analysis is of how mass-media institutions filter and frame information to produce public consent for elite policy.
  Burawoy's analysis is of how the labor process at the point of production manufactures workplace-level consent.
  The two analyses are compatible — they describe distinct consent-producing apparatuses — but the literatures rarely cross-reference, and confusion between them is common.
- **Hegemony** (Gramsci) — Burawoy is partly indebted to the Gramscian framework but locates hegemony at the point of production rather than primarily in civil society and the cultural sphere. *Hegemonic factory regime* is Burawoy's term for the labor-process-internal version of the Gramscian concept.
- **False consciousness** — Burawoy explicitly rejects the false-consciousness frame.
  Workers in his account are not deceived; they engage in real activities that produce real outcomes.
  The activities have political effects the workers do not endorse, but the activities themselves are not erroneous beliefs.
- **[Contested terrain](contested-terrain.md)** (Edwards [edwards1979]) — Edwards's framework treats the workplace as the site of struggle between capital and labor over control regimes; Burawoy treats the workplace as the site at which consent to capital's organization is reproduced through games.
  The frameworks are complementary — Edwards on the structure of conflict, Burawoy on the structure of compliance — and frequently cited together.
