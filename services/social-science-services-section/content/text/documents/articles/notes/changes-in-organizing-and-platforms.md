From early mass politics through 2020, the core story is that media and organization were once meaningfully distinct, then gradually collapsed into each other as institutions decayed and communications systems became the main survivable “container” for collective life. That collapse trains people—regardless of radical content—into capitalistic political economy: competition for attention, brand differentiation, customer retention, metric optimization, and a permanent scarcity mindset. Burnout is not incidental; it is an endemic output of that training regime.

In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, “political organization” meant durable apparatus first: dues, membership rolls, officers, meeting discipline, strike funds, mutual aid, print shops, and alliances with unions and parties. Media existed, but as a subordinate organ. Newspapers, pamphlets, posters, and speeches did not constitute the organization; they were produced by it, financed by it, and constrained by its priorities. The political economy was materially grounded: the key “metrics” were attendance, dues stability, strike participation, and whether the organization could reliably coordinate action under risk. Burnout existed, but it was moderated by role differentiation and by a clearer boundary between expressive life (culture, art, rhetoric) and binding work (minutes, votes, collections, logistics).

Mid-century mass media (radio and television) shifted the balance. Charisma and broadcast reach became important, and politics increasingly became something that happened through representation. But even then, in most successful formations, broadcast did not replace membership discipline. Parties and unions still anchored meaning. The critical distinction remained: a speech was not a vote; an audience was not a membership; persuasion was not governance. That distinction kept the political economy from becoming purely attention-driven, even when leaders exploited broadcast.

From the 1970s onward, in the United States especially, the long decline of unions, local party machinery, and civic associations hollowed out the institution side of politics. Neoliberal restructuring reduced the places where people learned collective obligation as a normal social form. What grew in its place was professional advocacy (nonprofit and grant ecosystems) and media-centric politics (campaign marketing, punditry, think-tank content). This trained politics toward “outputs” and “messaging” rather than membership reproduction. It also imported capitalist managerial logics into politics: deliverables, branding, donor relations, and the separation of “staff producers” from “supporter consumers.”

Early internet politics accelerated this by making publishing cheap and immediate. The blog era trained a generation into the idea that political intelligence is proven by public articulation—posting, debating, responding—rather than by sustained coordination. Yet blogs still had some friction: writing took time, attention was slower, and communities were smaller. People could still imagine that the “real work” was elsewhere, even if they didn’t have access to it.

Social media removed most remaining friction and collapsed multiple social functions into one interface: publishing, meeting, fundraising, reputation, archive, and conflict arena. That collapse is the decisive training mechanism. When the same gesture—posting—simultaneously produces visibility, status, belonging, and money, it becomes easy to experience posting as politics itself. When the same place—feeds/servers—holds identity, strategy, and validation, it becomes hard to maintain the older distinction between organizing and expression. The medium teaches that what matters is what circulates, and what doesn’t circulate doesn’t exist. This is not about ideology; it is about reinforcement schedules.

Occupy (2011) is a hinge. It proved that networked mobilization plus spectacle can set agendas and create legitimacy crises without durable institutions. It also proved that building durable decision systems in open, high-attention environments is brutally difficult. Many people absorbed the wrong lesson (or the only survivable lesson): organizations are slow, coercive, infiltrated, and conflict-heavy; networks are fast, resilient, and self-renewing. In that inheritance, “the movement” becomes the network itself, and media becomes the way the network perceives its own power. The costs show up as burnout and fragmentation: constant discourse without binding decisions, constant conflict without adjudication, constant urgency without sequencing.

By the late 2010s, “creator economy” infrastructure (Patreon, Substack-like patronage, algorithmic recommendation, clip ecosystems) turned political expression into a livelihood model. This completes the capitalistic training loop. A political collective that relies on attention-based revenue is structurally pushed toward: regular output cadence, audience growth, retention, differentiation from competitors, and conflict as engagement. Even if nobody cynically chooses this, the system rewards it. The network becomes a firm-like entity: it has “customers” (patrons), “product” (episodes/streams/memes), and “brand” (stance/identity). Internal life is pressured to serve the external market.

Burnout becomes predictable here for two reasons. First, attention economies demand constant presence. The work is never finished, because circulation decays quickly. Second, when the collective lacks formal role boundaries, personal identity becomes the resource that is extracted. People burn out not only from labor but from being perpetually “on,” perpetually accountable to the audience’s affect, perpetually negotiating legitimacy in public. In older organizations, roles and procedures absorbed some of that pressure. In media-as-politics networks, the person is the institution.

COVID (2020) makes this ontology nearly inevitable. Physical organizing channels shut down or became risky; mutual aid and political community moved online; “politics” became what could be done through mediated coordination. For many groups that formed or re-formed then, there was no lived experience of an external organizational substrate. Media and chat were not tools; they were the only available space of collective life. That imprinted deeply. Even after restrictions eased, the habit remained: if politics happens anywhere, it happens where the group already is—online—so the medium is treated as the ground.

Decolonial Marxist formations add a further constraint: justified suspicion toward Western institutional forms makes formalization feel like betrayal or regression. That pushes groups to seek “organization” without the mechanisms that make organization distinct from a network: membership boundaries, decision rights, enforcement, exit rules, and bounded scopes. Those functions do not disappear; they relocate into informal power: charisma, founder control of channels, gatekeeping by social capital, and donor leverage. The politics produced can therefore converge with mainstream capitalist patterns even when content is radical: personality-centered authority, competitive signaling, marketplace differentiation, scarcity anxieties, and reputational warfare.

This is the heart of the point: media-as-politics networks train people into a capitalist political economy regardless of content. They reproduce the same politics because their operating logic is not “what we believe,” but “how value is produced and allocated.” Value becomes attention. Allocation becomes algorithms plus patronage. Competition is permanent because attention is scarce. Coordination becomes costly because conflict is engagement but also corrosive. Outcomes skew toward what is legible and repeatable as content, not what is durable or institution-building. Even “anti-capitalist” media networks can end up functioning like small firms competing in a niche market—differentiating brands, defending audiences, and optimizing outputs—because that is the substrate they are built on.

Once you see that history, the contemporary confusion stops being a mystery. A group starting in 2020 often lacks the felt distinction between media network and political organization because the organizational half of political tradition was not transmitted as lived practice; it was replaced by networks and platforms that feel like organization but behave like markets. That mismatch creates burnout and strategic stagnation: constant motion without accumulated capacity, constant talk without binding decisions, constant identity labor without insulation.

What follows from this, for CLN specifically, is not a scolding about “memes.” It is a sober recognition: if you accept the media-network identity, you can work with the substrate and reduce burnout by adding role boundaries and production discipline. If you reject it, you are forced either to abandon most existing assets or to fight the substrate continuously—burning people out faster while reproducing the same attention-market politics you think you’re escaping.
