## 1. Introduction

The politics of COVID-19 has often been narrated as a conflict between public health and personal freedom. That frame is too thin. It assumes that the relevant conflict begins with an already-formed individual deciding whether to accept or reject an external rule. But respiratory infection does not respect the fantasy of separate bodies. The air between people is a material relation before it is a matter of opinion. Every shared room, bus, classroom, workplace, clinic, shelter, protest, kitchen, and concert is already organized by a respiratory politics: who is expected to breathe what, who is expected to adapt, who is expected to stay home, and whose participation is treated as optional.

This paper argues that mask blocs are an important contemporary answer to that politics. A mask bloc is generally a grassroots mutual-aid formation that distributes high-quality respirators, and often tests or other COVID-19 safety tools, for free or at low cost. MaskBloc.org describes mask blocs as independent mutual-aid groups that provide high-quality masks to their communities and may also provide information about COVID-19, clean air, testing, vaccination, and accessibility [@maskbloc2026about]. Its directory listed 234 mask blocs and related community providers when checked for this paper on July 1, 2026 [@maskbloc2026directory]. Reporting by The Sick Times traces many of the earliest groups to late 2022 and early 2023 and emphasizes that many were created or led by disabled people and people with Long COVID [@shoot2024maskblocs].

The argument here is not that mask blocs are merely good because masks are useful. That claim is true, but insufficient. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention states that masks can reduce the spread of respiratory viruses from infected people and can also protect wearers from infectious particles; it further notes that fit and type matter, with N95 respirators among the most protective options [@cdc2025masks]. Aerosol scientists have shown why this matters: SARS-CoV-2 transmission occurs through short- and long-range inhalation of infectious aerosols, and ventilation, filtration, occupancy, time, and respiratory protection are therefore central to risk reduction [@greenhalgh2021ten; @tang2021dismantling; @ashrae2023standard241]. Long COVID research also makes prevention politically serious. CDC notes that anyone infected with SARS-CoV-2 can develop Long COVID and that each infection carries risk [@cdc2026longcovid]. Bowe, Xie, and Al-Aly found in a large Veterans Affairs cohort that reinfection increased risk of death, hospitalization, and sequelae across multiple organ systems compared with no reinfection, with risks persisting into the postacute phase [@bowe2022reinfection]. Davis and colleagues characterize Long COVID as a multisystem condition with insufficient diagnostic and treatment options, requiring stronger research and care infrastructure [@davis2023longcovid].

The more difficult question is how those facts are socially organized. A respirator can be sold as a private commodity, offered as a personal preference, framed as a symbol of anxiety, treated as an accommodation for exceptional bodies, or distributed as shared infrastructure. Mask blocs matter because they move respiratory protection toward the last of these. They do not only place masks on faces. They rework the social relation around shared air.

To analyze that relation, this paper uses Roberto Esposito's paired concepts of *communitas* and *immunitas*. Communitas names community not as shared identity or possession but as shared obligation. Immunitas names exemption from that obligation. The COVID-19 pandemic, and especially the normalization of repeat exposure after the withdrawal of many public protections, can be read as an immunitary privatization of risk: the social problem of shared air is reassigned to individual choice, consumer access, and private coping. Mask blocs are not outside immunity, because respirators are protective technologies. Rather, mask blocs contest immunitary exemption by reorganizing protection as common capacity.

The central thesis is this: mask blocs are communitarian public-health infrastructure. Their importance lies at the intersection of respiratory science, mutual aid, disability justice, disaster response, and political theory. They show that the choice is not between protection and community. The real choice is between protection organized as private exemption and protection organized as shared obligation.

## 2. Communitas and Immunitas

Esposito's political vocabulary begins by unsettling a familiar idea of community. Community is often imagined as a positive possession: a people, an identity, a culture, a property held in common. Esposito instead turns to the Latin *munus*, which carries senses of obligation, duty, service, gift, and burden. In *Communitas*, he argues that community is not defined by what its members own together but by what they owe, by the exposure to others that interrupts the fantasy of self-possession [@esposito2010communitas]. In his essay "Community, Immunity, Biopolitics," Esposito states the contrast sharply: communitas binds through reciprocal obligation, while immunitas unloads or exonerates one from that onus [@esposito2013community].

Immunitas, then, is not simply health or safety. It is exemption from the shared obligation that constitutes community [@esposito2011immunitas]. The term's force comes from its reach across law, biology, and politics. Legal immunity exempts a person or office from ordinary liability. Biological immunity protects an organism by distinguishing self from threat. Political immunity protects the subject by limiting exposure to common demands. Esposito's point is not that all protection is bad. It is that modern political life often preserves the individual by negating the common, and that this negation can become self-destructive when pushed too far.

This distinction helps avoid two common errors in COVID politics. The first error is to treat any demand for respiratory protection as anti-social. In that view, masks are barriers to intimacy, community, or normal life. The second error is to treat any protective tool as automatically emancipatory. In that view, the individual respirator is enough: if vulnerable people can wear one, others have no further responsibility. Both errors miss the social organization of protection.

Respiratory protection can be immunitary in two senses. In the narrow technical sense, a well-fitted respirator filters inhaled air and protects the wearer. That kind of immunity is often necessary. In the political sense, however, respiratory protection becomes immunitary when it is used to exempt others from shared mitigation. "You can wear a mask if you want" can be an access statement, but it can also be a withdrawal of obligation: your protection is your problem, and my behavior remains unchanged. The same physical object can therefore participate in different social relations. The question is not "mask or no mask" in the abstract. The question is whether masks are organized as private shields or as infrastructure for the common.

## 3. COVID-19 and the Privatization of Shared Air

COVID-19 is a respiratory pandemic, but its social meaning has been governed through much more than virology. Foucault's account of biopolitics describes modern power as a power that manages life at the level of populations [@foucault2003society]. Mbembe's necropolitics extends the analysis toward the distribution of death-worlds: social arrangements in which some populations are exposed to premature death or debility as a condition of order [@mbembe2019necropolitics]. Povinelli's account of late liberal abandonment shows how governing systems can withdraw support while narrating endurance as ordinary life [@povinelli2011economies]. Berlant's cruel optimism names attachments to forms of life that promise continuity while undermining flourishing [@berlant2011cruel].

Together, these frameworks describe a pattern visible in post-emergency COVID governance: harm is not always denied outright; it is made administratively and affectively tolerable. Infection becomes "just life." Reinfection becomes expected. Long COVID becomes an unfortunate individual outcome. Clean air becomes a perk or personal project. Masking becomes a preference. People at higher risk are told to assess their own risk, negotiate their own access, and bear the social cost of requesting accommodations.

This is not simply a failure of information. Public-health agencies and engineering bodies acknowledge the relevance of masking, cleaner air, and infectious-aerosol control [@cdc2025masks; @ashrae2023standard241]. Long COVID research has not made reinfection harmless [@bowe2022reinfection; @davis2023longcovid]. The problem is the conversion of shared exposure into private responsibility. When the dominant public script says that everyone can choose their own level of precaution, it obscures the fact that respiratory risk is co-produced. One person's "choice" not to mask in a crowded indoor room is another person's exposure condition.

The word "choice" does considerable ideological work here. It makes social conditions appear individual. It hides the uneven distribution of money, time, knowledge, disability, workplace power, housing density, access to healthcare, access to paid leave, and access to high-quality respirators. It also hides the fact that some people cannot safely participate in shared spaces unless other people change their behavior. The immunitary public is therefore not a public without rules. It is a public governed by an unstated rule: the burden of adaptation falls on those who need protection.

Mask blocs intervene at this point. By making respirators and related knowledge available as common goods, they expose the inadequacy of "personal choice" as a public-health settlement. They show that the problem was never merely whether individuals care enough to buy masks. The problem is whether a community treats respiratory access as part of its own infrastructure.

## 4. Mutual Aid as Crisis Infrastructure

Mutual aid has a long theoretical and political history. Kropotkin used the term to contest Social Darwinist accounts of competition, arguing that cooperation is a major factor in survival across animal and human worlds [@kropotkin1902mutualaid]. Contemporary organizers often distinguish mutual aid from charity: charity preserves hierarchy between giver and recipient, while mutual aid is solidaristic, reciprocal, and oriented toward changing the conditions that produce need [@spade2020mutualaid].

COVID-19 gave this distinction a new practical urgency. Carstensen, Mudhar, and Munksgaard argue that mutual aid, self-help, and spontaneous community measures were vital to early COVID-19 responses globally and that formal organizations should learn to "let communities do their work" [@carstensen2021communities]. Kenworthy, Hops, and Hagopian found that grassroots COVID-19 mutual-aid networks in the United States often aligned stated principles with practice, including solidarity rather than charity, horizontalism, and political education [@kenworthy2023praxis]. Knearem and colleagues studied online mutual-aid groups in the United States and emphasize that COVID-19 required sustained disaster relief, not merely short-term emergency response [@knearem2024solidarity]. Renedo and colleagues show how Gypsy and Traveller communities in England developed community-led protective responses amid institutional mistrust and marginalization [@renedo2023community].

Three findings from this literature matter for mask blocs.

First, mutual aid is not a supplement to formal systems only after those systems temporarily fail. It often exists because formal systems are structurally slow, mistrusted, exclusionary, or absent. In pandemic conditions, state and market infrastructures did not simply leave small gaps; they also produced gaps through unequal access to tests, masks, paid leave, healthcare, isolation space, and trustworthy information.

Second, COVID-19 was a long-haul disaster. The usual disaster timeline of impact, response, recovery, and return does not fit a pandemic that persists through waves, variants, reinfections, chronic illness, and institutional normalization. Mutual aid under these conditions must become durable without becoming bureaucratically rigid, visible without becoming only performative, and responsive without burning out its organizers.

Third, mutual aid is epistemic as well as material. It distributes not only goods but ways of knowing: which masks fit, which tests are available, what local wastewater shows, which venues ventilate well, which clinics are safer, which policies are changing, and which claims are propaganda. In a pandemic shaped by uncertainty and institutional withdrawal, shared knowledge is part of shared survival.

Mask blocs are a concrete form of this pandemic mutual-aid infrastructure. Their distinctive object is respiratory access.

## 5. Disability Justice and the Politics of Access

Mask blocs cannot be understood apart from disability justice. Long COVID, immunocompromise, chronic illness, asthma, cancer treatment, pregnancy, age, and other embodied conditions make COVID risk uneven. But disability justice does not merely ask that high-risk individuals be included after a dominant norm is set. It asks how norms are built, whose bodyminds they assume, and what forms of interdependence they make possible.

Sins Invalid's disability-justice principles emphasize collective access and collective liberation: access is not an individual inconvenience but a shared practice needed for community [@sinsinvalid2020principles]. Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinha's *Care Work* frames disability justice as a politics of sick and disabled queer and trans people of color building care webs, survival practices, and movement infrastructure [@piepzna2018carework]. Mia Mingus's concept of access intimacy names the relief and trust that arise when access needs are understood without constant explanation or justification [@mingus2011access].

The COVID relevance is direct. A world of individualized COVID precaution destroys access intimacy. It requires vulnerable people to repeatedly explain, justify, negotiate, and enforce their need for safer air. It turns every gathering into a new access dispute. It makes participation depend on a person's energy to ask, the group's willingness to listen, and the social cost of being seen as difficult.

Mask blocs lower that cost. They make access more routable. A group planning a meeting can request respirators. A household can obtain masks without mastering procurement channels. A small venue can learn how to support safer attendance. A protest or zine event can treat masking not as an embarrassing special request but as part of logistics. A person who cannot safely enter unmasked spaces can point to an existing infrastructure rather than appearing as a solitary exception.

This is why mask blocs should not be reduced to supply distribution. They are access infrastructure. They make disability justice more materially available by converting a recurring individual negotiation into a reusable collective practice.

## 6. What Mask Blocs Do

Mask blocs vary by locality, resources, risk tolerance, politics, and capacity. Some primarily distribute respirators through request forms. Some table at events. Some provide rapid tests, information, clean-air resources, fit guidance, or connections to COVID-cautious social networks. Some are disabled-led, queer-led, abolitionist, anti-racist, or explicitly mutual-aid oriented. Some operate quietly because of stigma, harassment, or legal concerns. The category is therefore not a single organization but a distributed practice.

Three publicly visible sources help establish the pattern. MaskBloc.org describes mask blocs as independent mutual-aid groups that provide free high-quality masks and may also provide tools and information related to COVID, clean air, testing, vaccination, and accessibility [@maskbloc2026about]. Its directory lists hundreds of groups and community providers across multiple regions [@maskbloc2026directory]. The Sick Times oral history describes mask blocs as grassroots mutual-aid groups distributing high-quality masks such as N95s in response to public-health failures; it also notes that many organizers are disabled and that some have Long COVID [@shoot2024maskblocs]. The collaboratively produced "How to Start a Mask Bloc" zine frames bloc-building as a practical mutual-aid problem: how to start, center marginalized people, help people effectively, pool resources, and share knowledge across local experience [@maskuppgh2024zine].

These activities create at least five kinds of infrastructure.

First, procurement infrastructure. High-quality respirators are often cheaper in bulk but unaffordable or confusing for individuals. Mask blocs aggregate purchasing, donations, storage, and distribution.

Second, access infrastructure. Respirators become available at the point of need: households, events, protests, schools, workplaces, and community spaces.

Third, knowledge infrastructure. Mask blocs circulate information about fit, respirator types, reuse, tests, ventilation, and local COVID conditions.

Fourth, relational infrastructure. Request forms, delivery routes, tabling, and event support create channels through which COVID-cautious people can find each other and through which broader communities can be asked to change practice.

Fifth, political infrastructure. Mask blocs keep public attention on a crisis many institutions have attempted to normalize. They make ongoing pandemic risk visible without waiting for state declaration.

These infrastructures are small relative to the scale of the pandemic. But their smallness is part of their significance. They show how public health can be practiced below the level of the state without accepting the neoliberal premise that everyone is on their own.

## 7. Mask Blocs Against Immunitary Privatization

The respirator is an ambiguous object. It protects a wearer by filtering air, and in that sense it participates in immunity. But the same respirator can be organized as a private commodity or a common resource. This ambiguity is where Esposito's distinction becomes useful.

In an immunitary public, the N95 becomes evidence that no shared obligation remains. If a vulnerable person can wear a respirator, then unmasked social life can proceed. The respirator becomes a technology of exemption for others: it lets the majority preserve normalcy while assigning the work of protection to the minority. This is not because the respirator fails technically. It is because the social relation around it is privatized.

In a communitarian practice, the respirator becomes part of the munus: a material way of carrying obligation to one another. A mask requirement at an event, free respirators at the door, portable air filters, outdoor options, remote access, and a culture in which illness is not minimized all say the same thing: your breath and mine are not politically separable. This does not eliminate risk. It refuses to displace all risk onto those least able to bear it.

Mask blocs push respirators toward this communitarian function. They do so by changing the practical default. Instead of "bring your own mask if you want one," they make masks available. Instead of "do your own research," they circulate situated knowledge. Instead of "ask the organizer and hope," they create event-support practices. Instead of "public health is over," they maintain public-health capacity at community scale.

This helps explain why mask blocs can feel politically disproportionate to their material size. A box of N95s is not just a box of N95s when the dominant public settlement depends on acting as though shared air no longer matters. The box reopens a settled question. It asks why the event was not already prepared. It asks why respiratory access was treated as optional. It asks who was silently excluded. It asks why the community's care language did not become infrastructure.

## 8. Masking, Criminalization, and the Return of Visibility

Mask blocs also operate in a legal and political environment where masking is increasingly contested. The International Center for Not-for-Profit Law reported in a September 2025 update that 23 states and Washington, D.C. had laws limiting face coverings in public spaces and warned that such laws can raise First Amendment concerns, chill expressive activity, and be used against people wearing masks for health, religious, privacy, symbolic, or other lawful purposes [@icnl2025antimask]. The Sick Times oral history notes that some mask bloc participants used anonymity because of personal and political concerns, including criminalization of mutual aid and spreading mask bans [@shoot2024maskblocs].

This legal context matters conceptually. A society that first privatizes respiratory protection and then criminalizes or stigmatizes public masking produces a double bind. Vulnerable people are told to protect themselves, but visible protection can mark them for exclusion, harassment, surveillance, or policing. The immunitary public permits protection only when it remains private, quiet, and non-disruptive. It resists protection when protection becomes collective, visible, or linked to protest.

Mask blocs therefore sit at the intersection of public health and civil liberties. They are not only distributing medical supplies. They are defending the public legitimacy of appearing masked. They make visible a form of care that normalization pressures people to hide.

This visibility is politically important because public health is not only a set of recommendations; it is a struggle over what a public can perceive. If masks disappear from shared spaces, many people infer that COVID risk has disappeared. If masked people remain visible, especially in organized forms, they interrupt that inference. Mask blocs help keep the reality of ongoing respiratory risk socially perceptible.

## 9. Limits and Failure Modes

A serious account of mask blocs must also name their limits.

First, mask blocs can be overburdened. Mutual-aid infrastructures often absorb demand created by state and market abandonment without receiving stable resources. The more institutions withdraw, the more grassroots groups are asked to compensate. This can produce burnout, scarcity, conflict, and quiet collapse.

Second, mask blocs can be captured by platform dynamics. Public request forms, donation campaigns, social media posts, and visibility campaigns may be necessary. They can also shift attention from embedded relation to performance metrics: followers, shares, public gratitude, crisis branding, and proof of political alignment. A mask bloc is strongest when platforms remain tools for distribution and coordination, not substitutes for local accountability.

Third, mask blocs can reproduce exclusions. A group may distribute respirators while failing to address language access, rural access, racialized policing, disability beyond COVID risk, housing instability, digital access, or the needs of people who cannot easily use standard respirators. Communitarian infrastructure requires continual revision.

Fourth, mask blocs cannot replace structural public health. Community distribution cannot substitute for universal paid sick leave, high indoor-air standards, free testing, accessible healthcare, workplace protections, school ventilation, wastewater surveillance, or robust Long COVID care. Mutual aid can expose and mitigate abandonment, but it should not become the excuse for abandonment.

Fifth, respirators alone are not enough. Aerosol risk is shaped by source control, filtration, ventilation, time, crowding, activity, and local prevalence [@greenhalgh2021ten; @tang2021dismantling; @ashrae2023standard241]. Mask blocs are most powerful when linked to clean-air work, event design, sick policies, remote access, and broader disability justice.

These limits do not weaken the argument. They clarify it. Mask blocs are not a total solution to COVID-19. They are a strategically important form of community public health because they reveal where the dominant system has privatized a shared problem.

## 10. Research Agenda

Mask blocs deserve sustained study. A graduate-level research program should ask at least six questions.

First, organizational form: How do mask blocs govern inventory, donations, request prioritization, conflict, safety, and burnout? What forms of horizontalism work under long-haul disaster conditions, and where do informal hierarchies appear?

Second, access outcomes: Who receives masks, tests, and clean-air support? Which populations remain unreached? How do race, disability, class, rurality, language, housing, and digital access shape distribution?

Third, event transformation: Do mask bloc interventions change event norms, or do they merely provide optional supplies? Under what conditions do respirator distribution, mask requirements, air filtration, and remote access become durable practice?

Fourth, knowledge circulation: How do mask blocs translate aerosol science, public-health guidance, and local risk data into usable community knowledge? How do they handle uncertainty without reproducing misinformation or expert gatekeeping?

Fifth, political conflict: How do mask bans, policing, harassment, and surveillance affect mask bloc strategy? How do blocs navigate the overlap between health masking, protest masking, anonymity, and criminalization?

Sixth, mutual-aid theory: When do mask blocs function as solidarity rather than charity? What practices keep them accountable to disabled, immunocompromised, low-income, racialized, and otherwise highly exposed people? What practices convert them into branding or service provision detached from movement?

These are empirical questions, but they are also theoretical questions. They ask how communitas is built, how immunitary exemption is resisted, and how public health can be reconstructed at community scale without romanticizing abandonment.

## 11. Conclusion: Breath as a Common Obligation

COVID-19 normalization has taught many publics to experience shared air as private fate. That lesson is immunitary. It preserves the appearance of normal life by exempting people from the obligations that shared life requires. It allows communities to speak of care while making high-risk people negotiate access one room at a time. It allows institutions to withdraw protections while presenting precaution as personal preference. It allows respiratory risk to become background noise.

Mask blocs contest that settlement. They do not do so by rejecting protection, but by reorganizing protection. They take an object that could become a private shield and make it circulate as common capacity. They turn respirators into mutual-aid infrastructure, masking into access practice, and shared air into a site of political obligation.

This is the central theoretical point: communitas is not the absence of boundaries, filters, or precautions. Community is not proven by unprotected exposure. Under pandemic conditions, shared obligation may require barriers, filtration, distance, remote access, and refusal of certain forms of normalcy. The problem is not immunity itself. The problem is immunity as exemption from others.

Mask blocs matter because they show another route. They make it possible to say: protection is not merely mine, and exposure is not merely yours. The air between us is not a private matter. Breath is a common obligation.
