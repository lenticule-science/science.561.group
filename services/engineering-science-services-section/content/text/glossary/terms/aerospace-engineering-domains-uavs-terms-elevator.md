A control surface on the horizontal tail, deflected to pitch the aircraft nose-up or nose-down.

An elevator is a hinged [control surface](../../../../services/aerospace-engineering-science-services-section/services/uavs-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/control-surface.md) on the horizontal stabilizer (tail). Deflecting the elevator trailing-edge-up produces a downward force on the tail, pitching the aircraft nose-up and increasing [angle of attack](../../../../services/aerospace-engineering-science-services-section/services/uavs-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/angle-of-attack.md). Deflecting trailing-edge-down does the opposite.

The elevator&rsquo;s effectiveness depends on the **tail volume coefficient** — the product of tail area and moment arm (distance from wing to tail) divided by wing area and mean [chord](../../../../services/aerospace-engineering-science-services-section/services/uavs-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/chord.md). A longer fuselage with a smaller tail produces the same pitching authority as a shorter fuselage with a larger tail, but with different dynamic characteristics: the longer-arm configuration responds more sluggishly but damps pitch oscillations better, while the shorter-arm version is more agile but less stable.

### Trim and elevator deflection

In steady flight, the elevator maintains [trim](../../../../services/aerospace-engineering-science-services-section/services/uavs-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/trim.md) — the condition where pitching moments sum to zero. The [center of gravity](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/applied-sciences/technology/aerospace-engineering/archive/center-of-gravity.md) position determines how much elevator deflection is needed for trim: a forward CG requires more up-elevator (trailing-edge-up) to balance the nose-heavy moment, which generates a downward force on the tail. This &ldquo;trim drag&rdquo; — the [drag](../../../../services/aerospace-engineering-science-services-section/services/uavs-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/drag.md) from the tail producing a net downforce instead of zero or positive lift — is one reason that CG position matters for cruise efficiency.

On conventional fixed-wing UAVs, the elevator is driven by a single [servo](../../../../services/aerospace-engineering-science-services-section/services/uavs-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/servo.md). On delta wings and flying wings that lack a horizontal tail, the elevator function is handled by [elevons](../../../../services/aerospace-engineering-science-services-section/services/uavs-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/elevon.md) on the trailing edge of the wing.

Some UAVs use a **full-flying tail** (stabilator) — the entire horizontal surface rotates rather than having a separate hinged elevator. This provides more powerful pitch control per unit area but requires more precise [center of gravity](../../documents/articles/research/2026/07/31/lenticule-import/library/applied-sciences/technology/aerospace-engineering/archive/center-of-gravity.md) management because the surface is more sensitive to deflection.
