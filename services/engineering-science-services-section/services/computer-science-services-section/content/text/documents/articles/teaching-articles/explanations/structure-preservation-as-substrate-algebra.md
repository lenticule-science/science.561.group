# Structure preservation as substrate algebra

The disciplinary literature on refactoring — Opdyke's 1992 dissertation, Fowler's 1999 catalog, Mens & Tourwé's 2004 survey — converges on a single substantive claim: that there is a meaningful equivalence relation on codebases distinguishing "same behavior, different structure" from "different behavior." The literature gives the relation a name (refactoring), enumerates its generators (the catalog), and develops a discipline for composing them safely. What it has historically lacked is a *substrate-algebraic* reading: the formal apparatus that makes the equivalence relation an object one can reason about with the tools of category theory and bilattice cohomology.

The substrate-algebraic reading turns out to be available — for free, as it happens — in the predication-categories framework. The equivalence relation is the action of the [invisible-automorphism subgroup](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/logic-services-section/content/text/glossary/concepts/invisible-automorphism-subgroup.md) on a codebase's [predication settling](../../../../../humanities/theories/math/theories/categories/theories/predication-categories/objects/predication-settling.md), parameterized by a witness language. The catalog is a generating set for this subgroup. The disciplinary results — closure under composition, reversibility, the safety of private renames, the substantiveness of public-API renames — are corollaries of the subgroup structure and the [visible-automorphism-sources](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/logic-services-section/content/text/glossary/concepts/visible-automorphism-sources.md) theorem.

The convergence is not coincidental. Refactoring as a discipline emerged from the empirical observation that, in real codebases, *some* transformations leave behavior unchanged and *some* don't, and the boundary is structurally regular enough to merit a catalog. The substrate-algebraic apparatus identifies *why* the boundary is regular: because the codebase's settling has a well-defined automorphism group, that group has a normal subgroup acting trivially on the witness sheaf, and the disciplinary catalog is a natural generating set for that subgroup. Different witness languages produce different subgroups; the disciplinary catalog generalizes well across languages because the kinds of automorphisms it names — entity-permutations on identifiers, predicate-extensions for new function signatures — are *substrate-level* features that each witness language's sheaf either does or does not see.

## A surprising consequence

The math identifies a duality that was not visible in the disciplinary literature: refactoring is the *same kind of substrate operation* as cosmetic organizational restructuring (renaming an office, anonymizing inactive contributors, rotating personae). Both are elements of an invisible-automorphism subgroup; both are characterized by failing to act on a settling-associated sheaf. The work theorem [restructure-as-visible-automorphism](../../../../../../../../../philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/content/text/glossary/concepts/restructure-as-visible-automorphism.md) and the refactoring theorem [refactor-as-invisible-automorphism](../../../../glossary/concepts/refactor-as-invisible-automorphism.md) are *the same theorem* on different cell-systems.

This is the kind of thing the substrate is for. Two literatures that developed independently — Fowler-style refactoring, organizational HR practice — turn out to be talking about specializations of one mathematical object. Recognizing the duality lets each literature's lessons inform the other: rotating a development team's responsibilities is a refactoring of the team-cell-system; renaming an internal package is an organizational restructuring of the codebase's identifier-namespace.

## What remains open

The classification is parameterized by witness language, and choices of witness language are *underdetermined* by the substrate. The math tells us that test-only, public-API-aware, and stack-trace-aware witness languages produce different invisible subgroups; it does not tell us which to honor. Project communities settle this by convention — semantic-versioning is, structurally, a public commitment to a particular witness language for refactor-vs-breaking-change classification. The substrate-algebraic apparatus turns the question "which witness language?" into a tractable design question with quantifiable consequences (size of the invisible subgroup, which catalog generators remain in it, which transformations require coordination), but does not answer it.

What the apparatus *does* deliver: a way to talk about refactoring that is precise enough to be checked by tooling, general enough to apply to substrates other than source code, and sturdy enough to survive the introduction of agent-driven development as a first-class case. Refactoring is not an art-form on this reading; it is the practice of producing automorphisms in a particular subgroup, which is a thing the substrate already knows how to characterize.
