## klensin2008 — RFC 5321, Simple Mail Transfer Protocol

Author John C. Klensin; published October 2008 by the IETF; obsoletes
RFC 2821. Defines the SMTP protocol: the mail transaction, the
envelope (`MAIL FROM`, `RCPT TO`), and the store-and-forward relay
model by which a message moves from a sender's mail submission agent
through zero or more relaying mail transfer agents to a recipient's
mail delivery agent. SMTP places no restriction on what a sending host
supplies as the envelope sender or the `HELO`/`EHLO` domain — the fact
that makes SPF, DKIM, and DMARC (below) necessary rather than
redundant.

## resnickpartredge2008 — RFC 5322, Internet Message Format

Edited by Pete Resnick; published October 2008 by the IETF; obsoletes
RFC 2822. Defines the syntax of the message itself, distinct from
SMTP's envelope: header fields (`From`, `To`, `Subject`, `Date`,
`Message-ID`) and a body, with folding, encoding, and addressing
grammar. The header `From` an end user reads is a message-format field
this RFC governs and is independent of the SMTP envelope sender
klensin2008 governs — the split SPF checks the envelope and DMARC
aligns the two.

## crocker2009 — RFC 5598, Internet Mail Architecture

Author Dave Crocker; published July 2009 by the IETF. Not a protocol
specification — defines no wire format or command — but a reference
architecture: it names the functional roles (Author, Mediator,
Recipient), the trust boundary each Administrative Management Domain
(ADMD) draws around the mail service it operates, and the
distinction between the message-handling service (transfer) and the
message-store service (retrieval). The ADMD concept is the term this
section's `electronic-mail` primitive uses to state why a mail
collective's own administrative boundary is where deliverability and
abuse-handling obligations attach.

## huitema2000 — RFC 2369, list-command header fields

Published 1998 by the IETF (title: "The Use of URLs as Meta-Syntax for
Core Mail List Commands and their Transport through Message Header
Fields"). Defines the `List-Help`, `List-Subscribe`,
`List-Unsubscribe`, `List-Post`, `List-Owner`, and `List-Archive`
header fields, each carrying one or more URLs (commonly `mailto:`)
that a mail client can present as an automated action, replacing the
older convention of a human parsing free-text footer instructions.

## chandhok2000 — RFC 2919, List-Id

Authors Ronald Chandhok and Grant Wegrich; published March 2001 by the
IETF. Defines the `List-Id` header field: a single structured field
identifying which mailing list a message belongs to, independent of
the (frequently reused or reassigned) `List-Post` address, so a mail
client can filter or thread by list identity even when the list's
posting address changes.

## kitterman2014 — RFC 7208, Sender Policy Framework (SPF)

Author Scott Kitterman; published April 2014 by the IETF; obsoletes
RFC 4408. Defines SPF: an Administrative Management Domain publishes,
in DNS, the set of hosts authorized to use its domain name in the
SMTP envelope (`MAIL FROM`/`HELO`); a receiving host queries that DNS
record and can reject or flag a message whose sending host is not
listed. SPF authenticates the envelope sender's domain, not the
message's `From:` header, and breaks under naive forwarding because
the forwarding host, not the original sender, becomes the envelope
sender the receiving host checks.

## crockerhansenkucherawy2011 — RFC 6376, DKIM Signatures

Authors D. Crocker, T. Hansen, M. Kucherawy; published September 2011
by the IETF; obsoletes RFC 4871. Defines DKIM: the sending domain
signs selected header fields and the body with a private key; the
public key is published in DNS under a selector-scoped name; the
receiving host retrieves the key and verifies the signature. DKIM
authenticates that a specific domain accepted responsibility for the
signed content and that the signed content was not altered in
transit; it makes no claim about whether that domain's outbound policy
should be trusted, which is DMARC's question.

## kucherawyzwicky2015 — RFC 7489, DMARC

Edited by M. Kucherawy and E. Zwicky; published March 2015 by the IETF
as an Informational document. Defines DMARC: a domain owner publishes
a DNS policy record stating what a receiver should do (`none`,
`quarantine`, `reject`) with a message that fails SPF or DKIM
alignment with the visible `From:` domain, and where to send
aggregate and forensic failure reports. DMARC is the mechanism that
ties SPF's envelope check and DKIM's signature check to the header a
human actually reads, and is the policy layer a receiving mail
provider consults when deciding whether an unfamiliar sending domain's
mail is deliverable at all.

## danezisdiaz2008 — Danezis & Diaz, A Survey of Anonymous Communication Channels

Authors George Danezis and Claudia Diaz; Microsoft Research Technical
Report MSR-TR-2008-35, published February 2008. Surveys the field from
Chaum's 1981 mix-net proposal onward, organized by underlying
principle: semi-trusted relays, mix systems, remailers, onion routing,
and robust-mixing systems. States and compares the threat and usage
models each design family provides and the security/performance
trade-off each accepts; a recurring finding of the surveyed field is
that content encryption alone does not prevent an observer positioned
on the network from learning who communicates with whom, how often,
and when, from unencrypted routing and timing metadata — the
traffic-analysis problem end-to-end message encryption does not by
itself solve.

## koch2024 — RFC 9580, OpenPGP

Published by the IETF OpenPGP working group, July 2024; obsoletes RFC
4880 (OpenPGP Message Format), RFC 5581, and RFC 6637. Specifies
OpenPGP version 6 message, key, and signature formats: public-key and
symmetric encryption, digital signatures, and key management, with a
required algorithm suite of X25519, Ed25519, SHA2-256, and AES-128
(X448, Ed448, SHA2-384, SHA2-512, and AES-256 recommended). OpenPGP
keys and messages are handled explicitly by the sender and receiver
(the user manages key storage, trust, and message encryption as
discrete acts), which the excerpt below (perrinmarlinspike2016)
contrasts with a session-based ratchet that renegotiates keys per
message without user action.

## perrinmarlinspike2016 — Perrin & Marlinspike, The Double Ratchet Algorithm

Editor Trevor Perrin; co-author Moxie Marlinspike; published by Signal,
November 2016. Specifies the Double Ratchet: given an initial shared
secret (typically from a key-agreement protocol such as X3DH/PQXDH),
each message sent triggers a Diffie-Hellman ratchet step and each
message received or sent advances a symmetric-key ratchet, so that
every message is encrypted under its own derived key. The design goal
stated in the specification is forward secrecy (compromise of a
current key does not expose past messages) and post-compromise
security (the ratchet recovers security after a future key
compromise, once fresh randomness is mixed back in) — properties a
single static key exchange, including a static OpenPGP key pair, does
not provide on its own.

## saintandre2011 — RFC 6120, XMPP Core

Author P. Saint-Andre; published March 2011 by the IETF; obsoletes RFC
3920. Defines XMPP's core: XML-stream setup and teardown, TLS channel
encryption, SASL authentication, error handling, and the `<message/>`,
`<presence/>`, and `<iq/>` communication primitives exchanged between
two or more network entities identified by Jabber IDs. XMPP servers
federate directly, server to server, over the same core stream
protocol used between a client and its home server.

## matrixorg2026 — Matrix Specification, Server-Server API

Published and maintained by the Matrix.org Foundation; specification
accessed 2026-09-08 at spec.matrix.org (latest, and version 1.6).
States that Matrix homeservers use the federation (server-server) API
to push events between servers that share a room, over HTTPS,
authenticated by public-key signatures at both the TLS transport layer
and in HTTP Authorization headers; a room's state is a directed
acyclic graph of signed events resolved by a deterministic
state-resolution algorithm run independently by every participating
server, rather than a single server holding the authoritative history.

## dingledinemathewsonsyverson2004 — Tor: The Second-Generation Onion Router

Authors Roger Dingledine, Nick Mathewson, Paul Syverson; 13th USENIX
Security Symposium, August 2004. Presents Tor, a circuit-based
low-latency anonymous-communication overlay: a client builds a
multi-hop circuit through volunteer-run relays, each hop able to
decrypt only one layer of onion-wrapped routing information and
learning only the previous and next hop, never the full path. The
paper's design for location-hidden services (the mechanism now called
an onion service) uses rendezvous points so that neither the client
nor an observer learns the server's network location, at the cost of
added latency from the extra circuit hops the rendezvous requires.

## donenfeld2017 — WireGuard: Next Generation Kernel Network Tunnel

Author Jason A. Donenfeld; Network and Distributed System Security
Symposium (NDSS), February 2017. Presents WireGuard: a layer-3 secure
tunnel binding each peer's public key directly to a tunnel source
address (no certificate authority or negotiated cipher suite), using a
single-round-trip Noise IK handshake, Curve25519 key agreement, and
ChaCha20-Poly1305 authenticated encryption, implemented in under 4,000
lines of kernel code. WireGuard establishes a private, authenticated
point-to-point (or hub-and-spoke) network path between known peers; it
states no anonymity property and provides none — every packet's
destination is exactly the configured peer, which the paper
distinguishes explicitly from an onion-routing overlay's goal of
hiding who is talking to whom.

## kuroseross2020 — Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach

Authors James F. Kurose and Keith W. Ross; Pearson, 8th edition, 2020.
The standard undergraduate networking text's application-layer chapter
covers the client-server architecture of the Web: an HTTP server holds
content addressed by URL and a browser issues a request per object; a
static object is served unchanged from storage while a dynamic
response is generated per request by server-side code, and the text's
treatment of web caching and content distribution networks (also
covered in this section's `content-delivery-network` term) states the
operational reason for interposing a cache or edge network between
client and origin: reducing response latency and origin load by
serving repeated requests from a location topologically closer to the
requester.

## nygrensitaramansun2010 — The Akamai Network

Authors Erik Nygren, Ramesh K. Sitaraman, Jennifer Sun; ACM SIGOPS
Operating Systems Review 44(3), August 2010. Describes the Akamai
platform's architecture as of 2010: tens of thousands of servers
across roughly a thousand networks and seventy countries, using DNS-
based mapping to direct a client's request to a nearby edge server,
which serves cached static content directly and can also accelerate
dynamic content and application delivery through Akamai's overlay
routing between edge and origin. States the platform's own claim that
a highly distributed edge architecture, not a small number of large
data centers, is what a workload with global reach and low-latency
requirements needs.

## cloudflare2026 — Cloudflare, How Cloudflare's CDN Works

Cloudflare, Inc.; Cloudflare Developer Docs, accessed 2026-09-08.
First-party documentation stating Cloudflare's CDN operates as a
reverse proxy in front of an origin server: DNS for a hosted domain
resolves to Cloudflare's edge network rather than the origin directly;
an edge node serves a cached response when one exists and otherwise
fetches from origin; static content (images, CSS, JavaScript) is
cached by default and dynamic content (HTML) is not cached unless a
Cache Rule is configured. Documents that adopting a CDN in this
architecture routes the domain's DNS, and therefore its request
traffic, through the CDN operator's infrastructure rather than only
alongside it.

## icann2012 — ICANN, Guidance for Preparing Domain Name Orders, Seizures & Takedowns

ICANN, March 2012. States the technical mechanism by which a domain
name is made to stop resolving in response to a legal or regulatory
order: the registry operator (or, where the order reaches only the
registrar, the registrar) applies an `EPP` (Extensible Provisioning
Protocol) status such as `serverHold` to the domain object, which
removes the domain from the top-level-domain zone file so that DNS
resolution for the name fails everywhere on the public Internet,
without deleting the registration record itself. States that ICANN's
contracts with registries and registrars are the source of ICANN's
practical leverage over how a seizure order is carried out, and that a
registrar must maintain a dedicated abuse point of contact and review
a law-enforcement abuse report promptly.

## kopel2013 — Kopel, Operation Seizing Our Sites

Author Karen Kopel; Berkeley Technology Law Journal 28(3), 2013
(article "Operation Seizing Our Sites: How the Federal Government Is
Taking Domain Names without Prior Notice"). Analyzes Operation In Our
Sites, the US federal ex parte in rem civil forfeiture practice used
to seize domain names (over 1,700 seized, over 690 forfeited, by the
time of writing) without prior notice to the registrant, and the due
process and prior-restraint objections raised against it, including
the seizure of rojadirecta.com/.org — a domain whose operator had
already prevailed against the same copyright claim in a Spanish
court — as a case where the US seizure reached a domain a foreign
court had cleared.

## klyneloughneynottingham2012 — RFC 6650, Applicability Statement for ARF

Edited by J. Falk and M. Kucherawy; published June 2012 by the IETF as
a Standards Track document. An applicability statement over the
Abuse Reporting Format (below): describes how mailbox providers,
sending entities, and end users use ARF-formatted feedback loops in
practice — most commonly a private bilateral arrangement between two
mail systems in which a receiving system forwards a copy of a
recipient's "this is spam" report back to the sending system in
machine-readable form, letting the sender's own abuse desk act on
signal it would not otherwise see.

## shaferallmancunningham2010 — RFC 5965, An Extensible Format for Email Feedback Reports

Authors Y. Shafranovich, J. Levine, M. Kucherawy; published August
2010 by the IETF. Defines the Abuse Reporting Format (ARF): a
MIME-multipart machine-readable structure for a mail-handling entity
to report feedback (most often, a spam complaint) about a received
message to another mail-handling entity — the original message or a
redacted portion, machine-parseable fields naming the feedback type
and the reported entity, and free-text commentary, in one deliverable
report object.
