Scrum is a framework for developing and sustaining complex products,
created by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland. The two first presented
Scrum at the OOPSLA conference in Austin, Texas, in 1995. Their
approach drew on Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka's 1986 *Harvard
Business Review* article "The New New Product Development Game,"
which described cross-functional teams working in overlapping phases
rather than sequential handoffs. The definitive specification is
*The Scrum Guide* (Schwaber and Sutherland, 2020), now in its sixth
revision.

## The Sprint as planning unit

Scrum's planning structure centers on the Sprint, a fixed-length
iteration of one month or less (commonly two weeks). Each Sprint
contains five events that form a recurring planning cycle:

**Sprint Planning** opens each Sprint. The Scrum Team selects items
from the Product Backlog, defines a Sprint Goal that explains why
the Sprint is valuable, and creates a plan for delivering the
selected work. Sprint Planning is timeboxed to eight hours for a
one-month Sprint.

**Daily Scrum** is a 15-minute event held each working day. The
Developers inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the
Sprint Backlog as needed. It is a planning event, not a status
report --- the outcome is an adjusted plan for the next day of work.

**Sprint Review** occurs near the end of the Sprint. The Scrum Team
presents completed work to stakeholders, inspects the outcome, and
discusses what to do next. The Product Backlog may be adjusted based
on this discussion.

**Sprint Retrospective** closes the Sprint. The Scrum Team examines
its own processes, interactions, and tools, then identifies
improvements to carry into the next Sprint. The Retrospective is
timeboxed to three hours for a one-month Sprint.

**Product Backlog Refinement** is an ongoing activity (not a formal
event in the 2020 Scrum Guide) in which the Scrum Team breaks down
and clarifies Product Backlog items to prepare them for future Sprint
Planning.

## Core planning mechanism

Scrum's planning structure creates a feedback loop at Sprint
boundaries. Each Sprint begins with a forward-looking planning event
(Sprint Planning), runs a daily re-planning cycle (Daily Scrum),
and ends with two retrospective events --- one focused on the product
(Sprint Review) and one focused on the process (Sprint Retrospective).

The mechanism works through three artifacts that carry planning state
across Sprints: the **Product Backlog** (an ordered list of
everything that might be needed in the product), the **Sprint
Backlog** (the items selected for the current Sprint plus a plan for
delivering them), and the **Increment** (the sum of completed Product
Backlog items, meeting the Definition of Done).

The Sprint cycle enforces bounded iteration. Work not completed
within a Sprint returns to the Product Backlog for re-prioritization;
Sprints don't extend. This constraint drives scope management and
creates regular inspection points.
