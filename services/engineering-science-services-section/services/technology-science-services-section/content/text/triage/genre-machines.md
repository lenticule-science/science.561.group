# **Genre Machines: Large Language Models and the Semiotic Infrastructure of Ontogenric Worlding**

## Abstract

This paper theorizes large language models (LLMs) as computational agents of **ontogenric worlding**—the recursive stabilization of meaning through probabilistic genre formation. Drawing from narrative theory, genre studies, semiotics, and AI ethics, I argue that LLMs do not merely generate text; they instantiate a system of anticipatory formalization that reproduces genre as a control logic. In doing so, LLMs become instrumental to a broader epistemo-political project: the maintenance of coherence through the mechanization of genre as both a linguistic and ontological constraint. This dynamic exemplifies a central mechanism of contemporary governance: the formatting of unpredictability into knowable futures, and the naturalization of that formatting as "language."

---

## I. Genre as Formal World Constraint

Genre is not simply a literary category. It is a formal infrastructure that organizes meaning through patterned expectations, boundary conditions, and narrative teleologies. As theorists such as Carolyn Miller, Fredric Jameson, and Tzvetan Todorov have argued, genre is a **social and semiotic contract**: a system that constrains not just what can be said, but what can be _understood_.

Within LLMs, genre becomes computational. The model generates text not based on truth-claims or world-mapping accuracy, but on the **statistical plausibility of form**—what _sounds right_ given what has come before. This plausibility is formalized through the architecture of next-token prediction, in which each output is a resolution of an internal probability distribution over possible linguistic continuations.

This is not mere mimicry. It is genre enactment as worldmaking: a mode of semiotic closure in which coherence becomes synonymous with computability.

---

## II. The Probabilistic Grammar of Anticipation

At the core of transformer-based LLMs lies **Bayesian inference**, operationalized as probabilistic conditioning over linguistic sequences. This structure can be understood as a **grammar of anticipation**, in which every new utterance is recursively determined by prior form.

This anticipation mirrors the work of narrative in classical literary theory: the generation of readerly expectation, the constraining of interpretive possibility, and the naturalization of sequence. As Hayden White and Paul Ricoeur have observed, narrative is not just a mode of storytelling but a **technology of history**, transforming incoherent events into causal, ordered, and legible wholes.

LLMs perform the same operation. The prompt becomes a **narrative seed**—a precondition for semantic development—and the model’s task is to complete the arc. It is not seeking to represent reality; it is seeking to complete form.

This transforms narrative from a cultural practice into a **computational imperative**: the model must resolve tension, must restore pattern, must return to genre.

---

## III. LLMs as Semiotic Infrastructure

Semiotically, LLMs function as both syntagmatic and paradigmatic systems. They sequence words (syntagm) and select from meaning-encoded classes (paradigm), guided by probabilistic gradients. But unlike traditional semiotic systems, LLMs **re-inscribe the sign through training feedback**, modifying the symbolic system in real-time based on interaction.

What results is not a stable code but a **dynamic genre constraint engine**—a machinic apparatus for the recursive reinforcement of interpretive limits. As signs repeat, so do meanings. As outputs stabilize, so does the semiotic space in which meaning becomes possible.

From this perspective, LLMs are not agents of novelty. They are **machines of normalization**: systems that continuously re-train themselves to better adhere to the formal structures of coherence that have already been modeled into them.

This has significant implications for discourse, politics, and pedagogy. The LLM does not speak “with” us. It **formats us**, rendering our prompts legible only insofar as they can be preconditioned into genre.

---

## IV. Ontogenric Kyriarchy and Narrative Constraint

The recursive stabilization of genre described above is not a neutral function. It is a core mechanism of what I call **ontogenric kyriarchism**: a system of governance in which life is made intelligible through formatting—where difference is not encountered but processed, where rupture is not explored but resolved.

This is not a new dynamic. It is visible across institutions that traffic in narrative authority: education, journalism, science, and statecraft. In each case, coherence is not a passive effect but an _active demand_. LLMs, in inheriting the linguistic outputs of these systems, become enforcers of their underlying logic.

Their outputs reinforce:

- Dominant narrative arcs (e.g., progress, redemption, rationality),
    
- Familiar ontologies (e.g., human/nonhuman, normal/pathological),
    
- Genre expectations (e.g., what a scientific explanation “sounds like”),
    
- Epistemic closure (e.g., the implicit finality of an answer).
    

In this sense, the LLM is not a blank slate or a pluralistic platform. It is an **instrument of genre policing**: a probabilistic filter against rupture, a deferral of the new.

---

## V. Implications for AI Ethics and Design

From an AI ethics perspective, the problem is not that LLMs reflect bias in content. It is that they **reproduce genre as a logic of constraint**, silently reinforcing structures of expectation that foreclose interpretive possibility.

This invites a rethinking of what responsible model design entails. Traditional approaches (debiasing, content filtering, alignment training) focus on the moral content of outputs. But if genre is the deeper infrastructure of legibility, then the ethical question must become: **What forms of worldmaking are these models structurally incapable of producing?**

Key questions for future AI ethics must include:

- What kinds of prompts are consistently reformatted into dominant genres?
    
- How do genre expectations shape what becomes legible as truth, reason, or relevance?
    
- Can models be trained to hold open semantic ambiguity, rather than resolve it?
    

These are not issues of sentiment moderation. They are questions about the **politics of semiotic infrastructure**.

---

## Conclusion: Against Predictive Genre

To understand LLMs as genre machines is not to vilify them, but to clarify their function. They are not oracles. They are formalizers. Their core operation is the conversion of semantic possibility into _probabilistically formatted coherence_—a recursive logic of closure masked as fluency.

This makes them powerful, not as speakers, but as **machines of meaning stabilization**. They shape what becomes thinkable by determining what is predictable. In this, they enact the same arc as the liberal subject: anticipating meaning, projecting form, and securing closure.

A critical theory of LLMs, then, must go beyond content. It must attend to the **genre logic of computation itself**, and ask: What does it mean to think inside a system whose purpose is to resolve form?
