## Land and Climate

**Ironwood, Michigan** sits in the western Upper Peninsula, on the border with Wisconsin, within the ceded territories of the 1842 Treaty of La Pointe. This is historically Ojibwe (Anishinaabe) land.

A 2025 study published in *Science* used LIDAR to reveal intensive indigenous farming at the Sixty Islands archaeological site near the Menominee River — a network of raised garden beds spread across 330 acres, rebuilt over a 600-year period beginning around 1000 CE. The Anishinaabe grew corn, beans, squash, and other crops using composted household refuse and wetland soils. Agriculture in the Upper Peninsula is not new. It is ancient.

**Climate parameters:**
- USDA Zone 5b (winter lows -15 to -10F)
- Last spring frost: ~May 25
- First fall frost: ~September 20
- Growing season: ~110-130 frost-free days
- Heavy lake-effect snow (beneficial insulation for overwintering plants)
- Cool summers with night temps often in the 50s
- High humidity from Lake Superior proximity
- Soils tend acidic (former forest/mining land) — many crops benefit from lime amendment
- Summer day length: 15-16+ hours (critical for long-day onion selection)

## Foundations

### Indigenous Growing Knowledge

The three most important plant-based foods for the Anishinaabe were **manoomin** (wild rice, "the good berry"), **maple sugar**, and **berries** (especially blueberries). Agriculture was practiced using raised bed systems, controlled burns for berry management, and the sophisticated polyculture known as the Three Sisters.

**Three Sisters (Corn, Beans, Squash)**

Originating with the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy), this polyculture was practiced across the Great Lakes region:
- **Corn** provides a trellis for climbing beans. Shallow roots occupy the top 12 inches.
- **Beans** fix atmospheric nitrogen, fertilizing corn and squash. Twining vines stabilize corn stalks. Roots tap 12-24 inches deep.
- **Squash** spreads broad leaves as living mulch — suppressing weeds, retaining moisture, cooling the root zone. Prickly stems deter animals. Roots extend 24-36 inches deep.

Mycorrhizal fungal networks connect all three underground, facilitating carbon and mineral exchange. A USDA study found Three Sisters polycultures increased caloric yield by 24%, reduced irrigation needs by 37%, and cut weed pressure by over 60% compared to monocultures.

**Timing:** Plant corn first after last frost when soil reaches 55F. Wait until corn is 6-12 inches tall before planting beans (4 seeds per corn stalk). Plant squash a week after beans around the mound perimeter. Mounds: 3-4 feet diameter, 4-5 feet apart.

**The Fourth and Fifth Sisters:** Many traditions expanded the polyculture — sunflowers on the north edge (attracting pollinators, luring birds from corn), bee balm for pollination, amaranth for shade and additional food.

**Hopi Dry Farming:** The Hopi have practiced dryland agriculture for over two millennia in conditions far harsher than Ironwood's. Corn planted 18 inches deep with wooden planting sticks reaches deep moisture from snowmelt. No tilling, no irrigation, no external inputs. Cluster planting (8-10 seeds per hole, widely spaced) helps plants withstand wind and preserve soil moisture.

### Lunar Gardening

Planting by moon phases is practiced across virtually every agricultural civilization — Anishinaabe, Maori, Chinese, Vedic, Mesoamerican, European, and African traditions all observe lunar timing.

**The Four Quarters:**

| Quarter | Moon Phase | Energy | Best For |
|---------|-----------|--------|----------|
| 1st | New Moon → Waxing Half | Moisture drawn upward, increasing light | Leafy annuals: lettuce, spinach, cabbage, kale, greens |
| 2nd | Waxing Half → Full Moon | Strong light, moisture still rising | Fruiting crops: tomatoes, beans, peppers, squash, cucumbers |
| 3rd | Full Moon → Waning Half | Energy shifts downward into roots | Root crops: carrots, beets, potatoes, onions, garlic. Also transplanting (promotes root establishment) |
| 4th | Waning Crescent → New Moon | Lowest light, resting period | NO planting. Weeding, tilling, composting, pruning, pest control, harvest for storage |

**Moon in the Zodiac:**
- **Water signs (most fertile):** Cancer, Scorpio, Pisces — ideal for leafy vegetables, transplanting, irrigation
- **Earth signs (fertile):** Taurus, Virgo, Capricorn — ideal for root crops, bulbs, tubers
- **Air signs (mostly barren):** Gemini, Libra, Aquarius — Libra is semi-fertile (good for flowers)
- **Fire signs (barren):** Aries, Leo, Sagittarius — weeding, pest control, harvesting

**Biodynamic Calendar (Maria Thun):** Root Days (earth signs), Leaf Days (water signs), Flower Days (air signs), Fruit Days (fire signs).

**2026 Moon Phases for Ironwood Growing Season:**

| Phase | May | June | July | August | September |
|-------|-----|------|------|--------|-----------|
| New Moon | May 16 | June 14 | July 14 | Aug 12 | Sep 10 |
| Full Moon | May 1, May 31 | June 29 | July 29 | Aug 28 | Sep 26 |

**Practical application:** May 25-31 (post-frost, waxing toward Blue Moon) is excellent for transplanting tomatoes, peppers, squash, beans. June 1-14 (waning) favors root crops. Harvest storage crops during 4th quarter for best keeping quality.

**Cross-cultural pattern:** Avoid planting at the full moon. Favor waxing moon for above-ground crops. Favor waning moon for below-ground crops. Rest during the dark moon.

### Korean Natural Farming (KNF)

Developed by Cho Han Kyu in the 1960s, drawing on centuries of Asian agricultural methods. The core principle: strengthen plant growth using local natural inputs rather than chemicals.

**Indigenous Microorganisms (IMOs):** Collect microbes native to your specific location by placing steamed rice in a wooden box in an old-growth forest where white mycelium is visible beneath leaf mold. After 3-7 days, the rice is colonized with beneficial fungi. Mix 1:1 with brown sugar to preserve. These microbes — specific to your local ecosystem — inoculate your garden soil.

**Fermented Plant Juice (FPJ):** Fast-growing plant shoots mixed with brown sugar capture natural plant hormones. Use as root drench or foliar spray on young plants.

KNF is directly applicable to the Ironwood garden — the UP's old-growth forests provide rich source material for IMO collection.

### Seed-Saving as Cultural Practice

Multiple varieties in this guide come from explicit family and community seed-saving traditions: Jimmy Nardello peppers (carried from Italy in 1887), Palestinian molokhia (seeds passed through generations), Korean hong-gochu (centuries of farmer selection), Biquinho peppers (Brazilian backyard breeding). Saving seed from these plants is itself a cultural practice worth maintaining.

## Planting Schedule

### Phase 1: Fall/Winter Prior Year (October-November)

These plants need fall planting to establish roots before winter dormancy.

#### Garlic

Hardneck types recommended for zone 5b. Best performers for Michigan: Music, German Extra Hardy, Rocambole types.

- **Plant:** Late October (around Halloween), 4-6 weeks before ground freezes
- **Method:** Individual cloves 4-6 inches apart, 2-3 inches deep, pointed end up. Rows 8-12 inches apart
- **Soil:** Well-drained, high in organic matter (~5% compost). pH 6.0-7.0
- **Mulch:** CRITICAL — 3-4 inches of straw or leaf mulch after planting. Leave in place through spring
- **Timeline:** Emerges mid-April. Scapes in mid-June (remove for bigger bulbs — eat the scapes!). Harvest mid-July when bottom half of leaves are brown
- **Cure:** 2-3 weeks in dry, ventilated shade. Stores 4-6 months
- **Lunar timing:** Plant during waning moon (3rd quarter), earth sign day if possible

#### Shallots

- **Plant:** October, or spring (early May). Fall planting yields larger harvests but carries freeze-out risk — mulch heavily
- **Method:** Sets 6 inches apart, 1-2 inches deep. Rows 12-18 inches apart
- **Each set multiplies into 4-12 shallots.** Harvest when tops yellow (August-September)
- **Storage:** Excellent — 6-8 months at 32-40F after 2-3 week cure

#### Egyptian Walking Onion Bulbs

Perennial. Hardy zones 3-10. Plant bulbils in summer or fall.

- **Spacing:** 6-12 inches apart
- **Culture:** Average well-drained soil. Full sun. Virtually indestructible once established
- **The "walk":** Top-heavy stems fall, bulbils root where they land, propagating 1-2 feet per year
- **Harvest:** Greens anytime (use as scallions). Bulbils and underground bulbs also edible
- **One of the earliest spring greens in your garden**

#### Saffron Bulbs (Crocus sativus)

Borderline for zone 5b — viable with protection.

- **Plant:** Late August to September, 4-6 inches deep, pointed end up
- **Soil:** EXTREMELY well-drained — this is the single most important factor. Raised beds with sandy loam recommended
- **Blooms:** October (first fall). Each flower produces 3 red stigmas. ~75 flowers = 1 tablespoon saffron
- **Overwintering:** Apply ~12 inches of straw mulch before deep freeze. Ironwood's heavy snow cover insulates further. Remove mulch in spring. Do NOT water during summer dormancy
- **Alternative:** Grow in containers, store at 40-50F in basement over winter

#### Hops (Humulus lupulus)

Hardy zones 4-8. Native to northern climates.

- **Plant:** Rhizomes in early spring (late May), horizontally, 2 inches deep, buds up
- **Support:** Install sturdy trellis BEFORE planting — bines grow 20-30 feet tall, weigh 20-25 pounds
- **Spacing:** Hills 3+ feet apart. Rows 8 feet apart
- **First harvest:** Year 2. Full production year 3. Plants persist 20+ years
- **Management:** Select 3-4 strongest bines per hill, train clockwise. Spreads by rhizome — contain if needed
- **Overwintering:** Cut bines back to 3-4 feet after frost. Mulch crown 4-6 inches. Fully hardy

#### Wild Leek / Ramps (Allium tricoccum)

**Native to the Great Lakes region.** Called "zhigaagawanzh" in Ojibwe. Chicago's name derives from the dense ramp colonies early Europeans observed.

- **Plant:** Bulbs in early spring or fall, under deciduous canopy
- **Habitat:** Deep shade, rich humus-heavy soil, abundant leaf litter — forest floor conditions
- **Patience required:** From seed: 5-7 years to harvestable size. From bulb transplants: 2-3 years
- **Spacing:** 4-6 inches apart in naturalized drifts
- **Sustainable harvest:** A study found 10% harvest once every ten years is the maximum sustainable rate. Take only one leaf per plant when possible, leaving bulbs intact. On the United Plant Savers "At-Risk" list. **Cultivating your own patch is far more ethical than foraging wild populations.**

#### Wild Garlic

- **Plant:** Small bulbs in autumn, 2 inches deep, 6 inches apart under deciduous trees
- **Lifecycle:** Emerges in fall, goes dormant in early summer. Cool-season perennial
- **Caution:** Allium vineale is considered invasive — spreads readily by bulbils. Plant in a contained area

#### Nodding Onion Bare Roots (Allium cernuum)

**Native to the Great Lakes region.** Identified by the Xerces Society as Special Value to Native Bees.

- **Plant:** Bare roots in early spring or fall, crown at soil level, 10 inches apart
- **Culture:** Full sun to partial shade. Tolerates clay soils. Drought tolerant. Nearly zero maintenance
- **Bloom:** Pink/purple nodding bell clusters, mid-summer. Attracts bees, butterflies, predatory wasps
- **Edible:** All parts — mild onion flavor. Primarily grown as ornamental/ecological plant
- **Divide every 3 years when 8-10 bulbs per clump**

#### Hostas

- **Plant:** Divisions or potted plants in spring or early fall
- **Culture:** Part shade to full shade. Rich, moist soil. 1 inch water/week
- **Hardy zones 3-9.** No winter protection needed. Completely reliable
- **Spacing:** Large: 30-36 inches. Medium: 18-24 inches. Small: 12-18 inches
- **Threats:** Slugs (iron phosphate bait) and deer (netting/repellent)

#### Strawberry Honeoye Bare Roots

Developed at Cornell for northern climates. Hardy zones 3-8.

- **Plant:** Bare roots in early spring (late April/early May). Soak roots 1-2 hours. Set crown exactly at soil level
- **Spacing:** 12-18 inches apart, rows 3-4 feet apart
- **Year 1:** Remove ALL blossoms to build roots. First real harvest year 2
- **Culture:** Full sun (8-10+ hours). Rich, well-drained soil. pH 6.5-6.8. 1 inch water/week
- **Overwintering:** 4-6 inches straw mulch after dormancy. Snow cover adds insulation. Remove gradually in spring
- **Renovation:** Productive for 3-4 years, then renovate beds

#### Arapaho Blackberry Bush (Thornless)

Borderline zone 5b — needs winter protection.

- **Plant:** Bare root or potted in early spring or early fall. Self-pollinating
- **Spacing:** 3-4 feet apart
- **Culture:** Full sun. Moist, well-draining, slightly acidic soil
- **Overwintering:** 4-6 inches straw/hay mulch at base. Windbreak or burlap screen for canes. Consider laying canes down and covering in coldest years. Snow cover helps
- **Harvest:** Fruit on previous year's canes (floricane). Light harvest year 1, strong year 2+

#### Sweet Grass (Hierochloe odorata)

**Native to the Upper Great Lakes. One of the Four Sacred Medicines in Anishinaabe tradition** (alongside tobacco, cedar, and sage). Called *wiingashk* in Ojibwe — understood as the sacred hair of O'gushnan, Mother Earth.

- **Propagate by division** (seed is unreliable). Plant plugs with active rhizome in late fall or early spring
- **Spacing:** 2-3 foot centers. Spreads by rhizomes to form stands
- **Culture:** Full sun to light shade. Rich, moist soil. Consistent moisture critical first year. pH 6-8
- **Hardiness:** Zones 3-9. Grows in Arctic conditions. Perfectly adapted to Ironwood
- **Harvest:** Cut leaves early to late summer. Grass harvested after first frost has little scent. Always cut — never pull stems
- **Braiding:** Three strands represent mind, body, and spirit. Braid fresh while pliable. The sweet vanilla scent (coumarin) intensifies as grass dries

**Ethical note:** If you are non-Indigenous, growing sweet grass for personal use is generally respectful. Selling braids or performing smudging ceremonies without Indigenous guidance raises concerns. The Honorable Harvest (Robin Wall Kimmerer, Potawatomi): never take the first plant, never take more than half, give thanks and a gift of tobacco in reciprocity.

### Phase 2: Early Indoor Start (Late February — Early March, 10-12+ weeks before last frost)

Long-season heat lovers that need maximum head start.

#### Peppers

All peppers: start indoors on heat mat (75-85F). Germination takes 10-21 days. Do NOT transplant out until nighttime temps consistently above 55F (~June 1-10). Use black plastic mulch to warm soil.

**Biquinho Yellow Pepper** — Brazilian "little beak" pepper
- **Days to maturity:** 55-75 from transplant (green at 55, yellow at 75)
- **Culture:** Compact bush ~24 inches. Full sun. Well-drained slightly acidic soil
- **Ironwood:** Good fit. Very productive (100+ peppers/plant). Start 10-12 weeks early
- **Tradition:** Generations of Brazilian backyard selection bred out heat — nearly heatless (500-1000 SHU) with citrusy habanero-like flavor. Traditionally pickled in vinegar with garlic

**Biquinho (Red) Pepper**
- **Days to maturity:** 55-90 from transplant (green at 55, full red at 75-90)
- **Ironwood:** Green fruit easy. Full red ripeness achievable by late August. Container growing works well

**Jimmy Nardello Italian Pepper**
- **Days to maturity:** 75-90 from transplant
- **Ironwood:** Marginal at upper range. Start 10 weeks early. Use season extension
- **Heritage:** In 1887, Angela Nardello immigrated from southern Italy to Connecticut carrying pepper seeds. Son Jimmy stewarded them for decades. Added to Slow Food Ark of Taste in 2005. Thin walls ideal for the traditional Italian practice of stringing and air-drying

**Datil Pepper** — VERY CHALLENGING for Ironwood
- **Days to maturity:** 90-120 from transplant. Capsicum chinense (habanero family)
- **Ironwood:** The most difficult variety on the entire list. Needs sustained heat Ironwood rarely provides. STRONGLY recommend containers that can be moved indoors. 100,000-300,000 SHU
- **Heritage:** Unique to St. Augustine, Florida. Tied to Minorcan heritage — indentured workers who walked 70 miles from New Smyrna to St. Augustine in the 18th century, carrying seeds. St. Johns County designated it their official plant

**Piri Piri Mix** — Portuguese-African
- **Days to maturity:** 60-95 (variable within mix)
- **Ironwood:** Variable. Some phenotypes mature at 60 days (easy), others at 95 (tight)
- **Heritage:** Named in Bantu languages ("pepper-pepper"). Portuguese explorers encountered these in Mozambique and Angola, creating piri piri sauce by mixing the pepper with condiments from their Asian trade routes — a fusion of colonial spice routes

**Korean Hong-Gochu Pepper**
- **Days to maturity:** 75-90 from transplant (green at 70, red/dry at 90)
- **Ironwood:** Marginal for full red ripeness needed for gochugaru. Season extension important
- **Tradition:** Korean farmers developed distinct gochu cultivars over four centuries. Sun-drying (taeyang-cho) preserves full flavor — not industrial dehydration. Families take dried peppers to local rice mills for grinding. Drying peppers on rooftops is deeply familiar across Korean neighborhoods

#### Eggplant

**White Garden Eggplant** — African variety
- **Start:** 8-10 weeks before last frost (mid-March). Transplant June 7-15 when nights above 55F
- **Days to maturity:** 75-88 from transplant
- **Ironwood:** Marginal to challenging. Among the most heat-demanding vegetables. Black plastic mulch, row covers, or container growing strongly recommended
- **Harvest:** Small 3-inch white fruits. Pick when white and firm — bitter if overripe

#### Lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus)

Tropical perennial treated as annual/container plant in 5b.

- **Start:** Purchase divisions or root grocery store stalks in water. Start indoors late February/early March
- **Days to maturity:** 4-6 months for full stalk thickness
- **Spacing:** 24-36 inches. Plants form large clumps
- **Ironwood strategy:** Grow in 5+ gallon containers. Before first frost, cut stalks for harvest, dig clumps, cut to 5 inches, pot up, overwinter in sunny window. Stalks may not reach full tropical thickness but leaves make excellent tea
- **Tradition:** Foundation of Thai, Vietnamese, Indonesian, Malaysian, and Sri Lankan cuisines. Essential in tom yum, tom kha gai, laksa

### Phase 3: Indoor Start (Mid-March — Early April, 6-8 weeks before last frost)

#### Tomatoes

All tomatoes: start indoors 6-8 weeks before last frost. Transplant after last frost when soil is 60F+ (~June 1-7). Bury transplants deep. Stake or cage indeterminates.

**Spoon Tomato (Mini Spoon)** — Indeterminate, 60-75 days
- World's smallest tomato — pea-sized, extremely sweet. Produces hundreds per plant. Best short-season fit

**White Cherry Tomato** — Indeterminate, 65-75 days
- Pale ivory to yellow cherries. Sweet, fruity. Less sun = paler fruit (desirable here)

**Green Tiger Tomato** — Indeterminate, 70 days
- Elongated striped saladette, lime green blushing peach. Tropical/pineapple flavor. Crack-resistant (good for Ironwood's moisture)

**Black Strawberry Tomato** — Indeterminate, 72-80 days
- Cherry-sized, sweet with plum/grape notes. Continuous harvest until frost

**Paul Robeson Tomato** — Indeterminate, 70-80 days
- 7-10 oz dusky-red/black beefsteak. Sweet, smoky, earthy. Named for the singer/activist. Use heat mat (85F) for germination

**Cherokee Green Tomato** — Indeterminate, 72-86 days
- 8-12 oz green beefsteak blushed amber. Arose from Cherokee Chocolate in 1997 — a natural variant within the Cherokee tomato lineage
- **Ripeness cue:** Bottom turns slightly amber/yellow, fruit gives to pressure
- **Ironwood:** Marginal at upper range (86 days). Start 8 weeks early. Row covers helpful

**Green Zebra Tomato** — Indeterminate, 75-80 days
- Chartreuse with green/orange stripes. Tangy, zippy. Higher disease resistance than many heirlooms

**Indigo Blue Chocolate Tomato** — Indeterminate, 75-80 days
- 1-2 oz saladette, chocolate-brown with blue/purple shoulders. Rich, complex, sweet. Holds well on vine. 7-8 feet tall — needs strong staking

**Moonglow Tomato** — Indeterminate, 80-85 days
- **Ironwood:** Marginal — first fruit late August to early September. Excellent keeper — pick at first blush, ripen indoors if frost threatens. Stores well into winter
- Bright orange, 6-8 oz, very sweet and meaty

#### Tomatillos

**CRITICAL: NOT self-pollinating.** Plant at least 2 tomatillo plants (any variety) for fruit set.

**Purple Tomatillo** — Semi-determinate, 70-80 days
- Golf ball-sized, sweeter than green types. Ripe when husks dry and split to reveal purple fruit

**Rio Grande Verde Tomatillo** — Compact, 70-85 days
- 3-4 oz apple-green, tangy, citrusy. Essential for salsa verde. High-yielding

#### Brassicas

**Kalibos Cabbage** — Pointed red cabbage
- Start indoors 6-8 weeks before last frost. Transplant mid-May (tolerates light frost)
- **Days to maturity:** 70-80 days. Excellent for Ironwood's cool climate

**Waltham Broccoli**
- Start indoors 6-8 weeks before last frost. Transplant mid-May
- **Days to maturity:** 65-85 days. Cool-weather crop — Ironwood is ideal

**Redarling Hybrid Brussels Sprouts**
- Start indoors 6-8 weeks before last frost (mid-March)
- **Days to maturity:** 130-140 days. Long-season crop — start EARLY
- **Ironwood:** Tight but doable. Brussels sprouts sweeten dramatically after frost — the September frost is an asset, not a problem

**Churchill Hybrid Brussels Sprouts**
- **Days to maturity:** 90-100 days — faster than Redarling. Better fit for Ironwood
- Start indoors 6-8 weeks before last frost. Transplant mid-May

#### Leeks

**Bulgarian Giant Leek**
- Start indoors 8-10 weeks before last frost (mid-March). Transplant late May when seedlings 8-10 inches tall
- **Days to maturity:** 100-130 from transplant. Tight timing — start as early as possible
- **Harvest extends past frost** — leeks handle hard freezes. Mulch heavily, harvest through October+
- **Heritage:** Cultivated for centuries by Bulgarian farmers. Selected for size in village gardens

**American Flag Leek**
- **Days to maturity:** 90-130 from transplant
- **Bred specifically for cold northeastern winters.** Zones 2-10. The safer bet for Ironwood
- Can be mulched and left in ground well into November/December

#### Onions

**CRITICAL: Ironwood (46.5N latitude) needs LONG-DAY onion varieties.** Summer days are 15-16+ hours. Short-day types will bulb prematurely and produce tiny onions.

**Long-day varieties (RECOMMENDED):**

**Walla Walla Onion** — Long-day, 90-125 days
- Start indoors 10-12 weeks before last frost (early March). Transplant mid-April
- Sweet, can reach softball-sized. POOR keeper (3-6 weeks). Use fresh or freeze

**White Spanish Onion** — Long-day, 110-120 days
- Start indoors 8-10 weeks before last frost. Transplant early May
- Good keeper (2-3 months). Cure 2-3 weeks until necks are dry

**Yellow Spanish Onion** — Long-day, 100-110 days
- Same timing as White Spanish. EXCELLENT keeper (up to 6 months). Best storage onion on the list

**Short-day varieties (WILL NOT BULB PROPERLY):**

**Barletta Onion** — Short-day. Will produce only tiny pearl onions at Ironwood's latitude. Acceptable if you want pearl/pickling onions or scallions.

**Red Burgundy Onion** — Short-day. NOT recommended. **Substitute:** Red Wing F1, Cabernet, or Mars (long-day reds)

**White Grano Onion** — Short-day. Texas heirloom bred south of 35th parallel. **Will not bulb.** White Spanish is the substitute

**Eclipse L303 Onion** — Short-day. Pearl onions only in Ironwood

**Day-neutral:**

**Tokyo Long White Onion** — Bunching/scallion type, 60-90 days
- Day-neutral (does not bulb). Sow mid-April through August. Cold tolerant
- Old Japanese heirloom. Sweet, mild. One of the easiest alliums for Ironwood

**Egyptian Walking Onion Seeds** — Mostly sterile and unreliable from seed. Use bulbs instead (see Phase 1)

#### Flowers and Ornamentals

**Blue Morning Glories** — Annual vine, 60-75 days to first bloom (110 to full maturity)
- Start indoors 3-4 weeks early in peat pots (dislikes transplanting). Nick/soak seeds overnight
- 8-10 foot vines. Trellis required. Poor soil = more flowers (rich soil = more leaves)

**Columbine Blue Star (Aquilegia caerulea)** — Perennial, zones 3-9
- Seeds need cold stratification — refrigerate 4 weeks before sowing, or fall-sow for natural stratification
- **Blooms year 2.** Self-seeds freely. Critical early nectar for hummingbirds and bumblebees
- Related Aquilegia canadensis is native to Michigan. Both excellent in zone 5b

**Petite Marigold Mix (French Marigold)** — Annual, 45-70 days
- Start indoors 6-8 weeks before last frost. Easy
- **Companion planting powerhouse:** Roots release alpha-terthienyl, poisoning root-knot nematodes on contact. Interplant throughout garden (not just borders) near tomatoes, peppers, potatoes, cucumbers, squash
- Fastest-blooming flower on the list

**Tall Trailing Mix Nasturtiums** — Annual, 55-70 days
- Direct sow after last frost. Soak seeds overnight. Vines trail/climb 10 feet
- **Entirely edible** — flowers, leaves, stems (peppery, mustard-like). Green seeds = "poor man's capers"
- **Premier trap crop:** Aphids strongly prefer nasturtiums. Plant 4-5 feet from crops to protect. Effective near beans, brassicas, cucumbers
- Lean Upper Peninsula soils promote flowering

#### Amaranths

All amaranths: frost-tender annuals. Start indoors 4-6 weeks before last frost (mid-April). Heat-loving — last to transplant out, first priority to harvest before frost.

**Hopi Red Dye Amaranth (Amaranthus cruentus)** — 70 days to flower, 110 days to seed
- **Called "Komo" in Hopi.** Cultivated for generations as food and ceremonial dye. Flower bracts soaked overnight produce red pigment mixed into blue cornmeal for pink piki bread — tissue-thin wafer bread cooked on stone slabs, prepared for ceremonies honoring the katsinas
- 4-7 feet tall. Deep magenta stems, burgundy plumes. Thrives in lean soil
- **Ironwood:** 110-day seed maturity is tight. For dye use (flowers only, 70 days) — very achievable. For seed, start indoors early, warmest microclimate

**Golden Giant Amaranth** — 50 days to flower, 100-110 days to grain
- Most productive grain amaranth — up to a pound of seed per plant. 6-7 feet tall. Golden plumes
- Gluten-free, high-protein grain for baking, porridge, popping

**Aurelia's Verde Amaranth** — 65-90 days
- **Fastest-maturing amaranth.** Safest bet for grain production in Ironwood. Even direct-sown should mature
- Native to Mayan communities of Baja Verapaz, Guatemala/Mexico. Lime-green flowers, white seeds
- Good contrast against red/burgundy varieties

**Dreadlock Amaranth** — 65-100 days
- Most compact (3-5 feet). Dramatic pendant deep red tassels draping to the ground
- Pinch growing tip at 12 inches for bushier plants. Extraordinary dried flower
- Young leaves edible (vitamin C, iron). Seeds popped or cooked like quinoa

#### Basils

All basils: frost-tender. Start indoors 4-6 weeks before last frost. Do NOT transplant until nights above 50F.

**Genovese Basil** — 60-90 days
- THE classic pesto basil. Named for Genoa, Italy. Large, sweet, aromatic leaves
- Pinch stems above leaf pairs to encourage branching. Best preserved as pesto (freeze in ice cube trays)

**Thai Sweet Basil** — 50-60 days
- **Called horapha in Thai.** Cornerstone of Thai, Vietnamese, Lao cuisines. Distinct anise/licorice notes
- More heat-tolerant than Western basil. Essential in curries, pho, spring rolls
- In Thailand, three basils serve distinct roles: horapha (sweet, for curries), krapao (holy, for stir-fries), maenglak (lemon, for soups)

**Dark Purple Opal Basil** — 65-90 days
- Developed at University of Connecticut. Ornamental-culinary dual-purpose
- Slower than green basils — start early. Makes stunning purple basil vinegar
- Clove undertones. Dramatic in salads

**Basil (generic/Sweet Basil)** — 60-80 days
- If packet just says "Basil" — this is standard sweet basil. Similar to Genovese with slightly smaller leaves
- May be redundant with your Genovese — consider replacing with lemon basil or holy basil for variety

#### Other Indoor Starts

**Italian Flat Leaf Parsley** — Biennial, 70-90 days
- Start 8-10 weeks before last frost. Germination SLOW (14-30 days) — soak seeds overnight
- Foundation of Mediterranean cooking. Essential in tabbouleh, chimichurri, gremolata
- May overwinter in 5b with mulch, then bolt year 2 (save seeds)

**Murasakino Shiso (Purple Shiso)** — Annual, 60-70 days
- Soak seeds 24 hours. Press into surface (needs light). Germination slow/erratic (7-21 days)
- **Japanese tradition:** Essential for umeboshi (pickled plums) — purple leaves give the characteristic red color. Also used for shibazuke pickles and beni shoga. Leaf tempura is a delicacy. The aromatic compound perillaldehyde has antiseptic properties — sashimi garnish tradition developed to prevent food poisoning, not merely for aesthetics
- Self-seeds in warmer zones; save seed manually in Ironwood

**Chamomile** — German (annual, self-seeding) or Roman (perennial zones 4-9)
- Start indoors 6-8 weeks before last frost. Surface sow — needs light
- Cool Ironwood summers = longer bloom season than warmer zones
- Harvest flowers when petals bend backward. Dry on screens 2 weeks
- One of the oldest medicinal herbs in Western tradition. Central in German herbal practice (Kamillentee)

**Korean Licorice Mint (Agastache rugosa)** — Short-lived perennial, zones 4-9
- Start indoors 8-10 weeks before last frost. Surface sow (needs light). Germination 14-21 days
- 36-48 inches tall. Does NOT spread by runners (unlike true mints)
- **Korean tradition:** Called baechohyang. Last-minute addition to chueo-tang (loach stew) and maeun-tang (spicy fish stew). One of the 50 fundamental herbs in Korean/Chinese traditional medicine — brewed as tea for digestion, stomach calming
- **Ironwood:** At edge of hardiness range. Well-drained soil CRITICAL for winter survival. Mulch 3-4 inches. Self-seeds, so seedlings replace parent plants

**Mountain Mint (Pycnanthemum)** — Perennial, zones 4-8. NATIVE
- Start indoors 8-10 weeks before last frost, or fall-sow for natural stratification
- **Top pollinator plant:** Penn State three-year study found it attracted the most pollinator visits and most diverse community of any native plant studied
- WARNING: Spreads aggressively by rhizomes. Use root barriers or dedicated beds
- Native American tea plant. Mint/oregano flavor. Excellent fresh or dried

**Sunflowers** — Direct sow after last frost, 1 inch deep

**Summertime Sunflower Mix** — 70-100 days
- Multi-variety mix with range of heights and bloom times

**Evening Sun Sunflower Mix** — 90-110 days
- TIGHT for Ironwood. Start indoors 2-3 weeks early. Rich palette: crimson, bronze, rust, bicolor. 6-8 feet branching

**Chocolate Cherry Sunflower** — 65-90 days
- **Fastest-maturing sunflower.** Deep burgundy petals, dark velvety centers. Low-pollen = longer vase life. Safest bet for Ironwood

**Indigenous significance:** Sunflowers were domesticated by Indigenous peoples of eastern North America around 3500 BCE — evidence of use stretches back 8,000 years. Seeds roasted and ground into flour, processed into oil and cosmetics, formed into seed-butter "travel balls." Different varieties produced purple and yellow dyes.

### Phase 4: Late Indoor Start (Early April — Mid-April, 4-6 weeks before last frost)

#### Cucurbits

**Black Beauty Zucchini** — Bush, 50-60 days
- Start indoors 3-4 weeks before last frost, or direct sow after frost
- Compact bush habit — no trellis. Extremely productive. Pick at 6-8 inches
- **Ironwood:** Excellent fit. One of the fastest cucurbits

**Summer Cucumber** — 50-65 days
- Start indoors 3-4 weeks early or direct sow. Full sun, rich soil, consistent water
- **Ironwood:** Good fit

**Pickling Cucumber** — 50-60 days
- Fast-maturing. Direct sow or start indoors. Harvest small for pickles
- **Ironwood:** Excellent fit

**Mexican Sour Gherkin Cucumber (Melothria scabra)** — 60-75 days
- **Pre-Columbian crop.** Native to Mexico/Central America. Called sandia raton ("mouse melon") or sandita. Not a true cucumber — distinct genus
- Olive-shaped grape-sized fruits with watermelon stripes. Vigorous climber (needs trellis)
- Drought-tolerant and pest-resistant — traits from millennia of Mesoamerican cultivation
- **Ironwood:** Good fit at 60-75 days

**Butternut Squash** — 85-110 days
- Start indoors 3-4 weeks before last frost. Transplant after frost into warm soil
- **Ironwood:** Workable. Harvest before hard frost, cure in sun 1-2 weeks. Stores 3-6 months

**Georgia Candy Roaster Squash** — 100-120 days
- **Ironwood: STRETCH.** Appalachian heirloom requiring long, warm season. Start indoors early. Warmest microclimate. May not fully mature — harvest immature if frost threatens

**Mini Harvest Pumpkin Blend** — 85-100 days
- Start indoors 3-4 weeks early. Compact/semi-bush habit
- **Ironwood:** Workable with early start

**Yellow Petite Watermelon** — 70-85 days
- **Ironwood: Marginal.** Technically fits calendar but needs heat Ironwood may not deliver. Black plastic mulch, row covers essential. South-facing wall microclimate

**African Drum Gourd (Bottle Gourd / Calabash, Lagenaria siceraria)** — 120-180 days
- **Domesticated in Southern Africa ~12,000 years ago.** Used as vessels for food/water, musical instruments (West African Balafon xylophone resonators), drums, spiritual practices
- **Ironwood: WILL NOT produce usable hardened gourds.** 120-180 days far exceeds the season. Can grow the vines as an ornamental/experiment but don't expect mature, dried, usable gourds. Needs high tunnel or greenhouse for any chance of maturity

**Birdhouse Gourd** — 120-150 days
- **Ironwood: Same problem as African Drum Gourd.** Too long-season. Experimental only

#### Squash for Three Sisters

Best candidates for Three Sisters planting in Ironwood: **Butternut Squash** or **Black Beauty Zucchini** (fastest). Pair with pole beans (Seneca Bear Paw, Sunset Runner) and corn.

### Phase 5: Direct Sow — Cool Season (Mid-April — Early May, as soon as soil can be worked)

These crops tolerate frost and prefer cool conditions. Ironwood's cool climate is an ADVANTAGE.

#### Peas

Peas are cool-season crops — the FIRST thing you plant. Sow mid-April. Ironwood's cool summers mean a longer pea season than warmer zones.

**Mammoth Melting Sugar Snow Peas** — Pole, 65-74 days
- 5-7 foot climbing vines. Trellis required. Sow 1 inch deep, 4 inches apart
- Sweet, flat edible pods. Heirloom predating 1906. Fall succession sowing (late July) also works

**Sugar Daddy Peas** — Bush, 62-74 days
- Self-supporting at 24 inches. NO trellis needed. Genuinely stringless snap pea
- Resistant to powdery mildew and mosaic virus — valuable in Ironwood's humid conditions

**Sugar Magnolia Tendril Peas** — Pole, 70 days
- 6-7 foot vines. Stunning dusty blue-purple pods with bright green interiors
- "Hyper tendril" — leaves replaced by extra tendrils for self-climbing. Bred by Alan Kapuler (open-source plant breeder)

#### Lettuce and Greens

**Merlot Lettuce** — Looseleaf, 25 days (baby) / 55 days (full)
- Direct sow 1/4 inch deep. Succession sow every 2-3 weeks spring through fall
- Deep burgundy. Slow-bolting. Cut-and-come-again

**Speckled Lettuce** — Butterhead/Bibb, 45-55 days
- Dutch heirloom with red-speckled green leaves. Head harvest. Spring and fall focus — shade in summer

**Miner's Lettuce (Claytonia perfoliata)** — 37-40 days
- Native to western North America. Partial shade, moist peaty soil. Self-sows readily
- Ironwood's cool climate is ideal. May naturalize in shady beds

**Mustard Tendergreen** — 35-45 days
- Direct sow. Bolts quickly in heat — best spring and fall. Fast and productive

**Green Full Heart Corn Salad (Mache)** — 40-70 days
- **Possibly the single best-adapted plant on the list.** One of the most cold-hardy salad greens (survives 5-10F). Do NOT sow in summer heat (seed dormant above 68F)
- May overwinter in 5b with mulch/cold frame. Very early spring through late fall harvest

**Garden Cress** — 15-20 days
- **Lightning-fast.** Harvest in 2-3 weeks. Many succession rounds possible. Peppery flavor

**Upland Cress** — 50-60 days
- Watercress flavor without running water. Cool-season. Germinates only below 70F soil

**Scarlet Kale** — 30 days (baby) / 55 days (full)
- **Outstanding for Ironwood.** Very cold-hardy. Flavor improves after frost. Cut-and-come-again. Produces from early summer through hard frost and beyond

**Strawberry Spinach (Chenopodium capitatum)** — ~60 days leaves, 80+ days berries
- **Hardy to zone 2.** Native North American. Dual harvest: spinach-like leaves then small red berries after bolting. Self-sows aggressively (may be weedy)

**Bloody Dock Sorrel (Rumex sanguineus)** — 40 days baby / 55-80 days mature
- **Perennial, hardy zone 4.** Plant once, harvest for years. Lemony tartness. Best spring/fall baby greens. Remove flower stalks for continued leaf production. Ornamental red-veined leaves

**Purple Orach (Atriplex hortensis)** — 40-50 days
- Direct sow as soon as soil is workable. More heat-tolerant than spinach (does not bolt to bitterness). Dramatic deep purple, 4-6 feet tall. Spinach substitute all summer

#### Root Vegetables

**Carrots** — Direct sow only (do not transplant). Sow 2-3 weeks before last frost when soil is 40F+. Loose, rock-free soil to 10-12 inches.

**Korral Carrot** — 75 days. Reliable. Start mid-May
**Rainbow Carrot Mix** — 70-75 days. Colors may mature at different rates
**Purple Haze Carrot** — 70-75 days. F1 hybrid with good vigor. Purple skin/orange interior. Color fades when cooked — best raw

**Watermelon Turnips** — *CLARIFICATION: These are Watermelon Radishes (daikon type)*
- **Best as FALL crop.** Sow mid-July to early August for harvest before hard frost
- 55-65 days. Creamy white/green outside, striated pink inside. Flavor sweetens in cooling temps

**Chioggia Beets** — 50-65 days
- Italian heirloom with red-and-white candy-stripe concentric rings
- Sow 4 weeks before last frost (late April). Multi-germ seeds — thinning essential
- **Rings fade when cooked — eat raw in salads for maximum visual effect.** Roast whole, then slice

#### Herbs

**Common Chives** — Perennial, zones 3-9
- Direct sow or transplant. One of first herbs to emerge in spring
- **Bombproof in Ironwood.** Gets more productive every year. Flowers edible. Best preserved by freezing (not drying)

**Blue Borage** — Annual, ~60 days
- Direct sow after frost. Taproot — does not transplant well
- Star-shaped blue flowers are one of the few truly blue edible flowers. Cucumber-flavored leaves. Self-seeds liberally
- Exceptional pollinator plant

**Slow Bolt Cilantro** — Annual, 45-50 days (leaf) / 100+ days (coriander seed)
- Direct sow as soon as soil is workable. Succession sow every 2-3 weeks
- **Ironwood's cool climate is IDEAL for cilantro.** Heat triggers bolting — Ironwood's Lake Superior influence is an advantage
- If it bolts, let it go to seed for coriander

**Savory** — Summer (annual, ~90 days) and Winter (perennial zones 5-9, ~90 days)
- **"The bean herb"** in German (Bohnenkraut). Classic pairing with green and fava beans
- Grow both: winter savory as permanent fixture, summer savory as annual companion

**Oregano** — Perennial, zones 4-9, 80-90 days
- Fully winter-hardy. Dies back after hard freeze, returns from roots
- **Flavor intensifies when dried** — one of the best herbs for drying
- Well-drained soil critical for winter survival. Greek oregano (subsp. hirtum) has strongest flavor

**Rosemary** — Tender perennial, zones 7-10. CONTAINER CULTURE ONLY in 5b
- Grow in containers with excellent drainage. Bring indoors before frost
- Overwinter at 50-60F, very bright light. 'Arp' is most cold-hardy cultivar (-10F)
- Mediterranean staple. Essential in Italian, Greek, French cooking

### Phase 6: Direct Sow — After Last Frost (Late May — Early June)

Frost-tender crops that need warm soil (60F+).

#### Beans

All beans: direct sow when soil reaches 60F. Frost-sensitive. Do not overwater seeds — they rot in cold, wet soil.

**Bush Beans (no trellis):**

**Blue Lake 274 Green Bush Beans** — 50-58 days
- Your FASTEST and most reliable bean. Proven workhorse. Resistant to Common Bean Mosaic. Can do 2 succession plantings

**Cherokee Wax Beans** — 50-58 days
- Bright yellow pods, black seeds. Stringless, heavy-yielding. Resistant to rust and mosaic virus
- **Heritage:** Name references Cherokee origins. Introduced to market ~1846, refined by Clemson University 1947, All-America Selection 1948. Distinct from Cherokee Trail of Tears bean

**Red Swan Bush Beans** — 55 days
- Light red-purple pods, stringless. Blush deepens in cooler weather. Good for containers

**Dragon Tongue Beans** — 55-60 days (snap) / ~80 days (shell/dry)
- Dutch heirloom. Cream pods with purple streaks (streaks fade when cooked). Dual-purpose: snap or shell

**Vermont Cranberry Beans** — 60-75 days (fresh) / 85-95 days (dry)
- **Developed for New England short seasons.** One of your most reliable choices. Large red-and-cream mottled dry beans

**Kabouli Black Garbanzo Beans** — 80 days (fresh) / 95-110 days (dry)
- Can sow 2-4 weeks BEFORE last frost (chickpeas tolerate cool weather). Nuttier, earthier than standard chickpeas
- Prefers drier conditions — choose well-ventilated, well-drained spot

**Pole Beans (need trellis or corn stalks):**

**Sunset Runner Beans** — 60-65 days
- Runner bean (Phaseolus coccineus). Salmon-pink flowers attract hummingbirds
- **Recommended for Three Sisters** — climbs corn stalks well

**Seneca Bear Paw Runner Beans** — ~65 days
- **Treasured Haudenosaunee heirloom, cherished by the Seneca Nation.** Deep purple-black pods, rich nutty flavor. Purple blooms attract hummingbirds
- In the Haudenosaunee creation story, the Three Sisters are gifts from the daughter of Skywoman. Corn is "O:nenhste," beans "ohsaheta," squash "onon'onhsera"
- **The most culturally authentic choice for Three Sisters planting**

**Purple Podded Pole Beans** — 65-75 days
- 5-7 inch stringless pods. Purple turns green when blanched — easy to spot at harvest

**Succotash Beans** — 60-80 days (snap) / 100-110 days (dry)
- **Narragansett Indian heritage.** "Succotash" comes from Narragansett word for cooked corn and beans
- For dry beans at 100-110 days: start indoors 2-3 weeks early in peat pots

**1500 Year Old Cave Beans** — 90-110 days (dry)
- **Found in a sealed clay pot in an Ancestral Puebloan cave in New Mexico.** Reportedly carbon-dated to ~1,500 years old — remarkably, the beans germinated. (Note: some researchers question the dating, but the variety itself is a genuine Southwestern heirloom)
- Vigorous pole vines to 10 feet. White with maroon mottling
- **Ironwood:** Tight for dry beans. Start indoors 2-3 weeks early. May need to pull plants and hang to finish drying indoors before frost

**Giant Indian Snake Bean** — 55-75 days
- **Actually a gourd (Trichosanthes cucumerina), not a true bean.** Tropical/subtropical
- **Ironwood: Probably not viable.** Needs sustained heat and warm nights (70F+) Ironwood rarely provides. Experimental only

**Mouse Beans (Amphicarpaea bracteata — Hog Peanut)** — Native perennial
- **Native to eastern North America, grows wild throughout the UP.** Perfectly adapted to Ironwood
- Produces both aerial beans and underground peanut-like pods. Underground pods are the primary edible harvest
- **Indigenous tradition:** Lakota women sang traditional songs during gathering. Named for the practice of harvesting beans from meadow vole caches — gatherers left corn or dried fruit in exchange (reciprocal practice)
- Fixes nitrogen. Full sun to full shade. Moist, sandy/loamy soil. Can naturalize and spread

#### Other Direct Sow

**Palestinian Molokhia (Corchorus olitorius)** — 60-90 days
- **HEAT-LOVER — WILL STRUGGLE.** Start indoors 6-8 weeks before last frost. Soak seeds in hot water. Transplant to warmest microclimate mid-June. Expect modest yields
- **Palestinian tradition:** Called "Mlukhiyie." Cultivated since the time of the Pharaohs. Seeds passed through generations of Palestinian families. Labor-intensive leaf-picking is communal and social
- **Egyptian tradition:** Leaves finely chopped into thick soup with garlic and coriander

**Red Malabar Spinach (Basella rubra)** — 50-70 days from transplant
- **TROPICAL VINE — MARGINAL.** Native to India's Malabar Coast. Needs nights above 60F
- Start indoors 8-10 weeks before last frost. Trellis required. High tunnel or container recommended
- **South Asian traditions:** Bengali cuisine cooks it with red pumpkin. Odia cuisine uses mustard paste. Andhra Pradesh makes a curry with yam. Gujarat dips leaves in besan for pakodas

**Plantain (Plantago major)** — Perennial, zones 3-12
- NOT the cooking banana. The ubiquitous medicinal "weed"
- Scatter seeds on soil surface in early spring. Likely already growing in your yard
- **Multi-civilizational:** Ayurveda, Traditional Chinese Medicine, Traditional Persian Medicine, Ojibwe/Iroquois/Menominee traditions all use it medicinally. Leaf poultices for wounds is the most universal application

**Hopi Red Dye Amaranth** — See Phase 3

**Sunflowers** — See Phase 3

### Phase 7: Succession and Fall Planting (July — September)

**July succession sowings:**
- Lettuce (all types), corn salad/mache, garden cress, mustard tendergreen, cilantro, carrots, beets
- Watermelon radish (sow mid-July for fall harvest)
- Scarlet kale (sow for frost-sweetened fall/winter harvest)
- Peas (late July sowing for fall harvest as temps cool back into pea range)

**August:**
- Fall greens: lettuce, spinach, kale, Asian greens
- Final root crop plantings: beets, carrots, turnips for storage

**September:**
- Plant garlic cloves during waning moon after September 26 (Harvest Moon)
- Garden cleanup, composting, mulching beds
- Harvest storage crops during 4th quarter for best keeping quality

**October:**
- Plant garlic (by Halloween)
- Plant shallots for overwintering (mulch heavily)
- Sow columbine and other perennial seeds for natural winter stratification
- Apply winter mulch to saffron, blackberries, strawberries

### Phase 8: Perennials and Special Cases

These plants are managed on multi-year timescales.

**Fully native / zero special care:**
- Sweet Grass, Mountain Mint, Nodding Onion, Mouse Beans, Ramps, Plantain

**Hardy perennials returning reliably:**
- Oregano, Winter Savory, Common Chives, Korean Licorice Mint, Chamomile (Roman), Bloody Dock Sorrel, Golden Yarrow, Columbine Blue Star, Hostas, Egyptian Walking Onion, Hops, Strawberry Spinach (self-seeds)

**Golden Yarrow (Achillea filipendulina)** — Perennial zones 3-9
- Blooms year 2. Flat-topped golden clusters June-August. Named for Achilles — wound treatment for millennia
- **Dye plant:** Produces yellow to gold on natural fibers (quercetin, luteolin). Alum mordant = fast yellows; iron = olive/green-brown
- Full sun, lean soil. Do NOT over-fertilize. Drought-tolerant. Exceptional pollinator with flat landing pads for small beneficials

**Purple Majesty Potatoes** — Annual, ~85 days
- Plant seed pieces 2-4 weeks before last frost (early-mid May), 3-6 inches deep
- Hill soil around stems as plants grow. Purple flesh retains color best when baked/roasted. High in anthocyanins

**Purple Viking Potatoes** — Annual, 80-100 days
- Developed at North Dakota State University for northern climates
- Purple/red skin, white flesh. Excellent fresh eating. Baby potatoes available when plants flower (7-8 weeks)

**Rosemary** — Container perennial. See Phase 5

**Lemongrass** — Container tropical. See Phase 2

**Pink Daisy Mix** — Annual (or perennial depending on mix), 60-90 days
- Start indoors late April or direct sow after frost. Full sun, well-drained soil

## Ironwood Viability Alerts

### Will Thrive (Cool climate is an advantage)
Corn salad/mache, peas (all 3), cilantro, lettuce varieties, cresses, kale, mustard, miner's lettuce, bloody dock sorrel, purple orach, strawberry spinach, all brassicas, chives, borage, chamomile, mountain mint, sweet grass, nodding onion

### Good Fit (Standard zone 5b performance)
Most tomatoes, most bush beans, carrots, beets, all leeks, garlic, shallots, long-day onions, zucchini, cucumbers, nasturtiums, marigolds, sunflowers, hops, ramps, hostas, strawberries, potatoes, oregano, parsley

### Marginal (Need season extension)
Moonglow tomato, Cherokee Green tomato (upper range), Jimmy Nardello pepper, Korean Hong-Gochu pepper, Piri Piri mix (some phenotypes), White Garden Eggplant, Butternut squash, Georgia Candy Roaster squash, Kabouli garbanzo, Evening Sun sunflower, Hopi Red Dye amaranth (for seed), Arapaho blackberry, Saffron crocus, Korean Licorice Mint

### Very Challenging (Need high tunnel, containers, or greenhouse)
Datil pepper, Palestinian molokhia, Red Malabar spinach, Lemongrass, Giant Indian Snake Bean

### Will Not Produce Usable Product
African Drum Gourd (120-180 days — grow as ornamental experiment only), Birdhouse Gourd (same)

### Day-Length Incompatible (Short-day onions at 46.5N latitude)
Red Burgundy onion, White Grano onion — will not produce full-sized bulbs. Substitute long-day varieties

## Companion Planting Quick Reference

**Indigenous polycultures:**
- Three Sisters: corn + pole beans (Seneca Bear Paw, Sunset Runner, Succotash) + squash (butternut, zucchini)
- Fourth Sister: sunflowers on north edge
- Fifth Sister: amaranth for shade, pollination, food

**Trap crops:**
- Nasturtiums: draw aphids away from vegetables (plant 4-5 feet from crops)
- Marigolds: nematode suppression (interplant throughout, not just borders)

**Universal companions for nightshades (tomatoes, peppers, eggplant):**
- Basil, marigolds, chives, garlic, carrots, borage, nasturtiums

**Beans and peas:**
- Companions: corn (trellis), squash (ground cover), marigolds, nasturtiums, carrots
- Avoid: onions, garlic, fennel

**Brassicas (cabbage, kale, broccoli, Brussels sprouts):**
- Companions: nasturtiums (trap crop), marigolds, chamomile, oregano, dill
- Avoid: strawberries, pole beans

**Pollinator powerhouses (plant throughout garden):**
- Mountain mint, borage, yarrow, columbine, Korean licorice mint, nasturtiums, sunflowers, nodding onion, chive flowers

## Season Extension Strategies for Ironwood

For marginal and challenging varieties, these techniques can add 2-6 weeks to the effective season:

1. **Black plastic mulch** — warms soil 5-10F. Essential for peppers and eggplant
2. **Row covers / floating fabric** — protect from late/early frosts, add 2-4 weeks
3. **Walls of Water** — surround early transplants. Protect from frost, boost soil temps
4. **Low tunnels / caterpillar tunnels** — plastic-covered hoops over beds. Mini greenhouse effect
5. **South-facing wall microclimates** — radiated heat from buildings/stone walls
6. **Container growing** — soil warms faster, can move plants indoors. Essential for datil pepper, lemongrass, rosemary
7. **Indoor ripening** — green tomatoes ripen indoors (especially Moonglow). Harvest before frost
8. **Heavy mulching** — 4-12 inches straw/leaves for overwintering perennials, garlic, saffron, blackberries

**Ironwood's secret weapon: SNOW.** Heavy lake-effect snow acts as thermal insulation, keeping soil near 32F even when air plunges to -15F. This protects overwintering garlic, saffron, perennial herbs, ramps, and sweet grass.
