# The Google UX Design Certificate as of September 2026

This note is the research phase of the fourth book in the Group's study
companion series, written to the education science section's pattern
(`education-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/27/2026-09-27-note-on-how-a-study-companion-should-teach-toward-a-credential.md`).
It lives in design engineering science, whose notes already hold the
foundations the book stands on: the web design section's lesson on
accessibility and universal design, the information architecture section's
foundations and classification notes, and visual engineering's terms
*orientation before interpretation* and *premature closure*.

the course pages' estimates:

## Where sources disagree

1. **How many users a usability test needs.** Nielsen and Landauer modeled
   the share of problems found as $1 - (1 - L)^n$, with $L$ about 0.31 across
   their projects, so five users find about 85 percent
   [@nielsen-landauer1993]. Faulkner ran 60 users on one system and found
   that random groups of five found between 55 and 99 percent of the problems,
   averaging 85, while groups of ten found at least 80 percent
   [@faulkner2003]. *Reading:* both supported, for different claims. Five
   users per round is sound for iterative, qualitative testing with one kind
   of user; it's refuted as a guarantee for any single round, for several
   user groups, or for measuring. The book teaches "about five per round and
   per user group, then iterate."
2. **How many stages design thinking has.** The d.school names five modes
   (empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test) [@dschool2010]; the Design
   Council's Double Diamond names four (discover, define, develop, deliver),
   organized as two rounds of diverging and converging
   [@design-council2005]. *Reading:* both; the book follows the course's
   five and shows the diamond's diverge-converge shape, which the house theory
   below needs.
3. **"Dark patterns" or "deceptive patterns."** Brignull coined "dark
   patterns" in 2010 and later renamed his catalogue "deceptive design";
   the course uses "deceptive patterns." *Reading:* same referent; the book
   uses "deceptive patterns" and names the older term.
4. **The multi-device "Cs."** Levin's framework names three approaches:
   consistent, continuous and complementary [@levin2014]. The course's
   description mentions "the 4 Cs." *Reading:* the fourth could not be
   confirmed from public sources; the book teaches Levin's three, attributed,
   and doesn't claim the course's fourth.

## How the section's theory reads the same material

- **Premature closure is design fixation.** Visual engineering names
  *premature closure* as the early satisfaction that ends exploration before
  orientation is complete. The design literature found the same thing in
  designers: shown an example, they reproduce its features, flaws included
  [@jansson-smith1991]. Design thinking's diverge-then-converge rhythm, the
  sprint's rule of sketching alone before deciding together [@knapp2016], and
  the "How might we" reframe are all disciplines for delaying closure until
  the problem has been explored. The book teaches them as one idea.
- **Orientation before interpretation is visual hierarchy.** The same
  section's target, that an artifact first shows the reader how to move and
  what matters structurally, is what Gestalt grouping, hierarchy and
  wayfinding do in an interface. The book's visual-design chapter is built on
  it.
- **The social model of disability grounds accessibility.** The web design
  section's lesson locates disability in the environment, not the person, and
  that makes accessibility a design responsibility from the first sketch, not
  a final check. The book puts accessibility in every chapter.
- **Classification isn't neutral.** The information architecture note's
  point, that every organization scheme embeds assumptions about whose
  categories count, is the basis of equity-focused design
  [@costanza-chock2020]: design with the people most affected, not only for
  them.

## What the book is

*UX Design, Course by Course: An Independent Study Companion to the Google UX
Design Professional Certificate.* Series number 4. Chapters for courses 1 to
7, with course 2 split in two, a spaced study plan and a glossary; the
job-search course is out of scope. One running example, a neighborhood tool
library's app for borrowing tools, carried from empathy to a responsive
website and a portfolio case study. It teaches the methods from their
sources and describes Figma's role without reproducing course material.
