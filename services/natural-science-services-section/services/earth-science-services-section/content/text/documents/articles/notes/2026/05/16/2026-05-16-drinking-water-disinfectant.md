# Does Duluth use chlorine, chloramine, or something else for city drinking water?

Duluth's municipal drinking water comes from Lake Superior, drawn at the Lakewood Pumping Station and treated at the Lakewood Water Treatment Plant (~13 million gallons/day average). The question is which secondary disinfectant — free chlorine, chloramine (chlorine + ammonia), or some other agent — the city carries through its distribution system to suppress microbial regrowth between the plant and the tap.

This matters at the household scale because the two chemistries behave very differently in practice: free chlorine dissipates on standing or with a brief boil and is removed by a basic activated-carbon filter, while chloramine is far more persistent — it does not boil off on usable timescales, requires a chloramine-rated filter (catalytic carbon) or a chemical neutralizer like sodium thiosulfate, and is fatal to fish and aquatic invertebrates at residual tap concentrations.

## Answer

**Duluth uses free chlorine, not chloramine.**

Evidence:

- The City of Duluth's own water utility page describes chlorine as the disinfectant added at the Lakewood plant; the chlorine-dosing practice dates to 1912 at the original Pump Station and was continued when the Treatment Plant came online in 1976. See [duluth.gov/public-works-utilities/utilities/water](https://duluthmn.gov/public-works-utilities/utilities/water/).
- The disinfection byproduct profile reported in Duluth's water-quality data (chloroform and other total trihalomethanes, plus haloacetic acids including dichloroacetic acid) is the byproduct signature of *free chlorine* treatment of surface water containing natural organic matter. Chloramine systems produce a different and generally lower byproduct profile (notably less TTHM/HAA5, but more nitrogenous byproducts such as NDMA).
- The Duluth News Tribune report on aquarium fish die-offs traced to city water describes the chlorine-residual mechanism rather than the chloramine mechanism, consistent with a free-chlorine system.
- A commonly-cited list of Minnesota chloramine-using utilities does not include Duluth; the metro chloramine systems in Minnesota are Twin Cities-area (Minneapolis, Saint Paul Regional Water Services, and several suburbs), not Duluth.

## Practical implications

- Drinking-water aquarium and pond keepers in Duluth can dechlorinate by aging water 24 hours, by brief aeration, or with any standard dechlorinator; a chloramine-specific product is not required.
- Brewers, dialysis users, and others sensitive to chlorine taste/chemistry can remove the residual with a standard activated-carbon filter (e.g., a faucet or pitcher filter); catalytic carbon is not required.
- This answer should be re-verified if Duluth Public Works-Utilities announces a switch in disinfection regime, which utilities sometimes do for operational or regulatory reasons (e.g., to suppress disinfection byproducts on hot-weather peaks).
