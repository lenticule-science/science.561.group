## 1. What a spring is, and its one defining property

Springs form by four main geomorphic/hydraulic mechanisms — gravity/descending, artesian/ascending, contact, and karst — crossed with a temperature axis (cold/warm/hot, conventionally split at body temperature ~37 °C) set by the ~25–30 °C/km geothermal gradient over 1–3 km circulation depths (search synthesis).
The defining ecological property is **thermal constancy**: deep-sourced water is shielded from surface weather, so Comal Springs holds ~23.3 °C and San Marcos ~22.2 °C essentially year-round, and Silver Springs sits within 20–23 °C — making springs simultaneously winter refugia (ice-free) and summer refugia (cool) for aquatic life (search synthesis).
A spring is a point where the underworld's stable regime presents itself at the surface, at the underworld's temperature rather than the weather's.

## 2. Evolutionary archipelagos

Constancy plus isolation turns spring systems into archipelagos of endemism.
*Pyrgulopsis* springsnails run ~120–127 described species (the Great Basin alone holding >80); stygobiont amphipods include many single-spring endemics (*Stygobromus hayi* confined to springs in the District of Columbia; the genus *Niphargus* exceeds 425 species); and Australia's Great Artesian Basin mound springs carry >90 endemic taxa, many "ultra-short-range endemics" restricted to one spring cluster (*Phreatomerus latipes* the named case) — all with dispersal on the order of meters to tens of meters per year (search synthesis throughout).
Each spring is an island whose ocean is dry land; the fauna are the local sections of a system that never glues globally.

## 3. The finding that reframes the river: baseflow is the majority

Groundwater is not a contributor to rivers; it is most of them.
The global baseflow index — the fraction of streamflow that is groundwater — is **59% ± 7%** across 15,567 basins (Jasechko et al. 2024, *Nature Geoscience*; search synthesis of a named, recent primary), with regional figures from ~48% (Upper Colorado) through ~60–62% (Upper Yellow, Nida) and 22–68% across Hawaiian streams.
The visible river is mostly the invisible aquifer, presented continuously along the channel.

Snowpack ties the sky into the same story: in the western United States, 53% of total runoff originates as snowmelt from only 37% of precipitation falling as snow (Li et al. 2017, *GRL*; search synthesis), rising to ~70–78% in the Rockies, Sierra Nevada, and Cascades, with a quarter of the region's land area producing 90% of its runoff for ~70 million people.
And a 2025 USGS analysis adds the join: **58% ± 34% of snowmelt runoff is itself routed through groundwater** before reaching a stream (search synthesis) — the sky's water arrives at the river by way of the underworld, so the mountain's white reservoir and the valley's baseflow are one system with a subterranean middle.

## 4. The hyporheic zone: the river breathing through its bed

The stream/groundwater exchange zone under and beside the channel — centimeters to tens of meters — is a biogeochemical reactor: hyporheic and engineered-hyporheic systems remove 68–98% of nitrate, with denitrification dominating once residence time passes a threshold (~6.9 h in one study) and accounting for ~85% of total nitrate reduction in that setting; the same zone shelters invertebrates through floods and droughts (search synthesis).
The bed is not the river's floor but its organ of exchange.

## 5. Karst: fast, concentrated, and dirty

Karst aquifers underlie ~15.2% of ice-free land, host ~1.18 billion people, and supply roughly 9–10% of the world's drinking water — while their conduit flow passes contaminants with minimal filtration, the price of the famously concentrated discharge (Silver Springs >550 million gallons/day; Idaho's Thousand Springs complex ~5,200–5,800 cfs average; search synthesis).
Karst is the regime with the percolation network's threshold crossed: connected conduits instead of grain-scale seepage, throughput bought with vulnerability.

## 6. Headwaters: the network's bottleneck

Headwater streams' share of total US network length is quoted anywhere from 53% to 79% depending on methodology — a spread carried as such — and they drain >73% of the conterminous land area (search synthesis).
Functionally they are the nitrogen-processing bottleneck: Peterson et al. (2001, *Science*; **paywalled — search synthesis of abstract, flagged pending**) found ammonium stripped from headwater flow within tens to hundreds of meters, nitrate traveling only ~5–10× that uptake length; Alexander et al. (2007, *JAWRA*; **paywalled — flagged pending**) found first-order headwaters contribute ~70% of mean-annual water volume but only ~65% of nitrogen flux to second-order streams — the small streams eat the difference.
Meyer et al. (2007) on headwater importance is likewise paywalled and pending.
