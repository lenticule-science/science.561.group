A **place** is a location that has been made meaningful — through
habitation, use, memory, or attachment — as distinct from a *space* or a
*site*, terms geographers reserve for location considered without that
accumulated meaning. The distinction is standard in human geography since
Yi-Fu Tuan's *Space and Place* (1977): space is location before it is known;
place is what results once it has been experienced, named, and lived in
long enough to carry identity. The same physical coordinates can be a
space to a stranger passing through and a place to someone who grew up
there — the geography does not change, but what it means does.

Place is constructed rather than simply found: repeated practice (walking
the same paths, holding the same gatherings, telling the same stories about
a location) is what turns space into place, and a place can persist, in the
meaning it carries, even after its physical setting is altered or
destroyed. This is why "sense of place" and "placelessness" are treated as
real, measurable phenomena in geography rather than as loose figures of
speech: a location engineered for interchangeable, generic use — the same
retail interior repeated at every highway exit — is placeless precisely
because it resists the accumulation of the local meaning that would make it
a place rather than a space.
