A watershed is the land area that drains to one outlet: every drop of surface water that falls inside its drainage divide passes the outlet on its way out.
The divide is the ridge that bounds it; the outlet, or pour point, is the one place on the channel that names it; the basin is the interior.
Watersheds nest: a creek's basin lies inside a river's, which lies inside a larger river's, which lies inside the basin of an ocean or of a closed lake with no outlet.
The United States Geological Survey's Watershed Boundary Dataset writes this nesting as the hydrologic unit code, an even number of digits, two per level, so that a code is the prefix of every code nested inside it.

A watershed is distinct from its divide, the ridge that bounds it, and from its outlet, the point that names it; a catchment and a drainage basin are the same thing under other names, while a hydrologic unit is a watershed as a dataset delineates it, which may be a fragment of a basin rather than a whole one.
The visible basin is mostly the presented surface of a settled store: groundwater is about three fifths of global streamflow, and a spring is where that store instantiates at a point ([springs and headwaters](../../../../../hydrology-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-springs-and-headwaters.md)).

Because watersheds nest and every one drains to exactly one other or to a sink, a set of watersheds is a strict partial order, so a watershed record needs a drains-to and an outlet before it needs a boundary, and its extent is settled only by two witnesses of different kinds.
The causal reading of that order, the sun as its forcing, and the carriers that serialize it are in [the Earth as a topology of watersheds under the sun](../../../../../../content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/05/2026-09-05-note-on-the-earth-as-a-topology-of-watersheds-under-the-sun.md).
