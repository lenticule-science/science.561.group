not a semiotic reading of anything below. It is the mineralogy that a semiotic
reading is either licensed or not licensed to use.

Four standing questions organize it:

1. Is the nesosilicate → sorosilicate → cyclosilicate → inosilicate →
   phyllosilicate → tectosilicate classification actually a *settling ladder*
   — an ordered sequence a given parcel of silica climbs over time — or is it
   a static classification of connectivity that a given rock occupies for
   reasons having nothing to do with elapsed time?
2. What in nephrite's and jadeite's microstructure actually produces their
   fracture toughness, quantitatively, and how does it compare to hardness?
3. Does crack arrest at grain boundaries have a quantitative theory mineralogy
   and materials science already possess, that could be imported rather than
   reinvented?
4. What controls the rate of opal-A → opal-CT → chalcedony diagenesis, and
   what are the real timescales — because a claim was made elsewhere in the
   estate that this pathway licenses reading old records as more "matured"
   than new ones, and that claim has a testable, mineralogically specific
   truth condition.

## 1. The silicate polymerization taxonomy: classification, not chronology

The taxonomy is real and is the standard organizing scheme of silicate
mineralogy, built directly on the SiO₄⁴⁻ tetrahedron and how many of its four
corner oxygens are shared with neighboring tetrahedra (Si:O ratio falls
correspondingly):

| class | shared corners per tetrahedron | Si:O | exemplar |
|---|---|---|---|
| nesosilicate | 0 (isolated) | 1:4 | olivine, garnet, zircon |
| sorosilicate | 1 (pairs) | 2:7 | epidote |
| cyclosilicate | 2 (rings) | 1:3 | beryl, tourmaline |
| inosilicate | 2 or 2.5 (single/double chains) | 1:3 / 4:11 | pyroxene, amphibole |
| phyllosilicate | 3 (sheets) | 2:5 | micas, clays, talc |
| tectosilicate | 4 (3-D framework) | 1:2 | quartz, feldspar, zeolites |

This is the same classification the aqueous chemist's Qⁿ notation
(Q⁰ = isolated, through Q⁴ = fully cross-linked) restates for silicate glasses
and solutions — the estate's chemistry note on group-14 divergence covers the
solution-chemistry side of this; this note stays on the solid-mineral side.

**The corrective this note needs to state plainly:** the polymerization
taxonomy is a classification of *bonding topology*, not a record of *elapsed
settling time*, and reading it as a temporal ladder is a real error a
mineralogist would flag immediately. Olivine (nesosilicate) crystallizes
directly from mantle-derived basaltic melt at high temperature — it is not
"younger" or "less settled" than quartz (tectosilicate), which crystallizes in
the same rocks or in granites under entirely different conditions. Which
silicate class a given magma or fluid produces is governed by the **Si:O
ratio and the available network-modifying cations** (Al³⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺, Ca²⁺,
Na⁺, K⁺) in the melt or solution, by temperature, and by pressure — not by how
long the system has been evolving. A magma poor in silica relative to metal
cations crystallizes nesosilicates (olivine) because there is not enough
silica present to build chains, let alone frameworks; a magma or hydrothermal
fluid rich in silica and poor in modifying cations builds tectosilicates
(quartz) directly, with no nesosilicate stage passed through at all. Bowen's
reaction series — the classical igneous-petrology sequence in which olivine
crystallizes early from a cooling melt and quartz crystallizes last — *does*
put nesosilicates before tectosilicates in one specific context (fractional
crystallization of a single cooling magma body), and this is very likely the
empirical seed of any "ladder" intuition. But Bowen's series is a
compositional-fractionation sequence *within one melt*, not a universal
truth about the connectivity classes; it says nothing about mud
(phyllosilicate) forming "before" or "after" jade (inosilicate) in any
general sense, and both form under entirely different formation conditions
(low-temperature weathering/diagenesis for clays; regional metamorphism at
depth for jade) that have no ordering relation to each other.

**What this means for the estate's mapping:** the silicate ladder is a real
and useful *connectivity axis* — SEA's dissolved monomer, amorphous silica,
chain silicates, sheet silicates, and framework silicates are genuinely
different, genuinely ordered by degree of polymerization. But the axis
orders **connectivity**, not **time-in-service**, and any design conclusion
that leans on "this object is further along the ladder, therefore older/more
settled/more matured" needs a *separate* argument for why, in that specific
case, connectivity actually did increase monotonically with time. Sections 4
and 5 below give the one case (opal → chalcedony) where that separate
argument can actually be made, with real kinetics — and even there, the
timescales are decisive and surprising.

**Instruments/sources to consult for anyone wanting to firm this up further:**
X-ray diffraction and single-crystal structure refinement to confirm
tetrahedral connectivity class from unit-cell geometry; ²⁹Si MAS-NMR (the
solid-state analogue of the Qⁿ solution notation) to directly measure the
distribution of bridging vs. non-bridging oxygens in a given sample; any
standard igneous/metamorphic petrology text (Bowen 1928 for the reaction
series; Deer, Howie & Zussman's *Rock-Forming Minerals* for the
classification itself) for the compositional/thermal controls.

## 2. What actually makes nephrite and jadeite tough

Both minerals are real, and the toughness/hardness distinction is real
and important: Mohs hardness (resistance to scratching, a measure of bond
strength at a point) and fracture toughness Kᵢc (resistance to crack
propagation, a measure of energy absorbed per unit new crack area) are
independent properties, and jade is a paradigm case of a mineral whose
practical value comes almost entirely from the second while being unremarkable
on the first. Nephrite (tremolite–actinolite, a Ca-Mg double-chain amphibole,
Mohs ≈ 6–6.5) and jadeite (a Na-Al single-chain pyroxene, Mohs ≈ 6.5–7) are
both scratched by quartz and are not exceptionally hard; what distinguishes
them from most other rocks of comparable hardness is fracture toughness.

The one quantitative source this note found and can cite directly is Bradt,
Newnham & Biggers, "The Toughness of Jade," *American Mineralogist* 58
(1973), 727–732 — the classical materials-science-of-jade paper (a single source;
its figures should be treated as an order-of-magnitude estimate pending a
second citation). It reports Kc ≈ 77 × 10⁷ dyne·cm⁻³ᐟ² for nephrite and ≈ 71 × 10⁷ dyne·cm⁻³ᐟ²
for jadeite, which converts to roughly **Kᵢc ≈ 7.7 MPa·m¹ᐟ² (nephrite)** and
**≈ 7.1 MPa·m¹ᐟ² (jadeite)** in modern units — for comparison, ordinary silicate
glass runs about 0.7–0.8 MPa·m¹ᐟ² and single-crystal quartz is in a broadly
similar low range, so jade's toughness sits roughly an order of magnitude
above amorphous or single-crystal silica. The same source reports fracture
*strength* (a different, load-bearing-capacity quantity, not to be conflated
with Kᵢc) as roughly 200 MPa for nephrite versus roughly 100 MPa for jadeite —
a two-fold difference the Kᵢc numbers alone do not show, and the paper
attributes it to microstructure: nephrite's fracture surface is dominated by
fiber pull-out and interlocking felted amphibole needles, while jadeite's is
dominated by transgranular cleavage through its coarser, more equant granular
pyroxene crystals. **Honest hedge:** this note has only located the one 1973
paper with hard numbers; the general toughness/hardness distinction and the
qualitative nephrite-vs-jadeite microstructure contrast are well-established
and repeated across the gemological and mineralogical literature, but the
specific Kᵢc figures should be treated as a starting citation to be
corroborated against a second independent measurement before any downstream
document leans weight on the exact numbers.

**Toughening mechanisms, named specifically** (this is where mineralogy meets
fracture mechanics and the estate's own materials-science vocabulary):

- **Crack deflection** — a crack front meeting a fiber or grain at a large
  misorientation angle is forced off its most-favorable propagation plane,
  which increases the total crack-surface area created per unit of forward
  advance and therefore the energy consumed.
- **Crack bridging** — fibers spanning the wake of a crack continue to carry
  load after the crack tip has passed them, requiring additional energy to
  break or pull them out before the crack faces can fully separate.
- **Fiber pull-out** — frictional sliding of a partially-debonded fiber out of
  its socket dissipates energy directly as friction, distinct from and
  additional to the energy of creating new fracture surface.

These are the same three mechanisms fiber-reinforced composite engineering
names for glass-fiber or carbon-fiber composites (see the companion
materials-engineering inquiry note, §3, for the general theory) — nephrite is,
mineralogically, a naturally-occurring fiber-reinforced composite in which
both the fiber and the matrix happen to be the same amphibole. **This is the
concrete, checkable form of the claim the estate's jade note reached for
informally** ("cracks die at grain boundaries," "fibers felted, no shared
orientation"): it is correct, and the correctness has a name and a literature
(Faber & Evans 1983 gave the first quantitative crack-deflection model for
polycrystalline ceramics; see the companion materials note for the fuller
citation trail).

**Instruments/sources:** single-edge-notched-beam or indentation-fracture
Kᵢc testing (ASTM C1421 for advanced ceramics is the closest standard method
for a comparably brittle mineral aggregate); SEM fractography to identify
which toughening mechanism dominates a given fracture surface; EBSD
(electron backscatter diffraction) to map grain-to-grain misorientation
directly and test whether misorientation angle correlates with local crack
deflection, which is the direct experimental test of "no shared orientation
→ toughness" as a *quantitative*, not just qualitative, claim.

## 3. Does grain-boundary crack arrest have a quantitative theory to import?

Yes, in materials science generally, though the mineralogy-specific
literature applying it to nephrite by name is thinner than the ceramics
literature applying it to alumina, zirconia, and silicon nitride. The
governing framework is R-curve behavior (crack-growth resistance rising with
crack extension, rather than the flat resistance a Griffith/Irwin
single-crystal treatment assumes) combined with the specific mechanisms in
§2. The companion materials-engineering inquiry note develops this in full
(Faber–Evans crack deflection, transformation toughening, the general theory
of interface-mediated toughness) since it is squarely an engineering-mechanics
topic; this note's mineralogical contribution is narrower and more specific:

**An open empirical question this note poses rather than answers:** does
nephrite's specific fiber-orientation statistics (degree of felting,
characteristic fiber aspect ratio, the distribution of grain-to-grain
misorientation angles) actually satisfy whatever quantitative criterion
separates "toughening interfaces" from "merely present interfaces" in the
general theory — or has no one measured nephrite's microstructure with
sufficient quantitative rigor (EBSD misorientation statistics, fiber aspect
ratio distributions) to check? This note did not find a paper doing that
specific measurement. If one does not exist, it is a genuine, fundable,
tractable piece of primary mineralogical research, and cheap: it needs a
polished nephrite thin section, an SEM with EBSD, and a day of imaging.

**What the estate wants from this:** the jade note's §4 poses a
mathematical question — "under what conditions does an obstruction dissipate
propagating failure rather than constitute it" — and proposes the
rigidity-percolation frontier (Laman's generic rigidity matroid vs. the
graphic matroid) as "plausibly the right doorway." That mathematical question
is filed separately (see the obsmetrological inquiry note, item 5 in this
same task set) and is explicitly *not* answered there. What this mineralogy
note can supply, that the mathematics cannot supply on its own, is the
**empirical microstructure data** that would let someone actually test
whether nephrite's felting satisfies a rigidity-percolation-style
constraint-counting criterion (à la Phillips–Thorpe, cited in the
ossification note below) rather than a merely qualitative "many small
domains" story. Without that measurement, the toughening-obstruction question
stays a metaphor with a plausible home; with it, someone could actually check
whether real felted-fiber toughness correlates with a computable
rigidity-percolation order parameter.

## 4. Opal-A → opal-CT → chalcedony: rate, controls, and real timescales

The diagenetic sequence itself is well-established and uncontested:
amorphous, fully hydrated opal-A matures through opal-CT (a poorly-ordered,
metastable intergrowth of cristobalite and tridymite stacking) to chalcedony
or microcrystalline quartz, with each step involving loss of structural water,
solution-reprecipitation (silica dissolves from the less-stable phase and
reprecipitates as the more-stable one — this is the Ostwald step rule playing
out in real rock), and progressive long-range ordering.

**What controls the rate**, per the sources this note located this session:

- **Host lithology dominates.** The transformation is measurably faster in
  carbonate-rich sediment than in clay-rich sediment (Kastner, Keene & Gieskes
  1977, *Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta* 41, the classical experimental study
  of this transformation) — clay minerals appear to poison or slow the
  solution-reprecipitation pathway, plausibly by competing for dissolved
  silica or interfering with nucleation.
- **Temperature is the dominant control on rate**, as expected for any
  solution-reprecipitation process governed by an activation energy — silica
  solubility and reaction kinetics both rise steeply with temperature.
- **Pore-water chemistry and available surface area** are secondary controls
  named in the more recent literature on this transformation (a 2021
  *Marine Geology* study on temperature-time relationships in deep-sea silica
  diagenesis, located this session but not read past its abstract — flagged
  as a source to follow up rather than a claim fully verified here).

**The real timescales, and why they matter enormously for anything built on
this pathway:**

- In cold, deep-sea sedimentary settings (the classical marine
  siliceous-ooze-to-chert sequence), opal-CT precipitates roughly
  **10–65 million years** after deposition of the original ooze, and true
  quartz chert forms roughly **50–140 million years** after deposition. These
  are geological timescales — the transformation this note is discussing is,
  in its ordinary setting, slower than the existence of most human
  institutions by six to eight orders of magnitude.
- In hot, silica-saturated hydrothermal settings, the same transformation
  runs enormously faster: a documented study of a siliceous sinter deposit at
  Opal Mound, Roosevelt Hot Springs, Utah, found opal-A already transforming
  toward quartz in a deposit only **roughly 1,900 years old** — six orders of
  magnitude faster than the marine pathway, because near-boiling,
  silica-saturated geothermal water sustains a very high, continuously
  renewed silica supersaturation and a correspondingly short nucleation
  induction time.
- Laboratory experiments at 25 °C and 150 °C over periods of **one day to six
  months** (Kastner et al. 1977, same study cited above) measured the
  transformation rate directly and confirmed the strong temperature and
  lithology dependence in a controlled setting, rather than only inferring it
  from field ages.

**This is the single most important corrective finding this note has for the
estate.** The tabasheer note (§4, cited above) proposes that "the shard in
the culm's hollow is *early-stage* along the same road" as jade, and extends
this to a corpus-management claim: "a record consulted, recompressed, and
re-settled across years is opal-CT on its way to chalcedony, and the corpus
should expect (and permit) its oldest witness to be more ordered than its
newest." **The mineralogy makes this claim's plausibility entirely conditional
on which regime is being invoked, and the note does not specify which.** Under
the *marine/ambient-temperature regime* — which is the correct physical
analogy for records sitting in cold storage at ambient conditions, unheated,
unforced — a decade is not merely insufficient but is off the relevant scale
by roughly six orders of magnitude; nothing resembling opal-A → opal-CT
maturation would occur in any corpus on any human timescale under passive
conditions. Under the *hydrothermal/geothermal regime*, real, measurable
transformation toward greater order in centuries-to-millennia is documented —
but that regime requires sustained, elevated temperature and continuously
renewed silica supersaturation, i.e., **sustained active reprocessing**, not
mere elapsed time. Read straightforwardly, this makes the mineralogy's actual
message the *opposite* of "shards mature on their own given years" and instead
something closer to: **maturation requires continuous active
reprocessing under sustained conditions ("heat"); passive storage does not
mature anything, no matter how old it gets.** Whether "heat" has an honest
estate-side analogue (repeated re-consultation? active editorial revision?
something else) is exactly the kind of translation question this note leaves
to the estate rather than answering, but the mineralogy itself is decisive:
elapsed calendar time alone is not the operative variable in either regime;
temperature (or its estate-side analogue) is.

**Instruments/sources for anyone pursuing this further:** Raman spectroscopy
(opal-A, opal-CT, and chalcedony/quartz have distinct, well-resolved Raman
signatures and this is the standard rapid non-destructive phase-identification
method in the diagenesis literature); XRD for the same purpose with better
quantitative phase-fraction resolution; the Kastner, Keene & Gieskes (1977)
and Williams, Parks & Crerar (1985) papers as the foundational kinetic
studies; the Roosevelt Hot Springs sinter study (*Sedimentary Geology*, cited
above) as the best-documented fast-regime field case; a 2021 *Marine Geology*
temperature-time modeling paper (located, not fully read) for a more current
treatment of what actually sets the rate in the slow/marine regime.

## 5. Open questions

The most load-bearing correction in this note is stated plainly in §1 and
§4 and is worth restating together: reading the six-class silicate taxonomy
as a general temporal settling ladder is mineralogically incorrect — it is
a connectivity classification with only one specific, narrow, well-evidenced
temporal instance (opal diagenesis, §4), not a general rule — and the claim
elsewhere in the estate that "old records mature like opal" is decisively
regime-dependent in a way that claim does not currently state, with the
passive/ambient regime (the one that actually describes cold storage) giving
essentially zero maturation on any corpus timescale.

That regime-dependence argument would be overturned by a specific kind of
evidence: a controlled experiment or documented field case showing opal-A
measurably progressing toward opal-CT at ordinary ambient temperature (no
hydrothermal or otherwise elevated-temperature setting) within a timescale
of years to decades. This note did not find such a case in the sources
searched this session, though the search was not exhaustive and its absence
is not proof the case doesn't exist.

Two further questions are open and worth naming for anyone taking this
further: whether nephrite's specific fiber-orientation statistics
quantitatively satisfy a rigidity-percolation-style toughening criterion —
this would need the EBSD misorientation measurement described in §2 and §3,
which this note did not locate as having already been done; and whether
Bowen's reaction series is in fact the correct empirical seed of the
"settling ladder" intuition critiqued in §1, versus some other source. A
smaller thread: whether the "heat" analogy proposed in §4 (sustained active
reprocessing as the corpus-side counterpart to elevated temperature) has any
honest referent worth naming is left to the estate rather than resolved
here.
