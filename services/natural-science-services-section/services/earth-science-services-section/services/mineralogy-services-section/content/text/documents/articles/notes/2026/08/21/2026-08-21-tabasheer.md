Tabasheer (also tabashir, tabaxir; Sanskrit *tvakṣīra*, "bark milk"; Hindustani
*banslochan*; Chinese *tianzhuhuang*, 天竺黄) is a translucent to opaque,
whitish-to-bluish concretion of hydrated amorphous silica that forms inside
the hollow internodal cavities of certain bamboo species. It is not a soil
mineral or a hydrothermal deposit: it is biogenic opal, precipitated from
silica-laden plant sap rather than from groundwater or magmatic fluid, and it
is one of the oldest substances in the pharmacological and mineralogical
literature to have been correctly identified — after some false starts — as
a form of silica rather than a plant sugar, salt, or resin. Its interest to
mineralogy is threefold: it is a naturally occurring, essentially pure
sample of opal-A whose formation conditions (ambient temperature, biological
rather than geological time, an unusually narrow and reproducible starting
chemistry) are far better constrained than almost any inorganic opal
deposit; its optical behavior — a refractive index far below that of solid
silica, and a reversible loss and recovery of transparency on wetting and
drying — was one of the first clearly documented cases of a mineral's bulk
optical properties being governed by nanoscale porosity rather than by the
intrinsic refractive index of its solid phase, worked out by David Brewster
in 1815–1819; and it has been in continuous, well-documented human use as a
traded medicinal substance for at least two thousand years, giving it an
unusually long paper trail connecting mineralogical, botanical, and
pharmacological sources.

## 1. Composition and hydration state

Tabasheer is silica (SiO₂) hydrated to a degree and in a structural state
that places it unambiguously in the opal-A class — X-ray-amorphous,
water-bearing silica, as opposed to the better-ordered opal-CT or the fully
crystalline chalcedony/quartz that opal matures toward given geological time
and heat (see §6). The most detailed modern structural study is Klinowski,
Cheng, Sanz, Rojo & Mackay, "Structural studies of tabasheer, an opal of
plant origin," *Philosophical Magazine A* 77(1), 1998, 201–216, which
examined two related silica samples from bamboo — "tabasheer" proper and a
related material the authors call "bambusa" — using X-ray fluorescence,
X-ray diffraction, thermogravimetric analysis, and ¹H, ²⁷Al and ²⁹Si
magic-angle-spinning solid-state NMR. Their findings:

- Silicon is overwhelmingly the dominant element; aluminum runs on the order
  of 1–2 wt %, iron roughly 0.3–0.4 wt %, with smaller amounts of calcium,
  magnesium, and phosphorus, matching the older wet-chemical figure of
  roughly 70% silica with the remainder potash, lime, and alumina that goes
  back to nineteenth-century analyses.
- The material is genuinely hydrated, not merely surface-wetted: the
  maximum theoretical water loss calculated from the OH/Si ratio came to
  4.92 wt % for one sample and 3.82 wt % for the other. Thermogravimetric
  analysis showed this water is lost in two distinguishable steps —
  physically adsorbed water evolving below roughly 250 °C, followed by a
  slower, higher-temperature loss attributable to dehydroxylation
  (condensation of structural Si–OH groups into Si–O–Si linkages with loss
  of water) — the same two-step behavior seen in synthetic amorphous silica
  gels and in inorganic opal-A generally.
- ²⁹Si MAS-NMR — the solid-state analogue of the aqueous-silicate Qⁿ
  notation — showed the silicon environment to be predominantly Q³ and Q⁴
  (three or four bridging oxygens per tetrahedron, i.e., substantially but
  not completely cross-linked, consistent with amorphous, partially
  condensed silica rather than a well-ordered framework), with ²⁷Al NMR
  indicating a small amount of aluminum substituting into the silicate
  network rather than sitting only as a separate impurity phase.

The earlier structural benchmark is L. H. P. Jones and A. A. Milne,
"Tabashir: An Opal of Plant Origin," *Science* 151(3709), 1966, 464–466,
which established by electron microscopy that tabasheer's amorphous silica
is built from sub-particles roughly 100 Å (10 nm) in diameter, clumped
together in a way that leaves visible irregularities on fracture surfaces —
and, importantly, that this microstructure is measurably different from
both synthetic silica gel and inorganic amorphous opal of geological origin.
Jones and Milne also reported that tabasheer carries more water, but less
of the alkali and alkaline-earth content typical of most natural opals — a
chemical signature consistent with its biological rather than geological
origin: the silica is precipitated from a comparatively pure aqueous
solution of monosilicic acid inside living plant tissue rather than from a
groundwater or hydrothermal fluid carrying whatever cations happen to be
locally available.

No source located in this review reports a measured bulk density,
solid-phase refractive index (as distinct from Brewster's bulk,
porosity-dominated value, §5), or Mohs hardness for tabasheer using modern
instrumentation; these are gaps a materials characterization study could
close cheaply.

## 2. Formation in bamboo internodal cavities

Bamboo culms (the woody, jointed stems of the grass subfamily Bambusoideae)
are hollow between nodes, and it is inside these internodal cavities that
tabasheer forms. The mechanistic picture assembled from the plant-silicon
literature is as follows. Bamboo, like other grasses, takes up silicon from
soil water almost exclusively as monosilicic acid, Si(OH)₄, which moves
passively with the transpiration stream through the xylem. Once inside the
plant it polymerizes and precipitates as amorphous silica — the same
process, at much smaller scale, that deposits the phytoliths (opaline silica
bodies) found in grass cell walls, lumina, and intercellular spaces
generally, and for which bamboo and rice are the standard examples in the
phytolith literature. Where an internodal cavity accumulates enough of this
silica-bearing fluid — either because the internode has been damaged,
because an insect has bored into it, or simply through the ordinary
transpiration-driven flow of a mature culm — the fluid concentrates by
evaporation and slow silicic-acid condensation until it sets as a solid or
semi-solid mass filling part or all of the cavity. Early European accounts
describe tabasheer-bearing culms as having their internal lining
noticeably torn or damaged relative to tabasheer-free culms of the same
species, consistent with damage or insect action as a trigger rather than a
routine feature of every internode.

Not every culm, and not every species, produces it. The classical
tabasheer-yielding species named in the older pharmacological and
mineralogical literature are *Bambusa bambos* (long synonymized as
*Bambusa arundinacea*) and *Melocanna baccifera* (also cited under the
older synonym *Melocanna bambusoides*), both South and Southeast Asian
bamboos; a 2022 ecological review — Burton, "Treasure troves of tabasheer,"
*Frontiers in Ecology and the Environment* 20(6), 2022 — adds the South
American species *Guadua angustifolia* to the list of bamboos known to
produce it, indicating the phenomenon is not restricted to Old World
bamboos. For the Chinese pharmacopoeial product specifically, the named
source species differ somewhat from the classical Indian ones — commonly
*Phyllostachys nigra* (black bamboo) along with *Bambusa textilis*, *B.
tuldoides*, and *B. breviflora* — and modern Chinese pharmaceutical supply
in some cases relies on artificially inducing the exudate (by deliberately
wounding or boring culms) rather than relying solely on naturally occurring
concretions, since natural yield is low and unpredictable. A stem is
checked for tabasheer non-destructively by shaking it and listening for the
rattle of the solid concretion loose inside the cavity, after which the
culm is split to extract it — a detection method reported essentially
unchanged from eighteenth-century accounts through modern harvesting
descriptions.

The claim, repeated in several review sources, that tabasheer "hardens over
2–3 years following culm maturation" is not traced in this review to a
primary measurement and should be treated as an unverified folk figure
pending a dated citation; it is plausible on general grounds (silica gel
dehydration and condensation is slow at ambient temperature) but this
review did not find the underlying study.

## 3. Microstructure, porosity, and adsorption behavior

Brewster's nineteenth-century optical work (§5) established, well before
any electron microscope existed, that tabasheer's bulk is riddled with
gas-filled voids — his own estimate was a substance that behaved optically
like roughly two and a half parts air to one part solid colloidal silica by
volume, an extraordinarily high void fraction for a substance solid enough
to be carved, weighed, and traded. The 1966 Jones and Milne electron-
microscope study gave this a real microstructural basis: the material is
built of ~10 nm silica sub-particles clumped into a porous mass rather than
of a continuous glassy solid, which is exactly the kind of architecture
that produces a high volume fraction of interconnected void space between
particles.

No source located in this review reports a direct BET nitrogen-adsorption
surface-area or pore-size-distribution measurement performed specifically
on tabasheer — a real and citable gap, not merely an omission from this
search. What is documented, and stands in for direct porosity data, is
functional evidence that the porosity is real, interconnected, and
exploitable:

- Brewster's own reversible-transparency observations (§5) are themselves
  an adsorption/desorption experiment: air occupying the pore network is
  visibly evolved as bubbles when the material is immersed in water, and
  the water that replaces it changes the material's bulk optical density
  enough to make it transparent — direct, if qualitative, nineteenth-
  century evidence of a continuous, wettable, gas-permeable pore network
  rather than isolated closed bubbles.
- Modern materials-science work has used tabasheer's natural porosity and
  chemical purity as the starting point for producing mesoporous silicon:
  Kalluri, West, Akkaraju, Canham & Coffer, "Plant-Derived Tandem
  Drug/Mesoporous Silicon Microcarrier Structures for Anti-Inflammatory
  Therapy," *ACS Omega* 4, 2019, 8359–8364, converted milled tabasheer
  powder to mesoporous silicon by magnesiothermic reduction, then loaded the
  resulting porous microcarriers with a plant-derived anti-inflammatory
  extract (from *Equisetum arvense*, horsetail) and demonstrated sustained
  release with measurable inhibition of the inflammatory signaling protein
  NF-κB over several hours. This is not a porosity measurement of
  tabasheer's native silica structure — the magnesiothermic reduction step
  converts SiO₂ to elemental Si and necessarily restructures the pore
  network in the process — but it is direct evidence that tabasheer's
  native silica is fine-grained and reactive enough to serve as a
  magnesiothermic-reduction feedstock at useful scale, and that the
  resulting silicon inherits a mesoporous (rather than dense or
  macroporous) architecture, broadly consistent with a sub-100 nm
  primary-particle precursor.
- One further FESEM-based report (found in a lower-quality secondary
  source and not independently verified against a primary paper in this
  review) describes surface pores in the mesoscale range on tabasheer
  microparticles; this is flagged here as a claim to verify against the
  original imaging study rather than a fully sourced fact.

Given the porosity is real and well above that of ordinary fused or
crystalline silica, tabasheer's traditional use as a desiccant-adjacent or
"cooling" medicinal substance (§8) is at least structurally plausible as a
material with real adsorption capacity for water and small molecules, even
though no source located here has run the direct adsorption-isotherm
measurement that would make this quantitative.

## 4. Etymology and nomenclature

The Hindustani/Persian *tabashir* is the term that entered European
scientific literature via colonial-era trade and correspondence and gave
English "tabasheer"/"tabashir." In Sanskrit it appears as *tvakṣīra*
("bark-milk"), *vaṃśa-śarkarā* ("bamboo sugar"), and *vaṃśa-karpūra*
("bamboo camphor"); in Hindustani also as *banslochan*. In Chinese
materia medica it is *tianzhuhuang* (天竺黄). The etymology of this last
name is worth stating precisely because a naive character-by-character
reading is misleading: 竺 (*zhú*) happens to also be a bamboo-radical
character, inviting the folk reading "heavenly bamboo yellow," but 天竺
(*Tiānzhú*) as a whole is a historical Chinese name for India used from
roughly the Later Han through the Song dynasties, itself a phonetic
transliteration (via Persian *Hindu*) of the Sanskrit *Sindhu*, the Indus
river. Read this way, *tianzhuhuang* is closer to "the yellow substance
from India" — a name that records the substance's documented trade route
into China from the Indian subcontinent, rather than a description of
bamboo turning yellow in heaven. 黄 (*huáng*, "yellow") itself is
consistent with the yellowish cast of lower grades of the material noted
in the trade literature, as against the finer bluish-tinted grades
("neel"/"neelkanth" in Indian trade terminology).

## 5. The optical work: Brewster and successors

The chemical case that tabasheer is silica, not a plant sugar or resin, was
made considerably before Brewster's optical work. James Louis Macie — who
later took the name James Smithson and founded the Smithsonian Institution
— published "An Account of Some Chemical Experiments on Tabasheer" in the
*Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London* (read 7 July
1791; the paper is also reprinted in *Medical Facts and Observations* 4,
1793, 193–200). Macie worked on samples sent to London by Patrick Russell,
a physician-naturalist stationed in India, and showed by direct chemical
testing that the material — widely assumed at the time to be of vegetable
origin, given where it was found — was in fact a siliceous earth. This
established tabasheer's mineral identity a generation before its optical
peculiarities were investigated.

David Brewster's contribution, communicated as a letter to Sir Joseph
Banks and read to the Royal Society in 1815 (published in the
*Philosophical Transactions*, with an abstract appearing in 1819 in the
*Philosophical Transactions* abstracts and in the newly founded *Edinburgh
Philosophical Journal* Brewster co-edited that year — the 1815/1819 dating
found across different secondary sources reflects the gap between the
paper being read and being fully published, not two different papers), was
"On the Optical and Physical Properties of Tabasheer." Brewster measured
refractive index across a range of tabasheer specimens differing in
density and found it varied continuously from about 1.111 in the
lowest-specific-gravity specimens up to about 1.182 in the hardest, most
dense ones — in the densest, most compact specimens approaching the
refractive index reported for semi-opal. These values sit far below the
refractive index of solid vitreous or crystalline silica (roughly 1.46 for
fused quartz glass, 1.54–1.55 for crystalline quartz); Brewster's own
interpretation was that tabasheer is optically behaving as an intimate
mechanical admixture of a large volume fraction of air — on the order of
two and a half parts air to one part colloidal silica — rather than as a
continuous solid whose refractive index one could look up in a table. This
is, in modern terms, an effective-medium argument: measured bulk refractive
index of a porous material sits between that of the solid matrix and that
of whatever fills the pores (air, here), weighted by volume fraction, and a
sufficiently high void fraction can pull the bulk value down close to that
of air itself (n ≈ 1.0003).

Brewster also documented — and this is the observation of most lasting
interest — that tabasheer's transparency is reversible and water-driven,
and that it is a more particular phenomenon than ordinary hydrophane opal's
similar behavior. He distinguished three varieties by appearance: a
milky-translucent form transmitting yellowish and reflecting bluish light;
a plain translucent form; and an opaque form. Immersed in water, the first
two varieties become transparent while visibly evolving air (bubbles rising
from the pore network as water displaces trapped gas and, more
importantly, as the refractive-index contrast between silica and its pore
filling drops sharply when water, n ≈ 1.33, replaces air, n ≈ 1.0003, in
the pores — internal light scattering at every pore wall falls
correspondingly, and a porous solid that scattered light strongly with air
in its pores becomes far more transparent with water in them); the third,
opaque variety also evolves air on wetting but remains opaque throughout.
Brewster noted, and later commentary agrees, that this is qualitatively the
same physical principle that makes hydrophane opal turn transparent in
water — but flagged tabasheer's behavior as showing "a singularity of
structure" relative to ordinary hydrophane opal, in that a small quantity
of absorbed water could render some specimens *more* opaque before a larger
quantity rendered them transparent — a non-monotonic effect a simple
two-component effective-medium picture does not obviously predict, and
which points to a more structured pore-filling sequence (partial wetting
creating new internal water–air interfaces before the pore network
saturates and those interfaces disappear) than "water simply displaces
air." This specific non-monotonic behavior does not appear to have been
given a quantitative explanation in the sources located for this review and
is flagged here as an open problem (§9).

Successors named in later nineteenth-century commentary (found via a
*Scientific American Supplement* piece, "The Relation of Tabasheer to
Mineral Substances," summarizing the state of the question some decades
after Brewster) include Professor Church, who worked on the related
silica pseudomorphs called beekites, and Professor Rucker, credited with
re-examining Brewster's optical results using more recent (for the 1880s–
90s) physical methods; neither of these secondary re-examinations was
traced to a primary citation in this review, and the commentary piece
itself calls for fresh Indian fieldwork on the grounds that market
specimens available in Europe by that point had typically undergone
"imperfect calcination" that could have altered their optical properties
relative to fresh material — an early and reasonable methodological worry
about sample handling that modern work (Klinowski et al. 1998, Jones and
Milne 1966) does not appear to specifically address either, since neither
paper discusses sample provenance or thermal history in enough detail to
rule out post-collection alteration.

## 6. Opal diagenesis and why tabasheer does not (in practice) experience it

Opal matures, given the right conditions and enough time, from opal-A
(X-ray-amorphous, fully hydrated, tabasheer's own structural class) through
opal-CT (a poorly ordered cristobalite/tridymite intergrowth) to chalcedony
or microcrystalline quartz, by a solution-reprecipitation mechanism in which
the less stable phase dissolves and the more stable phase nucleates and
grows in its place. This pathway, its rate controls (host lithology,
temperature, pore-water chemistry), and its very different real timescales
in cold marine/ambient settings (opal-CT after roughly 10–65 million years,
quartz chert after roughly 50–140 million years) versus hot hydrothermal
settings (opal-A already transforming toward quartz within about 1,900
years at the Roosevelt Hot Springs sinter deposit in Utah) are covered in
detail, with primary citations (principally Kastner, Keene & Gieskes 1977,
*Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta* 41, 1041), in this section's companion
note, `2026-08-21-note-on-silicate-connectivity-and-polycrystalline-toughness.md`
(§4); that treatment is not repeated here.

What is specific to tabasheer, and worth stating plainly, is that this
review found no report — in the structural papers, the diagenesis
literature, or the historical accounts — of tabasheer itself having been
observed progressing along that pathway. This is not surprising once the
formation setting is considered: tabasheer forms and is collected on a
human timescale (a bamboo culm's own lifespan is a matter of years, and
tabasheer is typically harvested from living or recently cut stems), and
the organic scaffold housing it — the bamboo culm — decomposes on an
ecological timescale far shorter than any timescale on which opal-A
diagenesis proceeds under ambient conditions. By the time a buried,
compacted sediment could plausibly hold opal-A for the tens of millions of
years the marine pathway requires to reach opal-CT, any bamboo-derived
silica within it would necessarily have long since been liberated from its
original botanical context by the decay of the culm around it, and nothing
in the literature located here suggests anyone has traced what happens to
that liberated silica afterward as a distinct, identifiable phase. The
opal-A → opal-CT → chalcedony pathway is therefore best treated, for
tabasheer specifically, as a theoretical continuation available in
principle to any opal-A material rather than as an observed or observable
stage of tabasheer's own natural history.

## 7. History of study and trade

Tabasheer's documentary trail is unusually long for a mineral substance,
and unusually tangled with the separate history of cane sugar, because
several early Western sources describing a sweet, salt-like, brittle
substance "found in reeds" in India appear to conflate the two. Pedanius
Dioscorides, the Greek military physician whose *De Materia Medica* (1st
century CE, written under Nero) is the foundational text of Western
pharmacology, describes *sakcharon* (σάκχαρον) as "a kind of concreted
honey... found in reeds in India and Arabia Felix, similar in consistency
to salt, and brittle enough to be broken between the teeth, as salt is."
Some later commentary identifies this passage as an early Western
reference to tabasheer rather than to crystallized cane sugar, on the
strength of the brittle, salt-like, non-honeylike physical description;
but the etymology of *sakcharon* itself (from Sanskrit *śarkarā*, via Pali
*sakkharā*, into Greek and eventually into English "sugar") and
Dioscorides' further remarks about dissolving it in water for digestive
complaints sit at least as comfortably with a description of hardened cane
sugar. This review treats the Dioscorides identification with tabasheer
specifically as contested rather than established, since the same passage
is the foundational citation for the etymology of "sugar" in most
histories of that word, and no source consulted here resolves the
ambiguity definitively.

What is better attested is tabasheer's standing as a distinct medicinal
export commodity by the early medieval period. A colonial-era district
gazetteer account (Konkan-region gazetteer material from 1882, as
summarized in modern secondary reviews) places tabasheer preparation in
the Konkan coast of western India, with the port then known as Thana
(modern Thane) cited as a clearing point for the trade with Arab and
Persian intermediaries; broader histories of medieval Indian Ocean trade
place Arab merchants as established, peaceable commercial partners along
this coast especially from the tenth through twelfth centuries CE, after
the more disruptive Arab raids on Gujarat-coast ports (including Thana
itself) in the seventh and eighth centuries. The Arabic/Persian *tabashir*
is the route by which the substance's name, and the substance itself,
entered the Latin and then English materia medica as tabasheer, alongside
its separate transmission into the Sanskrit-medicine and Chinese-medicine
literatures under their own names (§4).

The modern chemical and mineralogical literature begins with Macie/
Smithson's 1791 analysis (§5) confirming the mineral, not vegetable,
nature of the material — a genuinely surprising result at the time, given
that the substance is found packed inside a plant stem and had been
assumed, reasonably enough, to be some kind of plant secretion or
crystallized sap. Brewster's 1815/1819 optical work (§5) followed,
establishing tabasheer's anomalously low refractive index and its
reversible water-driven transparency. The gap between Brewster and the
next substantial structural advance is large: it is not until Jones and
Milne's 1966 electron-microscope study and, more comprehensively,
Klinowski et al.'s 1998 multi-technique NMR/XRD/TGA study that tabasheer's
structure was reinvestigated with instrumentation capable of resolving its
nanoscale architecture and hydration chemistry directly, rather than
inferring them from bulk optical and gravimetric behavior.

## 8. Materia medica

Tabasheer appears, under its various regional names, in Ayurveda, Unani
medicine, and Traditional Chinese Medicine, with broadly convergent
traditional indications: as an antipyretic (fever-reducing) agent, a
"cooling" constitutional remedy, an antispasmodic, and — particularly in
its Chinese use as *tianzhuhuang* — as a treatment for phlegm-heat and
febrile convulsions in children, alongside claimed antiparalytic and
restorative properties in older Ayurvedic and Unani sources. It is worth
being explicit that the traditional-medicine literature on tabasheer
attributes to it a wide and somewhat generically favorable set of claims
(supporting bone, skin, cardiovascular, and cognitive health among them)
that mirror the broader alternative-medicine literature on dietary silicon
generally, most of which is asserted rather than demonstrated in the
sources reviewed here, and none of which this review attempts to evaluate
beyond noting that it is largely unreplicated.

Where tabasheer-containing formulations have actually been put through
controlled or semi-controlled clinical evaluation, the results reported are
modest and, on their own terms, ambiguous. Hussain & Ali (2015), evaluating
the Unani polyherbal formulation *Qurs-e-Tabasheer* in endoscopically
confirmed gastritis and duodenitis, reported symptomatic improvement in
the treatment group but explicitly noted that outcomes in the test and
control groups were "almost the same with no significant difference" — the
authors framed the result as favorable on cost and side-effect grounds
rather than as evidence of superior efficacy, which is a materially weaker
claim than "the treatment worked" and should be read that way. Ahmed,
Sharma, Mukerjee, Kant & Kumar (2012), in a rat model of the same
formulation for antidiabetic and hepatoprotective effects, attributed
observed hypoglycemic effects to other phytochemical constituents of the
polyherbal mixture (rose and pomegranate components, specifically) rather
than to the silica component itself, explicitly declining to attribute the
effect to tabasheer's silica content. More recent work is computational
rather than experimental: a 2021 in-silico screening study examined
constituents of *Habb-ul-aas* and tabasheer as potential SARS-CoV-2 main
protease inhibitors — this is a molecular-docking prediction, not a
demonstrated pharmacological effect, and should not be read as evidence
that tabasheer has any activity against the virus.

Taken together, the materia medica literature on tabasheer is long-
standing and cross-culturally consistent in its traditional claims, but
thin and largely unreplicated as controlled clinical or mechanistic
science; the strongest empirical ground in the entire tabasheer literature
remains the structural chemistry (§1) and the optics (§5), not the
pharmacology.

## 9. Modern analyses and open problems

Summarizing where the instrumented literature on tabasheer actually stands:
X-ray fluorescence, X-ray diffraction, thermogravimetric analysis, and
¹H/²⁷Al/²⁹Si solid-state NMR (Klinowski et al. 1998) have established its
bulk elemental composition, its opal-A (X-ray-amorphous) structural class,
its two-stage water loss on heating, and its predominantly Q³/Q⁴ silicate
connectivity with minor tetrahedral aluminum substitution. Electron
microscopy (Jones & Milne 1966) established a roughly 10 nm primary-
particle microstructure distinct from both synthetic silica gel and
inorganic geological opal. Materials-science work (Kalluri et al. 2019) has
used tabasheer as a real, functioning feedstock for magnesiothermic
conversion to mesoporous silicon, indirectly corroborating its fine-
grained, reactive microstructure at application scale.

Set against this, the gaps are specific and, in most cases, cheaply
closeable with existing instrumentation:

- No direct BET nitrogen-adsorption surface-area or pore-size-distribution
  measurement on native tabasheer silica was located in this review, despite
  more than a century of qualitative and indirect evidence (Brewster's
  optics; the 1966 and 1998 structural studies; the 2019 magnesiothermic-
  reduction work) that the porosity is real, connected, and functionally
  significant.
- Brewster's non-monotonic wetting behavior in the opaque variety
  (transparency-then-opacity-then-transparency-again as water content rises,
  §5) has not, as far as this review located, been revisited with modern
  optical or imaging methods since 1819; it is a specific, well-posed, and
  apparently still-open question in the physics of porous-medium optics.
- No source located here reports a modern, directly measured Mohs hardness,
  bulk density, or solid-phase refractive index for tabasheer using current
  instrumentation, as distinct from Brewster's bulk (porosity-averaged)
  optical values.
- The specific claim that tabasheer hardens over 2–3 years post-formation
  is repeated in secondary review literature without a located primary
  citation and should be treated as unverified.
- Whether tabasheer's amorphous structure differs measurably, in a
  reproducible and quantified way, from inorganic amorphous opal or
  synthetic silica gel — beyond the qualitative fracture-surface difference
  reported by Jones and Milne in 1966 — does not appear to have been
  re-examined with modern small-angle scattering (SAXS) or high-resolution
  TEM methods, which would be a natural extension given how much
  characterization technology has advanced since 1966 and 1998.
- The medieval trade history (§7) rests substantially on a single
  nineteenth-century colonial gazetteer account as filtered through modern
  secondary sources; a direct check against primary medieval Arabic or
  Indian Ocean trade records, if any survive naming tabasheer specifically,
  would strengthen or correct that account considerably.

## A note on relevance to the estate's research program

This review was prompted by an earlier same-day note in this section
(`2026-08-21-note-on-silicate-connectivity-and-polycrystalline-toughness.md`)
that took up, among other things, a claim elsewhere in the estate reading
tabasheer's opal-A state as an early stage of the same maturation pathway
jade's silicate chemistry sits at a different point on — and used the real
kinetics of that pathway to argue that maturation requires sustained active
reprocessing, not mere elapsed calendar time. This tabasheer-specific
review supports and sharpens that argument in one further way: tabasheer is
a case where the opal-A state is not incidental but is the entire natural
history of the material as anyone has ever studied it — no observed
