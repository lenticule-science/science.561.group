Eudicot: a flowering plant with two cotyledons (seed leaves), one of the two major clades of [angiosperms](2026-04-02-angiosperm.md). With approximately 210,000 species, eudicots represent the vast majority of angiosperm diversity and include most familiar flowering plants and trees. The term "eudicot" (true dicot) replaces the older, paraphyletic category "dicot," which included both eudicots and several basal angiosperm lineages that molecular phylogenetics has now shown to be more distantly related.

Eudicots are characterized by a constellation of anatomical features: two cotyledons in the seed, net-like (reticulate) leaf venation with a prominent midvein and secondary branches, vascular bundles arranged in a ring within the stem (a geometry that enables secondary growth), flower parts typically in fours, fives, or multiples thereof, a taproot system (one primary root with lateral branches), and, in many species, the capacity for secondary growth via a [vascular cambium](2026-04-02-cambium.md) that produces wood.

The two largest eudicot clades are the **Rosids** and the **Asterids**, each containing multiple orders. Rosids include the legumes (Fabaceae: peas, beans, clovers), rose family (Rosaceae: apples, strawberries, almonds), mustard family (Brassicaceae: cabbage, broccoli, rapeseed), and gourd family (Cucurbitaceae: squashes, melons). Asterids include the nightshade family (Solanaceae: potatoes, tomatoes, peppers), mint family (Lamiaceae), parsley family (Apiaceae), and daisy/aster family (Asteraceae: sunflowers, lettuce, chrysanthemums). These families dominate temperate and tropical forests, grasslands, and agricultural ecosystems.

The presence of a vascular cambium and secondary growth enables eudicots to form woody trees and shrubs that can persist for decades or centuries, accumulating biomass and dominating many terrestrial ecosystems. The ring arrangement of vascular bundles allows efficient vertical transport and provides a scaffold for wood formation. This anatomical foundation, combined with diverse leaf forms, reproductive strategies, and life histories, has made eudicots the primary trees and shrubs of Earth's forests.
