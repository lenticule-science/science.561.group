Prion theory studies infectious conformational propagation. Its central object is not an organism, cell, virus, gene, or ordinary toxin, but a protein state that can induce other molecules of the same or related protein to adopt that state.

This theory is placed under biology because prions act inside living systems, depend on cellular proteins and tissues, and can participate in inheritance, pathology, and evolution. That placement does not settle whether prions fit inherited biological categories such as life, organism, pathogen, inheritance, or information. That boundary problem is part of the theory.

## Core orientation

A prion system has at least four components: a carrier protein capable of multiple relatively stable conformational states; a conversion process by which one state increases the probability that another molecule adopts that state; a propagation ecology involving fragmentation, clearance, transport, tissue context, and host background; and a phenotypic consequence, which may be pathological, neutral, adaptive, or experimentally induced.

The theory should not reduce prions to disease. Mammalian transmissible spongiform encephalopathies are the classic pathological case, but yeast and fungal prions show that protein-based inheritance can also operate as a heritable regulatory mechanism. Conversely, not every amyloid, aggregate, or misfolded protein is a prion. The prion condition requires transmissible templating, not merely structural abnormality.

## Relation to semantic-conformation questions

Prion theory is relevant to semantic-conformation work because it supplies an external theory of stable alternative conformations, templated conversion, strain-specific propagation, host-context barriers, and refolding or aggregation. The analogy should not be used loosely. A prion-theoretic comparison must identify a carrier, alternative conformational states, a conversion relation, propagation conditions, and a persistence or strain mechanism.
