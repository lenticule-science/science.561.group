Dermal tissue: the protective outer tissue system that forms the boundary between a plant and its environment. All plant organs (roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruits) are wrapped in dermal tissue, which serves as the primary barrier to water loss, microbial invasion, herbivory, and excessive ultraviolet radiation.

In primary growth (the initial growth phase of most plant organs), the dermal tissue is the **epidermis** — a single layer of tightly packed parenchyma cells. The epidermal surface is coated with a hydrophobic layer called the **cuticle**, composed mainly of wax and cutin, which reduces water evaporation and provides defense against pathogens. Within the epidermis lie specialized structures: **guard cells** that flank [stomata](./stomata.md), regulating gas exchange and water loss; **trichomes**, hair-like outgrowths that can reflect light, reduce water loss, or secrete defensive compounds; and, in roots, **root hairs**, tubular extensions of epidermal cells that dramatically increase absorptive surface area.

In secondary growth — the process by which stems and roots increase in diameter — the epidermis is shed and replaced by the **periderm**, a multi-layered protective tissue produced by the **cork cambium** (a lateral meristem). The periderm consists of three layers: the cork cambium itself (a thin meristematic layer), cork cells (dead at maturity, with suberized walls that are water-repellent), and the phelloderm (a layer of living cells lying inward from the cork cambium). This arrangement forms the bark visible on tree trunks.

The dermal system fulfills multiple functions. It provides a hydrophobic seal that maintains the plant's water balance. It produces trichomes and other defensive structures that deter herbivores, secrete secondary metabolites, or trap dust and insects. [Stomata](./stomata.md) in the epidermis allow gas exchange for [photosynthesis](./photosynthesis.md) while remaining responsive to drought stress. In woody plants, the bark produced by the periderm is an insulating barrier, a repository for secondary metabolites (giving bark its medicinal and industrial value), and a mechanism for compartmentalizing wounds and infections. The dermal system is thus far more than a passive wrapper — it is an active, multi-functional interface.
