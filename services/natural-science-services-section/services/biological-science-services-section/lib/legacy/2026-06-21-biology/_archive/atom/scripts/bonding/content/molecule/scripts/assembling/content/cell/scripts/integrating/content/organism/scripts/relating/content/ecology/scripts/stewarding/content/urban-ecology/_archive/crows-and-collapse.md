There’s a tendency in collapse discourse—particularly among white, settler-descended environmentalists—to imagine collapse as a return. A rewilding. As if the built world might simply fall away and underneath it, a pure wilderness will re-emerge, like some kind of ecological palimpsest. What this obscures is that collapse is not subtraction. It is not an undoing. Collapse is additive. Layered. A thickening of crisis on top of crisis. It is not a return to nature, but the transformation of all environments into compromised zones of adaptation. Collapse is the crows soaking bread in the creek behind my apartment.

Let me explain.

The creek isn’t what most people picture when they think of water and nature. It cuts through a run-down pocket of the city, through and underneath one of the older neighborhoods. There are no trailheads, no overlook signs. But it’s still a corridor. It still hosts life. Most notably: a group of crows.

Every morning, the crows arrive carrying bread. Not fresh loaves, but scavenged remnants. Sometimes white slices. Often donuts. Once, a hamburger bun. They land on the concrete lip of the culvert, dip the bread into the water, set it on a rock, wait a minute, and then eat it. I’ve seen one do it, then another watch, and mimic the behavior. This is learned. This is culture. I’ve watched them teach.

Why do they do it? The bread is too dry, or too chemically treated, to eat comfortably.  It causes digestive issues. But soaked in water—it softens. It’s safer. This isn’t instinctual behavior. This is responsive intelligence. This is adaptation.

And it only makes sense here. In this intersection of human waste, urban waterway, and animal persistence. This isn’t nature in the romantic sense. This is anthropogenic ecology. This is the only kind of ecology left.

The behavior emerges from conditions. The intelligence was always there, but this situation draws it out. That’s what adaptation _is_. And this is the environment that draws it out. It is not a healthy environment. It is not a choice. But it is survivable—if you learn fast, and pass on what you learn.

That’s the real story of collapse. Not a romantic regression to wilderness. But the development of complex coping behaviors in fractured and toxic systems. This is what people mean when they talk about collapse as a process already underway. It is not about some future event horizon. It is about the present. The creek. The bread. The crows.

I moved to Duluth because of a family emergency. I didn’t want to come here. I didn’t have a choice. It wasn’t an adventure or an intentional relocation. It was the kind of move many of us are making now—under duress, in response to a chain of crises that are already shaping our lives. Climate displacement doesn’t always look like wildfires and flooded coastlines.

So I ended up near this creek. And without meaning to, I found myself watching. Patterns. Habits. Morning movements. The crows became familiar. Not pets, not wild in the mythic sense, but co-inhabitants. Neighbors.

And in their behavior, I saw a version of my own. Making use of what’s available. Watching others for how to live here. Teaching what works. Adapting not because it’s noble, but because it’s necessary.

There is a settler fantasy—and it is a fantasy—that after collapse, people will return to the land. Hunt and gather. Build cabins. Form small, pure communities. But that land was never empty. And it’s not empty now. It’s full of chemicals. Property lines. Zoning laws. Utility easements. Wildlife corridors hemmed in by freeways. The people who imagine a clean return ignore both history and present.

They forget that collapse is deeply uneven. Some people are already living in its effects. For Indigenous people, for the disabled, for the poor, for the landless—collapse is not a looming threat, but an ongoing condition. And for all of us, the systems we live under are already destabilizing. Adaptation isn’t optional. It’s happening whether we like it or not.

What the crows show us is that intelligence under collapse isn’t measured by innovation, but by transmission. They teach each other what works. They learn, visibly, collectively. This is not rugged individualism. This is social learning. This is knowledge as survival.

We should be taking notes.

People are so often looking for the big gestures. The solutions. The clean breaks. But collapse doesn’t offer clean anything. It offers the creek behind the apartments. The loaf behind the bakery. The ritual of soaking and eating. The cultural memory of not getting sick.

This is where we are. And this is what we have.

To survive, we have to do what the crows are doing. Not pretend the system isn’t broken. Not dream of some pure world outside it. But learn, and share, and adapt, and do it all in the mess. Because there is no outside the mess.

If the crows can teach each other to make bread safe in a polluted runoff creek, what could we be teaching each other? What do we owe each other, in terms of shared knowledge? In terms of presence? In terms of witness?

We’re not getting out of this clean. But we might get through it.

That’s what the crows are doing.

So watch them.

And teach what you learn.
