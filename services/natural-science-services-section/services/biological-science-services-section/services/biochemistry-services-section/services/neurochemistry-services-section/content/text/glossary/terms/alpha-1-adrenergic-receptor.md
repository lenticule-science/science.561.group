The alpha-1-adrenergic receptor is a Gq-protein-coupled receptor for norepinephrine and epinephrine, expressed in three subtypes and concentrated on vascular smooth muscle, whose activation raises intracellular calcium and produces vasoconstriction [docherty2010]. The **acts-on** relation to norepinephrine names its principal endogenous agonist at the vascular seat.

It differs from the alpha-2 subtype, which is chiefly presynaptic and inhibits further transmitter release, and from the beta subtypes, which couple to Gs and produce vasodilation and cardiac stimulation, so the same catecholamine has opposite vascular effects through different receptors [docherty2010]. **Established.**

The **controlled-by** relation to cortisol is the molecular content of glucocorticoid permissiveness. Munck, Guyre and Holbrook's framework and Sapolsky, Romero and Munck's review establish permissive glucocorticoid action as a class — glucocorticoids enabling other mediators' effects rather than producing the effect themselves [munck1984; sapolsky2000] (**established as a framework**). The direct molecular evidence at this receptor is narrower: Sakaue and Hoffman showed glucocorticoids inducing transcription and expression of the α1B-adrenergic receptor gene in a smooth-muscle cell line [sakaue1991] (**cell-line evidence, not a human in-vivo dose-response**).

Because glucocorticoids upregulate its expression, the vascular effect of a given catecholamine level is not fixed but depends on concurrent glucocorticoid tone, and a person can have abundant catecholamines and poor vasoconstriction at the same time.
