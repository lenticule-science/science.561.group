## 0. Framing

A companion note treated fear-repair pharmacology at the layer where it becomes a clinical intervention: D-cycloserine as an extinction augmenter, CBD trials, the reconsolidation-blockade controversy. This note goes underneath that layer, to the synapse. If safety is something that has to be *settled* into a nervous system rather than merely told to it — written into standing structural change rather than left as a fact held in working memory — then the question of mechanism is the question of what "standing" is made of at the molecular level: which receptor subunits get trafficked where, which transporters clear the transmitter that would otherwise erode the signal, and which retrograde messenger decides whether a given bout of activity is allowed to become a change in wiring at all. The two systems reviewed here — ionotropic/metabotropic glutamate signaling and the endocannabinoid (eCB) system — are not analogies for settlement; they are among its literal substrates. Section 4 returns briefly to the settlement vocabulary to state hypotheses; sections 1–3 are pure mechanism.

## 1. Glutamatergic plasticity of extinction

### 1.1 NMDA receptor subunit composition: GluN2B and GluN2A

Fear conditioning and its extinction are not mirror-image processes run on the same receptor pool; they lean on different NMDA receptor (NMDAR) subunit compositions at the same synapses. In the thalamo-amygdala pathway onto the lateral amygdala (LA), GluN2A-containing NMDARs are more strongly implicated in the induction of long-term potentiation (LTP) that supports fear acquisition, while GluN2B-containing NMDARs are more closely tied to long-term depression (LTD)-like plasticity and to extinction: pharmacological blockade of GluN2A disrupts conditioned fear expression, whereas GluN2B blockade selectively impairs extinction of the conditioned response (Dalton et al., *Neuropharmacology*, 2012, and related lateral-amygdala LTP/LTD dissociation work). This is consistent with the broader developmental and activity-dependent principle that GluN2B-rich synapses are more plastic (slower kinetics, larger charge transfer, more permissive of the calcium signals that drive depression-like weakening), while a shift toward GluN2A predominance — the classic "GluN2B→GluN2A switch" seen with maturation and experience — tends to stabilize synaptic weights.

Extinction pharmacology exploits this same axis from a different subunit angle: D-cycloserine, the partial NMDAR glycine-site agonist used clinically to augment exposure therapy (treated in the companion note), appears to work in part through GluN2C-containing receptors, and direct infusion of a GluN2C/GluN2D-selective potentiator into the basolateral amygdala (BLA) after extinction training enhances extinction retention. More striking is the discovery that GluN2D subunits are required specifically for extinction learning and for extinction-related plasticity in a subclass of amygdala interneurons, while GluN2D deletion leaves original fear acquisition and retention intact (work reviewed in *Frontiers in Synaptic Neuroscience*, 2021, on GluN2D gating of extinction and interneuron plasticity). The overall picture is a division of subunit labor: GluN2A/GluN2B set the acquisition/extinction balance at principal-cell synapses, and GluN2C/GluN2D contribute a separate, more extinction-specific channel — one of the reasons GluN2B-selective and GluN2C/D-selective compounds have both been pursued as extinction enhancers distinct from nonselective NMDAR drugs.

### 1.2 AMPA receptor trafficking: the Clem & Huganir erasure window

The clearest single result linking AMPAR subunit trafficking to the *reversibility* of a fear memory is Clem and Huganir's 2010 *Science* paper, "Calcium-Permeable AMPA Receptor Dynamics Mediate Fear Memory Erasure." Auditory fear conditioning drives synaptic insertion of GluA2-lacking, calcium-permeable AMPARs (CP-AMPARs) at thalamic input synapses onto the LA. This insertion is transient — it decays over roughly a week — and defines a temporal window during which extinction training, rather than merely suppressing the fear response, can drive genuine synaptic depotentiation ("erasure") keyed to phosphorylation state of the GluA1 subunit. Outside that window, the same extinction protocol produces ordinary (context-dependent, renewal-prone) inhibitory learning rather than erasure. This is a mechanistic account of exactly the settlement/suppression distinction: whether extinction produces a new, portable structural change or a fragile, context-bound overlay depends on the receptor population present at the synapse at the moment of re-exposure.

CP-AMPARs reappear on the *other* side of extinction as well. In the "ABA renewal" paradigm (conditioning in context A, extinction in context B, testing the return of fear in context A), renewal is accompanied by a re-enhancement of GluA2-lacking AMPAR activity and GluA1 Ser831 phosphorylation at thalamic-LA synapses, and the CP-AMPAR-selective blocker NASPM microinjected into the LA attenuates renewal (Kim et al., *Molecular Brain* / related lateral-amygdala renewal studies, ~2014–2018). So the same molecular signature — GluA2-lacking, calcium-permeable AMPARs — marks both the labile window in which erasure is possible and the receptor pool whose re-recruitment mediates relapse. This double role is a caution against reading "CP-AMPAR presence" as intrinsically good or bad for settlement; it marks *plasticity availability*, which can be resolved toward either safety or renewed threat depending on what training experience meets it.

### 1.3 Infralimbic vs. prelimbic PFC and BLA microcircuitry

The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) is not a unitary "extinction center." The standard division — prelimbic (PL) cortex driving fear expression, infralimbic (IL) cortex driving extinction recall — is supported by lesion, pharmacological, and optogenetic dissections (Sotres-Bayon & Quirk and successors; Do-Monte et al., *Nature*, 2015, on prefrontal inputs instructing amygdala extinction memory formation). IL projects directly to the BLA and to basomedial amygdala, and IL output is necessary for the neural and behavioral consequences of successful extinction (translational work reviewed at *pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5838104*). PL and IL are not independent; direct excitatory PL→IL projections from layer 5/6 pyramidal neurons have been shown to causally support extinction, meaning "prelimbic drives fear, infralimbic drives safety" is an oversimplification of a circuit where PL activity can also feed the extinction pathway (Ozawa et al., *Nature Neuroscience*, 2020, "Excitatory connections between the prelimbic and infralimbic medial prefrontal cortex").

### 1.4 Intercalated cells as the amygdala's inhibitory gate

The IL does not extinguish fear by directly silencing central amygdala (CeA) output; it recruits the intercalated cell masses (ITCs) — clusters of GABAergic neurons sitting between the BLA and CeA — which inhibit CeA output neurons and thereby gate the behavioral expression of fear. IL excitation of the ITCs is a load-bearing node in the extinction circuit: it is the site where a prefrontal "this context is safe" signal is translated into amygdala output suppression, rather than into erasure of the BLA fear trace itself. This is one reason extinction is widely understood as new inhibitory learning layered over an intact fear memory rather than as necessarily overwriting the original trace — with the important exception carved out by the CP-AMPAR erasure window above, where genuine depotentiation, not just ITC-mediated gating, appears achievable.

### 1.5 mGluR5 and mGluR2/3

Metabotropic glutamate receptors contribute a slower, modulatory layer on top of the ionotropic circuitry. Group I mGluR5, acting within IL neurons, is required for the *consolidation* (next-day recall) of extinction memory without being required for within-session extinction itself: MPEP blockade of mGluR5 in IL leaves the immediate reduction in freezing intact but abolishes its retention, and mGluR5 activation drives changes in IL intrinsic excitability and AMPAR subunit composition that normally follow successful extinction (Fontanez-Nuin, Santini, Porter & Quirk lineage; Sepulveda-Orengo et al., *Journal of Neuroscience*, 2013). Group II mGluR2/3, acting presynaptically at cortical input synapses onto the LA (but not at synapses onto the adjacent CeA), is similarly required for extinction retention and not for within-session extinction — LY341495 microinjected into LA blocks retention specifically (Fitzgerald, Chess, Milad, Orr lineage; *Neuropsychopharmacology*, 2015). Both receptor classes therefore separate the online expression of reduced fear from its conversion into a stored, retrievable memory — a distinction with direct bearing on which forms of "settlement" are structurally durable versus session-bound.

## 2. Glutamate under chronic stress: damage to the clearance and structural machinery

### 2.1 The Popoli/Sanacora/McEwen/Yan synthesis

The 2012 review by Popoli, Yan, McEwen, and Sanacora ("The stressed synapse: the impact of stress and glucocorticoids on glutamate transmission," *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*) remains the organizing synthesis for this section, updated by a 2022 sequel ("The stressed synapse 2.0," same journal). Its core claim: acute stress and glucocorticoid exposure produce a biphasic pattern — a fast, non-genomic increase in glutamate release probability and readily-releasable pool size at cortical and hippocampal synapses (via glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid receptor signaling and increased synapsin activation), followed, under chronic or repeated stress, by dysregulated clearance, altered receptor trafficking, and ultimately structural retraction. Musazzi et al. (*Molecular Psychiatry*, 2010, 2017) showed directly that acute stress and corticosterone increase the readily releasable pool of glutamate vesicles in prefrontal/frontal cortex synaptic terminals, and that this glutamate-release enhancement is sustained rather than transient after acute stress — an early step toward the excess extracellular glutamate load that chronic stress converts into a clearance problem.

### 2.2 Astrocytic EAAT2/GLT-1 downregulation

Glutamate has no enzymatic degradation in the synaptic cleft; its removal depends almost entirely on astrocytic uptake via the transporter GLT-1 (the rodent homolog of human EAAT2). Chronic stress paradigms across multiple models — chronic unpredictable/mild stress, chronic restraint, learned helplessness — reduce GLT-1/EAAT2 expression in hippocampus and cortex, and this reduction tracks with depressive- and anxiety-like behavior (reviewed across several lines converging in the *Popoli/Sanacora* framework; see also work on TNF-α-mediated GLT-1 downregulation and on disrupted astrocyte-neuron glutamine-glutamate cycling in medial PFC). Mechanistically proposed routes include glucocorticoid suppression of GLT-1 transcription, stress-induced circulating TNF-α acting directly on astrocytes, and disruption of the glutamine synthetase step of the glutamate-glutamine cycle. The functional consequence is a synapse that, under chronic stress, both releases more glutamate and clears it more slowly — a double insult that raises ambient (tonic, extrasynaptic) glutamate tone, which in turn favors extrasynaptic NMDAR signaling implicated in excitotoxic and atrophic downstream cascades rather than the synaptic NMDAR signaling that supports adaptive plasticity.

### 2.3 Dendritic remodeling: structural readout of the clearance failure

At the structural level, the Arnsten/McEwen line has repeatedly shown that chronic uncontrollable stress produces dendritic retraction and spine loss specifically in glutamatergic pyramidal neurons of the PFC — a circuit-specific, NMDAR-dependent structural plasticity in which the same receptor machinery that stabilizes useful synapses under normal conditions actively dismantles them under sustained glucocorticoid/glutamate excess (Arnsten, Datta, and colleagues, "Chronic Stress Weakens Connectivity in the Prefrontal Cortex," 2021; earlier "Brain on Stress" work). Hippocampal CA3 apical dendrites and PFC layer-specific pyramidal populations both show this retraction pattern, and the degree of structural change correlates with the degree of behavioral/cognitive impairment in PFC-dependent tasks. This is where clearance failure (2.2) becomes settlement failure: a system that cannot buffer ambient glutamate cannot maintain the dendritic architecture that extinction-related plasticity (1.1–1.5) depends on to consolidate. Reduced synapse and axon numbers in PFC under chronic stress models (e.g., *pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5797661*) is a direct structural correlate.

### 2.4 The Wiehler et al. 2022 fatigue result and clearance physiology

A distinct but mechanistically related line comes from human magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS). Wiehler, Branzoli, Adanyeguh, Mochel, and Pessiglione (*Current Biology*, 2022, "A neuro-metabolic account of why daylong cognitive work alters the control of economic decisions") found that a full day of high-demand cognitive-control task performance produced a measurable rise in glutamate concentration (and glutamate/glutamine ratio) specifically in lateral PFC, not in a control visual-cortex region, and that this rise co-occurred with classic markers of cognitive fatigue — reduced pupillary engagement and a behavioral shift toward less effortful, more impulsive economic choices. The authors interpret this as evidence that sustained cognitive-control activity outpaces local glutamate clearance/recycling capacity over a single day, and that the resulting local glutamate accumulation itself imposes a homeostatic brake on further lPFC engagement (rather than fatigue being a purely subjective or purely dopaminergic phenomenon). This is a non-pathological, acute-timescale demonstration of the same clearance-capacity logic that chronic stress damages structurally: lPFC is a rate-limited system with respect to glutamate handling, and the rate limit is expressed behaviorally as reduced willingness/ability to deploy top-down control — the same control machinery extinction recall depends on (Section 1.3–1.5). What clears the accumulated glutamate overnight is not fully characterized at the human MRS level, but converges with sleep and glymphatic-clearance physiology: sleep-dependent glymphatic flux, driven by norepinephrine-linked vasomotion from locus coeruleus and expanded interstitial space during sleep, is an established general route for interstitial metabolite clearance (Xie et al., *Science*, 2013, "Sleep Drives Metabolite Clearance from the Adult Brain"; more recent noradrenergic-vasomotion mechanistic work, *Cell*, 2024), though direct evidence that glymphatic flux specifically normalizes the Wiehler-type prefrontal glutamate signal overnight is, as of this writing, inferred rather than shown.

### 2.5 Ketamine: NMDAR blockade to AMPA throughput to mTORC1 synaptogenesis

Ketamine's antidepressant mechanism is the pharmacological demonstration that reversing the stress-damaged state described in 2.1–2.3 is achievable through the same glutamatergic machinery that stress corrodes. The canonical model (Li et al., *Science*, 2010, "mTOR-Dependent Synapse Formation Underlies the Rapid Antidepressant Effects of NMDA Antagonists," Duman lab) runs: subanesthetic ketamine preferentially blocks NMDARs on GABAergic interneurons (which are tonically more active and more sensitive to blockade at these doses), producing disinhibition of glutamatergic principal neurons; the resulting burst of presynaptic glutamate release drives increased AMPAR-mediated throughput; this activates mTORC1 signaling and downstream synaptic protein synthesis (including BDNF-dependent pathways), producing new dendritic spine synapses in PFC within hours; and mTOR blockade abolishes both the synaptogenesis and the behavioral antidepressant effect. This is mechanistically continuous with Section 2.3: it is a rapid reversal of exactly the spine-loss phenotype that chronic stress produces.

The details remain genuinely contested. A GluN2B-selective account (Miller et al., *eLife*, 2014, "GluN2B-containing NMDA receptors regulate depression-like behavior and are critical for the rapid antidepressant actions of ketamine") holds that GluN2B receptors are tonically activated by ambient glutamate and therefore disproportionately sensitive to a nonselective blocker like ketamine, which would explain ketamine's apparent GluN2B selectivity despite pharmacological non-selectivity. But GluN2B-selective compounds (e.g., traxoprodil/CP-101,606) have not reliably reproduced ketamine's clinical antidepressant effect, and the R-enantiomer of ketamine — a weaker NMDAR antagonist than the S-enantiomer used in esketamine (Spravato) — shows equal or greater antidepressant-like effect in some rodent models, a dissociation that argues against NMDAR blockade potency being the sole active ingredient (reviewed in *Molecular Psychiatry*, 2018, and *PMC11505277*, 2024). A GluN2D-selective account has separately emerged, implicating GluN2D-containing NMDARs on interneuron subpopulations as sufficient for rapid antidepressant-like effects on their own. Esketamine's FDA approval rests on the S-enantiomer/NMDAR-blockade framework, but the field has not converged on whether NMDAR blockade, its downstream AMPA/mTORC1 cascade, active metabolites (e.g., (2R,6R)-hydroxynorketamine, which shows antidepressant-like activity with minimal NMDAR affinity), or an unrelated action (there is a live and separately contested opioid-receptor-mediated hypothesis for part of ketamine's acute effect) is the necessary and sufficient mechanism. The honest state of the field is: robust rapid AMPA/mTORC1-dependent synaptogenesis is well replicated; which upstream trigger is required to get there is not settled.

## 3. The endocannabinoid system as settlement modulator

### 3.1 2-AG versus anandamide: division of labor

The two principal endocannabinoids, 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG) and anandamide (AEA), are synthesized, degraded, and physiologically deployed differently enough that they should not be treated as interchangeable "cannabinoid tone." 2-AG, degraded primarily by monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL), is the dominant mediator of fast, activity-dependent retrograde signaling — DSI/DSE and eCB-LTD (Section 3.6) — released on-demand by postsynaptic depolarization and calcium influx or by group I mGluR activation. Anandamide, degraded primarily by fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH), behaves more like a *tonic* signal, maintaining a standing level of CB1 activation that sets baseline inhibitory constraint on circuits (most thoroughly worked out in the BLA; Section 3.4). This division matters pharmacologically: chronic MAGL inhibition (chronically elevated 2-AG) produces CB1 desensitization and tolerance, whereas chronic FAAH inhibition (chronically elevated AEA) does not produce comparable CB1 downregulation in the same paradigms — a functional dissociation between the phasic and tonic endocannabinoid pools (reviewed in dual-inhibitor and MAGL/FAAH comparison literature, e.g., *PNAS* 2009 on dual FAAH/MAGL blockade behavioral dissociation).

### 3.2 CB1 retrograde signaling as a circuit-breaker

CB1 receptors sit presynaptically on both glutamatergic and GABAergic terminals across cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala. Postsynaptic depolarization and calcium influx (or mGluR/muscarinic activation) drive on-demand eCB synthesis, which travels retrogradely to presynaptic CB1 and transiently suppresses neurotransmitter release — depolarization-induced suppression of inhibition (DSI) at GABAergic terminals and of excitation (DSE) at glutamatergic terminals. The logic is a circuit-breaker: a postsynaptic cell that has just fired strongly can transiently throttle its own inhibitory or excitatory drive, reshaping the short-term dynamics of the circuit it sits in without any change to synaptic strength per se. Sustained versions of the same signaling — eCB-LTD — convert this transient throttle into a lasting reduction in release probability (Section 3.6), which is the mechanistic bridge from "circuit-breaker" to durable plasticity.

### 3.3 Marsicano's foundational result and its circuit dissection

The foundational demonstration that this system is required for extinction, not merely modulatory of it, is Marsicano, Wotjak, Azad et al., *Nature*, 2002, "The endogenous cannabinoid system controls extinction of aversive memories." CB1-knockout mice showed strongly impaired short- and long-term extinction of auditory fear conditioning with acquisition and consolidation of the original fear memory left intact, and pharmacological CB1 blockade (SR141716A/rimonabant) in wild-type mice reproduced the knockout phenotype — establishing that CB1 signaling is required specifically at the moment of extinction learning, not for fear memory in general. Notably, CB1 was later shown to be dispensable for extinction in an appetitively-motivated (non-aversive) learning task, indicating the requirement is specific to aversive/fear-related extinction rather than a general extinction mechanism (Holter et al., *European Journal of Pharmacology*, 2005) — an important boundary condition rather than a contradiction. Subsequent circuit dissection has localized much of this requirement to eCB gating of GABAergic transmission within the amygdala and to eCB control of glutamatergic input at BLA and prefrontal synapses (Sections 3.4, 3.6, 4), refining the original global-knockout result into a synapse-specific mechanism.

### 3.4 Hill and Patel: tonic anandamide and HPA axis gating

Work from the Hill and Patel laboratories established that basal, tonic anandamide signaling within the BLA constrains both local circuit excitability and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) stress response. Under resting conditions, a standing AEA tone acting on CB1 receptors on BLA glutamatergic terminals suppresses glutamate release onto BLA principal neurons, restraining their excitability; this same tone tonically suppresses HPA axis activation. Acute stress engages corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) signaling within the BLA that increases local FAAH activity, rapidly lowering AEA and thereby releasing both the local glutamatergic brake and the HPA-suppressing brake — i.e., acute stress produces its neuroendocrine and circuit-excitability effects in part *through* a rapid drop in anandamide tone (Hill & Patel lineage; Gray, Hill, Patel and colleagues, *Journal of Neuroscience*, 2010, "Functional Interactions between Stress and the Endocannabinoid System"; Hill review, "Neurobiological Interactions Between Stress and the Endocannabinoid System," *Neuropsychopharmacology*, 2016). Local FAAH inhibition in the BLA, or direct CB1 agonism there, blunts stress-induced HPA activation — demonstrating a pharmacologically actionable node. This tonic-AEA-as-brake framework is distinct from, but complementary to, the phasic 2-AG retrograde signaling of Sections 3.2 and 3.6.

### 3.5 The human FAAH C385A variant

The C385A single-nucleotide polymorphism (rs324420) in the human FAAH gene reduces FAAH protein stability and enzymatic activity, elevating basal anandamide levels in carriers of the A allele. Hariri and colleagues (*Biological Psychiatry*, 2009) first linked the A allele to lower threat-related amygdala reactivity and to a functional-connectivity phenotype consistent with more efficient fronto-amygdala regulation. Gunduz-Cinar, Hariri, Holmes and colleagues (*Molecular Psychiatry*, 2013, "Convergent translational evidence... in amygdala-mediated fear extinction, threat processing and stress-reactivity") extended this with a mouse model carrying the human C385A variant and parallel human imaging: both showed enhanced fear extinction and, critically, faster *habituation* of amygdala reactivity to repeated threat presentation, alongside lower self-reported trait stress-reactivity in human carriers. This is the strongest available human genetic evidence that a single polymorphism shifting eCB tone shifts extinction/habituation capacity in a dose-dependent, translatable way — a natural experiment in individual variation in "settlement rate."

### 3.6 eCB-LTD and the metaplasticity reading

Beyond transient DSI/DSE, sustained eCB-CB1 signaling can produce long-term depression of presynaptic release probability (eCB-LTD) at both excitatory and inhibitory synapses across striatum, hippocampus, amygdala, and cortex. Because eCB-LTD acts presynaptically to durably lower release probability, it functions as a standing modification of how readily a given synapse's activity will translate into postsynaptic depolarization in the future — which is precisely a metaplasticity mechanism: it does not itself encode new information so much as it resets the *gain* on subsequent plasticity induction at that synapse. This reading is reinforced by the demonstration that eCB signaling interacts with, and can gate the induction of, other forms of retrograde and postsynaptic plasticity operating on different timescales (Castillo, Younts, Chávez, Hashimotodani, *Neuron*, 2012, "Endocannabinoid Signaling and Synaptic Function").

### 3.7 CBD's actual pharmacology versus its reputation

Cannabidiol (CBD) is widely marketed and popularly understood as a direct, potent modulator of the endocannabinoid system, but its receptor pharmacology does not straightforwardly support that reputation. CBD has very low affinity for the CB1 and CB2 orthosteric binding sites; its most well-characterized cannabinoid-system action is as a negative allosteric modulator of CB1 — it reduces the efficacy and potency of orthosteric CB1 agonists (including THC and anandamide) without itself substantially activating or blocking the receptor at the orthosteric site (Laprairie, Bagher, Kelly, Denovan-Wright, *British Journal of Pharmacology*, 2015). Whether CBD meaningfully inhibits FAAH at physiologically relevant concentrations in vivo remains debated — some biochemical assays show inhibition, but concentrations required are higher than what is reliably achieved with typical oral dosing, and the clinical significance of any resulting AEA elevation is unresolved. CBD's better-supported mechanisms outside the eCB system proper include agonism at the serotonin 5-HT1A receptor (implicated in its anxiolytic and antidepressant-like effects in animal models) and agonism/desensitization at TRPV1 channels. The practical upshot for a synaptic-mechanism account of settlement: CBD's clinical anxiolytic and anti-fear-relapse effects (treated in the companion clinical-trial note) are not well explained by a simple "CBD boosts endocannabinoid tone" story, and mechanism-level claims about CBD should be routed through 5-HT1A and CB1-negative-allosteric-modulation pathways rather than through FAAH inhibition until that question is better resolved.

### 3.8 BIA 10-2474: what a FAAH-inhibitor disaster taught about off-target risk

In January 2016, a Phase I trial in France of the FAAH inhibitor BIA 10-2474 (Bial, intended as an analgesic/anxiolytic candidate) killed one healthy volunteer and caused lasting brain injury in four others after repeated-dose administration — despite FAAH itself being a well-validated, and by that point extensively studied, target with other selective inhibitors (e.g., PF-04457845) having cleared safety testing without comparable toxicity. Subsequent activity-based protein profiling (van Esbroeck et al., *Science*, 2017) showed that BIA 10-2474, unlike the selective comparator PF-04457845, inhibited several off-target lipases not meaningfully engaged by other FAAH inhibitors, and produced substantial disruption of broader lipid-network metabolism in human cortical neurons in vitro — including engagement of PNPLA6, an enzyme independently implicated in organophosphate- and chemical-warfare-agent-induced neurodegeneration. The lesson generalizes beyond this one compound: FAAH is a validated and apparently safely druggable node (target-level risk was not the problem), but compound-level polypharmacology within a chemically promiscuous lipid-hydrolase family can produce catastrophic, non-obvious toxicity that standard target-based safety reasoning would not have predicted — a caution relevant to any future MAGL- or FAAH-directed strategy for accelerating fear settlement pharmacologically.

### 3.9 Stress-induced eCB adaptations

Chronic stress does not leave the eCB system untouched; it degrades it, in a direction that plausibly compounds the glutamatergic damage of Section 2. Chronic mild or restraint stress paradigms produce CB1 receptor downregulation across essentially every limbic region examined (roughly 50% reductions reported in hippocampus after three weeks of chronic mild stress), together with reduced anandamide across hippocampus, amygdala, striatum, medial PFC, and hypothalamus, and reduced 2-AG specifically in medial PFC. Marcus, Bedse, Patel and colleagues (*Neuron*, 2020, "Endocannabinoid Signaling Collapse Mediates Stress-Induced Amygdalo-Cortical Strengthening") showed this is not merely a passive correlate but a causal driver of pathological circuit change: stress-induced collapse of 2-AG-CB1 signaling specifically at BLA–prelimbic PFC synapses removes the retrograde brake on glutamate release there, producing persistent strengthening of that amygdalo-cortical pathway and anxiety-like behavior, and experimentally disrupting 2-AG-CB1 signaling at that synapse alone is sufficient to phenocopy the stress effect. This closes a loop with Sections 2.1–2.3: chronic stress both increases glutamate release/impairs its clearance directly, and independently removes the eCB retrograde brake that would otherwise have limited that same glutamatergic strengthening — a double disinhibition of the exact pathway (BLA→cortex) whose over-strengthening is a plausible substrate of fear over-generalization and settlement failure.

## 4. The coupling: eCBs as the tuner of glutamatergic settlement rate

Sections 1–3 converge on a single synapse-level picture. Glutamatergic transmission is the substrate that must change for extinction to become durable — subunit switches (1.1), AMPAR trafficking (1.2), and the mGluR-dependent consolidation step (1.5) are all descriptions of how a fear-associated synapse gets weakened and how an extinction-associated one gets strengthened and stabilized. But whether a bout of postsynaptic activity gets to *become* such a change is gated, retrogradely, by the eCB system sitting one step upstream at the presynaptic terminal: CB1-mediated DSE and eCB-LTD directly set glutamate release probability (3.2, 3.6), and both the phasic (2-AG) and tonic (AEA) eCB pools constrain how much glutamatergic drive a circuit like BLA→PFC is allowed to carry at all (3.1, 3.4, 3.9). This is the mechanistic anchor for reading the eCB system as a *metaplasticity tuner* for the glutamatergic settlement machinery rather than as an independent parallel pathway: it does not encode "this is safe" itself, but it sets the gain on how readily glutamatergic synapses in fear-relevant circuits can be pushed toward a new, stable configuration by whatever training experience — extinction, exposure, D-cycloserine-augmented exposure — is on offer. A circuit with intact eCB tone (adequate AEA, functional CB1, undamaged 2-AG-dependent LTD) is a circuit in which glutamatergic plasticity is available for use; a circuit whose eCB tone has collapsed under chronic stress (3.9) is a circuit in which glutamate release is disinhibited but plasticity is *less* controllable — more prone to the kind of unconstrained strengthening that produces over-generalized threat responses than to the constrained, extinction-favoring weakening that produces settlement. The FAAH C385A variant (3.5) is the clearest existing human handle on this tuning parameter, and Hypothesis 1 below is built directly on it.

## Hypotheses

1. **FAAH C385A carriers show faster context-generalization of learned safety, not merely faster within-context extinction.** *Prediction:* elevated tonic anandamide (A-allele carriers) accelerates the transfer of an extinguished safety signal to novel, non-training contexts, over and above the already-documented within-context extinction and amygdala-habituation advantage. *Test:* genotype a cohort for rs324420, run standard fear conditioning/extinction, then test renewal/generalization in a novel context (ABC or AAB renewal design) with fMRI amygdala/vmPFC and, where feasible, PET AEA/FAAH occupancy imaging (e.g., [11C]CURB) to confirm the biochemical gradient tracks genotype. *Kill condition:* if A-allele carriers show the expected within-context extinction/habituation advantage (replicating Gunduz-Cinar et al. 2013) but show no reduction, relative to C-allele homozygotes, in context-renewal magnitude, the tonic-AEA account is specific to habituation/extinction rate and does not extend to generalization — falsifying the stronger "settlement is more portable" claim.

2. **Stress-induced astrocytic EAAT2/GLT-1 downregulation predicts settlement fragility (renewal susceptibility) independent of acquisition strength or extinction learning rate.** *Prediction:* animals or humans with reduced glutamate-clearance capacity in mPFC/BLA will extinguish fear at a normal or near-normal rate (clearance failure does not block plasticity per se) but show elevated spontaneous recovery and context renewal, because ambient glutamate elevation destabilizes the newly formed extinction trace. *Test:* in rodents, use chronic stress or conditional astrocytic GLT-1 knockdown, confirm equivalent acquisition and within-session extinction across groups, then compare renewal/spontaneous-recovery rates; in humans, use ceftriaxone (a GLT-1 upregulator) or MRS-measured glutamate/glutamine ratio in lPFC as a clearance-capacity proxy against extinction-retention/renewal outcomes in a fear-conditioning paradigm. *Kill condition:* if renewal magnitude does not differ between clearance-impaired and clearance-intact groups once extinction learning is matched, clearance capacity is not a settlement-durability factor and the effect (if any) of chronic stress on renewal runs through a different node (e.g., eCB collapse, Hypothesis 3, or dendritic spine loss directly).

3. **eCB tone at the time of memory reactivation determines whether reopening (reconsolidation-window plasticity induction) resettles toward safety or toward threat.** *Prediction:* reactivating a fear memory under low eCB tone (e.g., pharmacological CB1 blockade, or naturalistic states of stress-driven AEA depletion) during a subsequent extinction/reconsolidation-update protocol will bias the outcome toward re-strengthening of fear (via disinhibited glutamate release, cf. Section 3.9) rather than toward updating to safety, whereas normal or pharmacologically elevated eCB tone (FAAH inhibition) at reactivation will bias the same protocol toward successful resettlement. *Test:* in rodents, pair memory reactivation + extinction training with systemic or BLA-local CB1 antagonism (rimonabant/AM251), FAAH inhibition (e.g., PF-04457845 or URB597), or vehicle, and compare subsequent renewal/spontaneous recovery; in humans, this maps onto testing whether acute stress exposure (which transiently depletes BLA AEA per the Hill/Patel mechanism) immediately before a reconsolidation-update or extinction session worsens outcomes relative to a non-stressed control, and whether this is rescued by FAAH-variant genotype or pharmacological pre-treatment where ethically tractable. *Kill condition:* if CB1 blockade at reactivation does not shift the fear/safety balance of the post-reactivation outcome (i.e., extinction proceeds equivalently regardless of eCB tone at the moment of reopening), the "eCB tone gates which way reopening resolves" model is wrong, and eCB signaling should instead be understood as acting only on baseline circuit excitability (3.4) rather than on the valence of plasticity induced during a labile window.

4. **2-AG-specific (MAGL-pathway) and AEA-specific (FAAH-pathway) manipulations produce dissociable effects on phasic extinction-trial learning versus tonic baseline fear reactivity, respectively.** *Prediction:* acute MAGL inhibition (2-AG elevation) will selectively enhance the trial-by-trial rate of within-session extinction (a phasic, DSE/eCB-LTD-dependent process) without much changing baseline (pre-extinction) fear reactivity, while FAAH inhibition (AEA elevation) will selectively lower baseline/tonic fear reactivity and HPA reactivity without proportionally accelerating the within-session extinction rate. *Test:* within-subject or between-group rodent pharmacology comparing a selective MAGL inhibitor (e.g., JZL184) against a selective FAAH inhibitor (e.g., URB597 or PF-04457845) on (a) baseline acoustic startle/freezing and corticosterone response and (b) trial-by-trial extinction curve shape (rate constant) in the same animals, with CB1 antagonist co-administration arms to confirm CB1-dependence of any effect. *Kill condition:* if both inhibitor classes produce statistically indistinguishable effects on both baseline reactivity and extinction-trial rate, the phasic/tonic division of labor between 2-AG and AEA (Section 3.1) does not hold functionally for fear circuitry specifically, even if it holds biochemically for the two lipids' synthesis/degradation kinetics.

5. **Chronic-stress-induced 2-AG-CB1 collapse at the BLA–prelimbic synapse (Marcus et al. 2020) is reversible by a time-limited window of MAGL inhibition, restoring settlement capacity without inducing CB1 tolerance.** *Prediction:* a short (sub-tolerance-inducing) course of MAGL inhibition administered after chronic stress exposure but before extinction training will restore normal BLA-PFC glutamate release probability and normalize subsequent extinction/renewal outcomes, whereas a chronic/continuous course of the same drug will reproduce the CB1-desensitization/tolerance problem documented for long-term MAGL blockade (Section 3.1) and fail to improve, or worsen, extinction outcomes. *Test:* in the chronic-stress rodent model used by Marcus et al., compare short-course versus long-course MAGL inhibitor administration on (a) ex vivo BLA-PFC synaptic glutamate release probability and CB1 receptor density/function, and (b) subsequent fear extinction and renewal behavior. *Kill condition:* if short-course MAGL inhibition fails to normalize BLA-PFC release probability or extinction/renewal outcomes relative to untreated stressed animals, the 2020 causal mechanism, while valid for the acute stress-induction phase, does not license a reversal strategy via the same pathway — meaning the collapse, once established, requires a different (e.g., structural, AEA-pathway, or non-eCB) route to reverse.
