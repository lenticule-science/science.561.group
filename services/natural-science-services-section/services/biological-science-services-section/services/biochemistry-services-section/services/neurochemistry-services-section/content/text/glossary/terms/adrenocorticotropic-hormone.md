Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) is a 39-amino-acid peptide hormone synthesized in the anterior pituitary gland, specifically within corticotrope cells. It is not pre-formed but rather is cleaved on-demand from proopiomelanocortin (POMC), a larger precursor peptide that also yields beta-endorphin and other bioactive fragments. ACTH is released into systemic circulation where it travels throughout the body and acts on melanocortin-2 receptors (MC2R) on cells of the adrenocortical zona fasciculata and reticularis, triggering the synthesis and release of cortisol and other adrenal steroids. The pituitary release of ACTH is itself triggered by CRH from the hypothalamus and is suppressed by negative feedback from circulating cortisol, making ACTH the middle level of the three-tiered hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis.

A critical characteristic of ACTH secretion is that it is not tonic and steady but pulsatile and episodic. ACTH pulses occur roughly every 1–2 hours in the baseline, unstressed state, with larger pulses and accelerated secretion triggered by stress. Because of this pulsatility, a single ACTH measurement is nearly uninterpretable in isolation — an ACTH value of 30 pg/mL might reflect the peak of a normal pulse or the trough between pulses of a suppressed baseline, with radically different implications for HPA axis function. Clinical interpretation therefore requires pairing each ACTH measurement with a simultaneous cortisol measurement: a normal cortisol at normal ACTH suggests intact responsiveness; a low cortisol at normal or elevated ACTH suggests adrenal insufficiency; a normal cortisol at suppressed ACTH suggests pituitary or higher-level insufficiency; and a low cortisol at suppressed ACTH could indicate secondary (pituitary) or tertiary (hypothalamic) insufficiency. This is the reason ACTH stimulation tests (such as the short ACTH or CRH stimulation test) measure cortisol response rather than ACTH itself.

ACTH deficiency — a failure of the pituitary to produce and release ACTH in response to CRH or to stress — results in secondary adrenal insufficiency. This is distinct in clinical consequence from primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease), in which the adrenal cortex itself is destroyed or absent. Secondary insufficiency from ACTH deficiency preserves aldosterone production because the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, not ACTH, is the primary driver of aldosterone synthesis in the adrenal glomerulosa. A patient with secondary insufficiency therefore typically loses cortisol but retains enough aldosterone to prevent severe salt-wasting, whereas a patient with primary Addison's disease loses both cortisol and aldosterone, leading to profound salt-wasting, hyperkalemia, and hypotension as part of the acute adrenal crisis. This distinction has direct therapeutic consequences: secondary insufficiency requires glucocorticoid replacement (cortisol or synthetic equivalents); primary insufficiency requires both glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement (cortisol and fludrocortisone). Misidentifying the level of failure can therefore lead to incomplete or inappropriate replacement therapy.

The pulsatile nature of ACTH secretion and its short half-life of 10–20 minutes in circulating blood means that ACTH levels vary dramatically over the course of a single hour. For this reason, ACTH-based diagnostics require careful attention to timing: morning ACTH is typically higher than evening ACTH in the normal circadian rhythm; ACTH levels rise sharply in response to stress; and ACTH's effect on the adrenal cortex is itself somewhat delayed relative to the ACTH pulse. An ACTH value interpreted without knowledge of the time of day, the patient's stress state, and the simultaneous cortisol level is of minimal clinical value. In research and clinical settings, ACTH and cortisol are therefore always measured together, with the understanding that the relationship between the two — not either value alone — is the informative quantity.
