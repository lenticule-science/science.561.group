Serotonin, or 5-hydroxytryptamine, is a monoamine neurotransmitter synthesized from tryptophan, produced in the raphe nuclei for central signalling and in far larger quantities in the gut, and measured centrally in humans chiefly through its cerebrospinal-fluid metabolite 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid or through experimental depletion of its dietary precursor.

What distinguishes serotonin's place in the affective literature is the gap between its foundational single studies and its aggregate evidence. Linnoila and colleagues examined 36 violent offenders and found cerebrospinal 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid significantly lower in those whose index crime was classified impulsive than in those whose crime was premeditated, proposing the metabolite as a marker of impulsivity rather than of violence. Experimental work pointed the same way: Bjork and colleagues tested 24 men, twelve with and twelve without a history of aggression, across acute tryptophan depletion, tryptophan loading and a food-restricted control, and found a significant condition-by-history interaction under high provocation, with aggressive men more aggressive when depleted. The meta-analytic picture is much weaker. Duke and colleagues pooled 175 independent samples totalling more than 6,500 participants and found a combined weighted correlation between serotonin measures and aggression, anger or hostility of r = -.12, falling to r = -.06 for cerebrospinal 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid specifically — collectively just over one percent of variance. Effect sizes shrank with later publication year, and other-reported aggression correlated positively rather than negatively with serotonin function, so not even the sign of the effect is stable across measurement approaches.

The consequence is that low serotonin cannot be carried into a clinical or research claim as a cause of impulsive aggression. The relationship is reportable as small, heterogeneous and measurement-dependent, and any study design that assumes a large serotonergic effect on aggression is powered against a quantity the aggregate literature does not support.
