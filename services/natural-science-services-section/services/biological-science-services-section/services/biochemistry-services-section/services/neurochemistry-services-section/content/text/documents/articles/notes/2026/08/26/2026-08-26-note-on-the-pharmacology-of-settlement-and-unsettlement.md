The settlement framework treats fear, belief, and perceptual interpretation as claims that occupy one of two states: open (undetermined, available for revision) or settled (resolved, structurally load-bearing, no longer routinely re-derived). Two operations act on this state variable. One consolidates an open claim into settled structure — this is what safety learning is, when it works. The other reopens a claim that settled prematurely or has become maladaptively rigid — this is what psychedelic and dissociative pharmacology appears to specialize in. Both operations have identifiable molecular machinery, and both have a literature honest enough to report failures alongside successes. This note surveys that machinery and ends with hypotheses that follow from treating "settlement" as a real neurobiological operation rather than a metaphor.

A methodological note before the pharmacology: "settlement" here is not being used as a synonym for "learning happened." It specifically denotes the transition from a state in which a claim is actively evaluated against incoming evidence to a state in which it is treated as given — retrieved rather than recomputed, and consequently resistant to disconfirmation that would have moved it while open. This is what makes premature settlement (a fear generalized past its evidentiary basis, a belief foreclosed before revision) pathological in a specific, mechanistic sense, and it is what makes the reopening literature interesting: the drugs discussed in Section 2 do not simply "add information," they appear to lower the threshold at which a settled claim becomes re-evaluable, which is a different pharmacological target than persuasion.

## 1. Consolidating settlement: making safety standing

### 1.1 NMDA-receptor-dependent consolidation and D-cycloserine

Fear extinction is not erasure of the original fear memory; it is new, inhibitory learning that must itself be consolidated into structure, and that consolidation is NMDA-receptor dependent. Foundational work in rats showed that D-cycloserine (DCS), a partial agonist at the glycine site of the NMDA receptor, facilitates extinction when given systemically or infused directly into the amygdala (Walker et al., 2002, *Journal of Neuroscience*), and that post-training NMDA receptor activity in the basolateral amygdala and ventromedial prefrontal cortex is required for extinction memory to consolidate rather than simply occur (Herry et al., 2010; Peters, Kalivas & Quirk, 2009, *Neuron*, on prefrontal bursting). This is the molecular signature of "safety learned into standing": the extinguished response is not available at all unless a genuinely NMDA-dependent consolidation step is allowed to occur after the learning trial.

DCS augmentation of exposure-based therapy in humans is one of the most extensively replicated — and most instructively shrinking — translational stories in anxiety pharmacology. Early trials and a 2008 meta-analysis (Norberg, Krystal & Tolin, *Biological Psychiatry*) reported a clear facilitation effect. But the effect size has not held up uniformly as the literature grew. The largest test of this, an individual-participant-data meta-analysis across 21 trials and >1,000 patients (Mataix-Cols et al., 2017, *JAMA Psychiatry*), found only a small overall augmentation effect on outcome, concentrated in early treatment response rather than end-state functioning, and with considerable heterogeneity across trials. A follow-up individual-participant-data reanalysis (Rosenfield et al., 2019, *Journal of Anxiety Disorders*) traced much of the apparent decline in DCS efficacy across the published record to systematic drift in dosing and timing: later trials increasingly dosed DCS in ways that deviated from the narrow window — a small number of doses, given close in time to the extinction/exposure session — under which the original consolidation mechanism predicts a boost. This is a clean case of a mechanistically well-motivated intervention (facilitate the NMDA-dependent consolidation step) producing a much smaller and more fragile clinical effect than the mechanism alone would predict, once tested at scale and across timing protocols that violate the mechanism's own boundary conditions. For the settlement framework, this is an important cautionary data point: pharmacologically lowering the threshold for consolidation does not guarantee that *contextually appropriate* consolidation occurs — timing relative to the learning event matters as much as the presence of the facilitator.

### 1.2 Sleep-dependent consolidation of extinction

Consolidation of extinction memory is also sleep-dependent, and the effect is not simply "sleep helps memory in general" — REM sleep specifically appears necessary for extinction recall the following day. Selective REM-sleep deprivation after extinction training impairs next-day extinction recall in humans (Spoormaker et al., 2012, referenced in subsequent reviews) and pontine-wave activity during REM sleep is required for fear extinction memory consolidation in rodent studies (Datta and colleagues, *Journal of Neuroscience*, 2013). Human work by Pace-Schott and colleagues has additionally found that sleep promotes *generalization* of extinction rather than simple retention (Pace-Schott et al., 2015) — a finding directly relevant to the settlement framework's concern with context-boundedness, discussed further in Section 1.6 below. Clinically, this literature underlies the observation that PTSD's characteristic sleep disruption (fragmented REM, nightmares) is not merely a symptom riding along with impaired fear regulation but a plausible causal contributor to *why* extinction learned in exposure therapy fails to consolidate into durable standing safety.

### 1.3 The endocannabinoid system in fear extinction

The endocannabinoid system is necessary for extinction learning itself, not merely helpful to it. CB1-receptor-knockout mice show severely impaired short- and long-term extinction of conditioned fear while acquisition and initial consolidation of the fear memory are unaffected (Marsicano et al., 2002, *Nature*) — a striking single dissociation showing that "learning to fear" and "learning that a cue is now safe" are separable pharmacological events, exactly as the settlement framework's distinction between initial settlement and revision would predict. In humans, cannabidiol (CBD), a non-intoxicating phytocannabinoid, enhances consolidation of extinction learning when given after an extinction session (Das et al., 2013, *Psychopharmacology*), and subsequent work has extended this to prevention of spontaneous fear recovery, though effects on cued re-extinction have been more mixed and sometimes null (see the 2023 *Psychopharmacology* replication attempt reporting decreased threat expectancy without enhanced re-extinction).

Translation via FAAH inhibition (elevating endogenous anandamide rather than dosing an exogenous cannabinoid) has been more disappointing. Proof-of-concept human work showed that FAAH inhibition elevates anandamide, enhances recall of fear extinction, and attenuates stress responses in a randomized experimental-medicine trial (Mayo et al., 2020, *Biological Psychiatry*, by way of prior fMRI work; see also the FAAH-inhibitor imaging trial in *Neuropsychopharmacology*, 2020). But when taken to a full clinical trial combining a FAAH inhibitor with internet-delivered CBT for PTSD (n≈100), the drug did no better than placebo despite confirmed target engagement (elevated anandamide) — both arms improved, with no separation attributable to the drug (published in *Neuropsychopharmacology*, 2025). This is a second clean example, alongside DCS, of a mechanistically well-supported consolidation-facilitator failing to translate into a clinically meaningful augmentation once tested rigorously — the endocannabinoid system is evidently necessary for extinction consolidation but boosting one of its ligands pharmacologically is not sufficient to reliably accelerate clinical settlement.

### 1.4 Glucocorticoid augmentation of exposure therapy

Glucocorticoids affect the settlement machinery from the opposite direction of the memory cycle: they facilitate consolidation of new (extinction) memory while simultaneously impairing retrieval of old (fear) memory, a double action that is directly analogous to what one would want from a pharmacological aid to reopening-then-resettlement. De Quervain and colleagues showed in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of virtual-reality exposure therapy for height phobia (n=40) that 20 mg oral cortisol given one hour before each of three exposure sessions produced significantly greater fear reduction at post-treatment and one-month follow-up than placebo (de Quervain et al., 2011, *PNAS*) — roughly a 60% reduction in fear ratings in the cortisol group versus 40% in placebo. Related work (Bentz and colleagues) found that endogenously higher pre-extinction cortisol predicted reduced conditioned fear at later retrieval, and a broader review traces the translational arc from animal extinction models to these human exposure-therapy trials (Bentz et al., reviewed in de Quervain, Schwabe & Roozendaal, 2018, *Psychopharmacology* / *Neuropsychopharmacology* reviews). As with DCS, the facilitating effect depends critically on the frequency and precise timing of administration relative to the exposure session — again consistent with the view that pharmacological consolidation aids work by narrowing a temporal window around the learning event rather than by acting as a general potentiator.

### 1.5 Propranolol and reconsolidation blockade — a genuine controversy

The most theoretically radical claim in this literature is not about consolidating new extinction memory but about directly editing old, already-settled fear memory during reconsolidation. Kindt, Soeter & Vervliet (2009, *Nature Neuroscience*) reported that oral propranolol given before reactivation of a consolidated fear memory in humans abolished the fear-potentiated startle response 24 hours later and prevented the return of fear — described by the authors as "erasing" the emotional expression of the memory while leaving declarative memory intact. This result, if it generalized, would be a strong existence proof that a settled claim's affective valence can be pharmacologically decoupled from its propositional content, rather than merely inhibited context-by-context as ordinary extinction requires.

It has not generalized cleanly. A series of high-profile studies failed to replicate the amnestic effect of propranolol on reconsolidated fear responding, including Bos et al. (2014), Thome et al. (2016), Schroyens et al. (2017), and Luyten et al. (2021) (the last published as "Lack of Effect of Propranolol on the Reconsolidation of Conditioned Fear Memory due to a Failure to Engage Memory Destabilisation," *Neuroscience*/*Behaviour Research and Therapy*, 2021). A comprehensive systematic review (Elsey, Van Ast & Kindt, 2018, *Psychological Bulletin*, "Human Memory Reconsolidation: A Guiding Framework and Critical Review of the Evidence") concluded that the human reconsolidation-interference literature contains a substantial number of findings consistent with reconsolidation blockade alongside a substantial number of null and inconsistent findings and plausible alternative explanations, and that reconsolidation's presence cannot yet be conclusively inferred at the neurobiological level from human behavioral data alone. The leading resolution offered within the field itself is a boundary-conditions account: reconsolidation blockade requires that the memory first be behaviorally *destabilized* upon retrieval (via prediction error, i.e., some violation of expectation during reactivation), and many null results plausibly reflect failure to engage destabilization rather than failure of the pharmacological blocker per se. This is directly relevant to the settlement framework: it suggests reconsolidation-based reopening is not a simple pharmacological switch but requires that the retrieval event itself already contain the informational signature of an open, re-evaluable claim (a prediction error) before the drug can act on it — settlement strength is not uniformly permeable, and the destabilization requirement is itself a "steering condition" internal to the fear-memory literature, prefiguring Section 3.

### 1.6 The renewal effect as the signature of incomplete generalization

Across all four consolidation mechanisms above, the clinical Achilles' heel is the same: extinction memory is context-bound in a way the original fear memory is not. The renewal effect — recovery of a conditioned fear response when context changes after extinction, even though the extinguished stimulus itself is unchanged — is the best-established behavioral signature of this asymmetry (Bouton, 1994, foundational theoretical account; Vervliet, Craske & Hermans, 2013, "Extinction, generalization, and return of fear: a critical review of renewal research in humans," *Annual Review of Clinical Psychology* / *Behaviour Research and Therapy*). Extinction inhibits fear expression within the context in which extinction training occurred; it does not overwrite the original association. This is precisely the "context by context" character the settlement framework attributes to safety learning: standing safety is not a single global re-settlement of a "this is dangerous" claim to "this is safe," but a context-indexed inhibitory structure layered on top of the still-intact original settlement. Practically, this motivates multiple-context extinction protocols, which measurably attenuate renewal and reinstatement relative to single-context extinction in both animal and human studies (e.g., studies using extinction across multiple virtual-reality contexts). Any pharmacological consolidation aid (DCS, CBD, cortisol) that strengthens extinction memory without addressing its context-specificity should be expected to reduce fear within the treatment context while leaving renewal risk largely intact — a prediction taken up directly in Hypothesis 1 below.

## 2. Reopening: relaxing settled priors

### 2.1 REBUS: a computational account of pharmacological reopening

The REBUS model (RElaxed Beliefs Under pSychedelics; Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019, *Pharmacological Reviews*, "REBUS and the Anarchic Brain: Toward a Unified Model of the Brain Action of Psychedelics") gives the settlement framework's reopening operation its most direct existing computational analogue. Within a hierarchical predictive-coding / free-energy framework, high-level beliefs (priors) normally suppress bottom-up prediction error, keeping perception and cognition stable and "settled." REBUS proposes that agonism at cortical 5-HT2A receptors — the shared pharmacological target of classic serotonergic psychedelics (psilocybin, LSD, DMT) — reduces the precision-weighting (confidence) of these high-level priors, which "relaxes" them and allows previously suppressed bottom-up signal, especially from limbic and other subcortical sources, to reach and update higher-level representations. The result, at the level of neural dynamics, is increased spontaneous brain entropy and criticality; at the level of cognition and self-report, it is precisely a state in which "rigid or maladaptive" beliefs — the model's own language — become more available for revision. This maps onto the settlement framework's reopening operator almost without translation: 5-HT2A signaling is proposed as the molecular lever that lowers the confidence (precision) attached to a settled claim, making it provisional again rather than retrieved as given.

### 2.2 5-HT2A-dependent structural plasticity: psychedelics as psychoplastogens

REBUS describes a computational/functional relaxation; a separate and largely independent line of cellular work shows psychedelics also produce structural plasticity through the same 5-HT2A pathway, giving reopening a plausible synaptic substrate. Ly and colleagues (2018, *Cell Reports*, "Psychedelics Promote Structural and Functional Neural Plasticity") introduced the term "psychoplastogen" for this class of compounds and showed that serotonergic psychedelics, ketamine, and MDMA all increase dendritic arbor complexity, spine density, and synaptogenesis in cortical neurons, largely 5-HT2A-dependent for the classic psychedelics. In vivo two-photon imaging subsequently showed psilocybin induces rapid (within 24 hours) and unusually persistent (weeks) growth of new dendritic spines in mouse frontal cortex after a single dose (Shao et al., 2021, *Neuron*, "Psilocybin induces rapid and persistent growth of dendritic spines in frontal cortex in vivo"). More recent work has localized the relevant 5-HT2A pool to an *intracellular* receptor population, with a lipid-permeable agonist able to trigger plasticity that a non-permeable one cannot despite equivalent receptor occupancy (Vargas et al., 2023, *Science*, "Psychedelics promote neuroplasticity through the activation of intracellular 5-HT2A receptors") — a mechanistic detail suggesting the "reopening" signal depends on where the receptor is engaged, not merely whether it is engaged, with obvious (currently unrealized) implications for designing non-hallucinogenic reopening agents.

### 2.3 The psilocybin-therapy evidence base and its blinding problem

The clinical evidence most often cited for reopening-as-therapy is the psilocybin depression literature: a phase 2 double-blind trial comparing psilocybin (two 25 mg doses) against daily escitalopram found psilocybin at least as effective as escitalopram on the primary depression outcome over six weeks, with several secondary outcomes favoring psilocybin (Carhart-Harris et al., 2021, *New England Journal of Medicine*, "Trial of Psilocybin versus Escitalopram for Depression"). A separate phase 2b dose-finding trial in treatment-resistant depression (n=233) found a single 25 mg dose of COMP360 psilocybin produced significantly greater MADRS reductions than 1 mg at three weeks (Goodwin et al., 2022, *NEJM*, "Single-Dose Psilocybin for a Treatment-Resistant Episode of Major Depression").

Both trials, and the field generally, face a blinding problem serious enough to be treated as a first-order threat to validity rather than a footnote. Psilocybin's acute subjective effects are unmistakable, so functional unblinding is close to total: a systematic review of 112 randomized psychedelic trials found psilocybin, LSD, and ayahuasca studies frequently reported blinding-failure rates above 90% among both participants and raters, yet only 29.5% of trials formally evaluated blinding integrity at all even though 57.1% cited it as a limitation (Butler et al., systematic review, *JAMA Psychiatry*, 2025/2026, "Blinding Integrity in Psychedelic Randomized Clinical Trials"). A pointed 2023 critique of the escitalopram-comparison trial specifically showed patients had markedly higher outcome expectancy for psilocybin than for escitalopram, and that — while expectancy for escitalopram predicted escitalopram response — psilocybin expectancy did not significantly predict psilocybin response in that dataset, complicating (without resolving) a straightforward "it's all expectancy" dismissal (reported in *Psychological Medicine*, 2023, "Assessing expectancy and suggestibility in a trial of escitalopram v. psilocybin for depression"). Independent commentators (Muthukumaraswamy and colleagues, 2022, *European Neuropsychopharmacology*/*Nature Medicine* correspondence, "On blinding and suicide risk in a recent trial of psilocybin-assisted therapy for treatment-resistant depression") and a broader methodological critique (van Elk & Fried, 2023, *Therapeutic Advances in Psychopharmacology*, "History Repeating: Guidelines to Address Common Problems in Psychedelic Science"; see also van Elk & Yaden, 2022, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*, "Pharmacological, Neural, and Psychological Mechanisms underlying Psychedelics: A Critical Review") have argued that the field's core efficacy claims cannot currently be cleanly separated from expectancy, demand characteristics, and the psychotherapeutic scaffolding delivered alongside the drug — prompting the U.S. National Institute of Mental Health to signal lower funding priority for psychedelic trials that do not rigorously assess blinding integrity. Reported in an "equal-unblinding" reanalysis comparing psychedelic therapy against open-label antidepressant controls (2025/2026), effect sizes shrink, though do not vanish, once functional unblinding is matched across arms. The honest summary: the psilocybin-therapy effect is real by the standard of standing regulatory and trial evidence, but its magnitude is currently confounded with the very feature (subjectively unmistakable acute drug effects, delivered inside an intensive relational/therapeutic frame) that the reopening model says should matter mechanistically — which is a genuine problem for inference, not merely a nuisance for blinding statisticians.

### 2.4 Ketamine as rapid plasticity induction

Ketamine offers a mechanistically distinct and more experimentally tractable route to induced reopening, via NMDA-receptor antagonism rather than 5-HT2A agonism. A single sub-anesthetic dose rapidly activates the mTOR signaling pathway in rodent prefrontal cortex, increasing synaptic protein synthesis and the number and function of new dendritic spine synapses within hours; blocking mTOR signaling abolishes both the synaptogenesis and the antidepressant-like behavioral effect (Li et al., 2010, *Science*, "mTOR-Dependent Synapse Formation Underlies the Rapid Antidepressant Effects of NMDA Antagonists," Duman lab). Moda-Sava et al. (2019, *Science*, "Sustained rescue of prefrontal circuit dysfunction by antidepressant-induced spine formation") extended this with longitudinal in vivo imaging, showing ketamine selectively reverses stress-induced spine loss and restores coordinated multicellular ensemble activity in prefrontal microcircuits, with the durability of the behavioral antidepressant effect tracking the durability of the new spines rather than the (much shorter) pharmacokinetic half-life of the drug itself — direct evidence that the clinically relevant action is the plasticity window it opens, not the drug's ongoing presence.

### 2.5 Dölen and colleagues: psychedelics reopening developmental critical periods

The most direct empirical anchor for "reopening" as a literal, dateable neurobiological event — rather than a metaphor for any drug that happens to help — is Gül Dölen's critical-period work. Nardou, Lewis, Rothhaas, Xu, Yang, Boyden & Dölen (2019, *Nature*, "Oxytocin-dependent reopening of a social reward learning critical period with MDMA") showed that the developmental critical period for social reward conditioned place preference, normally closed by adulthood in mice, can be pharmacologically reopened by a single MDMA dose acting through oxytocin-receptor-dependent plasticity — the reopened window persisted for roughly two weeks, and, notably, MDMA reopened the critical period only when administered while the animal was with a social partner, not in isolation, an early and striking demonstration that the drug's molecular action and the animal's concurrent context jointly determine what gets relearned.

A 2023 follow-up (Nardou et al., 2023, *Nature*, "Psychedelics reopen the social reward learning critical period") generalized this from MDMA alone to a full panel of pharmacologically distinct psychedelics — ketamine, psilocybin, LSD, and ibogaine — all of which reopened the same critical period, establishing reopening as a shared downstream property of a chemically heterogeneous drug class rather than an MDMA-specific idiosyncrasy. Critically, the *duration* of the reopened window tracked each drug's acute subjective-effect duration in humans: ketamine's reopening lasted about 48 hours (matching its brief 30 minutes–2 hour subjective effect), psilocybin's about two weeks, LSD's about three weeks, and ibogaine's about four weeks — the longest of the panel. Mechanistically, the paper frames this as induced metaplasticity (an "open state" in which synapses become newly sensitive to oxytocin and other plasticity-gating signals) accompanied by reorganization of the perineuronal-net extracellular matrix that normally locks circuits into closed-critical-period configurations, with differential gene expression between open and closed states as supporting evidence. A subsequent companion paper (Nardou/Dölen group, 2023, *Communications Psychology*, "Psychedelics reopening windows of development in mice") and a 2024 review (Nardou, Dölen, et al., *Annual Review of Neuroscience*, "The Emerging Neurobiology of Psychedelics: Critical Periods, Metaplasticity, and Extracellular Matrix Remodeling") consolidate this into a general framework: psychedelics act as "master keys" reopening critical periods across multiple domains (not only social reward), with duration-of-opening dose-titratable by choice of compound. This is the cleanest existing literature-level operationalization of "controlled reopening" in the theory context — it is dateable, dose/compound-titratable in duration, and mechanistically distinct from (though likely convergent with) the REBUS/5-HT2A account for the classic psychedelics.

## 3. The steering condition: does holding determine whether reopening resettles well

The theory's central empirical wager — that a relaxed field (safety, holding, adequate context) is what determines whether reopening resettles beneficially rather than harmfully — has partial, suggestive, but not conclusive support in the existing trial literature, mostly indirect.

**Therapeutic alliance as a moderator, not just a correlate.** In psilocybin-assisted-therapy trials for depression, therapeutic alliance measured during preparation sessions (before the drug is given) predicts depression outcomes not only at 4 weeks but at 6 and 12 months post-treatment, and formal mediation analyses indicate this operates substantially *through* its effect on the acute drug experience — stronger pre-dosing alliance predicts more mystical-type and emotional-breakthrough experience during the session, which in turn predicts better outcome (Murphy et al., 2021/2022, *ACS Pharmacology & Translational Science* / *Frontiers in Pharmacology*, "Therapeutic Alliance and Rapport Modulate Responses to Psilocybin Assisted Therapy for Depression"; a related PLOS ONE analysis, "The therapeutic alliance between study participants and intervention facilitators is associated with acute effects and clinical outcomes in a psilocybin-assisted therapy trial for major depressive disorder," 2024). This is evidence that the *relational holding present before the reopening event even occurs* shapes what happens once priors are relaxed — precisely the steering-condition claim, though the effect is observational/correlational within single-arm or open-label designs rather than experimentally manipulated.

**Predictors of challenging (potentially harmful) experiences.** The Challenging Experience Questionnaire literature (Barrett, Bradstreet, Leoutsakos, Johnson & Griffiths, 2016, *Journal of Psychopharmacology*) and subsequent large surveys (Carbonaro et al., 2016; Haijen et al., 2018, "Predicting Responses to Psychedelics: A Prospective Study") identify dose, lack of prior psychedelic experience, younger age, and — most relevant here — lower psychological preparedness and less trust in the setting as predictors of more intense and more difficult challenging experiences. Carbonaro et al. additionally found a dissociation the steering-condition hypothesis would predict: greater *duration* of difficulty during a challenging experience was associated with *worse* subsequent well-being, while greater *degree* of difficulty (intensity) was, conditional on the experience being processed, sometimes associated with *better* subsequent well-being — consistent with a picture in which difficulty itself is not the harm-determining variable, but whether the difficult (reopened) material gets adequately held and resolved is. A 2023 exploratory post-hoc analysis in a healthy population ("Predicting the Intensity of Psychedelic-Induced Mystical and Challenging Experience," *Neuropsychiatric Disease and Treatment*) further associates neuroticism with elevated risk of challenging experience, i.e., a trait-level proxy for baseline settlement fragility predicting worse response to induced reopening — directly analogous to the theory's prediction that reopening a fragile settlement without adequate holding goes worse than reopening a robust one.

**Preparation and integration as structural, not incidental, components.** Clinical trial protocols (the COMPASS/COMP360 "psychological support model," critiqued and defended respectively in Yaden et al., 2022, *American Journal of Psychiatry*, "Must Psilocybin Always 'Assist Psychotherapy'?" and the COMPASS model's own 2023 *AJP* description) formalize preparation, in-session support, and integration as three structurally distinct phases, explicitly built around the premise that the safety and quality of the surrounding relational field determines the clinical value of the drug-induced state — treatment manuals from this tradition (building on Phelps, 2017, *Journal of Humanistic Psychology*, "Developing Guidelines and Competencies for the Training of Psychedelic Therapists," which frames "set and setting" as a co-created "therapeutic fourth") are, in effect, applied engineering built on an unstated version of the steering-condition hypothesis, without (to date) a factorial trial that isolates holding quality as an experimentally manipulated variable independent of dose or drug identity. That gap — steering-condition doctrine embedded in every current trial protocol, but never itself the randomized factor — is precisely what Hypothesis 4 below is designed to close.

## Hypotheses

**1. Context-boundedness of pharmacologically augmented consolidation.**
(i) *Prediction:* Pharmacological augmentation of extinction consolidation (DCS, cortisol, CBD) strengthens fear inhibition within the training context but does not proportionally reduce the renewal effect when context changes, because these agents accelerate the existing (context-bound) consolidation mechanism rather than altering its context-specificity.
(ii) *Discriminating test:* Reanalyze (or newly run) a DCS/cortisol-augmented exposure trial with a formal ABA or ABC renewal test (extinction in context A, augmented vs. placebo; test in context A vs. novel context B), comparing the augmentation-vs-placebo effect size separately in the same-context and novel-context test conditions. Existing datasets combining pharmacological augmentation with multi-context or virtual-reality test designs (e.g., the VR-height-phobia and VR multi-context extinction paradigms already used by de Quervain's and Vervliet's groups) could be reanalyzed for this contrast without new data collection.
(iii) *Disproof condition:* If augmentation reduces renewal-context fear at least as much as same-context fear (i.e., the augmentation-by-context interaction is null or reversed), this hypothesis is false — it would indicate these agents act on a genuinely context-general safety representation rather than merely accelerating context-bound inhibitory consolidation, contrary to the settlement framework's "context by context" claim.

**2. Reopening duration as a titratable, drug-identity-linked variable, not a uniform "psychedelic effect."**
(i) *Prediction:* Because Nardou et al. (2023) show reopening duration scales with each compound's subjective-effect duration (ketamine ~48h, psilocybin ~2wk, LSD ~3wk, ibogaine ~4wk) in mice, the *within-person window during which therapeutic reprocessing/integration work is maximally effective* should scale similarly by compound in humans — i.e., integration sessions timed to compound-specific windows should outperform a fixed integration schedule applied uniformly across drugs.
(ii) *Discriminating test:* A trial (or retrospective analysis of existing multi-compound psychedelic-therapy registries/cohorts) comparing outcome as a function of the interval between dosing and the primary integration session, separately for short-window (ketamine) versus long-window (psilocybin/LSD/ibogaine) protocols, testing whether outcome peaks at a compound-specific optimal delay rather than a universal one.
(iii) *Disproof condition:* If therapeutic outcome is insensitive to integration timing across all compounds, or if the optimal integration delay is uniform regardless of drug identity, the "open state" duration described in mice does not translate to a human treatment-relevant plasticity window, and reopening-duration titration is falsified as a design principle.

**3. Baseline settlement fragility predicts vulnerability to reopening, independent of dose.**
(i) *Prediction:* Trait measures indexing fragile/pre-destabilized settlement (elevated neuroticism, higher baseline experiential avoidance, or a documented history of premature/overgeneralized fear settlement such as diagnosed PTSD or panic disorder) predict a *steeper* dose-response relationship between psychedelic dose and challenging-experience intensity than in individuals without such markers, over and above the already-documented main effect of dose.
(ii) *Discriminating test:* A moderation (dose × trait) analysis in existing psilocybin dose-finding datasets that already collected baseline neuroticism/trauma-history measures (e.g., the COMP360 phase 2b dataset, or the healthy-volunteer dataset behind the 2023 NDT post-hoc analysis), testing for a dose-by-trait interaction on Challenging Experience Questionnaire scores, not merely main effects of each.
(iii) *Disproof condition:* If the dose–challenging-experience slope is statistically indistinguishable across trait-fragility strata (no interaction), settlement fragility as operationalized by these trait proxies does not moderate reopening risk, and the theory's claim that prior settlement quality — not just drug dose — determines reopening safety is falsified for this operationalization.

**4. Holding quality is a manipulable moderator of resettlement valence, independent of drug and dose.**
(i) *Prediction:* Experimentally varying relational/environmental holding quality (e.g., randomizing strength of pre-session alliance-building, sitter presence/absence or training level, environmental control) while holding compound and dose fixed produces a detectable shift in the *direction* of outcome (proportion of participants showing sustained improvement vs. sustained harm/worsening at follow-up), not merely its magnitude.
(ii) *Discriminating test:* A factorial trial (not yet run, to our knowledge, as of this note) crossing a validated holding-quality manipulation (e.g., therapist-training level, or a structured high-alliance vs. minimal-contact preparation protocol) with fixed psilocybin dose, powered to detect an interaction on a bidirectional outcome measure (e.g., proportion above vs. below a reliable-change threshold, not just mean symptom change). Short of a new trial, a secondary analysis stratifying existing multi-site trials (which vary somewhat in site-level alliance/preparation quality) by site-level alliance scores, testing whether site explains variance in *adverse* outcomes specifically (not just efficacy) would be a partial, currently available test.
(iii) *Disproof condition:* If holding-quality manipulation shifts only the magnitude of a uniformly positive (or uniformly null) outcome distribution, without changing the rate of negative/harmful outcomes, then holding functions as an ordinary efficacy moderator like any other (e.g., like dose) rather than as the specific safety-valence gate the theory proposes — falsifying the claim that holding is the necessary condition separating beneficial from harmful reopening, as opposed to merely a magnitude booster.

**5. Reconsolidation-based reopening requires an explicit destabilization signal; pharmacology alone is insufficient.**
(i) *Prediction:* Propranolol (or another reconsolidation blocker) will reliably attenuate fear responding only in retrieval protocols that include a documented prediction-error/destabilization component (e.g., partial reinforcement, unexpected omission of the expected outcome during reactivation), and will fail to do so in retrieval protocols that reactivate the memory without violating its prediction — directly explaining the split between the Kindt (2009) positive result and the Bos/Thome/Schroyens/Luyten replication failures as a difference in whether destabilization was engaged, not as a difference in propranolol's pharmacology.
(ii) *Discriminating test:* A systematic (or already-partially-available, via Elsey, Van Ast & Kindt 2018's review dataset) meta-regression coding each existing propranolol-reconsolidation study for presence/absence and strength of a destabilizing prediction-error manipulation during reactivation, testing whether that coded variable — rather than sample, dose, or lab — predicts which studies replicate the amnestic effect.
(iii) *Disproof condition:* If studies with clearly documented, strong destabilization manipulations fail to replicate the propranolol effect at rates statistically indistinguishable from studies without such manipulations, the destabilization-boundary-condition account is falsified, and the propranolol replication failures must be attributed to something else (e.g., a fragile or non-existent underlying reconsolidation-blockade phenomenon in humans generally) — which would itself be an important, harder-edged negative result for pharmacological reopening-of-old-settlement claims generally.
