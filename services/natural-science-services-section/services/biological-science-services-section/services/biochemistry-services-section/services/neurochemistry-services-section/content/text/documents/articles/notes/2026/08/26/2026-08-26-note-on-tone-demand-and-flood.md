A companion note treated the endocannabinoid system's role in fear extinction — how retrograde 2-AG signaling at amygdalar synapses helps consolidate a learned "safety" context. This note does not revisit that circuitry. Its object is more general: to ask whether the architecture that makes eCB signaling useful for extinction — synthesis on demand, release exactly where and when postsynaptic activity calls for it, destruction within the same terminal field — is in fact the cleanest existing pharmacological demonstration that a molecule's identity as a signal is inseparable from its *routing*. The test case offered from outside pharmacology is IL-6: the identical cytokine drives regenerative, anti-inflammatory signaling through its membrane-bound receptor (classic signaling, restricted to cells that express IL-6R) and drives pro-inflammatory, often pathological signaling through the same cytokine bound to a soluble receptor fragment complexed with gp130 on cells that never expressed IL-6R at all (trans-signaling). Same ligand, opposite biology, and the switch is not concentration or structure but which receptor complex the molecule happens to encounter. The claim under test here is that the endocannabinoid system runs a structurally similar experiment on every synapse, continuously, and that the pharmacology of exogenous cannabinoids is best read as a natural experiment in what happens when that routing is bypassed.

The note also inherits a second distinction from the originating research program: "spotlight" holding — a state maintained only by continuous, costly input, which collapses the moment supply stops — versus "standing" holding — a state that is structural, self-sustaining, and cheap to maintain once built. Vesicular neurotransmission and circulating hormone signaling both look more like spotlight holding at the systems level (transmitter must be continuously synthesized, packaged, and shipped; hormone must be continuously secreted to maintain a blood level). The claim to be tested is that the eCB system inverts this: nothing is held in the spotlight sense at all, because nothing is stored — the "holding" is standing in a stranger sense, encoded not in a reservoir of ligand but in the standing availability of precursor lipid and synthetic enzyme, ready to be converted into signal within milliseconds of the triggering event and gone again within seconds. Sections 1–5 work through the mechanism; the hypotheses at the end try to make the analogy falsifiable rather than merely evocative.

## 1. The architecture's unusual logic

Endocannabinoids are not neurotransmitters in the classical sense, and the ways they fail to fit that template are exactly the ways they support the "routed position, not presence" thesis.

**No storage.** Anandamide (AEA, arachidonoylethanolamide) and 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG) are not packaged into vesicles and are not pre-formed. They are synthesized from membrane phospholipid precursors at the moment they are needed. 2-AG is produced predominantly by diacylglycerol lipase-α (DAGLα), which cleaves diacylglycerol (itself generated by phospholipase C-β downstream of Gq-coupled receptor activation or postsynaptic Ca²⁺ influx) into 2-AG. DAGLα knockout abolishes depolarization-induced suppression of excitation/inhibition (DSE/DSI) in cerebellum, hippocampus, and striatum, establishing it as the enzyme responsible for the bulk of activity-dependent retrograde 2-AG signaling (Gao et al. 2010, *J. Neurosci.*; Tanimura et al. 2010, *Neuron*). Anandamide's biosynthesis is more distributed across parallel pathways, of which the best characterized runs through N-acylphosphatidylethanolamine-hydrolyzing phospholipase D (NAPE-PLD), though NAPE-PLD knockout mice retain substantial brain AEA, indicating redundant routes (a PLC/phosphatase route and an ABHD4-mediated route are the leading alternatives). Structurally, DAGLα is positioned postsynaptically at the base of dendritic spines, immediately adjacent to the machinery — mGluR5, Gq-coupled receptors, voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels — that triggers its activation; NAPE-PLD is concentrated presynaptically in some populations, consistent with anandamide occasionally acting in a non-retrograde or autocrine mode at those synapses.

**Retrograde direction.** The canonical eCB circuit runs backward relative to a conventional synapse: postsynaptic activity (depolarization, mGluR or Gq-GPCR activation, or coincident pairing of the two) drives on-demand synthesis of 2-AG at the postsynaptic membrane; 2-AG then diffuses across the synaptic cleft to activate presynaptic CB1 receptors, which are Gi/o-coupled and suppress neurotransmitter release by inhibiting presynaptic Ca²⁺ channels and activating GIRK-type K⁺ channels. This is the mechanistic basis of DSE, DSI, and eCB-dependent long-term depression at both excitatory and inhibitory synapses. No other major CNS transmitter system routinely signals backward across the cleft as its primary mode.

**Local, fast, enzyme-segregated degradation.** Termination is not diffusional dilution into extracellular space (as for many neuropeptides) or reuptake into a transporter-bearing terminal (as for monoamines) but immediate local hydrolysis by enzymes whose subcellular addresses mirror the direction of signaling. Monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL) is presynaptic, accounting for roughly 80–85% of brain 2-AG hydrolysis, positioned exactly where CB1 receptors sit and where the signal needs to be extinguished once it has done its retrograde job. Fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) is predominantly postsynaptic and somatodendritic — in Purkinje cells, for instance, FAAH immunoreactivity fills the cell body and dendrites while MAGL immunoreactivity fills the presynaptic parallel-fiber terminals that synapse onto them, a near-perfect anatomical complementarity documented by Gulyas et al. (2004, *Eur. J. Neurosci.*) across hippocampus, cerebellum, and amygdala. Because FAAH sits postsynaptically, close to where anandamide is made, it is well positioned to act as a brake on local AEA accumulation and to keep AEA signaling in the tonic, low-amplitude regime discussed in Section 2, whereas MAGL's presynaptic position lets it terminate phasic 2-AG transients precisely at the site where CB1 must be reset for the next round of retrograde signaling.

**Non-vesicular transport.** Endocannabinoids are lipids, membrane-soluble by default, and their movement across the synaptic cleft and into cells does not obviously require a dedicated transporter in the way monoamine or amino acid transmitters do (though the question of a facilitated "anandamide membrane transporter" remains contested — see Section 5's caution about single-target reasoning). Intracellularly, once released from the membrane, both AEA and 2-AG associate with fatty-acid-binding proteins (FABP5, FABP7, and albumin-like chaperones) for cytosolic trafficking to FAAH or to CB1; FABP5 knockdown or pharmacological FABP inhibition elevates extracellular/functional eCB tone, indicating these carriers are rate-limiting for intracellular routing (Haj-Dahmane et al. 2018; Kaczocha lab work reviewed 2022).

The composite picture: an eCB "signal" does not exist as a standing quantity anywhere in the brain outside of the instant of its synthesis. There is no reservoir to draw down, no packet to release, no store to deplete. What is standing — cheap, structural, ever-present — is the precursor phospholipid pool and the enzymatic machinery (DAGLα, NAPE-PLD, MAGL, FAAH) positioned at specific subcellular addresses. This is "standing holding" of *capacity*, not "spotlight holding" of *ligand*. Contrast this with a vesicular transmitter like glutamate or GABA, which is synthesized in bulk, packaged, and held ready in a releasable pool whose size determines how much signal is available before synthesis must catch up — a spotlight system with real reserve capacity that can be transiently depleted by heavy use. Contrast it further with a circulating hormone like cortisol or estrogen, whose "presence" is a bulk blood concentration sensed identically (in principle) by every receptor-bearing cell in the body regardless of that cell's local history — pure spotlight holding, addressed to no one in particular. Endocannabinoid signaling is neither: it is addressed, by construction, only to the cells and only to the interval in which the generative event occurred. Presence, if it can even be said to occur, is inseparable from the postsynaptic compartment and the millisecond-to-second window that produced it. This is the strongest existing case that "routed position, not presence" is not a metaphor imported from cytokine biology but literally how a class of endogenous ligands work.

## 2. Tonic versus phasic pools: what "tone" means for a messenger that is never stored

If nothing is stored, what does it mean to speak of endocannabinoid "tone" — a term used constantly in this literature (basal CB1 activation, tonic eCB signaling, tone-dependent homeostatic plasticity)? Mechanistically, tone cannot mean a standing pool of ligand sitting in a compartment. It means a *steady-state flux*: a continuous low rate of synthesis balanced by a continuous rate of degradation, sufficient to produce measurable constitutive occupancy of CB1 (detectable as CB1 inverse-agonist-induced increases in cAMP, or as a rimonabant-precipitated behavioral effect in eCB-tone-dependent paradigms) without any discrete synthesis-triggering event. This is functionally closer to a chemical steady state than to a warehouse.

The literature dissociates two such regimes and assigns them, imperfectly but reproducibly, to the two eCBs. Anandamide is generally cast as the tonic messenger: partial agonist efficacy at CB1 (lower intrinsic activity than 2-AG or THC), constitutive low-level production not obviously gated by a single discrete trigger, and functions consistent with a background "brake" — e.g., tonic AEA/FAAH signaling in the amygdala buffers stress reactivity, such that FAAH inhibition or knockout elevates AEA tone and produces anxiolytic-like phenotypes (Gunduz-Cinar, Hill, and colleagues, reviewed 2013 *PMC4169112*). 2-AG is generally cast as the phasic messenger: full efficacy at CB1, synthesized in sharp bursts by DAGLα in response to specific depolarization or Gq-GPCR triggers, and responsible for the well-characterized short-term (DSE/DSI) and long-term depression forms of retrograde plasticity. This division should not be treated as absolute — Best & Regehr and others have shown 2-AG can also sustain a tonic component in some circuits (e.g., dorsal raphe, where tonic 2-AG rather than AEA gates plasticity via CB1 and postsynaptic effects), and both molecules are produced "on demand" in the narrow sense that neither is pre-stored — but the tonic/phasic functional split is a working generalization borne out across multiple brain regions and multiple manipulations, not merely a stipulative simplification.

The cleanest evidence that the two pools are functionally, not just descriptively, distinct comes from what happens when each is chronically elevated by blocking its clearance enzyme. Elevating 2-AG tone chronically (repeated high-dose MAGL inhibition, e.g., JZL184 in mice) produces cross-tolerance to THC, physical dependence signs, impaired eCB-dependent synaptic plasticity, and genuine CB1 receptor downregulation/desensitization in the brain — a "functional antagonism of the endocannabinoid system" (Schlosburg et al. 2010, *Nat. Neurosci.* 13:1113; corroborated and dose-mapped by Ignatowska-Jankowska et al. 2013/2014, showing that low-dose, more modest JZL184 regimens preserve normal CB1 expression and function while higher doses reproduce full tolerance). Elevating AEA tone chronically by FAAH inhibition (PF-3845 and related compounds) produces sustained analgesic and anxiolytic-like effects across repeated dosing without producing CB1 downregulation, without cross-tolerance to THC, and without triggering rimonabant-precipitated spontaneous withdrawal in the way MAGL inhibition does (reviewed in Ghosh et al. and in the comparative FAAH/MAGL substance-use-disorder literature, 2025). This is not a small or contested effect — it is one of the most robust dissociations in eCB pharmacology, replicated across labs and species, and it recasts "tone" as functionally load-bearing: the phasic 2-AG/CB1 pathway appears to have a narrower dynamic range before the receptor protects itself by downregulating, while the tonic AEA/CB1 pathway tolerates sustained elevation far better, plausibly because AEA's partial agonism keeps receptor occupancy and effector coupling below whatever threshold triggers GRK/β-arrestin-mediated adaptation (Section 3).

## 3. Flood: what exogenous agonists do that endogenous signaling does not

An orthosteric CB1 agonist — THC, or a synthetic full agonist — binds the same pocket the endogenous ligands bind and activates the receptor wherever it is expressed, in proportion to its own systemic concentration, entirely independent of local synaptic history. This is the pharmacological definition of flooding: it substitutes a spatially and temporally undifferentiated signal for one whose entire informational content was, until that moment, its restriction to a specific place and a specific instant. Put in the language of the framework under test: THC does not compete for "routed position" — it erases position and replaces it with raw, ungated presence. Every CB1 receptor in reach of the drug's concentration is engaged, regardless of whether the postsynaptic compartment behind it had generated any 2-AG at all.

The physiological consequences of sustained flooding are well documented in humans and in animal models, and they run in the opposite direction from what tonic eCB elevation does:

**CB1 downregulation is real, measurable, and reversible.** Hirvonen et al. (2012, *Mol. Psychiatry* 17:642) used PET imaging with the CB1-selective radioligand [¹⁸F]FMPEP-d2 in chronic daily cannabis smokers admitted to a monitored inpatient unit. Chronic users showed roughly 20% lower cortical CB1 receptor availability relative to controls, with the degree of downregulation correlating with years of use; after approximately four weeks of continuously monitored abstinence, CB1 availability returned to control levels in every region examined except the hippocampus, where recovery was slower or incomplete within the study window. This is essentially the human PET correlate of the rodent JZL184 desensitization findings: chronic agonist (or chronically elevated agonist tone) drives the receptor into a downregulated state via GRK/β-arrestin-mediated phosphorylation, desensitization, and internalization, and removing the drive allows most — not obviously all — of that state to reverse. Independent PET work with a different tracer ([¹⁸F]MK-9470, Ceccarini et al. 2015, *Addiction Biology*) reports a more heterogeneous picture, including some studies finding no significant CB1 change or even regional increases in heavy users, a discrepancy plausibly tied to tracer sensitivity to endogenous ligand competition, abstinence duration at scan time, and population differences — a genuine point of contest in this literature that should not be smoothed over.

**Desensitization mechanism.** Chronic agonist exposure drives GRK-mediated phosphorylation of CB1's C-terminal tail (notably S426/S430) and subsequent β-arrestin2 recruitment, which uncouples the receptor from G-protein effectors (GIRK channels, adenylyl cyclase inhibition, MAPK signaling) — this is desensitization proper. Internalization (physical removal of receptor from the membrane) is mediated by distinct structural determinants and is not strictly required for desensitization, meaning the two processes are separable and can be pharmacologically dissociated (Jin et al.; Nguyen et al., reviewed in Ford lab and others through 2019–2023). β-arrestin2 knockout mice show region-dependent, sometimes bidirectional effects on THC tolerance — reduced tolerance to antinociception in some circuits, paradoxically enhanced tolerance to catalepsy in others — indicating the desensitization/tolerance machinery is itself compartmentalized rather than a single global dial, a further instance of the "position matters" principle operating one level downstream of the eCBs themselves.

**Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (CHS)** is the sharpest clinical illustration that chronic flooding does not simply produce "more of the same" effect at reduced amplitude — it can invert the effect entirely. THC is a well-established antiemetic (the basis for dronabinol and nabilone as prescribed antiemetics), yet chronic heavy cannabis use produces, in a subset of users, a syndrome of cyclic severe nausea and vomiting relieved paradoxically by hot showers/bathing. The mechanism remains genuinely unresolved — proposed explanations include regionally selective CB1 desensitization uncoupling the normally antiemetic dorsal vagal complex/area postrema circuit while leaving pro-emetic peripheral or enteric actions intact; a shift in THC's functional efficacy from agonist toward antagonist-like behavior at desensitized receptors; TRPV1-mediated effects (capsaicin cream is an empirically used treatment, consistent with a TRPV1 component); and pharmacokinetic accumulation of THC and metabolites in high-potency chronic users. No single mechanism has been established with confidence, and this should be reported as an open question rather than a solved one — but every candidate mechanism converges on the same structural point: continuous non-gated CB1 (and possibly TRPV1) engagement, sustained past the point where the tissue's local machinery can absorb it, produces qualitatively different — sometimes opposite — physiology from the same receptor's gated, self-terminating endogenous activation.

**Adolescent exposure and psychosis risk** is the domain requiring the most epidemiological honesty. A large recent cohort study (Kaiser Permanente Northern California, screened adolescents 13–17, followed to age 25 or through 2023) found that self-reported past-year adolescent cannabis use was associated with roughly double the hazard of incident psychotic and bipolar-spectrum diagnoses, with cannabis use preceding diagnosis by an average of 1.7–2.3 years, after adjustment for sex, race/ethnicity, neighborhood deprivation, insurance type, and time-varying alcohol/other substance use (Se de Bruijn et al., reported 2026; earlier converging evidence in the 2024 "meandering journey" review, PMC12032589). This is a substantial, adjusted, temporally-ordered association, and it should not be minimized. It should also not be over-read: residual confounding by genetic liability to psychosis (which independently predicts cannabis use, a gene-environment correlation problem that twin and Mendelian-randomization designs have only partially resolved), by co-occurring tobacco and other substance use, and by pre-existing subclinical psychotic-spectrum symptoms that could plausibly drive self-medication with cannabis, remains difficult to fully exclude even in well-adjusted cohort designs. Compounding the interpretive difficulty, average THC content in commercial cannabis flower has risen sharply over the same decades this literature covers (commonly cited at >20% THC in current California flower, versus single digits historically, with concentrates far higher), while THC:CBD ratios have also shifted, and dose-response and potency-response relationships in the human literature are inconsistent enough that "cannabis use" as an epidemiological exposure category conflates what may be pharmacologically very different exposures. The honest summary: the epidemiological signal for an adolescent-exposure/psychosis association is real, replicated, and temporally ordered; a fully causal, dose-quantified, confound-free account of the underlying pharmacology is not yet available, and adolescent CB1/eCB system maturation (ongoing synaptic pruning in which eCB-mediated plasticity itself participates) is a mechanistically plausible but not proven bridge between the epidemiology and the receptor biology above.

The unifying frame for (a)–(this section): an orthosteric agonist cannot distinguish a synapse that just computed something worth suppressing from a synapse that did nothing at all. It activates both identically. Whatever information was encoded in the restriction of 2-AG or AEA to a particular postsynaptic compartment at a particular moment is, under flooding, simply gone — overwritten by a signal that is the same everywhere the drug reaches. This is the pharmacological content of "presence is not the variable; routed position is": remove the routing (by delivering the ligand systemically instead of synthesizing it locally) and you remove the information, even while nominally activating the "same" receptor with a molecule that binds the "same" pocket.

## 4. The design answer: allosteric modulation and precursor-level intervention

If the problem with orthosteric agonism is that it substitutes ungated presence for gated position, the design principle that follows directly is: build a drug that cannot act unless the endogenous, positionally-gated signal is already present. This is exactly what a positive allosteric modulator (PAM) does at CB1. A PAM binds a topographically distinct site (for the well-studied 2-phenylindole/2-phenylpyrazole chemotype exemplified by GAT211 and its descendant ZCZ011, the allosteric site has been mapped by mutagenesis to residues distinct from the orthosteric THC/2-AG pocket — Sharma, Nikas, Kulkarni, and colleagues; site mapped in more structural detail through 2024, PMC10892375) and produces little or no signaling on its own (in the case of GAT211, some ago-PAM chemotypes do show modest intrinsic efficacy, an important caveat) while substantially potentiating the affinity and/or efficacy of whatever orthosteric agonist — meaning, in the physiological case, whatever eCB — is already bound. Where no 2-AG or AEA is being released, a PAM given systemically does essentially nothing; where a synapse is actively generating retrograde 2-AG, the same systemic dose of PAM amplifies that local signal. In the language of this note's opening frame, a PAM is a drug engineered to respect standing holding — it leaves the structural, low-cost, precursor-and-enzyme-based capacity of the system untouched and amplifies only the spotlight-like phasic events that capacity occasionally produces, rather than substituting a new, ungated spotlight of its own.

The preclinical case for this strategy is now fairly substantial. GAT211 and ZCZ011 produce antinociception in inflammatory and neuropathic pain models, reduce naloxone-precipitated opioid-withdrawal signs and attenuate morphine tolerance while potentiating morphine antinociception, and — critically for the tolerance argument in Section 2–3 — repeated administration of these PAMs does not reliably produce the spontaneous withdrawal, cross-tolerance, or CB1 downregulation that repeated THC or repeated high-dose JZL184 produces; rimonabant precipitates withdrawal in mice chronically treated with the full agonist WIN55,212-2 but not in mice chronically treated with GAT211 (Slivicki, Xu, and colleagues, several papers 2017–2022; Kulkarni lab structure-activity work through 2021–2024 extending the series to congeners tested for intraocular pressure reduction in glaucoma models). Resolved R- and S-enantiomers of ZCZ011 both retain PAM activity with no strong stereoselectivity, evidence that potentiation and any intrinsic agonism are mechanistically coupled rather than cleanly separable properties of the scaffold — a useful caution against assuming a "pure" allosteric potentiator with zero intrinsic activity is trivially achievable. As of the current literature search, however, no CB1 PAM chemotype has been reported to have entered human clinical trials; the entire body of evidence remains preclinical (rodent behavioral pharmacology, receptor binding/functional assays, and mutagenesis-based site mapping), and the translational gap — pharmacokinetics, blood-brain-barrier penetration, and human safety — is unaddressed in the public literature as of this writing. That gap should be stated plainly rather than glossed: the design logic is well-supported at the mechanism level and essentially untested at the level that would matter for a patient.

Negative allosteric modulators run the same logic in reverse and are useful precisely because they show the mechanism is bidirectional and genuinely allosteric rather than a redescription of partial agonism. ORG27569 and PSNCBAM-1 are the best-characterized CB1 NAMs: they increase orthosteric ligand *binding* affinity while decreasing functional efficacy — a probe-dependent, pathway-biased profile (ORG27569 acts as an antagonist/inverse agonist on ERK1/2 signaling while enhancing some G-protein readouts) that is difficult to reconcile with simple orthosteric competition and depends on an extracellular N-terminal disulfide bridge (Cys98–Cys107) for its allosteric action. Cannabidiol (CBD) is now well supported as acting, at least in part, through this same negative allosteric site: Laprairie et al. (2015, *Br. J. Pharmacol.*) showed CBD reduces the efficacy and potency of CB1 orthosteric agonists (including the endogenous ligands) at submicromolar concentrations well below its affinity for the orthosteric pocket, and inhibits agonist-induced CB1 internalization — a negative-allosteric profile distinct from CBD's numerous other reported targets (5-HT1A, TRPV1, GPR55 antagonism, and others, which is itself an instance of the entourage/promiscuity problem taken up in Section 5). The translational logic for pursuing NAMs is the mirror image of the PAM case: rather than replacing systemic blockade (rimonabant's failure mode — see Section 5) with a drug that shuts CB1 down everywhere, a NAM would only damp signaling where and when it is already elevated, potentially offering a way to blunt pathological overactivation (e.g., in obesity, where peripheral eCB tone is chronically elevated) without the global affective liabilities that sank rimonabant.

**Substrate-level intervention** targets a different node in the same architecture: not the receptor, but the precursor pool that limits how much eCB synthesis can occur regardless of what the degradation enzymes are doing. Because both AEA and 2-AG are built from arachidonic acid (AA) esterified in membrane phospholipid, and AA is the principal omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid product of dietary linoleic acid, the ratio of dietary omega-6 to omega-3 intake sets an upstream ceiling on phospholipid-AA content and thus on the raw material available for on-demand eCB synthesis. Alvheim et al. (2012, *Obesity*) showed that raising dietary linoleic acid from 1 to 8 energy-percent in mice roughly tripled brain/tissue 2-AG and AEA levels via elevated phospholipid-AA, with associated increases in food intake, feed efficiency, and adiposity — a demonstration that precursor supply, not merely synthetic or degradative enzyme activity, is a controlling variable for eCB tone. This motivates the intervention hypothesis explored below: that raising dietary omega-3 (EPA/DHA) relative to omega-6, which competitively displaces AA from the membrane phospholipid pool, should lower the achievable ceiling on eCB synthesis independent of anything done to MAGL or FAAH. The human intervention literature on this point is thinner and less consistent than the rodent dietary-manipulation data — most human evidence is observational or short-duration supplementation work looking at circulating (not synaptic) eCB and NAE levels, and effect sizes and specificity (2-AG vs. AEA vs. other NAEs, which are not selectively affected) are not yet well resolved — so this should be read as a mechanistically well-grounded but only partially clinically validated lever, not an established therapeutic pathway.

## 5. The endocannabinoidome, and why single-target reasoning keeps failing here

Anandamide and 2-AG are the founding members of a much larger family of bioactive lipid mediators sharing biosynthetic machinery, degradative enzymes, and — critically — receptors. N-acylethanolamines (NAEs) structurally related to anandamide but lacking meaningful CB1/CB2 affinity include oleoylethanolamide (OEA) and palmitoylethanolamide (PEA), both of which signal principally through PPAR-α (and, for both, additionally through TRPV1 and, contestedly, GPR55) rather than through cannabinoid receptors at all. OEA is a satiety signal produced in the small intestine upon fat ingestion and acting on PPAR-α to reduce food intake; PEA has anti-inflammatory and analgesic actions, also substantially PPAR-α-dependent (PPAR-α knockout abolishes the anti-inflammatory action of both OEA and PEA in edema models). GPR55, sometimes informally called "CB3," is activated by lysophosphatidylinositol and modulated by several cannabinoids (including, contestedly, THC and CBD) but is pharmacologically and structurally distinct from CB1/CB2, coupling to different G-proteins (Gα12/13, Gαq) with different downstream consequences (RhoA activation, distinct Ca²⁺ signatures). TRPV1, the capsaicin receptor, is directly gated by anandamide itself at higher concentrations, making AEA a dual-target ligand whose net physiological effect at any given site depends on the local ratio of CB1 to TRPV1 expression — another instance in which "presence" of the identical molecule means opposite things (CB1-mediated inhibition of transmitter release versus TRPV1-mediated excitation and, at high enough levels, desensitization) depending entirely on the local receptor complement it encounters, an intramolecular echo of the IL-6 classic/trans-signaling logic this whole note is testing.

This is what "entourage effect" is doing conceptually, and why it should not be waved off as marketing language for whole-plant cannabis extracts (its most commercialized usage) even though that usage often overstates the evidence: NAEs and monoacylglycerols that are themselves poor cannabinoid-receptor ligands nonetheless compete with AEA and 2-AG for FAAH- and MAGL-mediated degradation, extending the functional half-life of the "real" eCBs at their receptors — a genuine pharmacokinetic entourage mechanism, independent of any pharmacodynamic synergy at the receptor level, and independent of whether whole-cannabis-extract entourage claims for THC/CBD/terpene combinations hold up (that specific claim remains weakly evidenced and should not be conflated with the better-supported NAE-competition mechanism).

The repeated failure of single-target reasoning in this system is best illustrated by rimonabant, the first CB1-selective inverse agonist to reach market (approved in Europe 2006 for obesity). Rimonabant worked exactly as designed at its target — it produced meaningful weight loss by blocking CB1 systemically and non-selectively across every tissue and every synapse, spotlight-flooding the entire system with sustained blockade rather than local potentiation or damping. It was withdrawn worldwide by 2008 after data showed markedly increased risk of depression, anxiety, and suicidality, and every major pharmaceutical company working on CB1 antagonists for obesity halted the programs within months. The lesson the field drew, and that this note's argument would predict independently: a molecule that engages "the CB1 receptor" as a monolithic target, without respect to which synapses were using tonic AEA signaling to buffer stress reactivity (Section 2) and which were using phasic 2-AG for local plasticity, cannot help but produce systemic psychiatric liability, because it is blocking a signal whose entire function depended on being local and intermittent. The subsequent search for "neutral" antagonists, peripherally restricted antagonists (which cannot cross into brain and so spare central affective circuits), and now allosteric approaches is a direct, field-wide correction toward respecting the routed, compartmentalized nature of the underlying biology rather than treating CB1 occupancy as a single scalar to be turned up or down. The endocannabinoidome's redundancy and cross-talk (AEA at CB1 versus TRPV1; OEA/PEA at PPAR-α independent of any cannabinoid receptor; NAE competition for shared degradative enzymes) makes this an unusually poor system for reasoning about "the drug that hits the target" — the target is never encountered by the endogenous ligands in isolation, and drugs that pretend otherwise have a track record of failing for reasons the single-target model did not anticipate.

## Hypotheses

1. **PAMs preserve context-specificity that orthosteric agonists degrade.**
   (i) *Prediction:* In a Pavlovian fear-extinction or safety-learning paradigm, systemic administration of a CB1 PAM (e.g., GAT211/ZCZ011 class) during extinction training will enhance context-specific extinction retention (safety signal remains restricted to the training context) more than it produces generalized fear suppression, whereas an equivalent-behavioral-effect dose of a direct orthosteric agonist (e.g., THC or a synthetic full agonist) will degrade context-specificity, producing measurable fear suppression or safety generalization to non-trained contexts.
   (ii) *Discriminating test:* Multi-context renewal design (train extinction in context A, test spontaneous recovery/renewal in context B versus A) under PAM versus orthosteric agonist versus vehicle, with the outcome being the *ratio* of context-A to context-B freezing/suppression, not the absolute suppression magnitude in either context alone; ideally paired with fiber photometry or microdialysis confirming that the PAM's effect tracks endogenous 2-AG transients time-locked to the CS while the agonist's effect does not require or correlate with any endogenous eCB transient.
   (iii) *Kill condition:* If the PAM produces equal degradation of the context-A/context-B ratio (i.e., equal generalization) as the orthosteric agonist at doses matched for absolute behavioral effect size, the "preserves pattern vs. erases pattern" distinction collapses to a mere potency/efficacy difference rather than a qualitative mechanistic one, and the hypothesis is falsified.

2. **Precursor availability sets a hard ceiling on achievable eCB tone, independent of degradation-enzyme inhibition.**
   (i) *Prediction:* Under a low-omega-6/high-omega-3 dietary regimen (reduced membrane phospholipid-arachidonic-acid pool), maximal achievable brain 2-AG and AEA elevation from a saturating dose of a MAGL or FAAH inhibitor, respectively, will be measurably lower than the same inhibitor dose achieves under a high-linoleic-acid diet — i.e., the diet sets a ceiling the enzyme-inhibitor cannot push through.
   (ii) *Discriminating test:* 2×2 design in rodents (high-LA vs. low-LA/high-omega-3 diet crossed with vehicle vs. saturating JZL184 or PF-3845 dose), measuring peak brain 2-AG/AEA by LC-MS/MS and phospholipid-AA content directly; the prediction requires a diet×drug interaction (diminished drug-induced peak under low-AA diet), not merely a main effect of diet on baseline eCB levels.
   (iii) *Kill condition:* If saturating enzyme inhibition produces statistically indistinguishable peak eCB elevation regardless of dietary AA manipulation (i.e., the degradation enzyme, not precursor pool size, is rate-limiting even at the extremes tested), the "precursor ceiling" hypothesis is falsified and MAGL/FAAH activity alone should be treated as sufficient to explain achievable tone.

3. **Chronic-flood tolerance is pattern-destroying, not merely amplitude-reducing.**
   (i) *Prediction:* Chronic THC (or chronic high-dose MAGL-inhibitor) exposure will produce loss of discrete, stimulus-locked eCB transients at CB1-expressing synapses during a retrograde-signaling-dependent behavior (e.g., DSI/DSE-dependent plasticity, or CS-locked 2-AG transients during fear learning), rather than a simple downward-scaled but still time-locked transient — i.e., the temporal structure of the signal degrades, not just its size.
   (ii) *Discriminating test:* In vivo fiber photometry with a genetically encoded 2-AG or eCB sensor (e.g., GRAB-eCB1.0 or successor sensors) recording stimulus-locked transients before and after chronic THC/JZL184 exposure; the key measured variable is transient temporal precision/time-locking (e.g., jitter, peak latency variance, or presence/absence of a detectable stimulus-locked peak at all) compared against transient amplitude, tracked in the same animals longitudinally.
   (ii, cont.) A finding of preserved, merely-smaller time-locked peaks after chronic exposure would support a pure-amplitude account; a finding of flattened, non-time-locked, tonic-looking signal where a sharp phasic peak used to be would support the pattern-destruction account.
   (iii) *Kill condition:* If chronic flood exposure reduces transient amplitude proportionally while temporal locking, latency, and jitter remain statistically unchanged from baseline, the "pattern-destroying" hypothesis is falsified in favor of a simple gain-reduction (amplitude-only) account of tolerance.

4. **CB1 downregulation after chronic agonist exposure is regionally graded in a way that maps onto local tonic-vs-phasic eCB dominance.**
   (i) *Prediction:* Brain regions in which AEA/tonic signaling normally predominates (e.g., amygdala, prefrontal cortex) will show less PET-measurable CB1 downregulation and faster abstinence-driven recovery after chronic cannabis exposure than regions in which phasic 2-AG/DAGLα-dependent signaling predominates (e.g., striatum, cerebellum, hippocampus), extending the Hirvonen et al. finding of incomplete hippocampal recovery into a systematic regional prediction rather than an isolated exception.
   (ii) *Discriminating test:* Re-analysis or new collection of regional PET CB1-availability data (Hirvonen-style [¹⁸F]FMPEP-d2 or equivalent) in chronic users at baseline and after a standardized abstinence period, correlated against an independent regional index of tonic (AEA/FAAH-dominant) versus phasic (2-AG/DAGLα-dominant) signaling derived from post-mortem enzyme-distribution or in situ hybridization atlases.
   (iii) *Kill condition:* If degree of downregulation and recovery timecourse across regions correlates better with simple CB1 receptor density or regional THC exposure (blood flow, lipophilicity partitioning) than with the tonic/phasic enzymatic profile of the region, the region-specific tonic/phasic vulnerability hypothesis is falsified in favor of a pharmacokinetic/receptor-density account.

5. **NAM-class drugs (CBD-like) reduce pathological tonic eCB elevation without rimonabant's global affective liability, because they are efficacy-limiting rather than occupancy-blocking.**
   (i) *Prediction:* A CB1 NAM dosed to produce equivalent peripheral metabolic benefit to a historical CB1 inverse agonist (rimonabant-equivalent weight/appetite effect) will produce significantly less depressive/anxiogenic-like behavior in rodent models (forced swim, elevated plus maze, or equivalent) than the inverse agonist at a matched peripheral-effect dose, because the NAM only damps signaling where eCB tone is already elevated (e.g., peripherally, in obesity) rather than blocking CB1 uniformly including in central circuits with low ongoing eCB tone.
   (ii) *Discriminating test:* Head-to-head rodent dose-response comparison of a peripherally-unrestricted CB1 NAM versus rimonabant (or a modern inverse agonist such as those in current obesity-trial development), matched for peripheral metabolic endpoint (weight loss, food intake), with affective/anxiety behavioral battery and, ideally, regional c-Fos or ex vivo cAMP assay confirming central CB1 engagement magnitude differs between the two drug classes at matched peripheral effect.
   (iii) *Kill condition:* If the NAM produces equivalent central CB1 functional engagement and equivalent affective-behavioral liability to rimonabant at matched peripheral efficacy, the "efficacy-limiting NAMs are safer because pattern-respecting" hypothesis is falsified, and the rimonabant liability should instead be attributed to CB1 blockade per se (of any kind) rather than to the occupancy-versus-efficacy distinction between antagonism and negative allosteric modulation.
