Norepinephrine (noradrenaline) is a catecholamine synthesized from dopamine by the enzyme dopamine-beta-hydroxylase, which is expressed in sympathetic nerve terminals and in certain brainstem nuclei. This synthesis dependency means the availability of dopamine and the expression of dopamine-beta-hydroxylase determine norepinephrine production at each anatomical site. Norepinephrine is released from two distinct compartments with fundamentally different roles: as a neurotransmitter from the terminals of postganglionic sympathetic neurons, where it acts on adrenergic receptors on target organs (heart, blood vessels, lungs, adipose tissue, skeletal muscle); and as a central neuromodulator from the locus coeruleus, a brainstem nucleus containing roughly 10,000 neurons that project diffusely throughout the entire brain.

These two roles are mechanistically and functionally distinct, and conflating them is a persistent source of error in neuroscience. Peripheral sympathetic norepinephrine release occurs at the terminals of sympathetic neurons innervating target organs, where the transmitter acts locally on the organ's adrenergic receptors. The norepinephrine concentration achieved at the postsynaptic cell can be quite high (millimolar range), yet the duration of action is brief because reuptake transporters (the norepinephrine transporter, NET) immediately retrieve the transmitter from the synapse, and monoamine oxidase metabolizes it intracellularly. Circulating norepinephrine in plasma comes primarily from sympathetic nerve terminals (neuronal spillover) and from the adrenal medulla; the plasma concentration is much lower (picomolar range) than the synaptic concentration, so plasma norepinephrine is a weak proxy for actual sympathetic nerve terminal activity at target tissues. Central norepinephrine release from the locus coeruleus operates on a completely different timescale and spatial scale. The locus coeruleus sends diffuse, long-range projections throughout the entire brain, and norepinephrine acts as a neuromodulator — a substance that alters the gain or responsiveness of neurons to other inputs — rather than as a point-to-point transmitter. Locus-coeruleus activity increases during arousal, attention, and threat, promoting focused alertness and vigilance; the same norepinephrine molecule, acting through the same adrenergic receptors on postsynaptic neurons, has a different functional role depending on the brain region and the behavioral context. Measuring plasma norepinephrine says nothing about central noradrenergic tone; they are separate systems.

The receptor preference of norepinephrine versus epinephrine differs in a way that has functional consequences. Norepinephrine has strong affinity for alpha-1-adrenergic receptors (α1) and alpha-2-adrenergic receptors (α2), and moderate affinity for beta-1-adrenergic receptors (β1). It has relatively low affinity for beta-2-adrenergic receptors (β2). This means that at low to moderate concentrations, norepinephrine primarily activates α1 and α2 receptors, producing vasoconstriction and some cardiac effects; at higher concentrations, it also engages β1 receptors on the heart. Epinephrine, by contrast, has broader and more balanced affinity across all four adrenergic receptor types, but particularly strong β2 activity. This difference in receptor profile produces different physiological outcomes: norepinephrine-dominant activation produces intense peripheral vasoconstriction with only modest cardiac rate increase; epinephrine-dominant activation produces both vasoconstriction and potent β2-mediated effects (bronchodilation, hepatic glycogenolysis, skeletal-muscle vasodilation), yielding a different overall hemodynamic picture.

For interpreting sympathetic function in clinical and research contexts, the distinction between peripheral sympathetic norepinephrine release and central locus-coeruleus neuromodulation is essential. A patient or research subject with high plasma norepinephrine levels may be experiencing either peripheral sympathetic nerve activation (spillover from terminals innervating cardiovascular and metabolic targets) or adrenal release, but this measurement says little about whether the central arousal/attention system is activated or dysregulated. Conversely, a person can have normal plasma norepinephrine and severely impaired central locus-coeruleus function, producing inattention, poor arousal regulation, or failure to mount the attentional response needed for threat detection. The two systems require separate assessment: plasma or urine catecholamine levels and sympathetic-nerve-recording or challenge-testing probe peripheral sympathetic tone, while measures of central noradrenergic state might include neuroimaging of the locus coeruleus, pupil-size dynamics (a peripheral readout of locus-coeruleus firing), or behavioral markers of attentional regulation.

## Related terms

- Catecholamine — the neurotransmitter/hormone class of which norepinephrine is a member
- Dopamine — the precursor to norepinephrine in the synthetic pathway
- Epinephrine — the other major catecholamine, with different source and receptor profile
- Dopamine-beta-hydroxylase — the enzyme that converts dopamine to norepinephrine
- Locus coeruleus — the brainstem nucleus releasing norepinephrine as a central neuromodulator
- Adrenergic receptor — the family of receptors on which norepinephrine acts; α1, α2, and β1 subtypes are preferentially activated by norepinephrine
- Sympathetic nervous system — the neural system releasing norepinephrine from postganglionic terminals
- Glucocorticoid — the hormone that upregulates adrenergic receptors, amplifying norepinephrine effectiveness
