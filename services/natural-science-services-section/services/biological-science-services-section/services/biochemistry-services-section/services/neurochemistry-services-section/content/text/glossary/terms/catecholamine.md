Catecholamines are a class of neurotransmitters and hormones derived from the amino acid tyrosine. The principal members are dopamine, norepinephrine (noradrenaline), and epinephrine (adrenaline). All three are synthesized via a common pathway: tyrosine is converted to L-DOPA by tyrosine hydroxylase, L-DOPA is converted to dopamine by DOPA decarboxylase, dopamine is converted to norepinephrine by dopamine-beta-hydroxylase, and norepinephrine is converted to epinephrine by phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PNMT). This sequential pathway means that catecholamine synthesis depends on continuous tyrosine availability, adequate cofactor supply (including tetrahydrobiopterin for tyrosine hydroxylase), and the coordinated expression and activity of each enzymatic step. Catecholamines are released from two major sources: from synaptic vesicles in the terminals of sympathetic neurons (primarily norepinephrine, some dopamine), and from the adrenal medulla (primarily epinephrine and norepinephrine) into systemic circulation. These two sources — local neurotransmitter release and hormonal release — produce catecholamines in different compartments on different timescales, with different kinetics and different local concentrations, yet they are often conflated as a single "sympathetic output" in casual discussion.

Catecholamines act on a family of adrenergic receptors, generally classified into two types — alpha-adrenergic (α1 and α2) and beta-adrenergic (β1 and β2) — each with distinct tissue distributions and physiological effects. α1-adrenergic receptors, concentrated on vascular smooth muscle, mediate vasoconstriction when activated by norepinephrine or epinephrine. α2-adrenergic receptors, located presynaptically and on some postsynaptic sites, mediate feedback inhibition of norepinephrine release and some inhibitory central effects. β1-adrenergic receptors, predominant in the heart, mediate increases in heart rate and contractility. β2-adrenergic receptors, abundant in bronchial and vascular smooth muscle, mediate bronchodilation and vasodilation. The distribution and density of these receptors vary across tissues and across individuals, and chronic stress, sustained catecholamine exposure, or lack of glucocorticoid signaling can all alter the sensitivity of these receptors to their ligands — a key mechanism linking the sympathetic and HPA axes.

The vascular and hemodynamic effects of circulating catecholamines depend critically on glucocorticoid signaling, a relationship sometimes described as "glucocorticoid permissiveness." Catecholamines alone, without baseline glucocorticoid signaling, have reduced efficacy at α1-adrenergic receptors on vascular smooth muscle, producing weaker vasoconstriction and reduced blood-pressure support. In the complete absence of glucocorticoids (as in acute adrenal insufficiency or adrenal crisis), even very high circulating epinephrine and norepinephrine fail to maintain adequate vascular tone, resulting in profound hypotension, poor tissue perfusion, and hemodynamic collapse despite massive sympathetic activation. This is the physiological basis for the adrenal crisis: the sympathetic arm of the stress response (high catecholamine output from the adrenal medulla and sympathetic nervous system) decouples from its vascular consequence (vasoconstriction and blood-pressure support) when glucocorticoids are absent. For understanding conditions like ME/CFS in which the HPA axis is blunted while sympathetic tone is high, this permissive relationship means that elevated catecholamine levels without adequate glucocorticoid signaling produce erratic, maladaptive vasoconstriction and profound cardiovascular instability rather than the coordinated mobilization they would normally support.

Catecholamines measured in peripheral blood (plasma catecholamine levels) are a weak proxy for actual tissue-level sympathetic activity or tissue-level catecholamine effect. Circulating catecholamines reflect primarily the output of the adrenal medulla and do not reliably indicate the local concentrations and effects of norepinephrine released from sympathetic nerve terminals in target tissues — the concentrations achieved at a vascular smooth-muscle cell or a cardiac myocyte by direct sympathetic innervation are typically much higher than circulating levels would predict. Additionally, sympathetic nerve-terminal norepinephrine is rapidly reuptaken (by norepinephrine transporter, NET) and metabolized locally, so tissue-level sympathetic activity can be high while plasma catecholamine levels remain normal. Conversely, elevated plasma catecholamines from adrenal release may not produce equivalent tissue-level effects if the tissues in question have low receptor density, reduced receptor sensitivity, or adequate reuptake capacity. This mismatch between plasma catecholamine measurement and actual sympathetic tissue activity is a persistent source of confusion in interpreting sympathetic tone in clinical and research contexts: a normal plasma catecholamine level does not prove the sympathetic nervous system is calm, and an elevated level does not guarantee effective sympathetic action on target tissues.

## Related terms

- Dopamine — one of the three principal catecholamines, with roles in motor control, reward, and motivation
- Norepinephrine — a catecholamine released from sympathetic nerve terminals and the adrenal medulla
- Epinephrine — the predominant catecholamine released from the adrenal medulla into systemic circulation
- Glucocorticoid — the hormone class whose signaling is required for catecholamines to maintain vascular tone
- Adrenergic-receptor — the family of receptors on which catecholamines act, subdivided into alpha and beta types
- Sympathetic-nervous-system — the neural system using catecholamine neurotransmitters
- Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal-axis — the endocrine system that coordinates glucocorticoid and catecholamine responses to stress
