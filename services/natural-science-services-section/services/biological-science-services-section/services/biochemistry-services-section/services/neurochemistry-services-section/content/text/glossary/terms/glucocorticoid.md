Glucocorticoids are a class of steroid hormones, produced by the adrenal cortex under control of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, that regulate glucose metabolism, immune function, and the body's response to stress. In humans the principal glucocorticoid is cortisol; in rodents, the commonly studied laboratory species for much of the underlying mechanistic work, it is corticosterone. Release is triggered by a signaling cascade — corticotropin-releasing hormone from the hypothalamus prompts adrenocorticotropic hormone from the pituitary, which in turn prompts cortisol release from the adrenal glands — and the cascade includes negative feedback, with circulating cortisol normally suppressing further hypothalamic and pituitary signaling once levels rise sufficiently. Under chronic or repeated activation, this feedback loop can itself become dysregulated, which is part of why glucocorticoid signaling is a central mechanism in stress physiology rather than a simple on-off switch.

Glucocorticoids act throughout the body, but their effects on the prefrontal cortex are especially consequential for cognition and are the subject of Amy Arnsten's extensive research program, beginning with her influential 2009 review in *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*. Arnsten's work established that stress-induced catecholamine and glucocorticoid signaling rapidly impairs prefrontal-cortex-dependent functions — working memory, top-down inhibitory control, flexible decision-making — even under acute, mild, and entirely uncontrollable stress, while simultaneously strengthening amygdala-based threat processing. Chronic exposure compounds this at a structural level: sustained glucocorticoid signaling produces measurable dendritic retraction in prefrontal pyramidal neurons, while the same conditions can leave amygdala dendritic architecture unchanged or enhanced. The prefrontal cortex is, on this account, the brain region best equipped for high-order cognition and simultaneously the one most vulnerable to stress-induced impairment — a trade documented across acute pharmacological, chronic-stress, and human cognitive-performance studies.

The critical feature of glucocorticoid-mediated cognitive impairment, for any theory concerned with anticipatory or chronic threat, is that it is triggered by uncontrollability and unpredictability rather than by the magnitude of a realized harm. A brief, uncontrollable stressor produces measurable prefrontal impairment regardless of whether any actual damage follows it; the nervous system pays the cost of the threat signal itself. Successor work building on Arnsten's program has continued to trace the intracellular signaling pathways — including effects on glutamate transmission in prefrontal and hippocampal circuits — through which this impairment is produced and, under some conditions, reversed.
