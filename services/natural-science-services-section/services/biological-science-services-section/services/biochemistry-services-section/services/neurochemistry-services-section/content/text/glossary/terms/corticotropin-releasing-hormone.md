Corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), also called corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF), is a 41-amino-acid neuropeptide synthesized in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus and released into the hypophyseal portal circulation — a specialized venous system connecting the hypothalamus directly to the anterior pituitary. There, CRH binds to CRH-receptors on pituitary corticotropes, triggering the synthesis and release of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) into systemic blood, which in turn acts on the adrenal cortex to trigger cortisol synthesis and release. This three-level cascade — hypothalamic CRH, pituitary ACTH, adrenal cortisol — is the defining architecture of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, and CRH is the initiating signal that sets the entire system in motion under threat and stress.

The practical significance of CRH extends far beyond this endocrine role. CRH-synthesizing neurons exist throughout the brain, especially in the amygdala and the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis — core nodes of emotional and threat-processing circuitry. In these extra-hypothalamic locations, CRH acts as a neuropeptide coordinating anxiety, fear, vigilance, and defensive behaviors directly, without passing through the pituitary and adrenal glands. A single stressor simultaneously triggers hypothalamic CRH release into portal blood (driving the endocrine cascade toward cortisol) and amygdalar/BNST CRH release into local neural circuits (driving threat assessment and behavioral response). CRH is therefore not merely an endocrine relay but a coordinate signal spanning emotional, cognitive, and physiological dimensions of the threat response.

The most consequential practical fact about CRH is that it is essentially unmeasurable in peripheral circulation in humans. CRH is a peptide, rapidly degraded, and does not cross the blood-brain barrier reliably; circulating CRH concentrations are too low and too variable to serve as a usable biomarker of central HPA axis drive. This creates an insurmountable inferential gap: any claim about "blunted central CRH drive" is necessarily inferred from measurements taken two steps downstream — from the anterior pituitary's ACTH response and the adrenal cortex's cortisol response to a CRH-stimulation test. A reduced ACTH response to exogenous CRH could arise from pituitary insufficiency, from habituation of pituitary corticotropes to chronic CRH exposure, or from sustained negative feedback suppression by elevated baseline cortisol. A reduced cortisol response could arise from adrenocortical insufficiency or impaired ACTH sensitivity. The inferential distance — from the imputed central signal to the measured peripheral response — is not merely a technical limitation but the defining constraint on how CRH-axis hypotheses can be tested in living human subjects.

This gap becomes especially consequential in dysregulated states where the HPA axis does not collapse completely but decouples. In ME/CFS and related post-viral conditions, for instance, CRH and other hypothalamic neuropeptides continue to be released, and amygdalar/BNST CRH-driven threat circuits remain hyperactive, but downstream pituitary and adrenal responsiveness is blunted — a mismatch in which the central signal persists while the peripheral hormonal response is attenuated. Without direct peripheral measurement of CRH itself, distinguishing between "the central drive is strong but pituitary/adrenal are suppressed" and "the central drive is weak and pituitary/adrenal are somewhat compensating" requires either invasive CSF sampling or accepting fundamental ambiguity in the interpretation of the CRH-stimulation test.

CRH must be clearly distinguished from ACTH, the pituitary hormone it releases. CRH is released into a local, millimeter-range portal circulation connecting the hypothalamus to the anterior pituitary; ACTH is released into systemic circulation, traveling throughout the body. CRH acts primarily on the pituitary; ACTH acts on the adrenocortical cells in the adrenal glands. CRH has a half-life of minutes in portal blood; ACTH has a slightly longer half-life of 10–20 minutes in systemic circulation. These are distinct molecules in distinct compartments with distinct kinetics, yet they are often collapsed into "the HPA axis" in shorthand; understanding their separate identities and distinct failure modes is essential for interpreting cascade dysregulation.

## Related terms

- Corticotropin-releasing-factor — the alternative name, used interchangeably with corticotropin-releasing-hormone
- Adrenocorticotropic-hormone — the pituitary hormone released in response to CRH and the next step in the cascade
- Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal-axis — the three-level endocrine system in which CRH is the initiating level
- Glucocorticoid — the hormone class released downstream of ACTH, which feeds back to suppress further CRH release
