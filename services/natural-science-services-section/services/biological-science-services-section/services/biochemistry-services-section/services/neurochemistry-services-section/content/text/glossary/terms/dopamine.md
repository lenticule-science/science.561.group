Dopamine is a catecholamine neurotransmitter synthesized from tyrosine through L-DOPA and released by midbrain neurons whose projections reach the striatum, the nucleus accumbens, and the prefrontal cortex. The projection that concerns affective medicine most directly is the mesolimbic one, from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens, and what that projection does is narrower and stranger than the popular account of it.

Mesolimbic dopamine gates the exertion of effort and the activation of behavior, not the hedonic experience of reward. The rodent evidence separating the two is causal and old. Salamone, Cousins and Bucher trained rats on a T-maze in which one arm delivered four 45-milligram pellets and the other delivered two, with a 44-centimetre barrier placed in the high-reward arm to impose an effort cost. Haloperidol and 6-hydroxydopamine depletion of accumbens dopamine shifted the animals away from the high-effort, high-reward arm toward the low-effort, low-reward one, while leaving normal consumption of the same food intact once the effort requirement was removed. The companion study made the dissociation sharper still: accumbens dopamine depletion suppressed lever pressing for the preferred food and increased free consumption of the less preferred chow, so total intake was not suppressed and preference for the better food, when it was free, survived. Salamone and Correa state the conclusion for the whole programme: accumbens dopamine does not mediate primary food motivation or appetite, but is involved in appetitive and aversive motivational processes including behavioral activation, exertion of effort, approach behavior, sustained task engagement, Pavlovian processes, and instrumental learning.

The consequence for research design is direct: an intervention or a measure aimed at restoring pleasure through dopamine is aimed at a function this transmitter does not perform, and a study that reports reduced dopaminergic signalling alongside low mood cannot infer an anhedonic mechanism from it. The finding is also rodent-causal. Human effort-based decision-making tasks are behavioural proxies rather than neurochemical measurements, and no study links the two literatures directly, so the human inference remains an extrapolation from an animal model rather than a demonstrated causal chain.
