Epinephrine (adrenaline) is a catecholamine synthesized from norepinephrine by the enzyme phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PNMT). The critical physiological fact is that epinephrine is synthesized almost exclusively in the chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla, where it accumulates in dense-core vesicles and is released directly into the bloodstream when the adrenal medulla is activated by preganglionic sympathetic neurons. A small amount of epinephrine is synthesized in a few brainstem neurons, but this central production is negligible relative to the adrenal output, so circulating epinephrine is overwhelmingly derived from the adrenal gland. This means epinephrine is fundamentally a hormonal signal — a substance released into the general circulation to reach distributed targets — rather than a synaptic neurotransmitter like norepinephrine released from sympathetic nerve terminals.

The source difference — adrenal hormone versus neural transmitter — has profound functional consequences. The adrenal medulla can release epinephrine throughout the entire body within seconds, reaching all tissues via the bloodstream. By contrast, sympathetic norepinephrine acts only on tissues directly innervated by sympathetic neurons, with the highest concentrations at synapses and reduced spillover into plasma. This difference in reach and distribution means epinephrine can mobilize the entire body as a coordinated system in response to a single stimulus (fear, acute threat, hypoglycemia), whereas norepinephrine acts through multiple localized reflex arcs. The time course is similar — both act within seconds — but the scale of action differs.

The receptor preference of epinephrine differs from norepinephrine in ways that produce distinct physiological outcomes. Epinephrine has potent activity at all four adrenergic receptor subtypes (α1, α2, β1, and β2), with particularly strong β2 activity. This receptor breadth produces a characteristic epinephrine signature: cardiac stimulation via β1 (increased heart rate and contractility), potent bronchodilation via β2, hepatic glycogenolysis and glucose mobilization via β2 on liver cells, metabolic mobilization via β2 on adipose tissue, and skeletal-muscle vasodilation via β2 alongside the peripheral α1-mediated vasoconstriction. The result is a paradoxical hemodynamic pattern: epinephrine can raise cardiac output and heart rate while actually *lowering* peripheral vascular resistance through β2-mediated vasodilation, producing a blood-pressure profile quite different from what α1-dominated norepinephrine would produce. At low epinephrine concentrations, the β2 effects on metabolism and muscle vasodilation dominate, producing the 'metabolic mobilization' phase; at higher concentrations, the α1 effects on peripheral vasoconstriction compete with and can override the β2 effects, producing the classical vasoconstriction picture. This explains why blood pressure is a poor single readout of adrenergic activation — the same adrenergic level can produce very different blood pressures depending on whether norepinephrine or epinephrine dominates.

For interpreting sympathetic function and catecholamine measurement, the source distinction is essential. Plasma norepinephrine reflects sympathetic nerve spillover — the output of sympathetic postganglionic terminals — and is a reasonable proxy for peripheral sympathetic tone, though far from perfect. Plasma epinephrine reflects adrenal medullary output almost exclusively, so it reports a different physiological event: the degree to which the organism has escalated to a system-wide hormonal stress response. In a healthy person at rest, both are very low; during moderate exercise or mild stress, sympathetic norepinephrine spillover increases while epinephrine remains low; during acute threat, severe hypoglycemia, or major physical challenge, epinephrine rises substantially. A person with high sympathetic tone and normal epinephrine is relying on neural sympathetic control; a person with high epinephrine despite normal sympathetic tone is mounting an adrenal endocrine response. In pathological states, the two can dissociate: a patient with pheochromocytoma (epinephrine-secreting tumor) will have markedly elevated epinephrine with only modest sympathetic activation; a patient with sympathetic hyperactivity from spinal-cord injury or dysautonomia might have high norepinephrine spillover with normal or even low epinephrine, suggesting the sympathetic tone is neural rather than hormonal in origin.

The functional consequence for understanding dysregulated stress responses in myalgic encephalomyelitis and other conditions is that circulating catecholamine profiles — the ratio of norepinephrine to epinephrine, the timing of their rise and fall, the responsiveness of each to challenge — reveal the underlying physiology: whether the response is a coordinated neural sympathetic reflex, a system-wide adrenal endocrine surge, or a dyscoordination between the two. A single blood-pressure or heart-rate measurement cannot distinguish these; the catecholamine profile and the receptor-specific responses illuminate the mechanism.

## Related terms

- Catecholamine — the neurotransmitter/hormone class of which epinephrine is a member
- Norepinephrine — the other major catecholamine, with different source and narrower receptor profile
- Phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase — the enzyme that converts norepinephrine to epinephrine
- Adrenal medulla — the source of circulating epinephrine
- Adrenergic receptor — the family of receptors on which epinephrine acts; broadly active across all subtypes
- Sympathetic nervous system — the neural system that signals the adrenal medulla to release epinephrine
- Glucocorticoid — the hormone that upregulates adrenergic receptors, amplifying epinephrine effectiveness
- Stress response — the physiological cascade in which epinephrine plays a major role
