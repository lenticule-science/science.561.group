Gamma-aminobutyric acid is the chief inhibitory neurotransmitter of the mammalian central nervous system. It acts at two receptor families: the ionotropic GABA-A receptor, a ligand-gated chloride channel that benzodiazepines modulate allosterically, and the metabotropic GABA-B receptor. Inhibition here is not the absence of signalling but an active brake, and a brake that fails is as disruptive as an accelerator that sticks.

What distinguishes this transmitter's role in the catatonia literature from an established mechanism is the direction of the inference. The clinical fact is a treatment response: in an open systematic treatment study of 28 catatonic patients, 16 of the 21 who completed a full lorazepam trial, or 76 percent, had resolution of catatonic signs, and four patients who failed lorazepam responded promptly to electroconvulsive therapy. From that responsiveness the field infers a GABA-A hypofunction, and Northoff's model locates it in a right-hemisphere orbitofrontal and posterior parietal network, with lorazepam reported to normalize abnormal orbitofrontal activation. The imaging evidence behind that model was not confirmed against its primary text in the research pass this term rests on: its sample sizes and effect sizes are unverified, and the one functional study reached only through secondary description involved roughly six patients studied while asymptomatic.

The consequence for anyone reading a benzodiazepine response as evidence of a receptor deficit is that the response is a treatment fact and the deficit is a hypothesis. Lorazepam responsiveness in catatonia is well enough measured to guide treatment; it does not by itself establish which receptor population is abnormal, in which region, or in which direction, and a study that treats the drug response as a receptor assay has substituted a clinical outcome for a measurement it never made.
