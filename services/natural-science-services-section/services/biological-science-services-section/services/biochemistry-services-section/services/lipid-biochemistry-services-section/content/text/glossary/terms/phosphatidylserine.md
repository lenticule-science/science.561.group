Phosphatidylserine (PS) is an anionic glycerophospholipid — a membrane lipid
with a serine amino acid as its headgroup, which carries a negatively charged
carboxyl group. PS is structurally similar to other phospholipids but its
negative charge makes it functionally distinct. It is one of the most important
lipids in cell biology because it plays two critical roles: it is an "eat me"
signal that marks dying cells for macrophage clearance, and it is the anionic
membrane surface absolutely required for the prothrombinase complex to assemble
and generate thrombin in coagulation.

## Membrane location and maintenance through ATP-dependent flippase

In living cells, PS is normally restricted to the inner (cytoplasmic) leaflet of
the plasma membrane. It makes up roughly 5–10% of total membrane phospholipids
on the inner leaflet but is normally absent from the outer (blood-facing) leaflet.
This asymmetric distribution is actively maintained by an ATP-dependent enzyme
called flippase (more formally, the ATP11A and ATP11C ATPases that act as
phospholipid flippases). Flippase continuously transports PS from the outer to
the inner leaflet, using the energy of ATP hydrolysis. If flippase is active,
PS is pumped inward; if ATP is depleted or flippase is blocked, PS accumulates
on the outer leaflet.

This ATP-dependent maintenance has a major consequence: membrane-lipid asymmetry
is an actively run, energy-consuming process. Any cellular condition that
depletes ATP — such as mitochondrial dysfunction, anoxia, or metabolic failure —
will secondarily cause loss of asymmetry and PS externalization, not because PS
is being produced abnormally, but because the cell cannot afford to run the
flippase that normally excludes it from the outer membrane.

## Externalization by calcium-activated scramblase

Upon cell activation, injury, or stress, intracellular calcium rises. High
intracellular calcium activates a family of enzymes called scramblases (TMEM16A
and TMEM16F being the major ones). Scramblases are bidirectional lipid
transporters that are independent of ATP — they work by allowing lipids to
diffuse through the membrane protein in either direction. When scramblase is
activated, it scrambles the membrane, destroying the asymmetry and allowing PS
(along with other inner-leaflet lipids) to move to the outer leaflet.

The calcium dependence and ATP independence of scramblase is critical. A
scramblase-mediated externalization of PS is the normal response to activation
and is reversible — once the calcium signal resolves, flippase re-internalizes
the PS and asymmetry is restored. However, if ATP is depleted, flippase cannot
re-internalizing the PS, and asymmetry is lost permanently until ATP is
restored.

## The "eat me" signal for macrophage clearance

PS on the outer leaflet serves as an "eat me" signal recognized by macrophages.
Macrophages have PS-binding proteins (PS receptors, bridging ligands like
thrombospondin and gas-6) that recognize PS on the surface of dying or damaged
cells. This recognition triggers macrophage engulfment and removal of the marked
cell. PS externalization therefore is, under normal circumstances, a tightly
controlled mechanism for removing cells that are dying or that should be cleared.

This role of PS in cell clearance is not the same as its role in coagulation.
The same molecule (PS) plays both roles depending on cellular context — PS on
the surface of an activated platelet enables coagulation; PS on the surface of an
apoptotic lymphocyte triggers macrophage clearance. The two roles are functionally
distinct but mechanistically entangled: anything that externalizes PS more widely
or more durably will activate both the coagulation response and the clearance
response simultaneously.

## The catalytic surface for prothrombinase and coagulation

The second major role of PS is as the essential anionic membrane surface on which
the prothrombinase complex assembles. The prothrombinase complex is the core
amplification enzyme in the cell-based model of coagulation: it is the assembly
of factor Xa (a serine protease) and factor Va (its cofactor) on a membrane
surface in the presence of calcium. This complex catalyzes the conversion of
prothrombin to thrombin.

The critical fact is that this complex cannot assemble on a neutral membrane.
Coagulation factors are vitamin-K-dependent proteins that are modified by
carboxylation at their N-terminus, making them dependent on calcium-mediated
anchoring to a negatively charged (anionic) lipid surface. PS is the primary
anionic lipid serving this function. Factor Xa and factor Va are anchored to the
membrane through calcium bridges to PS. Without PS, the prothrombinase complex
cannot form, and thrombin generation fails.

This is why coagulation is said to be a "membrane-localized process." Coagulation
does not happen in bulk plasma; it happens where PS is available. In healthy
hemostasis, PS is available only on the surfaces of activated platelets, so
coagulation is confined to the site of injury. If PS becomes externalized more
widely — on many cells or for extended periods — coagulation can proceed in ectopic
locations and lead to unwanted clotting.

## The ATP connection: energy failure and procoagulance

Because flippase (the enzyme that maintains PS internalization) is ATP-dependent,
ATP depletion directly causes loss of membrane asymmetry and PS externalization.
This creates a mechanistic link between cellular energy failure and procoagulant
(clot-promoting) activity: a cell that is losing ATP — due to mitochondrial
dysfunction, hypoxia, or metabolic failure — will secondarily externalize PS and
become procoagulant. This is an inference from the transport biology, not yet a
demonstrated pathway in any specific illness, but it is consistent with the
observations that both energy failure and microclot burden are reported in
long COVID and ME/CFS, and it offers a testable connection between them.

## The supplement literature: a separate subject with the same molecule name

### Small clinical trials with bovine-cortex phosphatidylserine

A distinct body of literature examines oral phosphatidylserine supplementation as
a potential pharmacological agent. In the 1990s, Monteleone and colleagues
conducted small, controlled trials administering bovine-cortex-derived
phosphatidylserine to healthy human subjects undergoing stress challenges
(physical exercise, public speaking). They reported that the supplement blunted
the adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) response to these stressors, and
correspondingly blunted the cortisol response. These were very small samples by
modern standards, and the effect sizes reported should be read against that. Later work
by the same and other groups examined soy-derived phosphatidylserine supplements
with weaker and less consistent effects.

### Critical distinctions from membrane PS biology

These findings do NOT apply to the membrane PS role described above. There are
three critical differences:

1. **Material identity:** Bovine cortex PS, isolated decades ago and used in these
   trials, differs from modern soy-derived PS in fatty acid composition and in
   potential contamination. Bovine cortex material was withdrawn from supplement
   markets due to transmissible spongiform encephalopathy (TSE/mad cow disease) risk
   (bovine-derived materials could carry the prion). The compound actually studied
   in the 1990s human trials is not the compound now sold as "phosphatidylserine"
   supplement. Soy-derived PS differs in fatty acid composition from membrane PS.
2. **Route and context:** Oral PS supplement is absorbed in the gastrointestinal
   tract and enters systemic circulation. The mechanism by which an orally absorbed
   lipid could modulate HPA-axis tone is not established — it is unlikely to
   directly affect the hypothalamic neurons themselves, and any effect would have
   to be indirect (e.g., through immune signaling affecting the hypothalamus). In
   contrast, PS in the membrane context acts locally on the platelet surface,
   immediately adjacent to coagulation factors.
3. **Replication and effect size:** The bovine-cortex studies were small (n~30–60)
   and reported effect sizes in the range of 30% ACTH/cortisol blunting. Later,
   larger trials with soy-derived PS showed weaker or inconsistent effects, and
   the mechanism of ACTH modulation by oral PS remains unexplained. The trials
   were not replicated at scale.

### The incorrect conflation to avoid

It is tempting to read the membrane PS facts (it is required for platelet
activation and coagulation; it is externalized in energy failure) and the
supplement trial findings (ACTH blunting) and to suggest that PS supplementation
might "stabilize membranes" or "reduce platelet activation." This would be
incorrect. The membrane role of PS is to provide a coagulation surface; the
supply of PS to that surface is not a rate-limiting factor in physiological
conditions. The supplement literature is entirely separate and addresses a
different question (can oral PS modulate HPA-axis output). The two subjects
share a molecule name and nothing else.

The distinction is important because both subjects appear in the ME/CFS and long
COVID literature: membrane PS and platelet activation are discussed in the
microclot hypothesis, while supplement PS is discussed in discussions of HPA-axis
modulation. These are not two aspects of the same mechanism; they are two
entirely different topics that happen to use the same three letters.
