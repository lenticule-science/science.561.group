A phospholipid is an amphipathic lipid — a molecule that has both hydrophobic
and hydrophilic regions — composed of a glycerol or sphingoid backbone with two
fatty acids (or fatty acid-like groups) attached, and a phosphate group bearing
a charged or zwitterionic headgroup. The fatty acid regions are hydrophobic and
cluster together; the phosphate headgroup is hydrophilic and faces water. This
amphipathic architecture makes phospholipids the structural basis of cell
membranes.

## The bilayer structure

When phospholipids are placed in water, they self-assemble into a bilayer: a
sheet two molecules thick, with the fatty acid tails pointing inward (creating a
hydrophobic core) and the phosphate headgroups pointing outward (creating a
hydrophilic surface facing the water). This bilayer is the fundamental structure
of the plasma membrane of every cell and of all intracellular membrane-bound
organelles.

The bilayer is a dynamic structure, not static. Individual lipid molecules are
in constant lateral motion within their leaflet, and there is slow spontaneous
flip-flop of lipids between leaflets (occurring over hours to days). However, in
the living cell, flip-flop is tightly controlled by ATP-dependent lipid
transporters (flippases and floppases) that actively maintain different lipid
compositions on the two leaflets. This active maintenance is called membrane-
lipid asymmetry.

## Major phospholipid headgroups and their properties

The phosphate headgroup can bear different organic molecules, giving different
phospholipids different properties:

1. **Phosphatidylcholine (PC, lecithin)** — the headgroup is choline, a
   positively charged molecule. PC is the most abundant phospholipid in eukaryotic
   cell membranes, making up roughly 50% of the total phospholipid content. PC is
   zwitterionic (both positive and negative charge) at physiological pH.
2. **Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE)** — the headgroup is ethanolamine, a small
   polar molecule. PE is the second-most abundant phospholipid in eukaryotes,
   making up 20–30% of membrane phospholipid. PE is also zwitterionic. The
   smaller headgroup of PE makes it more prone to adopt non-bilayer phases and
   gives it a propensity to induce membrane curvature.
3. **Phosphatidylserine (PS)** — the headgroup is serine, an amino acid that
   carries a free carboxyl group. PS is anionic (negatively charged) at
   physiological pH. Although PS makes up only 5–10% of total membrane
   phospholipid, it is the critical anionic surface that allows coagulation
   complexes to assemble.
4. **Phosphatidylinositol (PI)** — the headgroup is inositol, a six-carbon sugar
   alcohol. PI is particularly important in cell signaling. The phosphate can be
   phosphorylated at multiple positions on the inositol ring, creating
   phosphorylated isoforms (PIP, PIP2, PIP3) that serve as substrates for
   signaling enzymes and as binding sites for proteins containing pleckstrin
   homology (PH) domains.
5. **Sphingomyelin** — not a glycerophospholipid, but a sphingolipid with a
   phosphocholine headgroup. Like PC, it is zwitterionic, but it is built on a
   sphingoid backbone rather than glycerol. Sphingomyelins are enriched in the
   nervous system and in lipid rafts (specialized membrane microdomains).

## Distinction from triglycerides

Phospholipids and triglycerides both have fatty acids, but their functions are
entirely different:

- **Triglycerides** are three fatty acids esterified to glycerol with no phosphate
  or headgroup. They are hydrophobic throughout, pack tightly in adipose tissue,
  and serve as energy storage. Triglycerides are not membrane components.
- **Phospholipids** have two fatty acids and a phosphate headgroup. The headgroup
  makes them amphipathic, able to form bilayers. Phospholipids are membrane
  structural components and are not used for energy storage (though they can be
  cleaved to fatty acids if needed).

This distinction has major physiological consequences. A lipid panel showing
elevated triglycerides is a statement about energy storage; a finding of altered
phospholipid headgroup ratios in a membrane is a statement about membrane
organization and protein-lipid interactions. These are different aspects of
lipid biology.

## How headgroup identity determines membrane function

The specific phospholipid headgroups present in a membrane determine what proteins
can assemble on it and what physical properties the membrane has:

- A membrane rich in PC and PE (both zwitterionic) has low net surface charge and
  can support many types of interactions.
- A membrane with high PS content (anionic) can bind cationic proteins and can
  serve as a platform for coagulation enzyme assembly (which absolutely requires
  anionic lipid).
- A membrane with high PI or PS content can serve as a substrate for signaling
  enzymes and as a binding site for proteins with specific lipid-binding domains.

This is why the concept of "a lipid abnormality" is too vague without specifying
which lipid. An increase in PS is procoagulant; an increase in PC with a decrease
in PE would alter membrane fluidity; an increase in oxidized phospholipids might
trigger inflammatory signaling. The specific lipid matters.

## Membrane asymmetry: the ATP-dependent aspect

In living cells, the two leaflets of the bilayer have different lipid compositions.
The outer leaflet is enriched in PC and sphingomyelin; the inner leaflet is
enriched in PE and PS. This asymmetry is actively maintained by ATP-dependent
flippase enzymes that transfer PS and PE from the outer to the inner leaflet,
driven by ATP hydrolysis. Flippase activity must be continuous because of
spontaneous flip-flop, and ATP depletion immediately impairs flippase, allowing
PS and PE to accumulate on the outer leaflet. This is why PS externalization is
not just a membrane rearrangement; it is a consequence of energy depletion and
therefore sensitive to mitochondrial dysfunction and ATP availability.

## Related terms

- Phosphatidylserine — the specific anionic phospholipid critical for coagulation
- Membrane-lipid-asymmetry — the ATP-maintained unequal leaflet composition
- Triglyceride — the energy-storage lipid, not a membrane component
- Lipid raft — specialized membrane microdomain enriched in sphingolipids
- Membrane — the lipid bilayer structure
- Coagulation cascade — the process requiring PS as its membrane surface
