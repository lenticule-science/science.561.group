A lipid is a class of biological molecule defined operationally by its chemical
property of hydrophobicity: lipids are soluble in nonpolar solvents (chloroform,
ether, hexane) and insoluble or poorly soluble in water. This is an operational
definition, not a structural one — lipids are not defined by sharing a common
chemical structure the way proteins are (all proteins are polymers of amino
acids) or carbohydrates are (all carbohydrates are polymers of monosaccharides).
Instead, lipids are a collection of molecules unified only by their
hydrophobicity, and this distinction is critical for understanding the lipid
literature.

## Why the operational definition matters

Because lipids are defined by solubility rather than structure, the class
includes chemically diverse molecules: fatty acids (straight hydrocarbon chains
with a carboxyl head), sterols (rigid four-ring structures like cholesterol),
glycerophospholipids (glycerol backbone with phosphate headgroups), and prenols
(long isoprenoid chains). These share no common scaffolding. The advantage of
the operational definition is that it is simple to apply: if a molecule
partitions into a nonpolar solvent and away from water, it is a lipid. The
disadvantage is that this grouping obscures their chemical and biological
differences.

The practical consequence is that the phrase "lipid levels" is ambiguous. It
could mean:

- Total plasma cholesterol (a sterol)
- Total triglycerides (glycerolipids, energy storage)
- A phospholipid species (a glycerophospholipid)
- Free fatty acids
- All of the above, lumped together

Each of these is biologically distinct, is affected by different metabolic
pathways, and has different physiological meaning. A claim about "lipids" or
"lipid dysregulation" is empty unless it specifies which lipid class or species
is meant.

## Major lipid families

1. **Fatty acids** — straight-chain hydrocarbons with a carboxyl group at one
   end. They are either saturated (no double bonds, C-C-C chains) or unsaturated
   (one or more C=C double bonds). Saturated fatty acids are more rigid and
   higher-melting; unsaturated fatty acids are more fluid. Fatty acids serve
   multiple roles: they are activated to acyl-CoA and fed into the Krebs cycle
   for energy; they are incorporated into triglycerides for storage; they are
   incorporated into membrane phospholipids and glycolipids; and some are
   modified to produce signaling molecules (eicosanoids, endocannabinoids).
2. **Glycerolipids** — fatty acids esterified to a glycerol backbone. The major
   form is triglyceride (three fatty acids on one glycerol), which is the primary
   energy storage form in animals, stored in adipose tissue. Triglycerides are
   not membrane components; they are fuels.
3. **Glycerophospholipids** — fatty acids esterified to glycerol, with a
   phosphate and headgroup (like choline, serine, ethanolamine, inositol). These
   are the major structural lipids of all cell membranes, making up the bilayer.
   Different headgroups confer different surface charge and membrane properties.
4. **Sphingolipids** — lipids built on a sphingoid base (sphinganine or
   sphingosine) rather than glycerol, with one or two fatty acids attached.
   Major subclasses are ceramides, sphingomyelins (which resemble
   glycerophospholipids but are built on sphingoid base), and glycosphingolipids
   (ceramide with attached oligosaccharides). Sphingolipids are especially
   abundant in the nervous system and are important for cell-cell recognition.
5. **Sterols** — rigid four-ring structures with a small side chain. Cholesterol
   is the major sterol; plant sterols (phytosterols) are also common. Sterols
   are incorporated into membranes where they regulate fluidity, and are
   modified to produce steroid hormones, bile acids, and vitamin D.
6. **Prenols** — long isoprenoid chains, including vitamin A, vitamin K, and
   coenzyme Q. These are important as cofactors in electron transport and as
   structural elements.

## The three functional roles

1. **Membrane structure** — Glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, and cholesterol
   form the lipid bilayer of all cell membranes. The bilayer is self-assembling:
   hydrophobic tails point inward, hydrophilic headgroups point outward.
   Proteins are embedded in or associated with this bilayer. The lipid
   composition of the membrane (the ratio of saturated to unsaturated fatty
   acids, the headgroup identities, the cholesterol content) determines membrane
   fluidity, protein-lipid interactions, and membrane protein function.
2. **Energy storage** — Triglycerides in adipose tissue are the major energy
   reserve in animals. During fasting, hormone-sensitive lipase cleaves them to
   free fatty acids, which are transported to liver and muscle for oxidation in
   the Krebs cycle. Fatty acids provide 9 calories per gram, more than twice the
   yield of carbohydrates or proteins (4 calories per gram).
3. **Cell signaling** — Specialized lipids and fatty acid derivatives act as
   signaling molecules. Eicosanoids (prostaglandins, leukotrienes, thromboxanes)
   are derived from 20-carbon fatty acids and regulate inflammation, hemostasis,
   and smooth muscle. Endocannabinoids are derivatives of arachidonic acid that
   bind cannabinoid receptors. Lysophospholipids are partial breakdown products
   of phospholipids that act as signaling lipids. Sterols are modified to
   produce steroid hormones. Glycosphingolipids are involved in cell-cell
   recognition. Lipid signaling is often a "second messenger" system, taking
   over after a receptor signal has been initiated at the cell surface.

## Measurement and the problem of classification

Because there are hundreds of individual lipid species (different combinations of
fatty acid chain lengths, saturation, and headgroups), and because lipids in
biological samples are typically measured as lumped classes (total cholesterol,
total triglycerides, total phospholipid), the literature often uses imprecise
terminology. A "lipid panel" ordered in a clinical lab typically measures total
cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides — four values
that are aggregates of many individual species. Advanced lipidomics can measure
individual lipid species by mass spectrometry, but this is not routine. The
consequence is that a claim from lipidomics literature about "lipid abnormalities"
may be describing a difference in one lipid species across one cohort, which
cannot be generalized to "the lipid biology in this disease" without much larger
replication studies.

## Related terms

- Fatty acid — the simplest lipid, a hydrocarbon chain with a carboxyl group
- Glycerophospholipid — the major membrane structural lipid
- Triglyceride — the major energy storage lipid
- Cholesterol — a major membrane sterol
- Lipidomics — the systematic measurement of lipid species
- Membrane — the lipid bilayer structure
