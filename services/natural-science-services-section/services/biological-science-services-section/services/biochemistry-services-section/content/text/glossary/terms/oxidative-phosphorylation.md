Oxidative phosphorylation is the metabolic process by which electrons derived from the oxidation of glucose, fatty acids, and amino acids are passed along a chain of protein complexes (complexes I through IV) embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane, and the energy released by this electron transfer is used to pump protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical gradient. This gradient is the proton-motive force — a combination of pH difference and electrical potential — that drives ATP synthase (complex V) to phosphorylate ADP to ATP. The process is called oxidative because the final electron acceptor in the chain is molecular oxygen (O₂), which combines with electrons and protons to form water.

The yield per glucose molecule is approximately 30 ATP (in eukaryotes; the exact number varies with mitochondrial efficiency and the costs of maintaining gradients). This is roughly 15-fold higher than the yield from glycolysis alone, which produces 2 ATP net per glucose. This enormous yield difference is why aerobic cells can maintain lower glycolytic flux and why the shift from oxidative to anaerobic metabolism is metabolically expensive: a cell relying on anaerobic glycolysis must consume and process much higher volumes of glucose to achieve the same ATP output, producing equivalent volumes of lactate and protons as waste.

The dependency on oxygen is absolute and immediate: without oxygen, the electron transport chain backs up, NADH cannot be reoxidized to NAD+, the Krebs cycle stalls, and oxidative ATP production ceases. This is why anoxic tissue (deprived of blood flow) dies rapidly — the cell cannot sustain ATP production long enough to maintain ionic gradients and cellular integrity. The consequence for physiology is often misunderstood: aerobic capacity is not a quantity of energy the cell can store, but a *rate ceiling* on ATP production. A cell with intact mitochondria but low oxidative capacity cannot produce ATP faster than its ceiling allows, no matter how much fuel is available or how desperately the cell needs energy. When demand exceeds this rate ceiling even at rest, the cell is forced to rely on anaerobic glycolysis, accumulating lactate and protons, and is therefore metabolically in a state of emergency even during activities that a healthy cell would handle aerobically. The practical consequence is that a lowered aerobic rate ceiling (whether from mitochondrial dysfunction, vascular insufficiency, or hematologic abnormality) will cause metabolic failure at low workloads in ordinary daily activities, not merely at peak exertion.

Oxidative phosphorylation is distinct from substrate-level phosphorylation, the direct transfer of a phosphate group from a high-energy substrate to ADP, catalyzed by specific enzymes. In glycolysis, substrate-level phosphorylation produces ATP at the steps catalyzed by phosphoglycerate kinase (producing 1 ATP per glucose) and pyruvate kinase (producing 1 ATP per glucose). Substrate-level phosphorylation is also the only ATP-producing step in the Krebs cycle, catalyzed by succinyl-CoA synthetase. Substrate-level phosphorylation is oxygen-independent and is therefore always available, but its yield per substrate is orders of magnitude lower than oxidative phosphorylation.

## Related terms

- Mitochondrion — the organelle where oxidative phosphorylation occurs
- Electron transport chain — the series of protein complexes that transfer electrons
- Proton-motive force — the electrochemical gradient that drives ATP synthesis
- ATP synthase — the enzyme complex that synthesizes ATP
- Krebs cycle — the metabolic pathway that feeds electrons into the transport chain
- Glycolysis — the glucose breakdown pathway that produces substrate-level ATP
- Substrate-level phosphorylation — direct phosphate transfer, oxygen-independent
