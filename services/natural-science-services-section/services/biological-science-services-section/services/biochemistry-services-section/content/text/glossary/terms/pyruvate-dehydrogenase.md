The pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex is a multi-enzyme machine (in humans, consisting of multiple copies of pyruvate dehydrogenase E1, dihydrolipoyl acetyltransferase E2, and dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase E3) that catalyzes the irreversible oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA: Pyruvate + NAD+ + CoA → Acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH + H+. This reaction is the committed entry point from glycolysis into the Krebs cycle; once pyruvate is converted to acetyl-CoA, it is committed to oxidation. The reaction is energetically favorable under cellular conditions and is essentially irreversible — meaning that cells cannot run it backward to make pyruvate from acetyl-CoA.

Because PDH catalyzes a committed, irreversible step controlling entry into oxidative metabolism, its activity is tightly regulated. The primary regulatory mechanism is phosphorylation by pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK), which inactivates the complex by phosphorylating the E1α subunit. PDK activity is increased by high ratios of ATP/ADP, NADH/NAD+, and acetyl-CoA/CoA — signals that the cell is energetically replete and does not need more ATP production. Conversely, dephosphorylation by pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatase (PDP) activates the complex, and PDP activity is increased by calcium and by low ATP/ADP and NADH/NAD+ ratios — signals that the cell is energetically stressed and needs more oxidation. This regulatory logic ensures that the cell runs oxidative metabolism when it is energetically expensive (high ATP demand) and reduces it when it is not needed (high energy charge).

A central finding in ME/CFS pathophysiology research is the 2016 study by Fluge, Mella, and colleagues, who reported elevated expression of PDK and reduced PDH activity in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from ME/CFS patients compared to matched controls. Their mechanistic hypothesis is that upregulation of PDK drives inhibition of PDH, thereby reducing the cells' capacity to enter oxidative metabolism and forcing them to rely on anaerobic glycolysis even at low metabolic workloads. This hypothesis has substantial appeal because it predicts a specific, testable metabolic signature: if PDH is inhibited, cells should accumulate lactate at low workloads while still oxidizing fatty acids and ketone bodies (alternative fuels that bypass the PDH bottleneck). However, the current state of the evidence is that this is a leading hypothesis with mixed replication. Some studies have replicated the finding of elevated PDK expression or reduced PDH activity; others have not. The mechanism of PDK upregulation — whether driven by endotoxin, viral infection, mitochondrial ROS, redox imbalance, or another factor — remains unclear. Critically, the connection between PDK/PDH changes in blood cells and systemic energy metabolism is indirect; a finding in circulating immune cells does not automatically predict the metabolic state of muscle, brain, or heart. The consequence for research is that the PDH hypothesis is worth testing rigorously with metabolic assays (two-day cardiopulmonary exercise testing with venous lactate and pyruvate sampling, muscle biopsy with respirometry and PDK/PDH immunochemistry) but should not yet be treated as an established mechanism of ME/CFS pathophysiology. Until it is replicated at the whole-organism level and a causal manipulation of PDK expression reverses the metabolic phenotype, the hypothesis remains in the category of "plausible and testable" rather than "proven."

## Related terms

- Pyruvate — the substrate of pyruvate dehydrogenase, the end product of glycolysis
- Acetyl-CoA — the product of pyruvate dehydrogenase and entry point to the Krebs cycle
- Krebs cycle — the oxidative pathway fed by acetyl-CoA
- Pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase — the kinase that phosphorylates and inactivates PDH
- Glycolysis — the pathway that produces pyruvate
- Anaerobic glycolysis — the backup pathway used when PDH is inhibited
