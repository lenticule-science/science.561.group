Anaerobic glycolysis is the breakdown of glucose to pyruvate and then to lactate, occurring in the absence of adequate oxygen or, more precisely, when the rate of aerobic ATP production is insufficient to meet cellular ATP demand. The pathway is: Glucose → (via 10 enzymatic steps) → Pyruvate → Lactate, with the net yield of 2 ATP per glucose and regeneration of NAD+ from NADH. This is in contrast to complete oxidation of glucose through the Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, which yields ~30 ATP per glucose.

The critical regulatory point is the regeneration of NAD+. In the glycolytic pathway, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase consumes NAD+, reducing it to NADH. If NADH is not reoxidized back to NAD+ quickly enough, the glycolytic pathway stalls because the enzyme cannot continue without NAD+ as a substrate. Under aerobic conditions, NADH is reoxidized by donating electrons to the electron transport chain (complex I), and the chain uses oxygen as the final electron acceptor. When oxygen is unavailable or when the electron transport chain is running at capacity (saturated), NADH cannot be rapidly reoxidized. The lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) reaction solves this problem by transferring electrons from NADH to pyruvate, regenerating NAD+ and producing lactate: Pyruvate + NADH + H+ → Lactate + NAD+. This is a rescue mechanism allowing glycolysis to continue. Lactate is then exported from the cell (via monocarboxylate transporters) into the bloodstream, where it is transported to the liver and, to a lesser extent, to other tissues. In the liver, the Cori cycle runs in reverse: lactate is converted back to pyruvate (by LDH), then to glucose (by gluconeogenesis), and glucose is returned to the bloodstream for reuse by the original metabolizing cell. This is energetically expensive for the whole organism (the Cori cycle costs 6 ATP per glucose) but allows the original cell to continue producing 2 ATP per glucose even when aerobic metabolism is unavailable.

A persistent misconception is that lactate itself causes muscle pain, fatigue, or the "burn" sensation in working muscles. This is false. Lactate is a fuel — it is oxidized by mitochondria in the heart and oxidative muscle fibers, and it is gluconeogenized by the liver. What does contribute to fatigue is the accompanying rise in protons (H+) that occurs during anaerobic glycolysis. The production of lactate is coupled to the production of protons; the accumulation of H+ lowers intracellular pH, which inhibits glycolytic enzymes, impairs excitation-contraction coupling, and interferes with cross-bridge cycling. The fatigue in anaerobic exercise is more accurately ascribed to intracellular acidosis than to lactate per se. This distinction matters for understanding metabolic diseases: a cell that accumulates lactate without producing the corresponding protons (or vice versa) would not be expected to show the same fatigue signature.

The anaerobic threshold is frequently confused with anaerobic metabolism, but they are distinct. The anaerobic threshold (or lactate threshold) is the workload at which blood lactate begins to accumulate above resting levels — typically operationalized as a blood lactate concentration of 4 mM, or the "point of maximal lactate steady-state." Below this threshold, lactate production and clearance are in equilibrium, and lactate does not accumulate in the blood. At or above this threshold, production exceeds clearance, and lactate rises. The confusion arises because lactate accumulation signals that anaerobic glycolysis is now producing lactate faster than it is being cleared — implying that aerobic capacity is saturated. However, anaerobic metabolism is always partly running (the LDH reaction is always present) — it is a question of degree and rate, not presence or absence. A lowered anaerobic threshold means the threshold is crossed at a lower workload — ordinary, low-intensity activities that in a healthy person are well below the threshold now push the individual above it. The metabolic consequence is that ordinary activity is now running at a rate that produces net lactate accumulation and intracellular acidosis. This is different from "the person is deconditioned" (which typically raises the threshold) and is distinguishable by two-day cardiopulmonary exercise testing, where a true lowered threshold shows a lower workload on day two relative to day one and, critically, a larger gap between oxygen uptake and ventilation (indicating accumulation of lactate and protons even at nominally aerobic workloads).

## Related terms

- Glycolysis — the complete glucose-breakdown pathway, of which anaerobic glycolysis is the lactate-producing portion
- Lactate — the end product of anaerobic glycolysis
- Pyruvate — the branching point between oxidation (via PDH) and reduction (to lactate)
- Anaerobic threshold — the workload at which lactate begins to accumulate in the blood
- Cori cycle — the pathway by which lactate is converted back to glucose in the liver
- Proton accumulation — the cause of intracellular acidosis in anaerobic exercise
