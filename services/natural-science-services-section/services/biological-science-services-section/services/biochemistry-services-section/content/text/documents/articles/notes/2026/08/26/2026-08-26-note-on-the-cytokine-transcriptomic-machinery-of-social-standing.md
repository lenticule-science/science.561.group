# The Cytokine–Transcriptomic Machinery of Social Standing

A companion note already laid out the survey-level case: chronic social threat is read by the body as sickness-worthy danger, inflammation and depressive affect share machinery, and Cole's Conserved Transcriptional Response to Adversity (CTRA) names the resulting gene-expression signature. This note goes under that layer. The question here is not *whether* social standing has a literal biology but *which molecules, which cells, and on what clock*. Where the program's vocabulary — abandonment as a maintained relation, standing as its durable reversal — is useful, it appears only in short framing remarks and in the hypotheses; the body of the note is mechanism.

## 1. The CTRA Cascade Proper

### 1.1 From adrenergic tone to transcription factor activity

The CTRA is not a single gene but a co-regulated program: up-regulated pro-inflammatory transcripts (IL1B, IL6, IL8/CXCL8, TNF, COX2/PTGS2, and related genes) alongside down-regulated Type I interferon and antibody-synthesis transcripts, first characterized in circulating leukocytes from chronically lonely adults (Cole, Hawkley, Arevalo, Sung, Rose, & Cacioppo, 2007, *Genome Biology*). Promoter-based bioinformatic analysis of the differentially expressed genes implicated three transcription-factor families as the proximal drivers: CREB, which responds to β-adrenergic receptor (β-AR)–driven cAMP/PKA signaling; GATA family factors, active in myeloid lineage commitment; and NF-κB, the canonical pro-inflammatory master regulator. The mechanistic claim is that sympathetic nervous system (SNS) outflow — chronically elevated under sustained social threat — acts on β2-adrenergic receptors expressed on leukocytes (and, as covered in §2, on bone-marrow stromal and progenitor cells) to shift the balance of transcription-factor occupancy toward CREB/NF-κB/AP-1-driven inflammatory genes and away from interferon-regulatory-factor (IRF)–driven antiviral genes.

This is a real molecular antagonism, not just a correlational pairing. NF-κB (RelA/p65) and IRF3 can each act as coactivators for the other at composite promoter elements, but the relationship is not simply additive: interferon-stimulated response elements (ISREs) that are co-occupied by NF-κB/IRF complexes are selectively repressible by glucocorticoid receptor (GR) agonism, and RelA-driven interferon responses can negatively regulate proliferative and antiviral programs depending on cellular context. In sickness-behavior terms, the CTRA looks like an evolved trade-off: mobilize the fast, non-specific bactericidal machinery (innate/myeloid inflammation) at the expense of the slower, more specific antiviral and humoral machinery — a trade plausibly tuned for a threat environment (wounding, bacterial infection from conspecific conflict) that predation and out-group violence would have made more probable for a chronically excluded organism than for one embedded in a group (Cole, 2019, *Brain, Behavior, and Immunity*, review; Slavich & Cole's broader social-genomics synthesis, referenced but not re-argued here).

### 1.2 Glucocorticoid receptor resistance as the permissive lesion

CTRA up-regulation of inflammatory transcription requires not just adrenergic push but withdrawal of glucocorticoid (GC) restraint. Under normal circumstances cortisol, acting through GR, terminates inflammatory transcription by several mechanisms (tethering to NF-κB/AP-1, competition for coactivators, induction of IκBα). Two independent lines of evidence converge on GR resistance/desensitization as the mechanism by which chronic social stress removes this brake:

- **Sheldon Cohen's rhinovirus-challenge paradigm.** In 276 healthy adults, quarantined and challenged with live rhinovirus, individuals who had experienced a chronic, severe interpersonal stressor (typically an enduring conflictual relationship or unemployment lasting a month or more) in the prior year showed both increased likelihood of developing a clinical cold and evidence of glucocorticoid receptor resistance — a diminished ability of cortisol to down-regulate ex vivo cytokine production (IL-6) by stimulated leukocytes. GCR, not average cortisol level, statistically mediated part of the stress–illness relationship (Cohen, Janicki-Deverts, Doyle, Miller, Frank, Rabin, & Turner, 2012, *PNAS*, 109(16), 5995–5999).
- **Gregory Miller's "functional genomic fingerprint."** In genome-wide monocyte expression profiling of chronic caregivers of brain-cancer patients versus matched controls, caregivers showed *diminished expression of transcripts bearing GC-response elements* alongside *heightened NF-κB-associated transcription* — despite normal ambulatory cortisol output. The lesion is downstream of the hormone: receptor sensitivity/signal transduction, not ligand availability (Miller, Chen, Sze, Marin, Arevalo, Doll, Ma, & Cole, 2008, *Biological Psychiatry*, 64(4), 266–272).

The mechanistic upshot for the CTRA cascade: chronic SNS activation both drives inflammatory transcription directly (via CREB/AP-1) and, in parallel, degrades the GR-mediated brake that would otherwise oppose it — a double hit rather than a single pathway, which is part of why the resulting transcriptional shift is durable rather than a simple oscillation with acute stressor onset and offset.

### 1.3 The signal is substantially a cell-population shift, not (only) a per-cell reprogramming

A finding with disproportionate importance for how one should read "CTRA up-regulation" is that a large fraction of the apparent transcriptional shift is attributable to a change in the *mixture* of leukocyte subtypes circulating, not (only) to reprogramming within a fixed population of cells. Cole and colleagues, using both deconvolution of bulk transcriptomes against reference cell-type signatures and direct flow-cytometric enumeration, showed that chronically lonely/isolated humans and experimentally isolated rhesus macaques both show a selective expansion of the CD14++/CD16− "classical" monocyte subset (immature, more inflammatory, weaker antiviral gene expression) relative to non-classical monocytes and lymphocytes, and that statistically controlling for this compositional shift substantially attenuates the apparent CTRA gene-expression signal (Cole et al., 2015, "Myeloid differentiation architecture of leukocyte transcriptome dynamics in perceived social isolation," *PNAS*, 112(49), 15142–15147). In the macaque arm of that study, mechanistic follow-up confirmed CTRA activation, up-regulation of the classical-monocyte transcriptome, functional GC desensitization, down-regulated Type I/II interferon signaling, and — functionally — impaired antiviral defense (worse control of experimental SIV infection). A five-wave longitudinal study of 141 older humans in the same paper showed the same temporal choreography: SNS activation precedes monocyte population expansion, which precedes/accompanies CTRA up-regulation.

This matters for how "reversal" should be modeled (§6, and Hypothesis 1): if the CTRA signature is substantially a population-mixture phenomenon, its kinetics are gated by cell production, egress, and turnover — not solely by how fast a transcription factor can be switched off in an existing cell.

## 2. The Sympathetic–Hematopoietic Axis

### 2.1 Chronic stress activates hematopoietic stem cells directly

Heidt, Sager, Courties, Dutta, Sebas, and colleagues showed that chronic variable stress (an unpredictable-mild-stressor paradigm in mice: crowding, isolation, cage tilt, wet bedding, in varying unpredictable order) increases proliferation of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) in the bone marrow, elevating circulating monocyte and neutrophil counts, and — in an atherosclerosis-prone model — worsening plaque inflammation (Heidt, Sager, Courties, et al., 2014, "Chronic variable stress activates hematopoietic stem cells," *Nature Medicine*, 20(7), 754–758). The mechanism: chronically stressed sympathetic nerve fibers in the bone marrow release excess norepinephrine, which signals through β3-adrenergic receptors on bone-marrow niche (stromal/mesenchymal) cells to down-regulate their production of CXCL12 — the chemokine that normally retains HSPCs in a quiescent niche state. Reduced local CXCL12 releases HSPCs from quiescence, increasing the cycling fraction and hence marrow output of monocytes and neutrophils. β3-adrenergic-receptor-knockout mice show a blunted version of this response (unchanged CXCL12, unchanged leukocyte output under the same stress paradigm), which is about as clean a causal demonstration as this literature offers.

### 2.2 Convergent mouse social-stress data and human translation

Independently, in a repeated-social-defeat paradigm, Powell, Sloan, Bailey, Arevalo, Chen, Miller, Kobor, Reader, Sheridan, and Cole showed that socially defeated mice up-regulate inflammatory gene expression in circulating leukocytes via exactly this route: increased bone-marrow output of Ly6Chigh monocytes and Ly6Cintermediate granulocytes, blocked pharmacologically by β-adrenergic-receptor antagonists and by antagonism of the myelopoietic growth factor GM-CSF (Powell, Sloan, Bailey, Arevalo, Chen, Miller, Kobor, Reader, Sheridan, & Cole, 2013, *PNAS*, 110(41), 16574–16579). This paper is useful specifically because it closes the loop between the human CTRA literature (Cole) and the animal hematopoiesis literature (Sheridan/Powell, later Heidt): the myeloid bias is not a downstream correlate of stress but a proximally β-adrenergic-driven output of the marrow.

A further finding bears directly on durability. McKim, Yin, Wang, Cole, Godbout, and Sheridan showed that repeated social defeat mobilizes HSPCs out of the bone marrow into the spleen, where they engraft and establish a *persistent extramedullary hematopoietic depot* — splenic progenitors continuing to differentiate into monocytes, neutrophils, and erythrocytes for at least 24 days after the stressor itself has ended, driven initially by β-adrenergic signaling (repeated isoprenaline injection reproduces the splenic mobilization) (McKim, Yin, Wang, Cole, Godbout, & Sheridan, 2018, "Social Stress Mobilizes Hematopoietic Stem Cells to Establish Persistent Splenic Myelopoiesis," *Cell Reports*, 25(9), 2552–2562). This is one of the few pieces of direct evidence that a socially-triggered myeloid bias can outlast its social trigger by weeks via a distinct anatomical mechanism (an ectopic hematopoietic organ), rather than simply decaying with SNS tone.

### 2.3 Structural remodeling of lymphoid innervation

The sympathetic side of this axis is not fixed wiring, either. Sloan, Capitanio, Tarara, Mendoza, Mason, and Cole showed that experimentally imposed chronic social subordination in rhesus macaques increases the density of catecholaminergic (norepinephrine-releasing) nerve fibers within axillary lymph nodes, concentrated in the T-cell/macrophage-rich paracortex, associated with elevated transcription of the neurotrophin NGF; functionally, denser sympathetic innervation was linked to blunted local Type I interferon response and increased SIV replication (Sloan, Capitanio, Tarara, Mendoza, Mason, & Cole, 2007, *Journal of Neuroscience*, 27(33), 8857–8865). This is a second, slower "installation" mechanism layered on top of the fast hormonal (β3/CXCL12) one: chronic social threat can literally re-wire the sympathetic nervous system's anatomical reach into lymphoid tissue, which would be expected to have its own — likely slower — reversal kinetics than circulating catecholamine level per se, since axon growth/pruning operates on a different timescale than adrenergic receptor signaling.

### 2.4 Installation versus decay asymmetry

Taken together, the myeloid bias appears to install fast (β3/CXCL12 signaling is essentially immediate once norepinephrine rises; monocytosis is detectable within days in the mouse chronic-stress models) but has at least two components with plausibly slower decay: (i) the ectopic splenic hematopoietic depot documented by McKim et al., persisting for weeks after stressor cessation, and (ii) structural sympathetic hyperinnervation of lymphoid tissue, which if it follows ordinary rules of activity-dependent axonal remodeling should decay on a timescale of weeks to months rather than hours to days. The asymmetry (fast installation, slow-and-multi-component decay) is the single most load-bearing empirical fact for this note's central hypothesis about reversal kinetics (§6, Hypothesis 1).

## 3. Microglia and Synaptic Editing

This is the program's key bridge — the point at which "the same danger biology that produces sickness behavior" stops being a metaphor about mood and becomes a literal claim about which synapses survive.

### 3.1 The baseline machinery: complement-tagged pruning

Beth Stevens and colleagues established that the classical complement cascade — evolved for opsonizing pathogens and dying cells for phagocytic clearance — is repurposed in the developing CNS to tag weak or superfluous synapses for elimination. C1q (and downstream C3) localize to synapses in the developing retinogeniculate system, and mice lacking C1q or C3 show a persistent, non-transient deficit in eye-specific segregation of retinogeniculate inputs — supernumerary synapses that remain functionally competent (they still evoke postsynaptic responses), indicating that complement tagging is required for the elimination itself, not merely for clearing debris from some independent pruning event (Stevens et al., 2007, "The classical complement cascade mediates CNS synapse elimination," *Cell*, 131(6), 1164–1178). Schafer, Lehrman, Kautzman, and colleagues then closed the cellular loop: microglia, via the complement receptor CR3 (which binds C3-opsonized targets), physically engulf complement-tagged synaptic material in an activity-dependent manner — less-active inputs are preferentially tagged and eaten (Schafer, Lehrman, Kautzman, et al., 2012, "Microglia sculpt postnatal neural circuits in an activity and complement-dependent manner," *Neuron*, 74(4), 691–705).

This machinery has independent genetic validation for a psychiatric phenotype. Sekar, Bialas, de Rivera, and colleagues (the Genomic Psychiatric Consortium / McCarroll lab) showed that structural variation at the complement C4 locus — specifically alleles producing higher expression of C4A — is associated with increased schizophrenia risk, that C4 protein localizes to synapses, dendrites, and axons, and that mice engineered to overexpress human C4A show excessive synaptic loss and schizophrenia-relevant behavioral changes, while C4-deficient mice show impaired developmental pruning (Sekar, Bialas, de Rivera, et al., 2016, *Nature*, 530(7589), 177–183; functional validation extended in Comer et al., 2020, *Nature Neuroscience*, 23(11), 1524–1535). The relevance here is not that social standing and schizophrenia share etiology, but that this establishes, independent of any stress paradigm, that quantitative variation in complement-tagging dose is sufficient to move synapse number and behavior in a mammalian brain — which is the precondition for taking seriously the claim that a stress-modulated increase in complement tagging could do the same thing on a different timescale and in different circuits.

### 3.2 Stress primes the microglial response — and priming looks controllability-dependent

Independent of complement/pruning work, Steven Maier, Linda Watkins, Matthew Frank and colleagues built a parallel line showing that prior stress "primes" microglia so that a subsequent immune or inflammatory challenge produces an exaggerated central cytokine response. In the paradigm case, rats exposed to inescapable tailshock show, eight days later, a potentiated hippocampal pro-inflammatory cytokine response to a peripheral LPS challenge relative to non-stressed controls, even though the stressor itself is long over by the time of the challenge (Frank, Baratta, Sprunger, Watkins, & Maier, 2007, *Brain, Behavior, and Immunity*, 21(1), 47–59). Mechanistically, stress-induced glucocorticoid release itself appears necessary for the priming — adrenalectomy or GR antagonism blocks the potentiation, making this a second, CNS-local instance of glucocorticoids acting as a "danger signal" that sensitizes rather than restrains inflammatory machinery, paralleling the paradoxical GR-permissive role documented peripherally in some contexts (Frank, Watkins, & Maier, and colleagues; "glucocorticoids mediate stress-induced priming of microglial pro-inflammatory responses"). A candidate receptor-level mechanism is down-regulation of the microglial inhibitory receptor CD200R following stress, which would disinhibit microglia's baseline suppressive brake in a manner structurally analogous to GR resistance in the periphery.

The Maier/Watkins learned-helplessness tradition is built almost entirely around *controllability*, not exposure per se, as the operative variable: in the classic triadic design, "escapable" stress (the animal can terminate shock by an operant response), "yoked inescapable" stress (identical shock timing and duration, but the animal has no control over termination), and no-shock control are compared, and it is specifically the inescapable/uncontrollable condition that produces the behavioral syndrome (passivity, deficits in subsequent escape learning) and the associated sensitization of dorsal-raphe serotonergic neurons — mediated by a corticotropin-releasing-factor pathway distinct from shock exposure per se, and overridable by a ventromedial-prefrontal-cortex signal that specifically detects behavioral control (Maier & Watkins, 2005; Maier, 2016, "Learned Helplessness at Fifty: Insights from Neuroscience," and follow-up mPFC-control fMRI/optogenetic work). The controllability variable has been established for the behavioral syndrome and its raphe substrate across decades of work; it has not, to this author's knowledge as of this search, been run as cleanly on microglial priming markers specifically (ex vivo cytokine potentiation, CD200R expression) in a full escapable/yoked-inescapable/control triad — the microglial-priming literature has mostly used inescapable-shock-only designs without a matched escapable-shock arm. That gap is precisely what makes it a genuine, falsifiable hypothesis rather than a restatement of known results (§7, Hypothesis 2).

### 3.3 Chronic/social stress recruits monocytes to the brain and drives synapse loss in adult circuits

Eric Wohleb and colleagues extended this system to social stress specifically, showing that repeated social defeat in mice increases trafficking of peripherally derived, bone-marrow-origin Ly6Chigh monocytes into the brain (a process dependent on stress-induced monocytosis of exactly the kind documented in §2), where they contribute directly to elevated central inflammatory signaling and anxiety-like behavior; blocking this monocyte recruitment (e.g., via CCR2 antagonism) attenuates the behavioral phenotype even though resident microglial activation still occurs, indicating monocytes and microglia are separable, additive contributors rather than one simply being a proxy for the other (Wohleb, McKim, Sheridan, & Godbout, "Repeated Social Defeat, Neuroinflammation, and Behavior: Monocytes Carry the Signal," *Neuropsychopharmacology* review; primary data in Wohleb, Powell, Godbout, & Sheridan, *Journal of Neuroscience*, and related papers, 2011–2015).

More recent work has closed the loop from this monocyte/microglia activation to complement-tagged synapse loss specifically. Wang and colleagues showed that chronic social defeat stress in mice drives microglia-dependent *excessive* synaptic pruning — elevated complement C3 during stress tags excitatory synapses for microglial phagocytic engulfment, producing cortical hypoconnectivity and behavioral abnormality; pharmacological or genetic interruption of the microglial phagocytic/complement axis rescues both the synapse loss and the behavior (Wang et al., 2023, *Brain, Behavior, and Immunity*, 109, 23–36). A parallel 2024 report localized complement- and microglia-mediated synapse loss specifically to layer 2/3 of the medial prefrontal cortex under chronic stress (*Nature Communications*, 2024), and a further line has implicated microglial Type I interferon signaling — notably the inverse of the peripheral CTRA's IFN suppression — as a distinct route to chronic-stress-induced synapse loss and social-behavior deficits (*Molecular Psychiatry*, 2024), and separable, even opposing, roles for microglial versus macrophage-expressed C3ar1 in stress-induced synaptic change (*Molecular Psychiatry*, 2025). The picture that emerges is not a single clean pathway but a small family of convergent and partially redundant routes — complement/CR3 engulfment, monocyte infiltration, and local Type I interferon — by which chronic stress edits the synaptic population of adult limbic/cortical circuits, echoing (and mechanistically extending) the developmental pruning logic established by Stevens and Schafer.

### 3.4 Minocycline: suggestive but not clean human evidence

Minocycline inhibits microglial activation and phagocytic activity in preclinical models and reliably attenuates depression-like behavior and stress-induced synapse loss in rodents. In humans, evidence is real but conditional. Nettis, Lombardo, Hastings, and colleagues, in a double-blind randomized 4-week trial of adjunctive minocycline (200 mg/day) in treatment-resistant depression, found no overall treatment effect in the full sample but a significant effect specifically in the subgroup with baseline CRP ≥ 3 mg/L (Nettis, Lombardo, Hastings, et al., 2021, *Neuropsychopharmacology*, 46(5), 939–948) — a result that is honestly a post hoc-flavored subgroup finding and was explicitly flagged by commentators for the general hazards of interpreting subgroup analyses in small trials (n=39 total). A larger, more recent randomized clinical trial of minocycline in treatment-resistant depression (2022) found no overall benefit, and minocycline likewise failed to improve depressive symptoms in a large schizophrenia trial where it was tested for a different primary indication. The honest summary: minocycline data are consistent with microglial/neuroinflammatory mechanisms mattering in a biologically-defined (CRP-high) subset of depression, but the field does not have a clean, adequately powered, prospectively-stratified positive trial, and null results in unselected samples are common enough that minocycline cannot be treated as established proof of the microglial-synapse-editing model in humans — only as compatible with it.

## 4. Embedding and Its Limits

### 4.1 The Meaney/Szyf line and what survives replication

Michael Meaney and Moshe Szyf's rodent work established that variation in maternal licking/grooming behavior produces stable differences in DNA methylation at the glucocorticoid receptor exon 1F promoter in hippocampus, associated with differences in GR expression, HPA-axis reactivity, and behavior in adult offspring — and that these differences are reversible by cross-fostering, demonstrating an epigenetic (not genetic) mechanism, later extended to a broader coordinated methylation signature spanning >100 kb (Weaver, Cervoni, Champagne, D'Alessio, Sharma, Seckl, Dymov, Szyf, & Meaney, 2004, *Nature Neuroscience*; McGowan, Suderman, Sasaki, Huang, Hallett, Meaney, & Szyf, 2011, *PLOS ONE*, broad-signature follow-up). Human candidate-gene work extended the same logic to NR3C1 exon 1F methylation in postmortem hippocampus from suicide victims with a history of childhood abuse, and in peripheral blood of maltreated and non-maltreated children and adults with early adversity.

The honest replication status, however, is weaker than the founding narrative suggests. A critical review of the NR3C1-methylation-as-mediator-of-early-adversity literature found substantial methodological inconsistency across studies — different CpG sites assayed, different tissues, different adversity measures, different statistical models — undermining direct comparison (a 2015 critical review in *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*). More decisively, when the field moved from single-locus candidate-gene studies to genome-wide, hypothesis-free epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS) of trauma-related phenotypes (PTSD, depression) at adequately corrected significance thresholds, none of the classic candidate genes — NR3C1 included, alongside OXTR and others — reached genome-wide significance; one large EWAS of World Trade Center responders found no epigenome-wide significant methylation associations with PTSD or depression at all at FDR-corrected thresholds. Effect sizes reported in the positive candidate-gene literature are also often small in absolute terms (methylation differences on the order of a few percentage points at individual CpGs), raising an open question about biological relevance even where statistically significant. This is a case where the mechanism (glucocorticoid-signaling epigenetic embedding of early adversity, demonstrated causally and reversibly in rodents) is much better established than the specific human biomarker (NR3C1 promoter methylation in blood) that was, for over a decade, treated as its human proxy. The rodent causal story stands; the single-locus human translation does not currently survive genome-wide scrutiny.

### 4.2 Epigenetic clocks: DunedinPACE as a better-behaved successor

A methodologically different approach — distributed, multi-CpG "pace of aging" clocks rather than single candidate loci — has fared better. DunedinPACE, trained on 19 longitudinal indicators of organ-system decline across two decades of the Dunedin birth cohort and distilled into a single blood-methylation score via elastic-net regression, shows high test-retest reliability and reproducibly predicts morbidity, disability, and mortality; critically for this note, it is accelerated in children and adults exposed to early-life socioeconomic disadvantage, neighborhood disadvantage, and victimization (Belsky et al., 2022, *eLife*, "DunedinPACE, a DNA methylation biomarker of the pace of aging"). Because DunedinPACE is a composite, its component CpGs are not individually interpretable the way NR3C1 methylation is claimed to be, but this may be precisely why it replicates better: it is not betting on a single locus having a large, biologically necessary, and reproducible effect, but on an aggregate signature that is more robust to noise at any one site — the same statistical logic that made single-SNP GWAS candidate-gene psychiatry give way to polygenic scores. As of current data, DunedinPACE-adversity associations are among the more replicated findings connecting early social/material adversity to a molecular aging readout; whether DunedinPACE itself indexes anything causally proximal to the CTRA/myeloid-bias machinery described above (as opposed to a more general biological-wear signature correlated with, but not mechanistically identical to, chronic inflammation) remains an open and testable question rather than an established fact.

### 4.3 Trained innate immunity: does social adversity train myeloid precursors?

Mihai Netea and colleagues established that innate immune cells and their bone-marrow progenitors can acquire a genuine, non-adaptive "immune memory" — trained immunity — following an initial stimulus (classically β-glucan or BCG vaccination): a durable epigenetic (histone methylation/acetylation) and metabolic (glycolytic shift) reprogramming of monocytes and, more durably, of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells themselves, such that subsequent unrelated challenges provoke an exaggerated response (reviewed in Netea et al., multiple; mechanistic HSPC-level demonstration in Kaufmann, Mermel, Netea et al. and follow-ups establishing that BCG reprograms HSPCs toward myeloid-biased differentiation). This is mechanistically the same *class* of phenomenon this note has already described for chronic psychological stress — HSPC-level reprogramming toward myeloid output — and the literatures have begun to explicitly converge: repeated social defeat stress in mice produces innate immune priming with features overlapping trained immunity (increased bone-marrow monocyte and granulocyte progenitor pools, enhanced inflammatory responsiveness on rechallenge), and reviews now explicitly discuss chronic stress as inducing a trained-immunity-like state in mice and humans. What is not yet established, as distinct from strongly suggestive, is a demonstration that specifically *social* adversity (rather than infectious/adjuvant stimuli) leaves a durable, HSPC-intrinsic epigenetic mark that outlasts the stressor and is transmissible through progenitor self-renewal (the defining feature that separates "trained immunity" from ordinary transient monocytosis) — most of the direct social-stress bone-marrow data to date (Powell 2013, Heidt 2014, McKim 2018) demonstrate elevated *output* and, in McKim's case, weeks-long persistence via an ectopic splenic depot, which is consistent with but does not by itself prove HSPC-intrinsic epigenetic training in the Netea sense. This is flagged explicitly as the least-settled claim in this section and is the basis of Hypothesis 4 below.

## 5. IL-6 Biology: Classic Signaling vs. Trans-Signaling

IL-6 is probably the single most cited cytokine in the social-genomics and inflammation-depression literatures, and the classic/trans-signaling distinction is not a technicality — it plausibly explains why IL-6 findings in this space are sometimes inconsistent.

**Classic signaling** requires the target cell to express membrane-bound IL-6Rα itself; IL-6 binds IL-6Rα, and the complex recruits gp130 (ubiquitously expressed) to activate JAK/STAT3. Classic signaling is largely restricted to hepatocytes and some leukocyte subsets and mediates IL-6's regenerative/anti-inflammatory and acute-phase (CRP induction) functions.

**Trans-signaling** occurs when IL-6 binds a soluble form of IL-6Rα (sIL-6R, shed proteolytically from cell membranes, chiefly by ADAM17) circulating in plasma; the IL-6/sIL-6R complex can then activate gp130 on *any* cell, including cells that do not themselves express membrane IL-6Rα. This matters enormously for CNS effects: neurons and astrocytes express gp130 broadly but membrane IL-6Rα only sparsely, and multiple lines of evidence (reviewed and directly tested by Rose-John's group) indicate that IL-6's pathogenic/pro-inflammatory actions in brain — synaptic and BBB effects — are substantially trans-signaling-dependent rather than classic-signaling-dependent (Rose-John and colleagues, e.g., "Trans-Signaling Is a Dominant Mechanism for the Pathogenic Actions of Interleukin-6 in the Brain," *Journal of Neuroscience*, 2014, 34(7), 2503–2512). A naturally occurring soluble gp130 (sgp130) selectively neutralizes trans-signaling (it binds the IL-6/sIL-6R complex but not IL-6 alone), and sgp130Fc — an engineered high-affinity version — is the standard pharmacological tool for dissociating trans- from classic-signaling effects in vivo, including demonstrations that blocking trans-signaling alone attenuates BBB leakage and neuroinflammatory angiogenesis. The practical implication for this program: a peripheral CTRA/myeloid-bias state that elevates circulating IL-6 could have qualitatively different CNS consequences depending on how much of that IL-6 pool is engaging classic versus trans-signaling — a distinction essentially invisible to standard serum IL-6 ELISA measurements, which is a genuine and underappreciated limitation of most human social-genomics biomarker work to date.

Human genetic (Mendelian randomization) evidence on IL-6 and depression is real but not fully resolved, and the details cut in a direction some readers will find counterintuitive. The IL6R Asp358Ala variant (rs2228145) reduces classical IL-6 signaling (it increases proteolytic shedding of IL-6R, raising soluble IL-6R and circulating IL-6 while lowering downstream classic-signaling transduction and CRP), and carriers show *lower* risk of depression and psychosis in longitudinal birth-cohort data — i.e., genetically dampened classical IL-6 signaling looks protective. Broader two-sample Mendelian randomization work using IL6R-region and CRP-region instruments has found a similar, sometimes divergent pattern: genetically predicted higher IL-6 (pathway) activity associates with increased depression risk in some analyses, while genetically predicted higher CRP (a downstream marker, not a driver) associates with decreased risk in the same framework — an apparent paradox most plausibly resolved by the classic/trans-signaling split (CRP indexes hepatic classic-signaling output; depression risk may track trans-signaling activity more directly), though the field itself flags this as unresolved and in need of instruments that can separate the two signaling modes genetically (Kelly, Smith, & Mezuk, 2021, "Depression and interleukin-6 signaling: A Mendelian Randomization study," *Brain, Behavior, and Immunity*, 95, 106–114; Khandaker and colleagues' functional IL6R-variant birth-cohort work). The honest state of the art is: IL-6 pathway activity is very likely causally, not just correlationally, related to depression risk in at least a subset of cases, but which arm of the receptor system (classic vs. trans) is doing the causal work is not yet nailed down by human genetics alone — which is exactly the gap the animal sgp130Fc pharmacology (above) is positioned to close, and has not yet closed for the specific case of chronic-social-stress-driven CNS effects.

## 6. Reversal Kinetics: How Fast Does the Machinery Un-Install?

This is the section with the thinnest direct evidence, and it deserves to be treated that way rather than papered over with extrapolation.

**Peripheral CTRA reversal under sustained intervention.** The cleanest human data come from Cole-collaborator trials pairing psychosocial/behavioral interventions with pre/post leukocyte transcriptomics. In breast cancer survivors with insomnia, a randomized trial found that both Tai Chi Chih and cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) reduced CTRA gene expression and plasma inflammatory markers over roughly 15 months of intervention and follow-up, with Tai Chi producing somewhat larger reductions in plasma IL-6 specifically. Cognitive-behavioral stress management (CBSM) in breast cancer patients reversed the CTRA's characteristic NF-κB-up/IRF1-down pattern, and — notably, in longer follow-up of the same cohort — CTRA reduction after CBSM was associated with longer disease-free survival at 8–15 years, which is about as strong a piece of external validation as this literature has for CTRA being mechanistically, not just descriptively, linked to health outcomes. These interventions run for weeks to months, so they establish that reversal is *possible* on that timescale but do not resolve how much faster (if any) it could go, nor decompose the change into per-cell transcriptional reprogramming versus monocyte-population re-weighting (§1.3) — none of the published trials, to this search's knowledge, report the cell-composition deconvolution needed to test that directly.

**Shorter, more surgical interventions show a floor.** Brief social-behavioral manipulations offer a cleaner dose–response on the fast end. An 8-week randomized trial instructing participants to act more extraverted reduced CTRA gene expression relative to active controls, partially mediated by reduced loneliness — but the effect was *not sustained* at 1-month follow-up after the intervention ended, i.e., as soon as the behavioral input stopped, the transcriptomic gain decayed within a month. Separately, brief (roughly 4-week) prosocial "acts of kindness" interventions have shown CTRA reductions that persisted to a 1-week follow-up but were not tracked longer. Read together, the pattern across this small set of trials is: CTRA can move within weeks given a sustained behavioral input, but neither the lower bound (how fast could it move if the change were larger/more durable) nor the decay dynamics after input withdrawal look like a simple on/off switch — the extraversion-trial finding in particular is consistent with reversal requiring *continued* input rather than being a one-time re-set, which would fit a population-turnover-gated model (§2.4, Hypothesis 1) better than a pure signaling-toggle model: if CTRA reversal reflected only removal of adrenergic/GR-resistance signaling, one might expect the gain to persist at least as long as loneliness itself stayed reduced, rather than eroding within a month.

**No published human trial, to this search's knowledge, has combined the CTRA-transcriptomic endpoint with the myeloid-population and bone-marrow-output measures from §2** (flow cytometric monocyte subset kinetics, let alone marrow imaging or splenic-depot analogues) in the same intervention design — which means the central empirical claim of this note's leading hypothesis (that population turnover, not signaling, is the rate-limiting step) is currently an inference from separately-measured pieces (monocyte lifespan data, the McKim splenic-persistence data, the CTRA-intervention timecourses) rather than a directly demonstrated fact. That gap is stated explicitly rather than elided, and is what Hypothesis 1 below is designed to close.

## Hypotheses

**Hypothesis 1 — CTRA reversal is rate-limited by myeloid population turnover, not by transcriptional signaling alone.**
*(i) Prediction:* When a genuinely durable improvement in social conditions abruptly reduces sympathetic/GR-resistance signaling, the per-cell transcriptional component of CTRA (measured within a fixed monocyte subset) will normalize within hours to a few days, but the population-composition component (fraction of classical vs. intermediate vs. nonclassical monocytes, and any residual splenic/extramedullary output) will lag by a period set by monocyte/granulocyte lifespan and marrow-to-blood transit time — empirically, classical monocytes circulate roughly one day and undergo a ~1.6-day postmitotic marrow interval, but a stress-expanded progenitor pool and any ectopic (splenic) hematopoietic depot would be expected to take substantially longer, on the order of one to several weeks, to fully clear. *(ii) Discriminating test:* Serial (daily-to-weekly) paired flow cytometry/CyTOF and monocyte-subset-sorted RNA-seq before and after an abrupt, high-credibility improvement in social standing (a real status/relationship change, or an experimental belonging manipulation sustained over weeks), decomposing total CTRA score into a per-cell-transcription term and a population-mixture term via deconvolution, and comparing each term's time-to-half-normalization against concurrently measured sympathetic tone (heart-rate variability, salivary alpha-amylase). *(iii) Kill condition:* If the population-mixture component normalizes as fast as sympathetic tone and per-cell transcription (i.e., no measurable lag beyond assay noise), population turnover is not rate-limiting and CTRA reversal is gated by signaling alone — the hypothesis is false and the "floor" claim should be dropped from the program's model of standing-conferral.

**Hypothesis 2 — Microglial priming by social threat is controllability-dependent, not exposure-dependent.**
*(i) Prediction:* Holding objective exposure to social-rejection/isolation cues constant, an arm with behavioral control over exposure onset/termination will show no significant microglial priming (ex vivo LPS-stimulated cytokine potentiation, CD200R down-regulation, complement C1q/C3 synaptic tagging where assayable), while a yoked arm receiving identical objective exposure without control will show significant priming on all three measures, replicating the escapable/yoked-inescapable logic of the Maier/Watkins learned-helplessness paradigm but for social (rather than physical shock) threat. *(ii) Discriminating test:* A triadic escapable/yoked-inescapable/no-exposure social-stress design (feasible in rodent social-defeat or resident-intruder variants with an operant escape contingency) with ex vivo microglial cytokine-priming assay and CD200R immunohistochemistry as primary endpoints, run alongside the standard behavioral (helplessness) readout to confirm the paradigm reproduced the known controllability effect before trusting the microglial result. *(iii) Kill condition:* If priming magnitude in the yoked and escapable arms is statistically indistinguishable (both differing from no-exposure controls by a similar amount), priming is dose/exposure-dependent rather than controllability-dependent, and the "standing requires restored agency, not just reduced threat input" framing loses its strongest piece of proposed neuroimmune evidence.

**Hypothesis 3 — CTRA/GR-response signatures track the stability of social rank, not its ordinal height, dissociable via experimental rank manipulation.**
*(i) Prediction:* In experimental social-hierarchy manipulations that can move an animal's rank independently of how long that rank has been held or how likely it is to change again soon (as in the Tung/Snyder-Mackler/Barreiro macaque social-status paradigm, which has already shown that immune gene expression and chromatin accessibility at GR-responsive elements are plastic, reversible, and history-dependent rather than fixed by current rank alone), animals with low but long-stable rank will show a smaller CTRA elevation and less GR-response blunting than animals of equal or higher current rank but recent/frequent rank reversal. *(ii) Discriminating test:* Whole-blood RNA-seq CTRA scoring plus ex vivo glucocorticoid-stimulation transcriptional/chromatin-accessibility response, sampled in animals whose rank history (time-since-last-change, number of reversals in a fixed prior window) is experimentally varied independently of current ordinal rank via sequential group reformation, with a statistical model testing whether rank-instability terms explain CTRA/GR-response variance beyond current rank alone. *(iii) Kill condition:* If a model containing only current ordinal rank explains the CTRA/GR-response variance as well as a model adding rank-stability/history terms (no significant improvement in fit), rank and its literal biology are adequately captured by position alone, and the "standing as durability of position, not position itself" distinction central to this research program would not be supported by this system.

**Hypothesis 4 — HSPC-level trained-immunity-like reprogramming, not NR3C1 candidate-gene methylation, is the physical substrate carrying early social adversity into adult CTRA bias.**
*(i) Prediction:* Chromatin/metabolic reprogramming markers in purified CD34+ hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (histone modifications and glycolytic-pathway priming at myeloid-commitment loci, following the Netea trained-immunity framework) will show a reproducible, dose-graded, cross-cohort-replicating association with well-characterized early-life adversity severity and with adult CTRA/monocyte-bias phenotype, in contrast to NR3C1 exon-1F peripheral-blood methylation, which has not survived EWAS-scale multiple-testing correction. *(ii) Discriminating test:* In at least two independent cohorts with comparable early-adversity phenotyping, compare (a) NR3C1 methylation and (b) CD34+ HSPC chromatin/metabolic trained-immunity markers as predictors of adult CTRA score, using pre-registered EWAS-scale correction in both arms, controlling statistically for current perceived stress/social integration. *(iii) Kill condition:* If the HSPC trained-immunity markers show the same cohort-specific non-replication pattern currently seen for NR3C1 methylation (nominally significant in a single cohort, failing to replicate at corrected thresholds in an independent sample), the "trained immunity as the embedding substrate" hypothesis collapses to just another unreplicated single-mechanism candidate, and a distributed multi-locus signature (DunedinPACE-like) should be preferred as the working model of embedding instead.

**Hypothesis 5 — IL-6 trans-signaling, not classical signaling, is the specific bridge from peripheral myeloid/CTRA bias to central microglial priming and synaptic engulfment.**
*(i) Prediction:* In a chronic social-stress animal model, selective pharmacological blockade of IL-6 trans-signaling (sgp130Fc) will prevent or substantially attenuate microglial priming markers and complement-tagged synaptic engulfment (and associated behavioral deficits) while leaving peripheral monocyte CTRA-equivalent transcription largely intact, whereas blockade of both classical and trans-signaling (anti-IL-6R) will attenuate both the peripheral and the central phenotype. *(ii) Discriminating test:* Three-arm chronic social-defeat experiment (sgp130Fc vs. anti-IL-6R vs. vehicle) with paired peripheral monocyte transcriptomics (CTRA score) and central endpoints (ex vivo microglial priming, hippocampal/PFC complement-C3-tagged synapse density, behavior). *(iii) Kill condition:* If sgp130Fc fails to protect against the central microglial/synaptic phenotype (i.e., the phenotype requires classical signaling, or is IL-6-independent and driven instead by IL-1β/TNF as some of the Wohleb-lineage data would also permit), trans-signaling is not the operative bridge, and the peripheral-to-central mechanistic link in this program's model needs to be relocated — most plausibly to direct CX3CR1-dependent monocyte trafficking into brain parenchyma, which the Wohleb data already show is sufficient on its own to move central inflammatory tone.
