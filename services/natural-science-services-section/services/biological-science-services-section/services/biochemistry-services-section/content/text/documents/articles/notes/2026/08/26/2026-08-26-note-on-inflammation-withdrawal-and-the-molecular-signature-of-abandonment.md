# Inflammation, Withdrawal, and the Molecular Signature of Abandonment

The theory this note serves treats abandonment as a *maintained relation* — an order actively holding someone as excluded, administering scarcity through defaults and silences rather than open denial — and treats acedia as the restless withdrawal that follows: sorrow at the good, flight from one's own place and task, generalized in modernity as the fatigue-syndrome of achievement society. The question this note takes up is narrow and empirical: is there a literal biology of that pairing? Does exclusion get metabolized, and does the resulting withdrawal look like an organized physiological program rather than mere breakdown? The literature reviewed below says, with real but uneven confidence: yes, there is a conserved inflammatory program that translates social threat into withdrawal behavior; yes, its cellular substrate (leukocyte gene expression) tracks chronic social adversity in a dose-graded way; and no, the strongest published claims about reversing it through inner reorientation (eudaimonia) do not currently survive independent statistical scrutiny. The clean part of the story is upstream (sickness behavior, social pain, the CTRA descriptive findings); the contested part is downstream (whether treating inflammation treats depression for everyone, and whether meaning without material or structural change reverses the genomic signature). Both parts matter to the theory, and this note tries not to blur them into each other.

## 1. Sickness behavior as an organized program, not damage

The starting fact, established well before any of the social science downstream of it, is that an organism's withdrawal during illness is not an incidental side-effect of being unwell — it is orchestrated. Hart's classic synthesis argued that fever, lethargy, anorexia, and social disengagement in sick animals are not signs of debilitation but a "highly organized, evolved behavioral strategy" that reallocates metabolic resources away from foraging, mating, and thermoregulatory activity and toward the metabolically expensive business of mounting a fever and an immune response (Hart, 1988). Robert Dantzer's subsequent decades of work gave this claim a mechanism: peripheral proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6), released by activated macrophages during infection or injury, signal the brain via both a fast neural route (vagal and other primary afferents) and a slower humoral route (circumventricular organs, choroid plexus) to produce a coordinated central motivational state — what Dantzer terms "sickness behavior": reduced locomotion, anhedonia, social withdrawal, altered sleep, decreased grooming, cognitive slowing (Dantzer, 2001; Dantzer et al., 2008; Dantzer & Kelley, 2007). The crucial theoretical move is that this is described as a *motivational* reorganization, not a loss of capacity: sick animals are not simply too weak to engage; they are differentially motivated toward energy-conserving, recovery-oriented behavior and away from behaviors — exploration, social contact, reproduction — whose payoff is discounted while the infection is active. The state is reversible (it resolves as the inflammatory stimulus resolves), dose-responsive (larger cytokine challenges produce more withdrawal), and blockable by anti-inflammatory or cytokine-antagonist pretreatment — properties that mark it as a program executed by the organism rather than damage inflicted on it.

This distinction — organized reallocation versus damage — is exactly what the theory needs for its biological hypothesis to have empirical teeth: if social exclusion recruits the same cytokine circuitry, then socially produced withdrawal should share sickness behavior's signature properties (motivational rather than purely capacity-limiting, reversible on removal of the inflammatory driver, dose-graded with the intensity/duration of the adverse signal) rather than behaving like generic fatigue or generic sadness.

## 2. Inflammation and depression: from IFN-α to inflammatory subtypes

The clearest causal demonstration that peripheral inflammation can *produce* a depressive syndrome in humans, not merely correlate with one, comes from interferon-alpha (IFN-α) immunotherapy. IFN-α is itself a cytokine, given at high doses for hepatitis C and some cancers, and induces a depressive syndrome in roughly 20–45% of previously euthymic recipients within weeks — with fatigue, anhedonia, psychomotor slowing and social withdrawal typically preceding mood change, echoing sickness behavior's own onset pattern (Capuron & Miller, 2004; Capuron et al., 2002; Musselman et al., 2001). Because the inflammatory trigger here is administered rather than observed, this body of work is the strongest causal evidence available that cytokine signaling alone, independent of any life stressor, is sufficient to produce a depressive state in a substantial minority of humans, and that pretreatment with SSRIs can blunt the syndrome — implying shared downstream neurochemistry with idiopathic depression (Capuron & Miller, 2004; Musselman et al., 2001).

Miller and Raison's synthesis situates this mechanistically and evolutionarily: across evolutionary history, wounding and infection risk were so tightly coupled to social threat and predation that a single integrated response — inflammatory activation coupled with sickness-behavior-like withdrawal, anhedonia, and vigilance — was adaptive whether the threat was a pathogen or a conspecific attack (Miller & Raison, 2016, *Nature Reviews Immunology*). In modern, comparatively pathogen-poor environments, the same circuitry is instead chronically triggered by psychosocial stress, obesity, poor sleep, and other low-grade inflammatory drivers, and — the argument goes — produces depression as a maladaptive echo of an old adaptive program. This is directly continuous with Hart's and Dantzer's framing: the theory's "energy reallocation" language is not a metaphor borrowed from immunology, it is immunology's own account of what the state is *for*.

The subtype claim follows: not all depression is inflammatory. Roughly a quarter to a third of patients with major depressive disorder show elevated CRP or other inflammatory markers, and this subgroup is disproportionately characterized by anhedonia, psychomotor slowing, and treatment resistance to conventional monoamine-based antidepressants (Miller & Raison, 2016; Osimo et al., 2019, meta-analysis of CRP prevalence in depression). This motivated a wave of trials stratifying patients by baseline inflammation before testing anti-inflammatory augmentation.

**The trial evidence is genuinely mixed, and should be reported as such.** Raison et al.'s 2013 randomized placebo-controlled trial of the TNF-antagonist infliximab in treatment-resistant depression found *no overall antidepressant effect* — but a significant CRP-by-treatment interaction: patients with baseline hsCRP > 5 mg/L showed improvement with infliximab relative to placebo, while those below that threshold showed a *trend toward worse* outcomes on infliximab (Raison et al., 2013, *JAMA Psychiatry*). This is the founding result for "inflammation stratifies response," but it was a single trial with a post-hoc-flavored subgroup analysis and a small sample (60 patients). Kappelmann et al.'s meta-analysis of anti-cytokine treatments in chronic inflammatory (non-psychiatric) conditions found a significant antidepressant effect of cytokine-targeting biologics that held up after controlling for improvement in the primary physical illness (Kappelmann et al., 2018, *Molecular Psychiatry*) — encouraging, but these are populations selected for rheumatologic or dermatologic disease, not depression per se. More recent, purpose-built inflammation-stratified trials are decidedly not uniformly positive: the PREDDICT trial (celecoxib augmentation of vortioxetine, stratified by hsCRP) and the follow-on INSTA-MD protocol were designed explicitly because prior post-hoc CRP-stratified signals needed prospective confirmation in adequately powered samples (Jha et al./PREDDICT results, 2023–2024; INSTA-MD protocol, Pmc11440344, 2024) — and a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* of anti-inflammatory augmentation specifically in elevated-inflammation depressed samples found effects on depressive symptom severity that were real but heterogeneous across agent class and modest in the anhedonia domain specifically (AJP meta-analysis, 2025). The honest summary: inflammatory subtyping is a real and replicated *phenotypic* distinction (a subgroup of depressed patients is measurably more inflamed and more anhedonic/treatment-resistant), but "anti-inflammatory drugs treat this subgroup's depression" is a much less settled claim than "inflammation can cause depressive symptoms" — the causal demonstration (IFN-α) is far more secure than the therapeutic reversal claim (anti-inflammatory augmentation), and effect sizes and agent-specificity vary enough across trials that no simple "give the inflamed patients an NSAID or biologic" recommendation is currently well supported for general psychiatric practice.

## 3. The social signal: rejection and inflammation without infection

If sickness behavior is the template, the next question is whether *social* threat alone — no pathogen, no wound — can drive the same cytokine machinery. Naomi Eisenberger's program supplies the strongest experimental evidence that it can, and does so bidirectionally. On one direction: experimentally induced inflammation (low-dose endotoxin) increases self-reported feelings of *social disconnection*, and this increase in social disconnection statistically mediates endotoxin's effect on depressed mood — i.e., an inflammatory challenge with no social content whatsoever makes people feel more excluded, and that felt exclusion is part of how it produces low mood (Eisenberger et al., 2010, *Brain, Behavior, and Immunity*). On the other direction: exposure to a laboratory social-evaluative stressor (the Trier Social Stress Test paradigm) produces measurable increases in circulating inflammatory markers (soluble TNF receptor II, IL-6), and individuals who show greater neural sensitivity to social rejection (dorsal anterior cingulate and anterior insula activity to exclusion in an fMRI paradigm) also show larger inflammatory responses to the social stressor — evidence for a shared, individually-variable neuroimmune sensitivity to social threat (Slavich, Way, Eisenberger & Taylor, 2010, *PNAS*). Eisenberger's broader "social pain" framework argues that social and physical pain share overlapping neural substrates (dACC/AI) precisely because, evolutionarily, social separation from the group was a survival threat comparable to physical injury (Eisenberger, 2020 review, *World Psychiatry*).

Slavich and Irwin's Social Signal Transduction Theory of Depression assembles this into an explicit causal chain: social-evaluative threat and rejection are appraised by the brain as danger signals, which activate the sympathetic nervous system and HPA axis, which in turn up-regulate pro-inflammatory gene expression and cytokine release in circulating immune cells; the resulting inflammation then acts back on the brain via the routes Dantzer described to produce the depressive/sickness-behavior phenotype (Slavich & Irwin, 2014, *Psychological Bulletin*). The theory's distinctive contribution is making "social" the trigger and "molecular/behavioral" the mechanism in one continuous pathway, and it has been tested prospectively — e.g., in adolescents, interpersonal life stress predicts subsequent inflammatory markers and depressive symptoms in the pattern the theory predicts (Slavich et al., 2020, *Depression and Anxiety*). This is the theoretical hinge the abandonment program needs: it is the piece of the literature that explicitly, not metaphorically, treats *social* exclusion as an inflammatory stimulus.

## 4. The transcriptomics of abandonment: the Conserved Transcriptional Response to Adversity

This is the sharpest empirical target for the theory, because it moves past mood and behavior to the leukocyte's own transcriptional program — a molecular readout of how the immune system's gene expression is reorganized under chronic social adversity.

**The core finding.** Steve Cole and colleagues, beginning with a 2007 study of gene expression in chronically lonely versus non-lonely older adults, identified a repeatable shift they termed the Conserved Transcriptional Response to Adversity (CTRA): up-regulation of pro-inflammatory gene transcripts (including IL1B, IL6, IL8/CXCL8, TNF, and related NF-κB-target genes) coupled with down-regulation of genes involved in type I interferon antiviral response and mature B-cell antibody synthesis (Cole et al., 2007, *Genome Biology*; Cole, 2013, *Annual Review of Psychology*). The mechanistic account is that chronic perceived threat sustains sympathetic nervous system activity, and prolonged norepinephrine/β-adrenergic signaling on leukocyte glucocorticoid and CREB/NF-κB pathways shifts a myeloid lineage bias (monocyte expansion, altered maturation) that produces exactly this pro-inflammatory/antiviral-suppressed transcriptional profile — described by Cole as an evolved response tuned for a world in which social threat predicted bacterial wounding risk more than viral risk, so the immune system pre-adapts toward the bacterial-fighting, antiviral-deprioritizing configuration (Cole et al., 2015, *PNAS*, on myeloid differentiation dynamics in perceived isolation).

**Loneliness and social isolation.** Cacioppo, Cole and colleagues extended this in a series of studies: chronically lonely individuals (identified via the UCLA Loneliness Scale, tracked longitudinally in some cohorts) show elevated CTRA scores relative to socially embedded peers matched on objective social contact — i.e., the effect tracks *subjective* isolation more than objective network size, which is a nontrivial point for a theory about "maintained exclusion" as a relational status rather than a headcount (Cole et al., 2007; Cacioppo, Cacioppo, Capitanio & Cole, 2015, *Annual Review of Psychology*, "The Neuroendocrinology of Social Isolation").

**Generalization beyond loneliness.** The CTRA signature is not loneliness-specific. It has been reported under low childhood and adult socioeconomic status (Cole et al., and later replications), chronic family caregiving strain — e.g., caregivers of a relative with brain cancer show elevated CTRA relative to matched non-caregivers (cited in reviews of "Only the Lonely," caregiving-CTRA literature), and racial discrimination — a 2023–2024 study of Black trauma-center patients found that higher lifetime experienced racial discrimination predicted increased CTRA gene expression over the following months, net of objective injury severity (study in *Brain, Behavior, and Immunity*, ~2023–2024). This breadth is important for the theory: CTRA looks like a general transductive pathway from *perceived, chronic, socially-mediated threat* — of several structurally different kinds — into the same leukocyte program, which is consistent with (though does not by itself prove) the theory's claim that it is the *relation* of exclusion, not any one material particular, that the body is registering.

**The eudaimonia-reversal claim, and why it needs qualification.** The most theory-relevant and most contested result in this literature is Fredrickson, Cole and colleagues' 2013 PNAS paper reporting that, across ~80 healthy adults, *eudaimonic* well-being (purpose, meaning) was associated with a *lower* CTRA profile, while *hedonic* well-being (positive affect, pleasure) — despite being highly correlated with eudaimonic well-being at the level of self-report — was associated with a *higher* CTRA profile (Fredrickson et al., 2013, *PNAS*). The claim, in short, was that two phenomenologically similar forms of "feeling good" have opposite molecular signatures, and that only the meaning/purpose route down-regulates the adversity program. This result was quickly and forcefully challenged. Brown, MacDonald, Samanez-Larkin, Vohs and Kenrick's critical reanalysis (2014, *PNAS*) reported that factor-analyzing the same well-being items did not recover Fredrickson's hedonic/eudaimonic distinction at all — plausible alternative factor solutions existed, and people who scored high on "hedonic" items also scored high on "eudaimonic" items, undermining the premise that these are separable constructs whose *differential* gene-expression correlates can even be meaningfully estimated; a further comment (Brown, Coyne et al., 2016, *Collabra: Psychology*) pressed additional statistical problems (including how the composite CTRA gene-set score was constructed and analyzed). Fredrickson and Cole's rebuttal (2015, PLOS ONE, "Psychological Well-Being and the Human Conserved Transcriptional Response to Adversity") reported the eudaimonia–CTRA association replicating across three independent samples using mixed-effects models on the individual genes, and argued Brown et al.'s reanalyses contained their own statistical errors — and a further volley ("Selective Data Analysis in Brown et al.'s Continued Critical Reanalysis," 2016) continued the exchange. As of this writing the dispute has not been resolved by a clean independent replication with a pre-registered analysis plan; both sides have published rebuttals rather than either side conceding. **For the theory's purposes, the correct posture is: the CTRA construct itself (adversity/isolation raising it) rests on a much broader and more consistent evidence base than the specific claim that eudaimonic reorientation, without any change in objective social or material circumstances, reverses it.** The eudaimonia-reversal papers are contested science; treating that specific pair of studies as if it settled "meaning alone fixes the molecular signature" would overstate what the field currently supports, and the theory should not lean on it as load-bearing evidence for a claim that sentiment/reframing without standing-conferral is sufficient.

**What does hold up better:** stress-reduction and psychosocial intervention studies with more mechanistically grounded designs — e.g., group-based cognitive-behavioral stress management in breast cancer patients — have reported reduced pro-inflammatory and increased interferon-related gene expression post-intervention relative to control (reviewed in Slavich, 2023, *Lifestyle Medicine*; see also recent CBT-and-CTRA and trauma-narrative-writing pilot studies, e.g. a 2024–2025 culturally adapted trauma-writing pilot reporting reduced CTRA gene expression). These are generally smaller, less contested studies than the Fredrickson/Cole eudaimonia papers, but they also intervene on subjective processing of stress and threat appraisal, not on objective social standing or material circumstances — which is exactly the gap the theory's own hypotheses (below) are built to probe.

## 5. Reversal by standing: structural interventions and inflammatory/CTRA markers

This is the area with the thinnest direct evidence, and the note should say so plainly rather than paper over the gap.

**Cash transfers.** Despite a large and growing randomized-trial literature on unconditional cash transfers and guaranteed income (e.g., the OpenResearch Unconditional Income Study, the Baby's First Years trial, multiple LMIC GiveDirectly-style trials), searches for direct inflammatory-biomarker (CRP/IL-6) outcomes from these trials turn up mostly *protocols and planned biomarker sub-studies* rather than published results at the time of writing — several ongoing large cash-transfer RCTs (e.g., Baby's First Years, OpenResearch's ORUS) include biomarker or health-outcome arms whose inflammatory-marker results were not yet located in this search. LMIC cash-transfer literature more broadly shows effects on child growth, health-service use, and mortality, which are consistent with a downstream physiological effect but are not themselves inflammatory-pathway evidence. This is a genuine evidentiary gap, not a null result — it should not be reported as "cash transfers don't affect inflammation," only as "the direct test has largely not yet been run and published with these specific biomarkers."

**Volunteering / social-integration interventions.** The HEAL-HOA dual randomized controlled trial (telephone-based volunteering and befriending interventions for lonely, low-income older adults in Hong Kong) found significant reductions in loneliness, gains in social network engagement, and reductions in depressive symptoms and perceived stress at 6 months (HEAL-HOA results, *Lancet Healthy Longevity*, 2024; PMC12762928, 2025) — and its protocol explicitly included diurnal cortisol as a planned biomarker in a subsample, but full inflammatory-marker (CRP/IL-6) results were not identified in this search as of the time of writing; this is again a promising design whose molecular readout is pending or not yet located, rather than a demonstrated null.

**Housing.** The Housing First / At Home–Chez Soi trials (Canada) and related supportive-housing RCTs show robust effects on housing stability, service utilization, and some mental health and substance-use outcomes (Goering et al., At Home/Chez Soi, 2011–2014), but this search did not locate published inflammatory-biomarker or cortisol sub-studies from these specific trials — a notable gap given how well-suited "unconditional housing regardless of sobriety/compliance" is as a live test of durable-standing-without-behavioral-conditionality.

**Structural stigma reduction (a related, better-evidenced adjacent case).** The clearest existing evidence that *changing a person's structural/legal standing*, independent of any change in their material resources, has a downstream health signature comes from research on legal recognition of same-sex marriage: reductions in structural stigma via marriage-equality legislation are associated with improved psychological and physical health outcomes among sexual-minority adults (public-health literature reviewed in *The Lancet Public Health*, 2023, and *AJPH*, 2011), and minority-stress models predict this operates via chronic stress physiology. However, direct CTRA/inflammatory-transcriptomic readouts specifically tied to marriage-equality legal changes were not located in this search; the mechanism is inferred from the broader minority-stress-and-inflammation literature rather than demonstrated end-to-end in a single study. This is the closest existing analogue to the theory's "standing without material transfer" comparison arm, and it is worth flagging as the most promising *existing* natural experiment to mine for CTRA data, even though that specific analysis appears not yet to have been published.

**Honest overall read of section 5:** the "does conferring standing reverse the biology" question is, at present, mostly *un-run* rather than *answered either way*. The infrastructure to run it exists (RCTs of cash transfers, housing, and volunteering with banked biospecimens; natural experiments in legal status change), but the specific inflammatory/CTRA readouts the theory needs have — as far as this search could establish — mostly not yet been published. This is itself informative: it identifies where the theory's hypotheses would break genuinely new empirical ground rather than restate settled results.

## Hypotheses

1. **CTRA tracks the institutional form of exclusion, not just its material severity.**
   *Prediction:* among populations matched on material deprivation (income, housing quality, food security), those held in *maintained, administratively-enacted* exclusion (e.g., undocumented immigration status, active parole/probation supervision, denied asylum status, being on an official "ineligible" list for a benefit one otherwise qualifies for) will show higher CTRA scores than those with equivalent deprivation but no such standing-denial (e.g., documented low-wage workers, benefit recipients awaiting routine processing).
   *Discriminating test:* a cross-sectional or short-longitudinal leukocyte-transcriptomic study (RNA-seq or the standard 53-gene CTRA panel) comparing matched groups on income/housing/food-security bands but differing in formal exclusionary status, with CTRA regressed on status category controlling for material covariates and perceived stress/loneliness scales (to check the effect isn't just re-deriving the loneliness result).
   *What would disprove it:* if CTRA differences between the groups disappear (or reverse) once material covariates and self-reported chronic stress are controlled — i.e., if administrative/institutional exclusion carries no CTRA signature beyond what its material and subjective-stress correlates already explain, the "form of exclusion matters independently" claim is false and CTRA should be treated as a general chronic-stress readout with no special sensitivity to *institutional* exclusion as such.

2. **Durable standing-conferral reduces CTRA where equivalent material transfer without standing-change does not.**
   *Prediction:* in a population under maintained exclusion, an intervention that confers durable, unconditional, secure standing (e.g., a legal status grant, an unconditional and non-time-limited housing guarantee, permanent program enrollment with no re-qualification requirement) will reduce CTRA more than an intervention delivering equivalent or greater material value but leaving standing conditional, discretionary, or renewable-at-risk (e.g., a cash transfer of matched dollar value, or housing contingent on periodic re-certification).
   *Discriminating test:* leverage an existing or new RCT/natural experiment with two arms differing in standing-security but matched (or over-matched, i.e. the conditional-standing arm receives *more* material value) on material transfer size — e.g., pairing a guaranteed-income trial's unconditional long-duration arm against a shorter/renewable arm of larger monthly value, or comparing permanent supportive housing against transitional/conditional housing of equal or higher unit value — with pre/post CTRA panels.
   *What would disprove it:* if CTRA reduction tracks dollar value or housing quality alone regardless of conditionality/durability (i.e., the conditional-but-larger-transfer arm shows equal or greater CTRA reduction than the smaller-but-durable-standing arm), the theory's specific claim — that *standing* rather than material sufficiency is the active ingredient — is false, and the CTRA literature's existing SES-gradient findings should be read as sufficient explanation without invoking a standing construct at all.

3. **Acedia-pattern behavioral withdrawal covaries with inflammatory markers within-person, not just between-person.**
   *Prediction:* within individuals tracked longitudinally, day-to-day or week-to-week elevations in circulating IL-6/CRP (or ecological-momentary inflammatory proxies) will precede or co-occur with same-person increases in behavioral withdrawal indices specific to the acedia pattern — reduced initiation of one's characteristic tasks/vocation, reduced social contact-seeking, increased restlessness/task-switching without engagement (not simply low mood or low energy globally) — and this within-person coupling should be detectable net of between-person trait differences.
   *Discriminating test:* an intensive longitudinal design (weekly or daily blood spots or dried-blood-spot IL-6/CRP assays paired with ecological momentary assessment or passive sensing of task engagement, social contact initiation, and restlessness) analyzed with a within-person multilevel or fixed-effects model isolating the person-mean-centered inflammation-behavior association, ideally with a lagged design to probe temporal precedence; the existing endotoxin-challenge EMA studies (e.g., inflammation-induced deficits in anticipated social reward, PMC/ScienceDirect 2024–2025 lines) provide an experimental-causal template this could extend into naturalistic chronic-exclusion populations.
   *What would disprove it:* if within-person inflammatory fluctuation shows no temporal or contemporaneous association with the specific acedia-pattern withdrawal measures (task-initiation avoidance, restlessness-without-engagement) after removing between-person confounds — i.e., if all of the inflammation-withdrawal correlation in prior cross-sectional work turns out to be a between-person artifact (chronically-more-inflamed people are also chronically more withdrawn, but neither varies together over time within a person) — the "within-person biological tracking" claim central to treating acedia as a live physiological process (versus a fixed trait or diagnosis) is false.

4. **Anti-inflammatory reversal of withdrawal will be conditional on the exclusion continuing, not on the drug's pharmacology alone.**
   *Prediction:* anti-inflammatory or anti-cytokine treatment (e.g., TNF-antagonism, IL-6-pathway blockade) will reduce sickness-behavior-pattern withdrawal and anhedonia in inflamed individuals *whose exclusionary social circumstance is unchanged*, but the effect will be smaller or absent in individuals whose objective standing has independently improved during the trial window (i.e., standing-change and pharmacological anti-inflammatory treatment will show a sub-additive, not additive, relationship) — because the drug is treating a signal whose upstream driver (the maintained exclusion) is doing the causal work.
   *Discriminating test:* a secondary/moderator analysis of existing or new anti-inflammatory augmentation trial data (in the style of Raison et al. 2013 or PREDDICT/INSTA-MD) that also captures objective change in participants' social/institutional standing over the trial period (housing status change, employment/legal status change, relationship status change involving increased security) as a moderator of drug response, tested for a drug-by-standing-change interaction.
   *What would disprove it:* if drug response is uniform regardless of concurrent standing change (no interaction term), the claim that the pharmacological effect is contingent on upstream exclusion being unresolved is false, and the simpler pharmacological-target account (inflammation causes the symptom regardless of its social cause) should be preferred.

5. **The CTRA-loneliness dose-response is graded by exclusion's duration and closure (whether the person can see an exit), not only its current intensity.**
   *Prediction:* holding current loneliness/isolation intensity constant, individuals who perceive their exclusion as closed-ended or resolvable (e.g., temporary unemployment with a return date, a known-duration hospitalization) will show lower CTRA than those who perceive equivalent-intensity exclusion as open-ended or permanent (e.g., indefinite detention, chronic unemployment with no institutional pathway back, permanent estrangement) — because a maintained relation of exclusion, per the theory, differs biologically from a bounded episode of the same subjective intensity.
   *Discriminating test:* recruit or reanalyze cohorts with naturally occurring variation in perceived closure/openness of an isolating circumstance at matched current-intensity levels (e.g., comparing fixed-term versus indefinite immigration detention, or time-limited versus indefinite furlough/layoff), assess CTRA and a validated "perceived permanence of exclusion" measure, and test whether perceived permanence predicts CTRA independent of current loneliness/isolation intensity scores.
   *What would disprove it:* if perceived permanence/closure adds no explanatory power to CTRA once current isolation intensity is accounted for, the theory's distinction between "maintained exclusion" and "acute/bounded adversity" collapses at the biological level — CTRA would then be shown to be sensitive only to how excluded someone currently feels, not to whether the exclusion is structurally open-ended, undermining a core conceptual plank of treating abandonment as categorically different from ordinary hardship.
