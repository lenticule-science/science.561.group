A mitochondrion is a double-membraned organelle found in eukaryotic cells whose primary role is the generation of ATP through aerobic oxidation of metabolic substrates. The outer membrane is permeable to molecules up to ~5000 Da; the inner membrane is highly impermeable and folded into cristae, whose surface area bears the electron transport chain and ATP synthase. Mitochondria retain their own DNA (mtDNA, typically 16,569 base pairs in humans, ~37 genes), their own ribosomes, and their own transcription machinery — an inheritance from the endosymbiotic bacterial ancestor. The mitochondrial matrix (the compartment enclosed by the inner membrane) is where the Krebs cycle oxidizes acetyl-CoA, and where complex I, II, III, IV, and V of the electron transport chain sit embedded in and anchored to the cristal membrane.

Mitochondria are not static; they undergo fission (division, typically under energetic stress or cell-cycle demand) and fusion (merging of two mitochondria, thought to allow mixing of genetic material and complementation of mutant genomes). The balance of fission and fusion is regulated by the mitochondrial membrane potential and by proteins of the GTPase family (Drp1 for fission, OPA1 and Mfn for fusion). Fission also precedes apoptotic cell death — a tightly packed, fused mitochondrial network is harder to permeabilize; fragmentation facilitates outer-membrane permeabilization and cytochrome *c* release.

Beyond ATP production, mitochondria function as a cellular signaling hub. They are the chief site of intracellular calcium buffering and are central to calcium-dependent signaling in neurons and muscle. They are the source of reactive oxygen species (ROS) — superoxide and hydrogen peroxide generated at the electron transport chain — which serve dual roles as both injurious by-products at high concentrations and as cellular signaling molecules at lower concentrations, regulating redox-sensitive transcription factors and immune signaling. They sit at the apoptotic threshold: permeabilization of the outer membrane by BAX/BAK oligomers releases cytochrome *c*, activating caspase-9 and the apoptotic cascade. They are also the site of innate immune signal transduction through the NLRP3 inflammasome, which senses mtDNA in the cytoplasm and mtROS as danger signals.

The phrase "mitochondrial dysfunction" is used across a wide range of clinical and research contexts, but it has a rigorous meaning in basic physiology and a looser meaning in clinical epidemiology, and this distinction is central to evaluating evidence in diseases like myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS). The rigorous use names a specific defect: reduced oxygen consumption rate (measured by high-resolution respirometry, the gold standard), deficiency of a specific electron transport chain enzyme (complex I, II, III, IV, or V deficiency, measured by spectrophotometry or immunoassay), or mtDNA mutation with bioenergetic consequences (detected by sequencing and functional assay). The looser use, common in clinical literature, calls any unexplained fatigue or exercise intolerance "mitochondrial dysfunction" without measurement of respiratory capacity or enzyme activity — a gloss that obscures whether the problem is mitochondrial, vascular, endocrine, neural, or something else entirely. The consequence for research is clear: when reading a claim about mitochondrial dysfunction in a disease, ask what assay was used. If the answer is "fatigue was present" or "lactate was elevated," the term is being used metaphorically, not descriptively.

## Related terms

- Oxidative phosphorylation — the process by which mitochondria generate ATP
- Electron transport chain — the series of protein complexes in the inner mitochondrial membrane
- ATP synthase — the enzyme that couples proton gradient to ATP synthesis
- Krebs cycle — the metabolic pathway in the mitochondrial matrix
- Reactive oxygen species — signaling molecules and byproducts of electron transport
- Apoptosis — programmed cell death in which mitochondrial permeabilization is central
