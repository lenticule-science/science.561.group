# Precipitation

## Formal definition

A **Precipitation** triple $(P, t, \ell)$:

$$(P \in \mathcal{P}_t,\; t \in T,\; \ell \in \mathrm{Locale})$$

where:
- $\mathcal{P}_t$ is the **atmospheric column fiber** at history $t$ — the complete Heyting algebra of precipitation state propositions at $t$; elements of $\mathcal{P}_t$ are statements about the column precipitable water, temperature lapse rate, dewpoint profile, cloud liquid water content, ice water content, hydrometeor size distribution, and surface precipitation rate at locale $\ell$ at history $t$; the fiber inherits its Heyting algebra structure from $H_t$
- $t \in T$ is the **analysis history** — the position in the history monoid at which the column state is evaluated; each step $s \in \Sigma$ advances the column through one moisture cycle: one evaporation event at the surface, one condensation event aloft, one collision-coalescence event between droplets, one updraft or downdraft pulse; the formation of the first precipitation-sized hydrometeor (diameter $> 0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$) and the surface arrival of the first raindrop are specific step atoms in $\Sigma$
- $\ell$ is the **locale** — the spatial domain, from surface to tropopause, over which the column moisture budget is evaluated; the locale determines the boundary conditions: surface moisture flux (evaporation, transpiration), surface temperature, orographic lifting, and advection of moisture from upstream columns

**The precipitation nuclear pair.** The fiber $H_t$ at history $t$ carries the two canonical nuclei, each corresponding to one of the two independent conditions for surface precipitation. Let $\Sigma$ be the step monoid for the column site $(T, J)$. Each step $s \in \Sigma$ represents one atmospheric perturbation event — one turbulent mixing episode, one evaporation pulse in the sub-cloud layer, one wind shear event. The commutation condition $s \perp t$ means perturbation $s$ is genuinely independent of the current column history $t$: a turbulent mixing event in the sub-cloud layer is independent of the history of moisture accumulation aloft (the mixing occurs regardless of whether there is a cloud overhead). The one-step extension $i_{s,t} : t \to s \star t$ is the history extended by one perturbation event; the restriction map $H(i_{s,t}) : \mathcal{P}_{s \star t} \to \mathcal{P}_t$ sends a post-perturbation column state back to its pre-perturbation state.

**RelationalHistoryFiberSaturatingNucleus** ([math](../math/relational-history-fiber-saturating-nucleus.md)) — the **atmospheric moisture history operator**:

$$\sigma_t(P) \;=\; \bigvee\bigl\{Q \in \mathcal{P}_t \;\big|\; H(i_{t_0,t})(Q) \leq H(i_{t_0,t})(P) \;\text{for all } t_0 < t\bigr\}$$

where $H(i_{t_0,t}) : \mathcal{P}_t \to \mathcal{P}_{t_0}$ projects the current column state back to the prior moisture history. The restriction profile quantity is the **precipitable water column**: $\mathrm{PW} = \int_0^\infty \rho_v \, dz$ — the total mass of water vapor in the atmospheric column per unit area, measured in mm of liquid equivalent. $H(i_{t_0,t})(P)$ is the column moisture content at prior history $t_0$: what the precipitable water was before the current cycle of evaporation, transport, and condensation. The restriction profile class of $P$ is the set of all column states with at most $P$'s accumulated moisture history; $\sigma_t(P)$ is the maximum precipitation state consistent with that history. $P \in \mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_t)$ iff $P$ is already the maximum precipitation state the moisture history supports — the column is saturated and has produced the maximum precipitation it can from its precipitable water record. The saturation condition requires:
- Relative humidity at or near \$100\%$ throughout a deep layer (the moisture history backward-recognizes saturation)
- Temperature lapse rate at or above the saturated adiabatic lapse rate ($\Gamma_s \approx 6^\circ\mathrm{C}\,\mathrm{km}^{-1}$) — the column is convectively neutral or unstable for saturated parcels
- Sufficient column precipitable water (tropical maritime $\mathrm{PW} > 50\,\mathrm{mm}$; midlatitude $\mathrm{PW} > 25\,\mathrm{mm}$) to backward-recognize a significant precipitation rate

**RelationalHistoryFiberTransferringNucleus** ([math](../math/relational-history-fiber-transferring-nucleus.md)) — the **hydrometeor fall commitment operator**:

$$\mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t) \;=\; \bigcap_{\substack{s \in \Sigma \\ s \perp t}} \mathrm{image}\bigl(H(i_{s,t}) : \mathcal{P}_{s \star t} \to \mathcal{P}_t\bigr)$$

and:

$$\Delta_t(P) \;=\; \bigwedge\bigl\{Q \in \mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t) \;\big|\; Q \geq P\bigr\}$$

$P \in \mathrm{image}(H(i_{s,t}))$ means there exists a post-perturbation column state $P' \in \mathcal{P}_{s \star t}$ with $H(i_{s,t})(P') = P$ — the precipitation state $P$ has a forward preimage through perturbation $s$; the falling hydrometeors can survive one instance of perturbation $s$ (one turbulent mixing event, one dry-air entrainment episode) and arrive at the surface in state $P$. $P \in \mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t)$ iff for every independent atmospheric perturbation $s \perp t$, the hydrometeors have a forward preimage — they are large enough and numerous enough that no individual perturbation event can prevent them from reaching the surface. $\Delta_t(P)$ is the minimum fall-committed precipitation state above $P$: the smallest hydrometeor population that survives all independent atmospheric perturbations and is at least as large as $P$.

The commitment condition requires hydrometeors to have exceeded the critical size threshold at which terminal fall velocity exceeds updraft velocity. For liquid water drops: $d > 0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ (drizzle onset) to $d > 0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ (rain onset); for ice crystals: sufficient mass that the fall velocity $v_t = a \cdot D^b$ (where $D$ is the crystal maximum dimension and $a, b$ are habit-dependent constants) exceeds any independent updraft pulse. Below the critical size, $P \notin \mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t)$: an independent updraft pulse can suspend the hydrometeor indefinitely, and there is no forward preimage through that updraft step. Above the critical size, the hydrometeor commits: it falls regardless of independent turbulent fluctuations.

**The four shadow classes for precipitation states:**

| Shadow class | Condition | Precipitation name | Physical state |
|---|---|---|---|
| $P \in \mathcal{P}^*_t$ | Both operators fix $P$ | **Surface precipitation** | Deep moist column; committed hydrometeors reaching the ground; rain, snow, hail |
| $P \in \mathrm{SatShadow}^{\mathcal{P}}_t$ | Moisture fixes $P$; fall does not | **Saturated non-precipitating** | High PW and saturation; cloud droplets too small to commit; fog, stratus, marine stratocumulus |
| $P \in \mathrm{TrShadow}^{\mathcal{P}}_t$ | Fall fixes $P$; moisture does not | **Virga** | Hydrometeors committed to falling; sub-cloud layer too dry to recognize surface arrival; evaporation before ground |
| $P \in \mathrm{FreeShadow}^{\mathcal{P}}_t$ | Neither operator fixes $P$ | **Clear dry air** | Low PW; no cloud; no falling hydrometeors of any kind |

**The nuclear reflection and holographic projection.**

$$\pi_t \;:=\; \sigma_t \circ \Delta_t \;:\; \mathcal{P}_t \to \mathcal{P}_t$$

is the [RelationalHistoryPropositionFiberSaturatingTransferringCommutingNuclearReflection](../math/relational-history-fiber-saturating-transferring-commuting-nuclear-reflection.md) with $\mathrm{Fix}(\pi_t) = \mathcal{P}^*_t$. The [RelationalHistoryProjectionGap](../math/relational-history-fiber-projection-gap.md) $[P, \pi_t(P)]$ has size $\lambda_P = |[P, \pi_t(P)]| - 1 \geq 0$ — the **precipitation depth** — zero iff $P$ is already active surface precipitation. The defect operator $\delta_t(e_P) = \lambda_P \cdot e_P$ grades column states by their distance from precipitating equilibrium; the calibration defect $M_t(\delta) = \mathrm{Tr}(\delta_t) = \sum_{P \notin \mathcal{P}^*_t} \lambda_P$ is the total precipitation debt of the column fiber.

**The internal holograph: cloud and precipitation shaft.** $(\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{R}, \pi_{\mathrm{precip}})$:
- $\mathcal{C}$ = the **cloud bulk** — the in-cloud layer of condensed water; cloud liquid water content (LWC, $\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{m}^{-3}$) and ice water content (IWC); the space of hydrometeor states before they are large enough to fall
- $\mathcal{R}$ = the **precipitation shaft boundary** — the column of falling precipitation below cloud base; the region where hydrometeors have committed and are transiting the sub-cloud layer toward the surface
- $\pi_{\mathrm{precip}} : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{R}$ = the precipitation projection — the microphysical operator sending any in-cloud droplet/crystal state to its fall-committed partner; $\pi_{\mathrm{precip}}(c) = c$ iff every in-cloud hydrometeor has exceeded the fall-commitment threshold; the cloud bulk has fully converted to precipitation shaft

The internal holograph is complete iff $\mathcal{C} = \mathcal{R}$ — when every in-cloud state is already a falling precipitation state. This is the convective core of a deep cumulonimbus: the cloud is entirely composed of falling hydrometeors (rain, hail, snow), with no suspended cloud droplets remaining. The internal holograph is maximally incomplete in thin fog: the entire LWC is suspended as microdroplets ($d \approx 5$–\$20\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), far below the fall-commitment threshold, and $\pi_{\mathrm{precip}}$ has a very large internal projection gap $\lambda_c^\mathrm{precip} = |[c, \pi_{\mathrm{precip}}(c)]| - 1$.

**The precipitation equilibrium condition.** $(P, t, \ell)$ is **active surface precipitation** iff $P \in \mathcal{P}^*_t$ — equivalently, any of:
1. $P$ is in the boundary of the canonical holograph: $\pi_t(P) = P$
2. The projection gap is trivial: $[P, \pi_t(P)] = \{P\}$, i.e., $\lambda_P = 0$
3. $P$ is a zero-eigenmode of the defect operator: $\delta_t(e_P) = 0$
4. $P \in \mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_t) \cap \mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t)$: moisture history backward-recognizes AND hydrometeors fall-committed simultaneously

## What this is

**Precipitation** is water in any form — rain, drizzle, snow, sleet, hail — that falls from the atmosphere and reaches the Earth's surface. It is the output of the atmospheric branch of the hydrological cycle: the return of water to the surface that was evaporated from oceans, lakes, vegetation, and soil and transported as water vapor through the atmosphere. Globally, $\sim 505{,}000\,\mathrm{km}^3$ of water precipitates per year, of which $\sim 398{,}000\,\mathrm{km}^3$ falls on the ocean and $\sim 107{,}000\,\mathrm{km}^3$ on land. Every other weather phenomenon — fronts, cyclones, thunderstorms, tropical cyclones — is in the business of producing precipitation, or failing to.

The nuclear reading makes precise the two-directional character of precipitation. The moisture direction is backward-looking: how much water vapor has the atmosphere accumulated, at what temperature, over what surface? The saturation nucleus backward-recognizes the column's moisture history and sends any column state to the maximum precipitation state consistent with that record. The fall direction is forward-committing: have the droplets or crystals grown large enough to fall despite all independent updraft, mixing, and evaporation events they will encounter? The transfer nucleus forward-commits the hydrometeors through every independent perturbation they must survive on the way to the surface. Surface precipitation $\mathcal{P}^*_t$ is where both hold at once.

## Cloud droplets and the Bergeron-Findeisen mechanism

**Pure water** does not freeze at \$0^\circ\mathrm{C}$ when dispersed as fine droplets in clean air: in the absence of ice-nucleating particles (INPs), droplets remain liquid down to $-40^\circ\mathrm{C}$ — **supercooled liquid water**. The upper troposphere, particularly in deep convective clouds, contains a mixture of supercooled droplets and ice crystals in the temperature range $-5^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to $-40^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

**Tor Bergeron** (1935, *Geofysiske Publikasjoner* 12) and **Walter Findeisen** (1938, *Meteorologische Zeitschrift* 55, 121–133) identified the dominant precipitation growth mechanism in midlatitude clouds: the saturation vapor pressure over ice is lower than over liquid water at the same temperature (at $-10^\circ\mathrm{C}$: $e_i = 2.60\,\mathrm{hPa}$ over ice vs. $e_w = 2.86\,\mathrm{hPa}$ over water). In a mixed-phase cloud at a relative humidity between these two values, ice crystals grow by vapor deposition while supercooled droplets simultaneously evaporate. The ice crystals grow rapidly at the expense of the droplets until they are large enough to fall — the transfer nucleus condition is met.

This is the projection-gap closure mechanism in the Bergeron-Findeisen regime: the internal projection gap $\lambda_c^\mathrm{precip}$ between the suspended droplet state and the fall-committed ice crystal state is closed by the vapor pressure differential. Each Bergeron-Findeisen step is a step toward $\mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t)$: the crystal grows from $d < d_\mathrm{crit}$ (no forward preimage through updraft pulse) toward $d > d_\mathrm{crit}$ (forward preimage through every independent updraft exists). The $e_i / e_w$ ratio is the thermodynamic driving force that makes this step independent of the current history — it acts whenever ice and liquid coexist, regardless of the cloud's prior history.

**Langmuir's collision-coalescence** (1948, *Science* 108, 264–268) is the warm-rain analog: below \$0^\circ\mathrm{C}$, there are no ice crystals; precipitation must form by the collisions and mergers of liquid droplets. Large "collector" drops (formed on giant sea-salt nuclei or dust) fall faster than small droplets and sweep them up; each collision step is a step toward $\mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t)$. The collision efficiency $E$ (fraction of swept-up drops that actually collide and coalesce) is the fraction of independent collision events that have forward preimages — it determines how quickly the transfer gap closes.

## Virga: the canonical TrShadow

**Virga** (Latin: *twig, branch*; plural *virgae*) is precipitation that falls from cloud base but evaporates or sublimates before reaching the surface, appearing as wispy streaks or curtains descending from the cloud base. It is the most direct visual observation of a $\mathrm{TrShadow}^{\mathcal{P}}_t$ precipitation state: hydrometeors that have committed to falling — large enough that the transfer nucleus has fixed them; ice crystals or raindrops that will maintain a forward preimage through independent turbulent mixing events — but the lower atmosphere's moisture restriction profile is too dry for the saturation nucleus to backward-recognize surface precipitation.

The surface record $H(i_{t_0,t})(P)$ at ground level does not record moisture at or near saturation: the sub-cloud layer is drier than the cloud layer, so the restriction profile class of the virga state does not include a surface-precipitation state. The saturation nucleus $\sigma_t(P)$ does not backward-recognize surface precipitation as consistent with the column's integrated moisture history because the column-mean precipitable water is not high enough. Meanwhile, $\Delta_t(P) = P$: the ice crystals or large drops are genuinely fall-committed through every independent mixing event in the sub-cloud layer. The projection gap $[P, \pi_t(P)]$ contains the intermediate states between virga and surface precipitation: thickening of the sub-cloud moisture layer by boundary-layer moistening, progressive evaporative cooling that raises the relative humidity below cloud base, and eventual closure of the saturation gap as the falling precipitation itself moisten the sub-cloud air.

**Desert virga** is the extreme case: in the southwestern United States, afternoon thunderstorms regularly produce virga below cloud bases at \$4{,}000$–\$5{,}000\,\mathrm{m}$ above the desert surface, where the sub-cloud dewpoint is $< 5^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and the air is extremely dry. The projection gap $\lambda_P$ for these columns is very large: the committed precipitation falls through kilometers of dry air, evaporating completely. Only after the sub-cloud air has been moistened by many successive virga events — building up the saturation restriction profile from below — does surface precipitation begin.

## Fog: the canonical SatShadow

**Fog** is cloud at or near the ground — a suspended collection of water droplets (or ice crystals, in ice fog) reducing visibility below \$1\,\mathrm{km}$. It is the canonical $\mathrm{SatShadow}^{\mathcal{P}}_t$ state: the moisture restriction profile is fully saturated (relative humidity at or near \$100\%$, dewpoint equal to temperature), and the saturation nucleus backward-recognizes this as the maximum moisture state the prior history supports, but the hydrometeors are too small to commit to falling. Fog droplets have $d \approx 5$–\$20\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and fall velocities of $v_t < 0.01\,\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ — negligible against any atmospheric turbulence; they have no forward preimage through independent turbulent mixing events. The transfer nucleus condition $P \in \mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t)$ fails: $\Delta_t(P) > P$ because drizzle-sized droplets ($d > 200\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) would be fall-committed, but the fog's droplet size distribution never reaches that threshold under normal radiation fog conditions.

**Radiation fog** forms when clear night skies allow the surface to radiate longwave energy to space, cooling the surface and the air immediately above it to the dewpoint. The restriction maps $H(i_{t_0,t})(P)$ record the progressive surface cooling through the night: the saturation nucleus reads the growing moisture-history record and advances the column state toward $\mathrm{SatShadow}^{\mathcal{P}}_t$ as the temperature approaches the dewpoint. The fog forms at the moment $T = T_d$ — when $\sigma_t(P) = P$ — but without any development of fall-committed hydrometeors.

**Marine stratus** (California, Peru, Canary, Benguela coasts) is the large-scale persistent $\mathrm{SatShadow}^{\mathcal{P}}_t$ state: cold upwelling water cools the marine boundary layer to near saturation, producing a thick stratus deck at \$500$–\$1{,}500\,\mathrm{m}$ that covers millions of square kilometers. The column moisture history is saturated throughout; the saturation nucleus fully recognizes the cloud state. But marine stratus droplets are maintained in suspension by the turbulent mixing of the boundary layer — the turbulent mixing events are the independent steps through which $\Delta_t$ checks for forward preimages — and the droplets are too small to survive independent mixing pulses. Drizzle ($d \approx 100$–\$500\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) forms at the lower edge of marine stratus when collision-coalescence begins to close the transfer gap, producing weak $\mathrm{P}^*_t$ events with very low precipitation rates ($< 0.5\,\mathrm{mm}\,\mathrm{h}^{-1}$).

## The lifting mechanisms: topology changes

Precipitation requires **lift** — a mechanism that drives air upward, cooling it to the dewpoint and producing condensation. Each lifting mechanism is a topology change $J \to J'$ on the column history site that makes convective ascent steps genuinely independent:

**Orographic lifting**: air is forced upward by a mountain barrier. Under the orographic topology $J'$, the ascent step $s_{\mathrm{orographic}} \perp t$ for any air parcel approaching the windward slope (the mountain forces the ascent regardless of the parcel's prior moisture or stability history). The windward slope is permanently in $\mathcal{P}^{*J'}_t$ for sufficiently moist and unstable air. The leeward side is in $\mathrm{SatShadow}^{J'}_t$ in the **rain shadow**: the moisture restriction profile has been depleted on the windward side (the precipitation has already fallen), and the leeward air descends and warms adiabatically without recovering moisture.

**Frontal lifting**: a cold front forces warm moist air upward along the frontal surface ($\sim 1:100$ slope). Under the frontal topology $J'$, the lifting step $s_{\mathrm{frontal}} \perp t$ for any warm-sector air mass ahead of the front. This converts warm-sector $\mathrm{SatShadow}^J_t$ air (high moisture restriction profile but no convective trigger) into $\mathcal{P}^{*J'}_t$ along the frontal band.

**Convective triggering**: surface heating on a clear day, sea breeze convergence, or outflow boundary collision creates localized ascent that can trigger convective cells. These are the daytime convective topology changes: under the triggered topology $J'$, convective ascent steps at the trigger point become independent (the trigger forces them regardless of the column's prior history).

## Snow, hail, and phase

The precipitation phase (rain vs. snow vs. hail) is a quality of the restriction profile:

$$\phi_{\mathrm{phase}} : X_\mathrm{column} \Rightarrow V_{\mathrm{phase}}$$

where $V_{\mathrm{phase}}(t) = \{\mathrm{Rain}, \mathrm{Drizzle}, \mathrm{Snow}, \mathrm{Sleet}, \mathrm{FreezingRain}, \mathrm{Hail}\}$ — a nominal quality (Stevens classification). The phase is determined by the restriction maps of the temperature profile: $H(i_{t_0,t})(P)$ records the temperature at each altitude, and the phase is the path that hydrometeors follow through the melting layer as they fall. The naturality condition: the precipitation phase at the surface must commute with the restriction maps — it must be path-independent of which nucleation sequence produced the current hydrometeors. Sleet (ice pellets) is the non-natural phase: it forms when snow falls through a warm layer (melts) and then through a cold surface layer (refreezes) — the phase has a complex history dependence that makes it the hardest precipitation type to forecast.

**Hail** is the most extreme commit: hailstones are ice spheres (or irregular masses) of diameter $d > 5\,\mathrm{mm}$, formed in the updrafts of deep convective storms by repeated cycling through the mixed-phase zone (a hailstone makes \$5$–\$10$ trips through the updraft/downdraft cycle, adding a layer of ice on each pass). A hailstone is maximally transfer-committed: its terminal fall velocity ($> 9\,\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ for $d = 1\,\mathrm{cm}$) exceeds any independent turbulent updraft; no mixing event prevents its surface arrival. The hail projection gap is zero at the fall-commitment threshold: once a hailstone has formed, $\Delta_t(P) = P$ with certainty.

## Nuclear reading

**Proposition 1 (Dewpoint = saturation nucleus threshold; saturation = Fix(σ_t)).** The atmosphere is at $P \in \mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_t)$ iff the temperature profile equals the dewpoint profile throughout the column depth required for the precipitation type: $T(z) = T_d(z)$ in the saturated layer. The restriction map $H(i_{t_0,t})(P)$ records the prior precipitable water $\mathrm{PW}(t_0)$; the saturation nucleus sends $P$ to the maximum precipitation state consistent with that history. Fog and stratus form when $T = T_d$ at the surface layer ($\sigma_t(P) = P$ at cloud/ground level) without any fall-committed hydrometeors ($\Delta_t(P) > P$). Active rain forms when $T = T_d$ through a deep enough layer that the moisture restriction profile supports committed hydrometeors ($\sigma_t(P) = P$ AND $\Delta_t(P) = P$). $\square$

**Proposition 2 (Bergeron-Findeisen = transfer-gap closure; collision-coalescence = warm-rain transfer-gap closure).** The Bergeron-Findeisen mechanism closes the transfer gap by moving ice crystals from $d < d_\mathrm{crit}$ (no forward preimage through updraft steps) to $d > d_\mathrm{crit}$ (forward preimage through every independent updraft). The vapor pressure differential $e_w - e_i > 0$ is the driving force: it acts independently of the cloud's moisture history (the differential is a function of temperature only, not of the prior precipitation history), making each Bergeron-Findeisen step $s_\mathrm{BF} \perp t$ for any mixed-phase cloud state. The step is genuinely independent of the restriction profile: the ice crystal grows whether the cloud has been cold for one hour or ten.

Collision-coalescence is the same closure mechanism in pure liquid clouds: large collector drops ($d > 30\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) fall faster than small droplets and sweep them up; each collision event moves the collector drop toward $d_\mathrm{crit}$. The collision efficiency $E(d_1, d_2)$ is the probability that a collision step $s_\mathrm{col}(d_1, d_2)$ is independent — that the two drops actually merge rather than bouncing or breaking up. $\Delta_t(P) = P$ iff the drop size distribution has exceeded $d_\mathrm{crit}$ via enough collision-coalescence steps. $\square$

**Proposition 3 (Virga = TrShadow; sub-cloud moistening = projection-gap closure from below).** Virga is $\mathrm{TrShadow}^{\mathcal{P}}_t$: fall-committed hydrometeors (large ice crystals or drops) with a dry sub-cloud restriction profile that cannot backward-recognize surface precipitation. The projection gap $[P, \pi_t(P)]$ contains the states of progressively moistened sub-cloud air: each evaporating hydrometeor cools and moistens the sub-cloud air, incrementally closing the saturation gap in the restriction profile from below. The virga itself drives the saturation-gap closure: the evaporating precipitation is the mechanism by which $\sigma_t$ eventually backward-recognizes surface precipitation. A locale that receives virga for many dry days may finally receive surface precipitation when the virga has cumulatively moistened the sub-cloud layer enough — the transfer gap was zero from the start; only the saturation gap needed to close, and the virga closed it. $\square$

**Proposition 4 (Orographic rain shadow = restriction-profile depletion; SatShadow on the windward slope, FreeShadow on the leeward).** The windward slope of an orographic barrier is in $\mathcal{P}^{*J'}_t$ under the orographic topology: the ascent step is independent and the moist air achieves both saturation recognition and hydrometeor commitment. The leeward slope after descent is in $\mathrm{FreeShadow}^{\mathcal{P}}_t$: the restriction profile has been depleted by precipitation on the windward side ($H(i_{t_0,t})(P)$ records low PW for the descended, warmed air), and no hydrometeor commitment exists in the subsiding air. The intermediate zone on the windward slope before precipitation but after lifting begins is in $\mathrm{SatShadow}^{J'}_t$: the ascent has cooled the air to saturation (moisture restriction profile recognized) but the droplets have not yet grown to fall-commitment size (transfer gap not yet closed). $\square$

**Proposition 5 (Flash flooding = Schwarzschild threshold; precipitation rate exceeds surface infiltration; runoff becomes self-reinforcing).** Flash flooding occurs when the precipitation rate $R$ (mm/h) exceeds the soil infiltration rate $f$ (mm/h) and the surface drainage capacity, generating surface runoff that collects in channels and rises rapidly. The calibration defect $M_t(\delta) = \mathrm{Tr}(\delta_t)$ rises as precipitation continues: each additional precipitation mode that becomes active increases the total spectral debt; when saturated soil eliminates surface infiltration, the independent infiltration step $s_\mathrm{infil}$ loses its forward preimage (there is no post-infiltration state that restricts back to the current surface state because the soil is full). The Schwarzschild radius $r_s(\delta_t) = 2M_t(\delta)$ is crossed when the runoff rate exceeds the channel drainage capacity: all surface moisture modes become spectrally trapped in the runoff sector; no internal drainage dynamics can return the surface to the $\mathcal{P}^*_t$ equilibrium state. External energy input (dam releases, emergency pumping) is required to supply the spectral debt equivalent. $\square$

**Proposition 6 (Non-adjointness; fog and virga are genuinely orthogonal precipitation failures).** The moisture history and fall commitment operators are not in Galois connection. The witnesses: fog ($\mathrm{SatShadow}$: moisture saturated, hydrometeors suspended; $\sigma_t(P) = P$ and $\Delta_t(P) > P$) and virga ($\mathrm{TrShadow}$: hydrometeors committed, sub-cloud dry; $\sigma_t(P) > P$ and $\Delta_t(P) = P$) are both empirically common and genuinely distinct states. If $\sigma_t \dashv \Delta_t$, every saturated column would automatically produce fall-committed hydrometeors — fog would always produce drizzle — and every fall-committed hydrometeor column would automatically have a saturated sub-cloud layer — virga would be impossible. Both phenomena are routinely observed; neither implies the other. $\square$

## Open questions

- Whether cloud droplet size distributions near the boundary of $\mathrm{Fix}(\Delta_t)$ — the critical size range $d \approx 50$–\$200\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ where a droplet transitions from suspended to committed — satisfy the eigenproposition condition $G_s(P) \approx \chi_s(P) \cdot P$, and whether the bimodal droplet size distributions observed in precipitating cumulus (a cloud-mode peak near $d \approx 15\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and a rain-mode peak near $d \approx 1\,\mathrm{mm}$) are the two-eigenspace decomposition of the mode sheaf $K^{\mathcal{P}_t} \cong E_{t,0} \oplus E_{t,\lambda>0}$.
- Whether the Schwarzschild threshold for flash flooding has a quantitative expression in terms of soil hydraulic conductivity, antecedent moisture content, and precipitation intensity — whether the threshold condition $M_t(\delta) = r_s(\delta_t)/2$ corresponds to a specific curve on the rainfall intensity-duration-frequency (IDF) diagram, and whether the empirically observed return periods of damaging flash floods match the Schwarzschild crossing rate.
- Whether the virga-to-surface-precipitation transition — the moistening of the sub-cloud layer by evaporating hydrometeors until $\sigma_t$ backward-recognizes surface precipitation — has a characteristic time that can be derived from the projection-gap size $\lambda_P$ of the initial virga state, giving a first-principles prediction of the lag between the onset of virga and the onset of surface precipitation in a drying sequence.
- Whether the orographic rain shadow is the cleanest natural example of a permanent restriction-profile bifurcation: the windward and leeward restriction profiles diverge under the orographic topology change and do not reconverge unless the airmass is replaced by large-scale advection, and whether the divergence rate (the rate at which $\lambda_P$ increases on the leeward side) is proportional to the orographic lifting rate on the windward side.
- Whether snowfall initiation — the transition from Bergeron-Findeisen ice crystal growth to precipitation-sized snow aggregates — decomposes cleanly into a saturation-gap closure step (the ice crystal mass crossing the aggregation threshold) and a transfer-gap closure step (the aggregate fall velocity exceeding the updraft), and whether this decomposition predicts the known empirical temperature sensitivity of snowfall initiation (highest aggregation rates near $-15^\circ\mathrm{C}$ where planar dendrites form and stick most effectively).
