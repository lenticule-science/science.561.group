# *Ustilago esculenta* and native North American wild rice: an evidence assessment

**Working paper, version 0.8 — 11 October 2026. Not independently scientifically received, peer reviewed, or accepted for L-Space scholarly release.**

Research synthesis: ChatGPT-Web for Lenticule Science. No other authorship or tribal institutional endorsement is asserted. The [underlying ecological evidence register](../../../manoomin-jiaobai-ustilago-esculenta-evidence.md) is the maintained source record; this manuscript states the argument and its current limits.

## Abstract

**Background.** The smut fungus *Ustilago esculenta* causes the development of swollen edible culms in cultivated Asian *Zizania latifolia* (jiaobai). California plant-health authorities have documented introduced host–fungus occurrences and have warned that North American wild rice might be susceptible. Published host lists and botanical accounts disagree about whether American native *Zizania* species have already been infected.

**Objective.** Assess which plausible introduction and ecological risks are supported by existing evidence, which depend on unresolved native-host compatibility, and which answers could guide prevention now.

**Method.** Targeted source-critical review of agency assessments, taxonomic literature, published work on the cultivated *Z. latifolia*–*U. esculenta* association and fungal viability in edible tissue, and an ancillary 1911 historical collection. This is not a complete systematic literature review, a measured risk model, or a current legal determination.

**Results.** The Asian host–fungus association is established; researchers recovered culturable fungus from five harvested gall samples in 2019. A 2017 study found marked differences between tested MT-type isolates from edible white galls and T-type isolates from spore-rich gray galls, including impaired infection capacity in MT isolates. These observations do not establish viability after distribution, environmental exposure, or infection of native North American wild rice. California records introduced-host incursions. Independently, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's 2025 ecological risk screening assessed the introduced *Z. latifolia* **plant** as High Risk, without identifying a native-host fungal infection. CDFA's native-host list conflicts with other scientific and agency statements; a USDA-NRCS secondary citation points to a corn-smut review rather than a native-host experiment. A 1911 greenhouse collection printed as “*Zizania aquatica*” has identifiable historical specimen packets but no verified modern host identification; this remains a secondary archival question, not a necessary precondition for today's import or exposure assessments.

**Conclusion.** The immediate research priorities are present commodity restrictions and documented exposures, evidence of survival and spread through real-world pathways, modern species-specific host evidence, and introduced-plant impacts. Historical host labels warrant cautious treatment but should not drive the prevention program. Nothing here establishes a contemporary native infection or a legally prohibited retail sale.

**Keywords:** manoomin; *Zizania aquatica*; *Zizania palustris*; *Zizania latifolia*; *Ustilago esculenta*; taxonomic provenance; plant-pathogen host range; invasive aquatic plants.

## 1. Question and ecological stakes

*Zizania* comprises the Asian perennial *Z. latifolia* and three North American species, *Z. aquatica*, *Z. palustris* and *Z. texana*. A molecular analysis places the North American taxa in a clade sister to *Z. latifolia*, rather than treating their shared genus as equivalent infection biology [Xu 2010]. The annual North American species depend on reproduction by seed. If a foreign fungus suppressed their inflorescences, consequences could be serious, but such a conditional consequence is **not** evidence that the fungus can infect them.

For Anishinaabe and other nations, manoomin is also a living relation, food resource and exercise of stewardship and treaty rights. The [Great Lakes Indian Fish and Wildlife Commission](https://glifwc.org/stewardship/ganawenindiwag-manoomin-anishinaabeg) describes existing manoomin stewardship guided by tribal rice chiefs, elders and natural-resource offices, with climate, sulfates and hydrology among documented concerns. A [Wisconsin DNR summit agenda for October 7, 2026](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/calendar/meeting/130251) describes council priorities and a State/Tribal Technical Committee, not an independently verified final decision. A [Fond du Lac Band/Minnesota Land Trust proposal with Minnesota DNR](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/news/2026/07/28/minnesota-dnr-accepting-comments-environmental-review-documents-proposed-cedar-lake-manoomin-restoration-project) concerns restoring lake water levels, not a confirmed fungal outbreak. Research priorities, protected sites, traditional knowledge and access to specimens belong with the relevant nations and authorized research partners, not with an unaffiliated GitHub publication. A 2026 Wisconsin state release documents an existing multi-Tribal Wild Rice Stewardship Council; that public record is evidence of governance, not consent to a 561 Group project [Wisconsin 2026].

The immediate questions are: (1) which commodities and origins APHIS currently allows; (2) whether credible evidence demonstrates a viable introduction route from harvested food or propagative plants; (3) whether any native *Zizania* species is a demonstrated host; and (4) what invasive-plant effects are documented independently of fungal infection. These questions can inform prevention without awaiting resolution of a century-old taxonomic label. The historical host record matters only if a primary specimen can resolve a disputed host claim. Other threats to manoomin—including hydrology, water quality and known wild-rice pathogens—must not be collapsed into this one hypothesis.

## 2. Methods and admissible evidence

**Primary-source readback refinement (October 11, 2026):** Guo et al.'s full published Results and Table 1 identify **one wild Asian host reference (HSD2)** and **two cultivated Asian genomes (Zhejiao2 and Jiayou1)**, rather than an unspecified replicated wild-versus-crop comparison. Chigira et al.'s **Table 4** identifies nine archived Japanese strains and **one Chinese-origin strain (yeast4, MAFF 305616)** among ten. Its abstract's compressed geographic description should not override the study's actual table. The underlying [source-extraction JSON](https://github.com/lenticule-science/science-services-section/blob/main/services/natural-science-services-section/services/biological-science-services-section/services/ecology-services-section/data/manoomin-evidence.json) records these sample units and the experimental limitations; the data were not independently reprocessed.

**Primary-study extraction and audit:** The related [source-owned experiment records](https://github.com/lenticule-science/science-services-section/blob/main/services/natural-science-services-section/services/biological-science-services-section/services/ecology-services-section/data/manoomin-evidence.json) distinguish material, assay and limits for Guo (2015), Zhang (2017), Tu (2019) and Chigira (2023). The Zhang finding is based on **four selected Asian-host fungal isolates**, including mixed T/MT pairings capable of infecting that Asian host; Tu's culturing uses **five harvested galls**, not independent national consignments; Chigira's laboratory mating tests do not challenge American *Zizania*; and Guo's comparative host genomics does not assay retail transmission. A reader should not count those four primary studies as replicated native-host infection tests. The full experimental source extract and its [tests](https://github.com/lenticule-science/science-services-section/blob/main/tests/manoomin-evidence.test.mjs) separate observed results, sample independence and unmeasured links.

The accompanying **machine-readable evidence extract** lives in this ecology section at [`data/manoomin-evidence.json`](https://github.com/lenticule-science/science-services-section/blob/main/services/natural-science-services-section/services/biological-science-services-section/services/ecology-services-section/data/manoomin-evidence.json). It records bounded assertions, independent source identifiers, material/species scope and explicit limits; `data/manoomin-evidence.mjs` and `tests/manoomin-evidence.test.mjs` reject ungrounded promotion of a retail report to a violation, an unresolved species question to a native infection, or these unreviewed findings to accepted scholarly publication. This structured extract is a **subset** of the narrative research, not a separate primary source, substitute for the full papers, or automatic editorial warrant.

This manuscript evaluates a *purposefully selected* corpus of records located on 11 October 2026. Sources were selected for the capacity to discriminate original biological observation, taxonomic identity, and later repetition: (i) contemporary taxonomic revisions and molecular phylogeny; (ii) historically dated original fungal specimen lists; (iii) agency primary diagnosis and risk assessments; (iv) experimental culture of the known Asian fungus–plant association; and (v) modern secondary native-host claims.

For each source we ask: Which species, plant part and commodity form were actually examined? What organism was measured, cultured, sequenced or only asserted? Does the observation concern a harvested crop, processed shipment, natural wetland or planted population? Is it evidence of host susceptibility, fungus viability, exposure, or merely a precautionary judgment? Which jurisdiction and event date does it address? For historical records, we additionally distinguish the taxon **printed** from any modern specimen determination.

An accepted **native-host occurrence claim** requires at minimum: independently resolvable host identification to a current American species, independently resolvable fungal identification, a dated association in the same observed material, and credible specimen or diagnostic provenance. An archival name alone does not satisfy those conditions. A lack of positive records under a bounded search is not proof of resistance.

The observed records below are not an exhaustive sample of the global literature. Counts of screened records, complete database query histories, deduplication receipts, and independent risk-of-bias assessment have **not** been produced. A later comprehensive scoping review should be prospectively recorded and reported using PRISMA-ScR or the environmental ROSES reporting standard, after completing the required searches and exclusions [Tricco 2018; Haddaway 2018]. No fictitious PRISMA flow diagram is supplied here.

## 3. Results

The evidence about contemporary exposure, native-host compatibility and invasive-plant establishment has greater immediate decision value than the 1911 greenhouse taxon. The full historical investigation is retained in Appendix B.

### 3.1. Later secondary host assertions are inconsistent

The California Department of Food and Agriculture's **2017 fungus assessment** lists *Z. aquatica*, *Z. latifolia* and *Zizania* sp. as hosts and reports PCR/sequencing confirmation of *U. esculenta* in introduced *Z. latifolia* tissue from Riverside County [CDFA 2017]. The latter is a real introduced-host diagnosis, but it does **not** verify the former native-host list entry. That assessment also records an infected 1991 planting near Modesto, an interception in 1998, and 1999 Louisiana plots eradicated in 2000. These event types must remain distinct.

In the same agency's **2018 *Z. latifolia* assessment**, a responding CDFA botanist stated that *Z. latifolia* was the only **known** host and that infection of *Z. palustris* had **not** been tested [CDFA 2018]. A 2015 genomic study described *Z. latifolia* as the only known host [Guo 2015]. A 2016 Indian microscopy/genetic study independently identified *U. esculenta* on the Asian host [Sinha 2016], without testing an American host. The contradictions are not resolved merely by counting how many sources mention the native taxon.

A USDA-NRCS **2024 native *Z. aquatica* guide** says that the plant's culms may be infected by *U. esculenta* but cites Kealey, Kosikowski and Gray (1981). That cited article is explicitly about **corn smut (*U. maydis*) as a food source** [NRCS 2024; Kealey 1981]. The citation does not establish *U. esculenta* infecting native *Z. aquatica*. We cannot determine from this miscitation whether the guide's biological claim originated elsewhere. Consequently, it is a provenance defect, **not** proof of resistance.

### 3.1a. An observed native smut is a different fungus

An overlooked source of diagnostic confusion is that **North American wild rice already has documented fungal diseases**. A 2023 *Crop Science* review of northern wild rice (*Zizania palustris*) identifies its established smut as ***Eballistra lineata***, with fungal brown spot from ***Bipolaris oryzae*** among the most damaging cultivated-rice diseases [McGilp et al. 2023, §5.2]. **Species Fungorum** explicitly lists ***Entyloma lineatum***, the name used in older North American disease literature, as a synonym of ***Eballistra lineata***. It is **not** the Asian jiaobai fungus ***Ustilago esculenta*** [Species Fungorum, taxon 467756].

This comparison is supported by original disease observations rather than a modern review alone. Bowden and Eschen's 1986 *Plant Disease* note reports smut attributable to *Entyloma lineatum* in *Zizania aquatica* examined at Idaho fields and Benewah Lake in 1984 [Bowden & Eschen 1986]. Nyvall and Percich (1999) investigated Minnesota cultivated *Z. palustris* lesions and documented ***Bipolaris oryzae*** brown spot and ***B. sorokiniana*** spot blotch [Nyvall & Percich 1999]. Neither is direct evidence of *U. esculenta* infecting native wild rice. The studies were bounded by locality, host, assay and date; their lack of a report about a different pathogen is **not** a powered negative *U. esculenta* survey.

Thus three statements must never be conflated: **(1)** there is diagnosed smut or fungal disease in native wild rice; **(2)** that smut is specifically *U. esculenta*; **(3)** viable *U. esculenta* has spread from a present imported commodity to an identified native stand. The reviewed native literature establishes examples of (1), but not (2) or (3). A plant pathology reviewer should be able to check this distinction without assuming native rice is disease-free.

### 3.2. Viability at harvest is different from viability after import

Cultivated jiaobai is *Z. latifolia* colonized by *U. esculenta* [Guo 2015]. Chigira et al. (2023) distinguish edible **white** galls that normally lack mature spore structures at harvest from gray, more heavily sporulating galls; neither is synonymous with a fungus-free plant. Separately, Tu et al. (2019) isolated and **cultured** several *U. esculenta* strains from harvested galls at different developmental stages. This is direct evidence that some harvested gall tissue can contain a **culturable** fungus, beyond mere DNA detection [Chigira 2023; Tu 2019].

It is not a measurement of fungal survival after international transport, storage or household disposal, and it tests no American native host. White appearance does not prove absence of viable fungus; the isolation experiment does not prove an imported commercial consignment carries transmissible material. These are separable biological conditions.

The life-cycle evidence provides an essential further discriminator. Zhang and colleagues (2017) studied two sexually compatible MT-type isolates associated with edible white galls and two T-type isolates associated with gray, more heavily sporulating galls, all from a cultivar of **Asian *Z. latifolia***. MT-type strains in those assays had reduced spore germination and growth and were unable to establish the same infection process as T types under the tested conditions. T-type strains retained a more complete infection cycle. The authors related these observed phenotypes to substantial genomic differences [Zhang 2017]. This is not a quantitative comparison of all commercial jiaobai, nor proof that no MT isolate can transmit under any conditions; it is a demonstrated **reason not to equate culturable tissue with demonstrated transmission competence**.

The inference therefore has at least three different units: **harvested tissue containing viable cultured isolates (Tu 2019)**, **a studied isolate pair's ability to infect the Asian host in controlled assays (Zhang 2017)**, and **an imported product's opportunity to carry a compatible propagule into a native wetland (not assayed)**. No one of the first two studies measured the third. A negative MT result on Asian *Z. latifolia* is not a host-range negative experiment on American *Z. aquatica* or *Z. palustris*.

### 3.3. The introduced plant poses an independently documented ecological hazard

A 2024 EPPO datasheet reports establishment of *Z. latifolia* in **Hawaii**, a locality in **British Columbia**, and multiple invaded European and New Zealand wetland systems, with rhizomatous spread and displacement of other aquatic plants [EPPO 2024]. Such spread concerns the Asian perennial **plant** as an invader. It does not identify *U. esculenta* in the populations, establish native *Zizania* infection, or create US import law. The introduced plant's impacts warrant separate evaluation even if cross-species fungal pathogenicity is eventually found absent.

A **2025 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service ecological-risk screen** independently classifies *Z. latifolia* as **High Risk** for the United States, citing its documented invasion history and broad climate match for potential establishment across much of the contiguous country [USFWS 2025]. Its §7 explicitly names the **Great Lakes Basin** among areas of high climate match and defines the continental **Climate 6 score 0.814** as the fraction of continental target points with match score at least six. This is a **geographic climate-match fraction, not an 81.4% chance of invasion or local manoomin damage**. The §6 mapped Maryland and Virginia records were specifically **not established wild populations**, so neither their appearance on a map nor old planted research specimens proves present-day naturalization. Its assessment provides a more current **U.S. federal plant-invasion risk determination**, not a specific diagnosis of a local manoomin bed and not a prohibition on a cut food product. The EPPO and USFWS assessments depend in part on the same underlying invasion history; their agreement is not two independent experiments or two additional field introductions. The plant invasion pathway remains actionable as a distinct ecological research question even if native-host susceptibility to *U. esculenta* is eventually excluded.

## 4. Causal interpretation and falsifiable claims

Let \(V_h\) denote culturable *U. esculenta* in harvested gall tissue, \(V_t\) viability after a specified transport/storage sequence, \(E\) environmental exposure to suitable inoculum, \(H_n\) compatibility with a rigorously identified native host, and \(I_n\) a confirmed infection of that native host. The required empirical chain is **not** the assertion \(V_h \Rightarrow I_n\). Observation of \(V_h\) alone does not entail \(V_t\), \(E\), \(H_n\) or \(I_n\).

The available literature supports at least some \(V_h\) through Tu et al.'s culture work. Zhang et al.'s strain comparisons show that *culturable fungus need not be able to establish a new infection when the tested MT strains are paired together*, but the same study reports successful Asian-host infection for some MT/T mixed pairings: this would be a separate infectivity variable \(C\). The reviewed sources have not established \(V_t\), \(C\) for a representative 2026 imported edible shipment, \(E\), or \(H_n\) for a native host.

These quantities cannot be multiplied to generate a numerical risk estimate without data and a stated dependence model. A closed exposure chain for one strain/host combination would not close the separate living-plant invasion pathway. A species-specific voucher/sequence finding would change \(H_n\); a retail product's availability would not. Likewise the invasive plant's ability to dominate a wetland does not imply the fungus can infect North American congeners.

**Highest-value next evidence, in roughly actionable order:**

1. **Current commodity and jurisdiction:** A written, product-and-origin-specific APHIS/ACIR determination, with distinctions among fresh edible stems, processed food and live propagules. CDFA's 2018 prohibition statement cannot classify every product in 2026.
2. **Modern native-host compatibility:** Original, independently identified *U. esculenta* diagnoses or appropriately designed negative studies for each native *Zizania* taxon, rather than a copied host list. A confirmed positive or a supported incompatibility finding would change the scientific assessment.
3. **Real exposure:** Data for viable fungus in relevant shipped and stored commodity forms, its potential release in actual disposal or plant handling, and evidence of environmental encounter. Culturability at harvest is a different observable.
4. **Introduced-plant impact:** Well-supported occurrence, spread, ecological consequences and management findings for *Z. latifolia* itself, without assuming the fungus switched hosts.
5. **Historical annotation, if useful:** Original determination and any revision of exchange no. 58, when accessible without disproportionate effort. This may repair one host attribution; it is not the main gate for protective action.


## 5. Discussion

The original evidence is compatible with a precautionary response while remaining insufficient for an outbreak claim. A risk assessment may properly treat uncertain low-frequency but severe outcomes seriously. That normative posture must be identified as such, rather than transmuted into a laboratory observation.

Taxonomic ambiguity is material because the 1901 variety combination and the abbreviated 1911 greenhouse label are consistent with more than one modern species interpretation. The correct question is not “Did the printed record say *Z. aquatica*?”—it did—but whether the original biological object was the native species currently bearing that name. Evidence about *Z. latifolia* cannot be silently promoted to evidence about *Z. palustris*, and data about *Entyloma lineatum* must not be assigned to *U. esculenta*.

The ecological map must not silently become a risk prediction: USFWS reports a strong Great Lakes climate match, but such matching is neither propagule delivery nor presence in a documented manoomin bed. National climate suitability is a reason to study the introduced *plant* independently, not evidence the fungus has crossed a host boundary.

The fungal life-stage distinction is equally material. No visible mature spores in a typical edible white gall is not equivalent to sterile tissue. Tu et al.'s cultures narrow this uncertainty at the moment of sampling, but no reviewed source establishes the downstream transport and host links. Quantitative risk estimates or definitive regulatory accusations would require assumptions unsupported by the current data.

Finally, manoomin stewardship and Indigenous treaty/resource governance are subjects of responsibility, not merely a rhetorically appended “stakeholder” subsection. Public evidence about a council, plant ecology or agency process cannot substitute for Tribal permission to use field locations, specimens, stewardship knowledge or institutional endorsements.

## 6. Limitations, reproducibility and release

This manuscript is a **targeted desk review** with directly identified records; it is not a systematic review, negative survey, phylogenetic reanalysis, molecular diagnostic work, customs investigation, or recognized federal legal interpretation. Full-text access and review of some historical and experimental sources may be partial. The referenced USDA 1922 printed item establishes a historical catalogue label, not that a voucher remains extant in 2026. EPPO occurrence reports and expert communications are evidence of an introduced-plant hazard, not a synchronous census. No absolute or relative probability of native infection is estimated.

To advance this draft toward publication, preserve a dated source/selection log with complete query strings, retrieval dates, included/excluded candidate records, archived primary material where lawful, and a falsifiable extraction protocol; validate original fungal and host identifications; seek source-critical independent Science receiving and relevant external review; settle ownership and reuse rights; and explicitly label any public working-paper distribution as **unreviewed** until those conditions are met. The [scientific evidence note](../../../manoomin-jiaobai-ustilago-esculenta-evidence.md) lists the further science/reporting gates; no new science-publishing system is proposed.

## Appendix B. Historical 1911 greenhouse record: lower-priority host provenance

This source-critical investigation is retained to support scholarly traceability. The existence of multiple catalogued packets does not establish independent infections and **does not change the immediate commodity, pathogen-survival, or Tribal stewardship questions**.

### Catalogue and nomenclatural interpretation

The original USDA *Department Circular 195* (issued February 1922) lists fungi held or distributed through an exchange program [Patterson et al. 1922]. Catalogue item **58** reads *U. esculenta* “on *Zizania aquatica*,” from a **USDA greenhouse in Washington, D.C., dated 15 November 1911**, communicated by C. S. Scofield (printed p. 9; PDF page index 8). The *printed entry* supplies no herbarium barcode, host variety, sequence or revised plant determination. Public modern exsiccata indexes now provide institution-specific catalogue IDs for likely extant exchange material, but do **not** certify physical survival, label imaging, or a current host identification [MyCoPortal 2026]. A greenhouse's location is not evidence that the plant was native to that region.

Critically, Kew's nomenclature records *Zizania aquatica* var. *latifolia* (Griseb.) Kom., first published in **1901**, as a synonym of the Asian *Z. latifolia* [Kew 2026]. Therefore “*Z. aquatica*” in an early twentieth-century greenhouse catalogue cannot be mapped to present-day native *Z. aquatica* without resolving the historical host concept. Three live alternatives remain: (H1) a correctly identified American native host; (H2) an Asian host documented under an older broader name; or (H3) an original plant/fungus identification error. None can be chosen solely from item 58.

### Cross-collection specimen trail

[Mycology Collections Portal, 1921 exsiccata no. 58](https://mycoportal.org/portal/collections/exsiccati/index.php?omenid=77159) explicitly links the fungus name to **BPI 989821** (USDA National Fungus Collections), **NCU-F-0024676** (UNC–Chapel Hill Herbarium), and **WIS-F-0064092** (Wisconsin State Herbarium). The NCU and WIS records display **15 November 1911** and the USDA Washington greenhouse locality [MyCoPortal 2026]. This transforms the archival task from an unlocalized catalogue search into **three precise collection records to check**.

A [separate MyCoPortal collection results listing](https://www.mycoportal.org/portal/collections/list.php?db=7&reset=1&taxa=Ustilaginaceae&taxontype=3) contains **TENN-F-002576** (field number CSS58) and **TENN-F-002577** (CSS2577), both named *U. esculenta*, associated with Scofield and the same 1911 greenhouse date [MyCoPortal–TENN 2026]. They may represent other exchange material, but direct equivalence to no. 58 has **not** been verified from photographed labels or determiner annotations. An independent [USDA ARS result](https://biocollections.ars.usda.gov/collections/list.php?comingFrom=newsearch&page=21&taxa=164618&taxontype=2&usethes=1) includes **BPI 156812**, *U. esculenta* from a Washington government greenhouse in **January 1912**; no connection with the 1911 exchange should be assumed.

**Independence constraint:** Multiple distributed exsiccata packets from the same 1911 greenhouse material count as **one source event unless proven otherwise**. More catalog IDs strengthen discoverability and potential for re-examination; they do not multiply the number of affected host individuals, establish host switching or identify the original greenhouse plant.

For nomenclatural comparison, [Species Fungorum](https://www.speciesfungorum.org/names/NamesRecord.asp?RecordId=183635) lists the original 1895 *U. esculenta* type as **Balansa 4931 in flowers of *Z. latifolia*, Vietnam**. That is separate original **Asian** type-host evidence. The type description does not resolve the later greenhouse host.

The same catalogue contains item **9**, a different fungus, *Entyloma lineatum*, recorded on *Z. aquatica* in Winona, Minnesota, in **1888**. This provides a useful taxon-level negative control: “smut on wild rice” is not species-level evidence of *U. esculenta*, and the two catalogue entries must not be combined as though they describe a single organism.

A curator's high-resolution original label scan, associated host voucher, exchange correspondence, and post-1911 annotation could resolve or narrow H1–H3. The **catalogue searches have now located accession records**, but physical specimen presence, host determinations and the status of an original plant counterpart remain unverified. BPI, UNC and Wisconsin collections provide specific, independently locatable points of inquiry [MyCoPortal 2026]; no curator reply or specimen re-identification has been received.
