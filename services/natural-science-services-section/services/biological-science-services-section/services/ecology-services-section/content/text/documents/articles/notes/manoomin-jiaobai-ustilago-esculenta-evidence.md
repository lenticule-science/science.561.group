# Manoomin and the *Zizania latifolia–Ustilago esculenta* pathway

**Finding:** The introduction hazard is credible enough to warrant ordinary plant-health reporting and commodity-rule clarification. Infection of an American native *Zizania* stand by *Ustilago esculenta*, a current US outbreak, and the legality of a particular retail vegetable shipment are **not established** by the evidence examined here. The risk claim must not silently become any of those stronger claims.

## What is worth resolving first

The goal is to protect manoomin, not to solve every historical nomenclature puzzle. The next research efforts should be ordered by how directly an answer changes present decisions. These questions can proceed in parallel where evidence and qualified expertise are available:

1. **Present import status and actual reports.** Establish the current APHIS/ACIR rule for fresh edible *Z. latifolia* culms from the specified origin, separately from processed foods and propagative plants. Avoid extrapolating a 2018 state assessment into a current federal ruling. Distinguish independently witnessed observations from online reposts.
2. **Viable exposure pathway.** Determine what direct evidence exists for viable *U. esculenta* in harvested edible tissue, and what is actually known about persistence through shipping, retail storage and disposal. A culturable harvested gall does not by itself establish transmission into a wetland.
3. **Modern native-host evidence.** Look for original diagnoses or defensible host-range studies for *Z. aquatica*, *Z. palustris* and *Z. texana*, keeping each species separate. The consequence of infection in an annual grain species is important, but cannot be treated as a measured outbreak.
4. **Independent introduced-plant risk and stewardship.** Evaluate actual establishment/dispersal of *Z. latifolia* and existing Tribal-led manoomin protections. Do not require proof of fungal host-switching to examine an invasive aquatic plant, and do not infer authority to use protected ecological knowledge.
5. **Historical labels (secondary).** Check the 1911 greenhouse packet only if its original host determination can be resolved with reasonable effort. An old name is a useful source-critical caution, not the primary evidence needed to answer contemporary regulatory, infection or exposure questions.

These are priorities for further evidence gathering, **not claims that any agency determination, experiment, Tribal partnership or survey has been completed**.

### Introduced-plant establishment is a separate hazard

The 2024 EPPO biological datasheet independently documents invasive *Z. latifolia* establishment in Hawaii and British Columbia and effects on wetland plant communities [S18]. This supports a **plant-invasion concern**, irrespective of whether *U. esculenta* can infect native annual *Zizania*. EPPO's A2 listing and policy recommendations concern the EPPO region; they are **not** United States federal prohibitions. A more recent EPPO distribution or occurrence claim does not itself prove the plant's current status at an exact US site.

Independently, a **U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service ecological risk screening revised March 2023 and issued as an April 2025 web version** rated introduced *Z. latifolia* **High Risk** for the contiguous United States [S27]. Its conclusion integrates reported invasiveness and climate matching, with broad medium-to-high climate matches across much of the contiguous country. This is a stronger U.S.-specific screening basis for **plant establishment and wetland displacement**, not a finding that *U. esculenta* has infected American wild rice, nor a prediction that every suitable waterway will be invaded. EPPO and USFWS partly draw on the same historical introduced populations; these two agency syntheses do **not** count as two independent field invasions.

**Great Lakes-specific climate screen, not a probability:** The federal assessment names high climate match around the Great Lakes Basin and calculates a **contiguous-U.S. Climate 6 score of 0.814**, the fraction of assessed target points meeting a climate similarity threshold [S28, §§6–7]. Neither 0.814 nor the map is a probability of invasion or a site-specific manoomin damage estimate. FWS also states its mapped Maryland and Virginia records were **not established wild populations**. Habitat suitability and occurrence must remain separately measured.

The [full USFWS screening](https://www.fws.gov/sites/default/files/documents/2025-06/ecological-risk-screening-summary-manchurian-wildrice-april-2025.pdf) cites **Terrell and Batra (1982)** on historical *living-plant* introductions into U.S. research facilities and universities, including a University of Minnesota transfer, and notes a seed listing observed in 2023 [S28]. It does **not** establish that those research collections survive today or prove fresh edible stems currently breach import rules. Those are distinct introduction mechanisms. More consequentially, [Biosecurity New Zealand](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/biosecurity/exotic-pests-and-diseases-in-new-zealand/long-term-biosecurity-management-programmes/national-interest-pest-responses-programme/manchurian-wild-rice-eradication) documents established invasive populations, an approximately 500-hectare Northland containment area, and an eradication programme for *living* plants [S29]. This is real management evidence, not a U.S. legal precedent.

Cultivated jiaobai lines maintained through infected rhizomes often lose flowering [S3], but **the perennial plant species is not universally sterile**: seed and rhizome fragments can disperse in introduced populations [S28, S29]. We must not substitute the reproduction of a selected edible cultivar for the whole invasive-plant species.

### Important bibliographic conflict

The USDA-NRCS 2024 *Zizania aquatica* guide [S5, p. 6, “Pests and Potential Problems”] says that native annual-wild-rice culms may be infected by *U. esculenta*, citing **Kealey, Kosikowski & Gray (1981)**. The identified Kealey–Kosikowski–Gray 1981 publication is a **review of corn smut as food** [S7], not an experimental report confirming *U. esculenta* infection of *Z. aquatica*. This is a **citation-provenance discrepancy**, not proof that native *Zizania* is resistant.

CDFA [S1] independently lists *Z. aquatica* as a host, while the 2020 primary study [S4] names only *Z. latifolia*. A qualified plant pathologist should trace the original host record behind the two agency claims. Do not cite the NRCS passage as if it were confirmed cross-species infection.

In a 2018 CDFA discussion [S11], a botanist said *Z. palustris* had not been tested as a host. This contemporary uncertainty matters more to present-day native-host risk than the species printed on a 1911 greenhouse label.

### Distinct sampling frames behind similar numerical claims

The **five** naturally infected wild Asian plants in Zhao et al. (2019) [S38], the **five** harvested cultivated galls in Tu et al. (2019) [S17], and the **168** wild Asian genomes sampled for climate adaptation in Zou et al. (2024) [S39] are **three separate research designs**, not repetitions of one assay. Zhao reports fungal infections among **824 putatively wild *Z. latifolia*** field specimens and genotyped cultivated lines; Tu cultured fungus from **five selected edible galls**; Zou assessed **population genomic adaptation** but cited Zhao rather than independently repeating that fungus survey. None of those three sample sets tests a native American *Zizania* species. Reporting a field proportion from Zhao as a global infection rate or transferring it to Great Lakes manoomin would be an unsupported inference.

A useful positive standard for native disease work is Doan and Davis's (2019) causal diagnosis of the **oomycete *Pythiogeton manoomin*** in *Z. palustris*: isolate and identify the organism, inoculate a known host with controls, observe compatible symptoms, and re-isolate [S40]. Its greenhouse treatment results (**84% symptomatic plants and 71% seedling damping-off**) pertain to that organism under those conditions, **not** to *U. esculenta*. The source prints physiologically implausible greenhouse temperature units (80–85°C/65–70°C), which require an author correction before a replication protocol could be trusted. This method example is not a call for the public or unauthorized laboratories to perform pathogen inoculations.

### What edible white versus gray jiaobai establishes

The full Guo et al. (2015) Results and Table 1 compare **one wild Asian plant genome (HSD2)** with **two cultivated Asian genomes (Zhejiao2 and Jiayou1)**; those are not replicated native American host experiments [S3]. Chigira et al. (2023), Table 4, lists **nine Japanese archived strains and one Chinese-origin strain (yeast4, MAFF 305616)** among its ten strain resources [S12]; an abstract's geographic shorthand should not replace the original sample table. The source-owned machine-readable extraction records those sample identities and the parts of the experiments that were not conducted.

A 2016 Indian morphogenesis study of *Z. latifolia* used plant microsatellite and fungal ITS comparisons to identify the Asian host–fungus association [S21]. This is independent modern evidence for the established **Asian** association, not a diagnostic test of native American *Zizania*.

A 2023 peer-reviewed study [S12] distinguishes edible **white** galls, associated with mycelial-teliospore (M-T) strains that lack visible sori at the harvesting stage, from **gray** galls full of mature teliospores. White edible tissue still contains fungal hyphae. It follows that a swollen edible stem is **not** equivalent to a visibly sporulating gall, but neither the absence of visible spores nor ordinary retail sale proves the tissue contains no fungus or presents zero phytosanitary risk. Tu et al. (2019) cultured several *U. esculenta* strains from **harvested galls** sampled at different swelling stages [S17]. Thus at least some harvested gall tissue contains culturable fungus: presence is not merely inferred from DNA or a visible gall. However, this study did **not** quantify organism survival after international shipping, retail storage, cooking or disposal, nor competence to infect an American native host. Those links remain **unmeasured**, not zero.

A particularly important **2017 controlled study** compared tested MT-type isolates from edible **white** galls with T-type isolates from **gray** galls on the Asian cultivar Longjiao 2 [S26]. The study also found that some crosses between T-type and MT-type isolates produced mating structures and successful infection in the tested Asian host [S26, Discussion]. **Therefore it would be incorrect to conclude that all MT-type-containing edible material is incapable of contributing to a future infection.** The experimental mixture is not a measurement of mixed spores or viability in an imported retail culm. In the study's mating and inoculation system, MT isolates showed impaired teliospore formation and germination and an infection defect; T isolates retained a more complete infection life cycle. The paper's finding is **strain- and experimental-context-specific**. It does **not** show that all edible jiaobai is biologically sterile, that retail material can never transmit fungus, or that an MT strain can infect an untested American host. It does show why detecting culturable fungus in an edible gall [S17] is not interchangeable with documenting an infection-competent, transmissible stage [S26].

**Four observations must remain distinct:** (1) fungal DNA or hyphae in tissue; (2) culturable fungus recovered from tissue; (3) viable reproductive material capable of initiating an infection in a known susceptible host; and (4) a demonstrated infection of a properly identified native North American *Zizania*. Evidence currently advances observation (2) for some harvested galls and strain-specific laboratory results relevant to (3) on **Asian** hosts. It does not establish observation (4), nor (3) for current U.S. retail specimens.

This changes evidence handling: record product form and declared processing state, but do not ask the public to dissect a culm, search for sori, culture tissue, or transport specimens. A plant-health specialist, not an uninstructed observer, must decide whether and how to assess viability.

### Exposure is a chain, not one binary fact

A meaningful introduction risk requires attention to **identity → material type → viability → access to susceptible hosts → establishment → reproductive damage**. One link cannot stand in for the whole chain. An identified commercial jiaobai culm is evidence of plant–fungus association in the source crop, but not automatically evidence of free spores, viability after transport or transmission to a native stand. Conversely, uncertainty about that chain is not a reason to ignore a credible, specific import or environmental report.

CDFA [S1] discusses dispersal involving infested plants, rhizomes, galled stems and soil; its statements concern the known host and risk assessment. No citizen collection, field culture, transport or experimental inoculation is justified by this desk review.

A negative survey only constrains the species, assay, place and time actually surveyed. Wisconsin DATCP describes a 2025 tribal-directed manoomin survey conducted with the Wisconsin Tribal Conservation Advisory Council [S8]. It targeted bacterial blight, bacterial leaf streak and brown spot—not a systematic *U. esculenta* screen. Do not report this survey as either detection or absence of *U. esculenta*.

### Real manoomin stewardship and current environmental work

The Great Lakes Indian Fish & Wildlife Commission's [Ganawenindiwag Manoomin Anishinaabeg](https://glifwc.org/stewardship/ganawenindiwag-manoomin-anishinaabeg) programme describes the living relationship between people and rice, and existing observation and research guided by tribal rice chiefs, elders and member nations' natural-resource departments [S30]. GLIFWC identifies changing water levels, water quality, contaminants and climate as documented pressures. The 2026 Wisconsin Wild Rice Stewardship Council published a [7 October summit agenda](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/calendar/meeting/130251) naming a State/Tribal Technical Committee and a review of priorities [S31]; an event notice does **not** verify an adopted decision. In Minnesota, a [July 2026 Fond du Lac Band–Minnesota Land Trust–DNR proposal](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/news/2026/07/28/minnesota-dnr-accepting-comments-environmental-review-documents-proposed-cedar-lake-manoomin-restoration-project) would restore water levels for manoomin at Cedar Lake [S32]; the notice and closed comment period do **not** verify restoration completion.

These existing primary institutional accounts should anchor useful applied research. They do not confer permission to inspect sacred/managed rice beds or to use private traditional knowledge. A new fungal-pathway hypothesis should not displace established water and habitat concerns absent evidence that would justify that priority change.

## Actionable technical interpretation

1. **For a direct, credible US retail or online observation:** Preserve the original date, exact product wording, vendor/location or URL, and any photographs or origin/lot information *already available*. A repost is not a second independent observation. Do not purchase, ship, cultivate, dismantle or experimentally handle suspect material to confirm the report. APHIS's **Smuggling Interdiction and Trade Compliance (SITC)** program explicitly accepts suspected restricted foreign plant-product reports and determines admissibility [S9]. A report is not an accusation or a confirmed violation.
2. **For a suspected living introduction or an affected rice bed:** Notify the competent state/tribal plant-health contacts promptly, following their instructions for observation and possible sampling. Keep sensitive rice-bed coordinates and traditional knowledge out of public repositories. Do not move stems, rhizomes, soil or specimens without agency direction.
3. **For current legal status:** Consult APHIS ACIR [S6] for the exact commodity, origin, processing state and intended use, or request a current written determination. The 2018 federal restructuring of 7 CFR 319.37 for plants for planting [S20] prevents treating CDFA's older 2017 citation as the operative 2026 rule for fresh edible stems; 7 CFR 319.56 has a separate fruits-and-vegetables frame [S14]. Current admissibility is still unknown, and no particular seller is therefore established to have violated a rule.
4. **For scientific correction:** Ask the agency or a qualified diagnostic laboratory for the native-host primary record underlying CDFA [S1] and NRCS [S5], and for a defensible distinction between infected *Z. latifolia* plant material and a harvested edible culm. Avoid generating speculative host-infection trials.
5. **For support to manoomin stewards:** Direct useful evidence and resource offers through the designated tribal authorities and their existing partners rather than assuming consent, collecting precise locations, or creating another independent survey. Materially different risks—including hydrology, contamination and existing rice diseases—remain distinct.

### Discriminating observations

- A new, professionally confirmed *U. esculenta* diagnosis in a native *Zizania* plant would substantially strengthen the native-host claim. A CDFA list without the underlying isolation record does not.
- A written APHIS determination covering a named commodity from a specified country would resolve that legal case; an old plant-for-planting quarantine does not settle every cut vegetable.
- A documented viable organism in an imported consignment would strengthen one exposure link, but would still not prove native infection.
- Verified correction of a public product identification would weaken that particular retail report without invalidating the broader agency record of earlier incursions.

## Research publication method (work not yet accepted as a paper)

**Research aim:** Determine what can be defensibly established about the *U. esculenta* introduction pathway, native *Zizania* susceptibility, and consequences for manoomin. The existing evidence note is a research input, not a peer-reviewed manuscript or an independently received scientific release.

Two candidate papers address materially different questions; their boundaries should be settled by the specialist science reviewer before writing conclusions.

**Paper A — Evidence synthesis and historical host-record audit.** A systematic, reproducible review of *U. esculenta* host records, native *Zizania* infection claims, plant/pathogen detections in North America, and the taxonomy and provenance of the 1911 greenhouse record. This can be completed from primary literature, archival specimen metadata, and expert record clarification without undertaking pathogen experiments.

**Paper B — Food-product exposure and phytosanitary decision analysis.** A defensible chain-of-evidence analysis for commodity form, fungus stage, viability in actual distribution, handling/disposal pathways, native-host encounter, and current import rules. Any claim about viable organisms in imported products needs direct qualified evidence; current APHIS commodity eligibility remains **unknown** pending written determination. Do not infer pathogen introduction from a grocery listing. Paper B may remain a prospective protocol if indispensable viability, exposure, or regulatory evidence is unavailable.

### Methods and source discipline

1. **Freeze a dated search protocol before treating the review as exhaustive.** Search primary scientific literature (including historical synonyms), USDA National Fungus Collections and herbarium records, USDA APHIS, CDFA, state plant-health guidance, and appropriate Tribal/Indigenous research institutions. Record database, exact query, search date, inclusion/exclusion rules, deduplication, screened counts, and reason for excluding each full-text candidate. This current note does not claim that search is done.
2. **Separate observations from host assertions.** Evidence rows must include a stable source URL/DOI or specimen accession, taxon as originally printed and modern proposed taxon, identification method, occurrence site type (field / greenhouse / lab / imported commodity), evidence type (field diagnosis, preserved specimen, molecular sequence, experiment, secondary list, or inference), collection and publication dates, geographic scope, and uncertainty. A secondary host list must link back to a primary observation; unresolved edges remain unresolved.
3. **Evaluate primary evidence independently.** Record diagnostic specificity, original material availability, possible contamination or host misidentification, assay controls, host-stage relevance, sample frame, conflicts and negative findings. The 1911 greenhouse specimen and Kealey et al. mis-citation warrant priority source checking, not unverified correction of the biological claim.
4. **Disentangle distinct hazards.** Evaluate invasive *Z. latifolia* establishment separately from movement of *U. esculenta*, host switching, and reduced native annual-rice seed production. For commodity exposure, distinguish white mycelial edible tissue, gray spore-bearing tissue, live propagules and processed products. Do not invent probabilities or convert a possibility chain into a measured risk.
5. **Handle legal evidence as time-bound.** Tabulate operative rule text, jurisdiction, commodity, origin, treatment, plant vs cut food category, effective date, interpretation source and confidence. Preserve historical CDFA statements; a written current APHIS answer supersedes guesses about federal eligibility. Do not use legal uncertainty to allege a retailer violation.
6. **Protect Tribal research authority.** Consult existing Anishinaabe and other affected nations' manoomin research and protection priorities. Credit source authority and consent terms; do not publish exact sensitive bed locations or harvest knowledge absent explicit release, claim co-authorship without agreement, or speak for a nation on the strength of publicly accessible sources.
7. **Release as existing Science carriers.** Keep research, supplementary search logs, bibliographic tables and resulting manuscript source within the appropriate Lenticule owning repository. Apply existing carrier-schema/provenance rules, PTGAE, scientific receiving, explicit reuse rights, and L-Space's existing publication candidate/rights mechanism; a public GitHub file is not automatically a scientifically accepted article. Follow the existing [research-release architecture audit](https://github.com/561-group/bare-cedar-fog/issues/255) rather than founding a new CMS or paper-publishing site. Favor persistent source revisions, plain text/Markdown, reproducible data and executable checks where justified.
**Reporting standard:** This is currently a *targeted, source-critical desk review*, **not** a completed systematic or exhaustive scoping review. For a future registered scoping review, report the full searches, selection counts, exclusions and extraction procedures against [PRISMA-ScR](https://doi.org/10.7326/M18-0850). For a formal environmental systematic evidence map, use [ROSES](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13750-018-0121-7). Neither reporting standard substitutes for source validation or Tribal authority over sensitive knowledge.

8. **Independent scientific and editorial scrutiny.** Obtain a named competent scientific review with claims checked against the underlying papers/specimens, a correction log, and a clear accepted/rejected/disputed conclusion. Archive reviewers' substantive counterarguments and responses. External scholarly review, DOI, journal/preprint deposit, and open licensing should be claimed only when actually obtained/authorized.

## Appendix — Historical host labels and specimen provenance

This material is retained because it may help correct a secondary host list. It is **not** a current-disease observation and does not need to be resolved before the import-status, exposure, native-host or plant-invasion questions above can be investigated.

### Historical host records and an agency contradiction

The CDFA *U. esculenta* profile [S1, 2017] lists *Z. aquatica* as a host. However, California's own **2018 *Z. latifolia* plant assessment** [S11] records the responsible botanist's statement that *Z. latifolia* was the **only known host** and that infection of *Z. palustris* had **not been tested** at that time. The apparent host-range disagreement exists within the same agency's contemporary materials; it is not merely an agency-versus-academic dispute.

A much earlier lead deserves specific provenance checking: USDA Department Circular 195, issued February 1922, lists exchange specimen **no. 58, *U. esculenta* “on *Zizania aquatica*,”** attributed to a USDA greenhouse in Washington, D.C. on November 15, 1911, communicated by C. S. Scofield [S13]. This establishes a *historical labelled greenhouse specimen entry*, not authenticated infection of native *Z. aquatica* in a natural US rice bed. The old plant name is demonstrably ambiguous: the combination *Zizania aquatica* var. *latifolia*, published in 1901, is a synonym of the Asian *Z. latifolia* [S15]. The 1922 printed entry does **not** specify a variety or current taxonomic determination. However, modern specimen catalogues now index **multiple collection records for exchange no. 58** [S22], so the earlier assertion that no voucher identifier was located has been superseded. Therefore its host might have been native *Z. aquatica*, an Asian plant classified under an old broad concept, or a misidentified planting; the printed record cannot discriminate these alternatives. It is unknown whether entry no. 58 underlies later fungal-host databases and CDFA's 2017 host list. The original USDA catalogue also lists a different smut, *Entyloma lineatum* on *Z. aquatica* from Winona, Minnesota (1888; no. 9); neither record may be used as evidence for the other fungus [S13].

The most useful next scientific step is to request original specimen metadata or a taxonomic re-determination of USDA specimen no. 58 and the primary evidence supporting CDFA's *Z. aquatica* host listing. Do not treat a secondary database host association as an independently verified infection, and do not erase that association merely because it conflicts with more recent papers.

Greenhouse” [S24]; its relation to the 1911 exchange is unknown and must not be inferred from proximity in time or taxon name.

**Critical unit of observation:** Three collection records linked to exchange no. 58 and two apparent 1911 collection counterparts are **not five independent infections**. They may be exsiccata duplicates from a *single* harvested greenhouse event. Their scientific value is that packet labels, determination annotations, host specimens and historical provenance may be recoverable from specific archives. The public lists do not prove a currently extant physical object, plant identity under modern taxonomy, fungal re-identification, or field transmission.

The [Species Fungorum nomenclatural entry for *U. esculenta*](https://www.speciesfungorum.org/names/NamesRecord.asp?RecordId=183635) independently reports the original 1895 taxon's type as **Balansa 4931 on flowers of *Zizania latifolia* from Vietnam** [S25]. This is an original Asian host association, separate from the ambiguous 1911 greenhouse exchange. It does not solve the 1911 identification or establish a North American native host.

**Highest-value next archival check:** ask the BPI, NCU and WIS curators, referencing their exact catalogue IDs, for existing high-resolution specimen/label images, original host annotation, determiner, any herbarium/seed counterpart, cross-exchange records and later taxonomic revisions. Seek guidance on whether identification can be established without disturbing objects or requesting new laboratory work. If a confirmed host is obtained, update claims S1/S5/this record and its dependent manuscript; otherwise record the unanswered status. No collection access, specimen shipment or destructive analysis has been authorized.
