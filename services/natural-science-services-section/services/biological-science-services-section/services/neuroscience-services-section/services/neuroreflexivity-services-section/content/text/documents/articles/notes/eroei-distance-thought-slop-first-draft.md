The Invisible Architecture: EROEI as the Measure of Distance
To understand the modern human condition, we must first look past the ideologies and economic theories we usually use to explain ourselves. Instead, we have to examine the literal, physical foundation of modern life: a metric known as Energy Returned on Energy Invested, or EROEI.

Strictly speaking, EROEI is a system-level measure of energy efficiency. It calculates the ratio between the energy required to extract a resource and the usable surplus energy generated by that extraction. If it takes one unit of energy to drill a well and you get ten units back, your EROEI is 10:1.

However, EROEI is not just a mathematical output; fundamentally, it is a measure of distance. When EROEI is high, you generate a massive surplus of energy, and that surplus creates separation. It builds a buffer between what a society plans to do and the immediate material consequences of doing it. It is the margin between human effort and sheer survival.

The 100:1 World and the Illusion of Liberation
In the early twentieth century, humanity unlocked an energetic lottery. The first major fossil fuel reserves, particularly oil, were incredibly easy to access. In the earliest, most accessible fields, operators enjoyed an EROEI ranging from 30:1 to well over 100:1. You could essentially dig a hole and get a geyser.

This 100:1 surplus is what built the modern world in its entirety. It funded the construction of highways, sprawling suburbs, global grocery supply chains, and eventually the physical infrastructure of the internet. It allowed cities to scale up far beyond the limits of their local water and food sources, and it permitted agriculture to rely on synthetic fertilizers made from natural gas.

But this massive energy surplus did not just change physical infrastructure; it gave modernity its defining psychological characteristics: confidence, verticality, and abstraction. When returns are incredibly high, a society can build far above the ground—not just materially, but cognitively and morally. This surplus is what allowed people to seriously discuss colonizing Mars while relying on fossil-fueled farming, and it permitted the writing of policy papers on decolonization by people who were completely disconnected from the aquifers keeping their cities alive.

Crucially, the story of this high-EROEI era is not one of collective human liberation. Fossil energy did not replace human labor; rather, it expanded and intensified it. On plantations, fossil-fueled infrastructure simply made enslaved labor more economically "productive," while in factories, coal power established time-discipline and wage labor as absolute global norms. The energy surplus fundamentally restructured work to optimize for scale, control, and massive output. The entire managerial economy we live with today—the corporation, the spreadsheet, the KPI—is a direct product of operating under surplus conditions.

Because the surplus was so vast, dominant institutions were able to externalize their costs. They could push the material, social, and ecological damage of their expansion out of sight. This immense energetic buffer allowed highly abstract forms of thought to become dominant—global development theory, liberal individualism, and the unquestioned assumption of infinite economic growth. These ideologies thrived exclusively because they could afford to ignore the energy and intense labor required to sustain them. People began to mistakenly believe that the abundance of the world was a function of their morality, rather than a temporary reality dictated by thermodynamics.

The Squeeze: Living in a Low-Return System
That era of easy surplus is ending. The easily tapped oil fields are dry, and extraction has moved to much harder, more energy-intensive places like deep water, tar sands, and shale.

As a result, the average EROEI of global energy systems has declined significantly. The historical 100:1 ratios have fallen to 20:1, 10:1, and in many cases even lower. Resources like shale oil, tar sands, and corn ethanol often yield only a 4:1 or 3:1 return after factoring in the hidden costs of extraction and processing. Even the transition to renewable sources like solar and wind requires massive upfront investments in metals, electrical grids, and batteries.

The system is still running, but it is operating on severely degraded margins. We are experiencing higher costs for lower returns. And because every other system in society is built on top of this underlying energy ratio, a falling EROEI means that available surplus shrinks everywhere.

This does not just tighten financial budgets; it tightens the realm of possibility. As the surplus drops, our societal altitude drops with it. Systems that were designed assuming a massive surplus begin to strain and creak under the load. Infrastructure ages faster than it can be repaired, bureaucracies become sluggish and brittle, and essential institutions like hospitals and schools begin operating with less slack and less trust. The cultural conversation involuntarily pivots from extraplanetary dreams of Mars to municipal triage—just figuring out how to keep the power grid running. The collective imagination is actively adjusting to a lowered collective potential.

The Metabolism of Thought: Fracking for Meaning
The most profound realization about EROEI is that it applies directly to human cognition. Because thought literally requires energy—from the glucose fueling the brain to the societal infrastructure that affords us time and communication bandwidth—attention, memory, and analysis all depend heavily on material supports.

In a high-surplus world, these supports were abundant, allowing for complex, abstract, and nuanced reasoning to flourish. A high-EROEI society could afford to imagine ten different futures before breakfast. But as energy becomes constrained, the cognitive budget is brutally cut.

You can see this cognitive decline most clearly on the internet. In its early days, the web possessed a phenomenally high "informational EROEI". The return on a single hour spent online could be mind-expanding and community-forming. One blog post could completely shift your worldview; one hyperlink could send you deep into a subculture that changed your life. It was a high-yield, low-input system.

Today, however, the internet's surplus has vanished. The signal-to-noise ratio has plummeted as feeds have been paved over with content farms, engagement traps, and algorithmic landfill. Social media now functions exactly like a tar sands extraction operation: it requires massive, highly toxic infrastructure just to produce microdrops of usable joy.

As a user, you are no longer exploring; you are "fracking for meaning". To deliver a single meme or thinkpiece, energy must move through dozens of machines, servers, and cables, yielding perhaps half a second of reaction before you scroll to the next. It costs a massive amount of energy to get almost nothing in return. You burn through a thousand posts and feel nothing because the dopamine wells are dry.

This explains why we are experiencing mass cognitive fatigue, doomscrolling, emotional burnout, and ideological polarization. These are not just individual psychological failings; they are the literal symptoms of a low-return system under severe energetic strain. The lights are on, but everything flickers. When there is no surplus energy left to sustain ambiguity, institutions collapse toward raw efficiency. Ethics are compressed into tribal slogans, complex futures shrink to fit into social media feeds, and nuance is lost.

If it feels like your brain is overheating just trying to read a single paragraph, you are not broken. You are simply trying to run 20th-century cognitive software on a 21st-century energy budget.

Reconfiguration: What Scales Down?
The fundamental challenge of our time is not figuring out how to power the old, high-surplus world with new, green energy. The real question is: what kinds of thoughts, systems, and institutions remain viable without the surplus? We are no longer living in a world of infinite choices; we have entered a world of yields, where every single action and thought has a noticeable draw on our energy.

Ideologies that were built on excess, distance, and denial—like transhumanist optimism or endless global development—are becoming incoherent because the return on abstraction is dropping. Philosophy itself has an EROEI, and the assumption that you can just keep feeding it time and coffee to get pure truth out the other end is a relic of the 100:1 era. Not every path or future is worth the energetic cost anymore.

So, we must ask: what scales down?

The answer lies in frameworks that never relied on a surplus to begin with. Indigenous land-based epistemologies, for example, have always been based on relation, maintenance, reciprocity, and renewal. These systems were systematically attacked by high-EROEI empires, but they remain perfectly scaled to a low-yield reality because they do not rely on endless extraction. Similarly, feminist economies of care prioritize sufficiency, feedback, and interdependence over raw output and control. These forms of life operate naturally under energetic constraint, making them some of the most coherent and viable strategies available to us now.

Degrowth as a Sensory Strategy
Ultimately, surviving a low-EROEI world requires more than just decarbonizing our electrical grids; we must decarbonize our minds and de-intensify our expectations.

This means actively seeking out experiences that are "cognitively convex"—small, low-input efforts that occasionally explode in massive returns. Doing one thing slowly, sitting with a friend, feeling the wind, or simply letting yourself be bored cost almost no energy, but they can yield insights and peace that last for days. You do not need more experiences; you need less distance from the ones you have.

Degrowth, in this sense, is not just a macroeconomic theory about shrinking the GDP. It is a vital sensory strategy and a spiritual EROEI recalibration. It invites us to stop valuing scale, reach, and progress, and instead focus on density, closeness, and resonance.

We need to start measuring our lives in new ways. We should judge our technologies by their "surplus-to-suffering ratios". We should begin thinking in terms of "watts per smile" and "relations-per-calorie". Because when the old world was built on excess, the new one has to run on elegance. The future isn't getting further away; it is found right under your senses, running on the profound metric of "qualia per joule". The ROI on silence is outrageous, and that is where new thought begins.
