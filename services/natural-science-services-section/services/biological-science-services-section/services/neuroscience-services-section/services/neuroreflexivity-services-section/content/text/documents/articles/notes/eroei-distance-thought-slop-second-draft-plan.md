## Beat by beat plan

Beat 1 should establish the frame cleanly. Open with EROEI not as a niche engineering ratio but as a measure of how much room a civilization has between extraction and necessity. This is where “distance” first appears, but it should be immediately unpacked into concrete terms: slack, reach, storage, specialization, mobility, institutional overhead, and abstraction. The goal here is to define surplus as usable civilizational margin, not just energy profit. Research to gather here: baseline definitions of EROEI, major debates about system-boundary problems, and broad historical estimates for conventional oil versus harder-to-extract fuels.

Beat 2 should ground the high-surplus century materially. This section should describe the early fossil era as an anomalous period of unusually cheap energetic access. Not “the modern world in its entirety,” but the buildout of its characteristic scale. Highways, refrigeration, synthetic fertilizer, container shipping, large metro regions, aviation, electrified communications, data centers later on. The point is that the built environment of modernity was subsidized by extraordinary surplus. Research to gather here: historical EROEI ranges for early oil, electrification timelines, fertilizer dependence on natural gas, urban scaling enabled by fossil energy, and key infrastructure histories.

Beat 3 should then ask what surplus does socially. This is where you move from infrastructure to labor regime. The argument is not that fossil energy replaced labor, but that it reorganized labor into larger, more tightly synchronized, more administratively legible systems. Plantations, factories, warehouses, global logistics, white-collar management, finance and planning layers. Surplus allowed societies to carry more coordination overhead and more hierarchical complexity. Research to gather here: labor history on fossil energy and factory discipline, logistics history, managerial capitalism, and work on how energy transitions changed labor intensity and control rather than simply reducing toil.

Beat 4 should introduce the central move about cognition, now in literal rather than analogical terms. High surplus created expanded cognitive territory by paying for the infrastructures that make thought possible beyond subsistence and locality. This includes educational institutions, telecommunications, digital networks, cheap mobility, urban concentration of specialists, time-saving appliances, and forms of leisure that allowed reflection, reading, and encounter. You can say something like: modern thought became geographically and institutionally larger because energy surplus widened the radius within which attention could travel. Research to gather here: history of leisure time and paid vacation, education expansion, media infrastructure, computing infrastructure energy use, knowledge-worker formation, mobility and tourism as cognitive expansion, and scholarship on cognition as socially distributed/materially scaffolded.

Beat 5 should sharpen the ideological consequence. Once societies can sustain vast energetic overhead, they start mistaking surplus-enabled distance for moral progress or civilizational maturity. This is where abstraction, confidence, universality, development discourse, liberal individualism, and infinite-growth assumptions appear. The argument should be careful: these ideologies were not caused solely by surplus, but they became credible and dominant under surplus conditions because they were buffered from immediate material contradiction. Research to gather here: critiques of development, ecological economics, empire and externalization, and intellectual history on how material abundance shaped postwar political imagination.

Beat 6 should mark the turn downward. Easy surplus erodes; extraction shifts to harder resources; the system pays more to get less net energy. This is where you can reintroduce EROEI decline, but keep the prose calm and concrete. The effect is not immediate collapse but tightening margins across interdependent systems. Repairs are deferred, redundancy shrinks, institutions become brittle, politics gets meaner, and future horizons narrow. Research to gather here: contemporary EROEI estimates across fuels, net-energy literature, maintenance backlog literature, infrastructure fragility, and state-capacity or institutional-fragility work.

Beat 7 should then revisit cognition under declining yield. This is where the internet section belongs, but now with clearer causality. Early digital culture emerged in a world where there was enough surplus to support relatively high-yield exploration: open web culture, hobbyist publishing, forums, weird archives, long-form attention. Over time, the same digital sphere became enclosed by ad-tech, platform extraction, recommendation systems, and industrial-scale content production. That is not just a cultural decline story; it is a story of increasingly heavy infrastructures and attention markets producing lower experiential return per unit of energy, time, and affect. “Fracking for meaning” belongs here, and works better once the scaffolding is in place. Research to gather here: internet history, platformization, ad-tech, data-center and AI energy demand if relevant, attention economy research, information overload, and mental-health or media-fragmentation research.

Beat 8 should ask the strategic question: what remains viable under lower surplus? This is where you shift from diagnosis to selection criteria. Instead of immediately naming preferred traditions, first define the traits of low-surplus coherence: maintenance over expansion, repair over replacement, local feedback over long opaque chains, sufficiency over maximization, resilience over throughput, reciprocity over externalization. Then introduce Indigenous land-based practices, feminist care economies, commons traditions, repair cultures, and public infrastructures as examples that often organize around those traits. That sequence will keep the essay from sounding like it reached for fashionable answers before defining the problem. Research to gather here: maintenance studies, commons governance, care economy literature, Indigenous political ecology, degrowth, resilience theory, and examples of low-energy social systems that preserve dignity without romanticization.

Beat 9 should conclude with a recalibrated sensibility rather than a stack of slogans. The ending should keep the lyrical register, but narrow it. The key idea is that as surplus declines, value shifts from scale and reach to density and return. Attention should go to low-input, high-yield forms of life: friendship, proximity, repair, slowness, embodied knowledge, silence, boredom, local competence. Pick one or two memorable formulations and stop there. “The ROI on silence is outrageous” is strong enough to carry the landing by itself. Research to gather here is lighter; this section is more philosophical, though it could still draw on work about boredom, attention restoration, conviviality, and well-being decoupled from consumption.

If I were turning that into a working draft structure, I would arrange it like this:

Section 1: EROEI as civilizational margin
Section 2: How high surplus built modern scale
Section 3: Surplus and the restructuring of labor/control
Section 4: Surplus becomes cognitive territory
Section 5: Abstraction as a luxury of buffered systems
Section 6: Net-energy decline and the squeeze on institutions
Section 7: The low-yield internet and cognitive exhaustion
Section 8: What forms of life cohere under lower surplus
Section 9: Degrowth as recalibration of sense and expectation

A few writing rules would help during revision.

First, separate hard claims from interpretive claims. Whenever you are making a quantitative or historical claim, keep the prose plain and supportable. Whenever you are synthesizing across domains, let the language rise. That alternation will make the essay feel both serious and alive.

Second, use one bridging sentence every time you move levels. From energy to infrastructure. From infrastructure to labor. From labor to cognition. From cognition to ideology. From decline to political affect. Those bridges are what will keep the essay from sounding like a brilliant series of leaps.

Third, define the cognition claim in one sentence that you can return to. Something like: “Cognition scales with energy not metaphorically but institutionally: surplus funds the time, tools, mobility, and infrastructures through which thought expands beyond immediate survival.” That can serve as the hinge of the whole piece.

Fourth, decide where you want to be maximally legible to skeptical readers. My view: the first six sections should be relatively disciplined, and only after that should the prose become more aphoristic and compressed.

A research map for drafting would probably have five folders.

One folder for EROEI and net-energy basics.
One for energy and the buildout of modern infrastructure.
One for labor, management, and coordination under fossil modernity.
One for cognition as materially scaffolded by institutions and networks.
One for platform decay, attention extraction, degrowth, care, and maintenance.

The most important conceptual correction to bake in now is this: the essay’s architecture should no longer say, “EROEI also applies to thought.” It should say, “High net energy built a civilization with unusually large cognitive range, and the degradation of surplus is now contracting that range while digital systems compensate by extracting harder for less return.”

That is a much stronger thesis.

That is the structural matrix. Under it, I would build a second layer: the actual research tasks.

For Beat 1, you want definitional sources that can anchor the whole piece. One or two rigorous overviews of EROEI, one source on net energy, and maybe one source that explains boundary disputes so you do not sound naive about the metric.

For Beat 2, you want a compact cluster of material examples that show what surplus built. It would be enough to document four domains well: agriculture, mobility, urban scale, and communications. That will give the essay breadth without turning it into a catalogue.

For Beat 3, you want sources that let you say, with care, that energy abundance increased the scale and coordination capacity of labor systems. This is where a concrete example will matter more than ten abstractions.

For Beat 4, this is the hinge research folder. You want work on distributed cognition, cognitive scaffolding, social infrastructure for knowledge, and the political economy of leisure time. This is the section that distinguishes your piece from a merely clever energy essay.

For Beat 5, the goal is not to “prove ideology from energy,” but to show that some ideologies became durable because surplus made them livable and buffered contradiction. So you want sources on development discourse, externalization, postwar abundance, and imperial subsidy.

For Beats 6 through 8, you want the contraction sequence: lower net returns, infrastructural strain, digital enclosure, lower cognitive yield. This is where the piece could become especially persuasive if it links systemic cost increases to the felt texture of everyday life.

For Beats 9 through 11, the research burden is lighter but the ethical burden is higher. You need enough specificity to avoid vague moralizing, and enough care to avoid romanticizing Indigenous or feminist frameworks as symbolic correctives.

A useful drafting tool here would be to tag each section by evidence mode. I would use four tags only: quantitative, historical, conceptual, and experiential.

Beat 1 is conceptual plus quantitative.
Beat 2 is historical plus quantitative.
Beat 3 is historical.
Beat 4 is historical plus conceptual.
Beat 5 is conceptual plus historical.
Beat 6 is quantitative plus institutional.
Beat 7 is historical plus experiential.
Beat 8 is experiential plus conceptual, lightly empirical.
Beat 9 is conceptual.
Beat 10 is conceptual plus case-based.
Beat 11 is experiential/philosophical.
Beat 12 is synthesis.

That tagging matters because it tells you where to tighten and where to let the prose breathe.

I would also add a “red flag” column for yourself while drafting. These are the sentences most likely to need hedging or evidence.

Examples:
“Early oil fields had EROEI above X.”
“Fossil energy did not replace labor.”
“Declining EROEI explains institutional brittleness.”
“The internet’s decline is a low-return energy story.”
“Indigenous epistemologies are scaled to low-yield reality.”

Each of those can stay, but each needs either evidence, narrowing language, or both.

A compact version of the matrix, as a writing workflow, would be:

First gather hard support for Beats 1, 2, and 6.
Then gather one strong historical example for Beat 3.
Then overbuild Beat 4, because that is your distinctive contribution.
Then gather just enough for Beats 7 and 8 to make the digital/cognitive turn feel grounded.
Then write Beats 9 to 12 with restraint.

The deepest research need is still Beat 4. If that section lands, the essay stops being “EROEI as a big metaphor” and becomes a materially serious theory of civilizational cognition.

# Final Beat Map with Evidence Weighting

This version treats the essay as a drafting architecture rather than a reading list. Each beat is labeled by evidence mode so you know where to stay concrete, where to synthesize, and where to keep rhetorical pressure under control.

## Evidence labels
- **Hard-evidence beat**: the core claims should be directly supported by cited scholarship or data.
- **Interpretive synthesis beat**: the section is still source-grounded, but the key move is yours.
- **Lightly evidenced synthesis beat**: the section is mostly philosophical or evaluative, supported by a smaller evidentiary base.

---

## Beat 1 — EROEI as civilizational margin
**Label:** Hard-evidence beat

### What this section does
Defines the essay’s governing lens in plain material terms.

### Core claim
EROEI is not just a technical ratio; it tracks how much usable surplus remains after the energetic costs of extraction are paid. That surplus is what funds slack, coordination, specialization, and discretionary social activity. Hall, Lambert, and Balogh explicitly connect declining EROI to shrinking discretionary surplus, while Murphy, Raugei, and Estrada stress that EROI comparisons depend heavily on system boundaries and harmonization choices.

### What to emphasize
Start with net energy, not metaphor. Only introduce “distance” after you have defined surplus materially.

### Draft hinge sentence
“The real issue is not gross energy, but how much remains after a society pays the cost of getting energy at all.”

### Tone guidance
Keep this section flat, precise, and restrained.

---

## Beat 2 — The high-surplus century
**Label:** Hard-evidence beat

### What this section does
Shows that the characteristic scale of modernity emerged under unusually high fossil-fuel surplus.

### Core claim
Twentieth-century industrial expansion took shape under historically high-return fossil systems relative to many later unconventional systems, and that surplus widened the margin for transport, industrial agriculture, large infrastructures, and long supply chains. Hall, Lambert, and Balogh support the broad decline framing, while fertilizer history supports the claim that industrial agriculture depends deeply on fossil inputs.

### What to emphasize
The point is not that fossil energy “caused everything,” but that it underwrote modern scale.

### Draft hinge sentence
“What surplus bought was not comfort alone, but reach.”

### Tone guidance
Use concrete examples. Avoid absolute formulations like “built the modern world in its entirety.”

---

## Beat 3 — Surplus and labor reorganization
**Label:** Hard-evidence beat

### What this section does
Complicates the simple story that fossil energy merely liberated people from labor.

### Core claim
Fossil-fueled modernity reorganized labor into larger, more synchronized, and more managerially coordinated systems. Chandler gives you the rise of managerial coordination as a structural feature of large-scale business, and Thompson gives you the shift toward clock-disciplined labor under industrial capitalism.

### What to emphasize
The key term here is reorganization, not denial of mechanization.

### Draft hinge sentence
“Surplus did not abolish work; it made work more scalable.”

### Tone guidance
Anchor this beat with one concrete example rather than too much abstraction.

---

## Beat 4 — Surplus becomes cognitive territory
**Label:** Hard-evidence beat

### What this section does
Establishes the essay’s most distinctive claim: cognition expands materially, institutionally, and historically under surplus conditions.

### Core claim
Cognition is not only metabolically sustained but socially and institutionally scaffolded. Gallagher argues that cognition can extend into social practices and institutions, and the twentieth-century expansion of higher education shows that organized knowledge systems scaled dramatically across the century. BLS paid-leave data also supports the narrower claim that discretionary time is institutionally allocated rather than purely personal.

### What to emphasize
This is not analogy. High surplus financed the real infrastructures of expanded thought: universities, communications networks, research systems, mobility, archives, and discretionary time.

### Draft hinge sentence
“High surplus did not merely power machines; it purchased thinking range.”

### Tone guidance
State this plainly. This is the hinge of the whole essay.

---

## Beat 5 — Abstraction as a luxury of buffered systems
**Label:** Interpretive synthesis beat

### What this section does
Explains how surplus conditions ideological style without reducing ideology to energy.

### Core claim
The issue is not that energy mechanically determines ideology, but that buffered systems make certain abstract, universalizing, and managerial ways of framing the world easier to stabilize and govern through. Scoones supports this by treating sustainability and development as political fields shaped by scarcity narratives, institutional struggle, and “regimes of truth, rule, and accumulation,” rather than as neutral technical programs.

### What to emphasize
Your real claim is about plausibility and durability, not deterministic causation.

### Draft hinge sentence
“What looked universal often rested on systems able to distance contradiction from decision.”

### Tone guidance
This is still the most interpretive beat. Keep it hedged and avoid grand monocausal language.

---

## Beat 6 — The squeeze: declining net returns
**Label:** Hard-evidence beat

### What this section does
Marks the transition from expansionary surplus to thinner margins.

### Core claim
As EROI declines, more energy output and economic activity must be diverted back into obtaining energy, reducing the surplus available for maintenance, redundancy, and discretionary activity. Hall, Lambert, and Balogh make this point directly, and Sahin et al. show that even net-zero transitions involve systemwide energetic overhead that has to be reckoned with in net-energy terms.

### What to emphasize
The question is not whether the system still runs, but how much margin remains after the cost of running it is paid.

### Draft hinge sentence
“The system continues, but on thinner margins.”

### Tone guidance
This section should be unusually plain and factual.

---

## Beat 7 — The low-yield internet
**Label:** Hard-evidence beat

### What this section does
Translates surplus logic into digital culture without reducing everything to energy alone.

### Core claim
The internet’s changing return profile is inseparable from platformization. Poell treats platforms as structural reorganizers of cultural production and circulation, and van Dijck shows that platforms now function as governance infrastructures rather than neutral intermediaries. That lets you argue that online experience degraded through changes in business model, institutional form, and infrastructural mediation, not just through “too much content.”

### What to emphasize
The internet did not simply become noisier; it became more extractive.

### Draft hinge sentence
“We are not roaming an open frontier anymore; we are working a depleted seam.”

### Tone guidance
Use “fracking for meaning” here, but only after the platform/governance setup is in place.

---

## Beat 8 — Cognitive fatigue under extraction
**Label:** Hard-evidence beat

### What this section does
Connects the digital environment to lived psychological strain.

### Core claim
Digitalized environments increase the risk of information overload, interruption, task switching, stress, and exhaustion. Arnold et al. identify information overload as exacerbated by digitalization and growing ICT use, especially in work contexts, and Behrend et al. connect enterprise social media to interruption overload, stress, overload, and exhaustion. Bayer et al. helps frame these effects ecologically rather than monocausally.

### What to emphasize
These conditions help explain cognitive fatigue; they do not explain everything.

### Draft hinge sentence
“The lights are still on, but cognition flickers.”

### Tone guidance
Use phrases like “helps explain,” “one structural driver,” and “can be understood in part as.”

---

## Beat 9 — What scales down?
**Label:** Hard-evidence beat

### What this section does
Moves from diagnosis to selection criteria for viable forms under lower surplus.

### Core claim
Lower-surplus futures favor systems organized around maintenance, repair, boundedness, and local feedback rather than expansion without consequence. Ostrom and Agrawal support the case for common-pool governance and contextual local management, Ureta shows that repair is politically constrained and limited rather than romantic, and Buser and Boyer make the bridge from maintenance to care in water infrastructure explicit.

### What to emphasize
Define the traits first, then name examples.

### Draft hinge sentence
“The question is no longer what can expand, but what can endure.”

### Tone guidance
This section should feel concrete and institutional, not merely ethical.

---

## Beat 10 — Indigenous, care, and degrowth frameworks
**Label:** Interpretive synthesis beat

### What this section does
Introduces alternative frameworks that are more coherent under constrained surplus.

### Core claim
Some Indigenous land-based practices, social-reproduction frameworks, and degrowth traditions are more compatible with lower-surplus conditions because they foreground reciprocity, maintenance, sufficiency, and bounded extraction. Brondizio et al. gives you a careful, non-romantic account of Indigenous peoples and local communities in environmental governance, Kotiswaran gives you the social-reproduction frame, and Kallis et al. defines degrowth as a political-economic reorganization aimed at lowering resource and energy throughput while preserving collective well-being.

### What to emphasize
These are not pure alternatives standing outside history. They are traditions and frameworks that preserve useful organizational logics.

### Draft hinge sentence
“The alternatives most worth studying are often those that never treated extraction as freedom.”

### Tone guidance
Use careful verbs: “some,” “often,” “can preserve,” “may be better aligned with.”

---

## Beat 11 — Degrowth as sensory recalibration
**Label:** Lightly evidenced synthesis beat

### What this section does
Translates system theory into a lived shift in evaluation, expectation, and pace.

### Core claim
A lower-surplus politics requires new evaluative metrics because GDP is an inadequate proxy for welfare, and some low-intensity environments and forms of time use support well-being and attentional restoration better than throughput-oriented life suggests. Jansen et al. and Jones and Klenow support the beyond-GDP move, while Ohly et al. provides evidence for attention restoration. Mogilner et al. and Sharif et al. help with the time-affluence side if you want it.

### What to emphasize
This is the section where you can move from systems language to sensibility, but it still needs restraint.

### Draft hinge sentence
“The future may belong less to scale than to density.”

### Tone guidance
Do not let this turn into wellness language. Keep it tied to surplus logic.

---

## Beat 12 — Conclusion
**Label:** Lightly evidenced synthesis beat

### What this section does
Reframes the whole essay as a new evaluative lens.

### Core claim
The task is no longer to preserve the fantasies of the high-surplus era, but to identify forms of life, infrastructure, and thought that remain coherent under tighter margins. The empirical foundation comes from the earlier beats; the conclusion is mainly synthesis. A beyond-GDP closing citation is optional if you want one final anchor.

### What to emphasize
Bring back the core shift: from altitude to proximity, from reach to yield, from scale to coherence.

### Draft hinge sentence
“The problem is no longer how to preserve the altitude of the surplus age, but how to live well closer to the ground.”

### Tone guidance
Use one memorable phrase and stop.
