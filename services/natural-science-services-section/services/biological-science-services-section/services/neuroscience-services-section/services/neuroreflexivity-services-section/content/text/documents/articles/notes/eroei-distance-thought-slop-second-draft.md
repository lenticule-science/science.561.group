The Invisible Architecture: EROEI as the Measure of Distance
Abstract

Energy return on energy invested, or EROEI, is usually treated as a technical metric for comparing fuels. This paper argues that it is better understood as a measure of civilizational margin: the usable surplus available after a society has paid the energetic cost of obtaining energy in the first place. That surplus does more than run machines. It finances storage, mobility, specialization, administrative overhead, redundancy, and the institutions through which cognition extends beyond subsistence and locality. Read in this way, the high-surplus fossil century appears not as the timeless baseline of modern life, but as an anomalous period in which unusually cheap energetic access subsidized modern scale, managerial coordination, and an expanded cognitive range. As easy surplus erodes and systems operate on thinner margins, the consequences appear not only in infrastructure and institutions but also in digital life, where platformized systems demand increasing attentional throughput for declining experiential and epistemic return. The paper concludes that lower-surplus futures will tend to favor maintenance, repair, boundedness, local feedback, and care-centered forms of organization over expansionary systems built on cheap distance.

1. EROEI as civilizational margin

EROEI is commonly defined as the ratio between the energy an energy-producing process delivers and the energy required to build, maintain, and operate that process. In ordinary discussion, that can sound like a narrow engineering concern. But the concept becomes far more revealing once it is moved from the level of technical comparison to the level of social consequence.

The central issue is not simply how much energy a society extracts in gross terms. The central issue is how much energy remains after the cost of extraction, processing, transport, and supporting infrastructure has been paid. That remainder is the real civilizational substance. Daniel Murphy, Marco Raugei, Matthew Carbajales-Dale, and Brenda Rubio Estrada make this methodological point in “Energy Return on Investment of Major Energy Carriers: Review and Harmonization” (2022) when they stress that EROEI comparisons depend heavily on boundaries, assumptions, and harmonization choices. Charles Hall, Jessica Lambert, and Stephen Balogh push the matter in a more openly social direction in “EROI of Different Fuels and the Implications for Society” (2014) by arguing that declining EROEI means a larger share of total social effort must be redirected toward securing energy itself.

That is why EROEI is best understood as a measure of margin. Margin is the room a society has between extraction and necessity. It is what pays for the difference between mere energetic survival and institutional complexity. When surplus is high, a society can sustain slack, redundancy, long supply chains, specialized professions, extensive training, bureaucratic layers, research systems, storage capacity, and non-immediate forms of attention. When surplus is thin, those same structures become more expensive to maintain relative to the net return they enable.

This framing clarifies a point that is often blurred. Surplus is not identical to wealth in the monetary sense, and it is not identical to technological sophistication. A society can be financially large and technically elaborate while still facing a net-energy squeeze if the energetic cost of maintaining its complexity rises faster than its usable returns. EROEI does not explain every dimension of historical change, and it should not be treated as a total theory. But it does identify a basic material question that many social theories prefer to leave implicit: how much room is left after the energy bill has been paid?

That room can be described in many ways. It is slack. It is reach. It is temporal buffer. It is the ability to store, repair, coordinate, and plan. It is the ability to keep systems functioning at a distance from the place where their costs are imposed. And in a more developed society it becomes something else as well: the ability to support forms of thought and reflection not immediately tied to subsistence. The argument of this paper begins there.

2. The high-surplus century

Once EROEI is read as a measure of civilizational margin, the twentieth century looks less like the natural horizon of human development and more like an energetically unusual interval. The buildout of industrial modernity took place under conditions of exceptionally favorable fossil access, especially during the era of conventional oil. Hall, Lambert, and Balogh argue in “EROI of Different Fuels and the Implications for Society” (2014) that the social effects of high-return fuels are profound because net surplus, rather than gross throughput alone, determines how much discretionary capacity remains available to the rest of society.

What that surplus purchased was not simply convenience. It purchased scale.

It purchased the ability to move goods across continents at prices low enough to normalize remote provisioning. It purchased refrigeration systems that broke the old tight coupling between perishability and place. It purchased aviation, suburban expansion, extensive road networks, cold chains, synthetic fertilizer, energy-intensive food processing, large building stocks, mass commuting, and a built environment whose ordinary functioning presupposed cheap energetic access. A modern city is not merely a concentration of people and buildings. It is a stabilized pattern of flows: electricity, water, food, waste removal, logistics, maintenance, signal transmission, and labor movement. High surplus made those flows affordable enough to become background conditions rather than visible achievements.

Industrial agriculture is one of the clearest examples. Modern nitrogen fertilizer is often narrated as a triumph of chemistry, which it is, but it is also a triumph of fossil throughput. The Haber-Bosch process depends on large energy inputs and, in its dominant contemporary form, on fossil-derived hydrogen feedstocks. This means that the productivity of industrial agriculture has long been inseparable from the energetic abundance of fossil systems. The point is not merely that farming uses fuel. It is that an enormous share of agricultural scale and reliability has been energetically subsidized from outside the field itself.

The same logic applies to transport and communications. Electrification and communications networks are often treated as abstract technical advances, but their social significance depended on a much broader material base: fuels, grids, extraction systems, steel, concrete, maintenance labor, and the surplus required to sustain them. Highways and airports are not just infrastructure objects. They are stored energetic commitments. Container shipping is not merely a logistics innovation. It is a system that presupposes durable energetic margins across ports, roads, warehouses, ships, cranes, insurance, customs, and data systems.

The crucial claim, then, is not that fossil energy caused modernity in some monocausal way. It is that the characteristic scale of modernity was built during a period of anomalously high surplus. Cheap energy widened the distance between what societies could do and what they had to spend, energetically, to keep doing it. That widened distance made modern scale appear normal.

3. Surplus and the reorganization of labor

The story is often told as though fossil energy simply liberated human beings from toil. This is partly true and deeply misleading. Mechanization did reduce many forms of brute exertion. But the broader historical effect of high surplus was not the disappearance of labor. It was the reorganization of labor into larger, denser, more synchronized, and more managerially legible systems.

Alfred Chandler argues in The Visible Hand: The Managerial Revolution in American Business (1977) that large-scale production and distribution required new forms of administrative coordination. What emerged was not merely “more business,” but a historically novel layer of managerial oversight whose function was to keep complex flows synchronized. E. P. Thompson, in “Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism” (1967), shows the same transformation from another angle. Industrial capitalism did not just change tools. It changed time. It replaced task-based rhythms with disciplined clock time, turning labor into something more measurable, more predictable, and more easily subordinated to centralized schedules.

These are not side effects. They are central expressions of surplus.

When a society has more net energy available, it can sustain more coordination overhead. It can carry more clerks, supervisors, logistics planners, engineers, accountants, compliance regimes, schedulers, and managers. It can support warehouses that hold inventory, transport systems that reduce local dependence, and planning layers that make far-flung operations legible to administrative centers. Surplus did not abolish work. It made work more scalable.

This is why the old contrast between “machines” and “labor” is too simple. Fossil modernity often did not replace labor so much as redistribute, intensify, abstract, and synchronize it. Some labor became less physically punishing. Other labor became more repetitive, more surveilled, and more tightly integrated into machine-paced systems. New white-collar strata emerged not outside the productive order but as organs of its coordination. The spreadsheet, the timetable, the inventory system, the traffic-control center, the managerial dashboard: these are all social forms of energetic surplus.

And because surplus allowed larger systems to run, it also allowed larger systems of discipline to run. Factories, plantations, industrial farms, ports, and later global logistics hubs did not merely consume energy. They converted energetic abundance into organizational scale and control. This matters because it links energy not only to production, but to the social architecture of command.

4. Surplus becomes cognitive territory

The central move of this paper is that cognition scales with energy not metaphorically but institutionally.

This claim requires care, because it can easily sound like a loose analogy. The point is not that “thought is energy” in some mystical or reductionist sense. The point is that thought depends on material and institutional scaffolds. Human cognition is not confined to isolated brains. It depends on schools, libraries, archives, media systems, transport networks, workplaces, laboratories, habits of note-taking, communities of interpretation, and discretionary time. Shaun Gallagher argues precisely this in “The Socially Extended Mind” (2013), where he shows that cognitive processes can be extended by social practices and institutions rather than merely supplemented by external tools.

Once that is understood, the history of surplus can be reread as a history of enlarged cognitive range.

High surplus financed education systems that kept more people in structured learning for longer periods. It financed universities, research institutes, journal systems, conferences, translation networks, and publishing infrastructures that allowed knowledge to circulate across distance. It financed telecommunications and digital networks that widened the field across which signals, texts, images, and expertise could move. It financed transport systems that allowed specialists to cluster, travel, and collaborate. It financed domestic appliances and other time-saving devices that reduced the share of daily attention consumed by household labor. It financed forms of leisure in which reflection, reading, experimentation, and encounter were not luxuries reserved only for tiny elites.

This is what it means to say that high surplus purchased thinking range. It widened the radius within which attention could travel beyond immediate survival.

Evan Schofer and John Meyer document one major part of this expansion in “The Worldwide Expansion of Higher Education in the Twentieth Century” (2005). Their work shows that higher education underwent a massive global increase in scale across the century. This is not incidental to the modern cognitive order. It is part of the infrastructure through which complex societies reproduce specialized knowledge, formal expertise, and abstract problem-solving.

The same logic applies to time. In ordinary discussion, leisure is treated as something soft, optional, or private. But discretionary time is one of the hardest infrastructures of cognition. Without time that is not fully captured by necessity, there is limited room for sustained reading, reflection, wandering attention, deep craft acquisition, or non-instrumental encounter. Paid leave statistics from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics make this visible in mundane form: time off is not a personal essence. It is institutionally distributed. It is socially provisioned margin.

To put the point sharply: modern cognition became geographically larger because energetic surplus made it institutionally larger. The modern mind is not just a brain in a skull. It is a brain moving through schools, media networks, offices, databases, transit systems, archives, and periods of discretionary time whose existence depends on a broader material order.

This is the hinge of the whole argument. Once cognition is understood as materially scaffolded, the link between net energy and social thought ceases to be a metaphor and becomes an institutional description.

5. Abstraction as a luxury of buffered systems

If high surplus expanded infrastructure, labor coordination, and cognitive range, it also altered the style of thought that became socially plausible. Buffered systems encourage abstraction.

This does not mean that energy mechanically determines ideology. That would be crude, and wrong. Ideological life is mediated by class formation, political institutions, imperial relations, cultural inheritance, and conflicts over distribution. But it is still true that high-surplus systems make certain forms of abstraction easier to inhabit. When the material contradictions required to sustain a way of life can be externalized geographically, socially, or ecologically, actors are more likely to experience their own institutions as universal, mature, or morally elevated rather than as contingent achievements built on hidden subsidy.

This is one way to understand the confidence of postwar development discourse, growth ideology, and certain forms of liberal individualism. These were never merely ideas. They were ideas rendered credible by buffered conditions. A society with enough surplus can carry large administrative systems, long supply chains, distant extraction zones, and ecological externalities without forcing ordinary decision-makers to confront those costs directly. Abstraction becomes livable because contradiction is held elsewhere.

Ian Scoones and his coauthors argue in “The Politics of Sustainability and Development” (2020) that development and sustainability should be understood not as neutral technical categories but as contested political formations shaped by institutions, narratives, and regimes of rule. That perspective helps here. The point is not that ideology floats above material life. Nor is it that ideology can be reduced to fuel quality. The point is that buffered systems make some ideological styles more durable by spacing decision from consequence.

High surplus societies can therefore mistake surplus-enabled distance for civilizational depth. They can read reach as maturity, scale as rationality, and abstraction as moral progress. They can believe that what is in fact subsidy is simply normality. They can universalize what is really an energetically exceptional way of organizing life.

What looked like elevation was often buffered distance.

6. Declining net energy and thinner margins

The downward turn is best described without melodrama. The issue is not whether systems continue to function. The issue is how much margin remains once the energetic cost of keeping them functional has been paid.

Hall, Lambert, and Balogh make the central point in “EROI of Different Fuels and the Implications for Society” (2014): declining EROEI means that more of society’s energy and economic activity must be devoted to the energy sector itself, leaving less discretionary surplus for all other sectors. Daniel Murphy, Marco Raugei, Matthew Carbajales-Dale, and Brenda Rubio Estrada reiterate in “Energy Return on Investment of Major Energy Carriers: Review and Harmonization” (2022) that measurement is methodologically complex, but the broader issue remains the same. Gross output is an inadequate guide to social capacity if the energetic cost of maintaining that output is rising.

This is why the effects of declining surplus are usually not immediate collapse. They are squeeze effects.

Repairs are deferred. Redundancies are reduced. Spare capacity shrinks. Systems that once had slack begin operating closer to their limits. Institutions become more brittle because they have less room to absorb shocks. Staffing gets tighter. Maintenance becomes less glamorous and more frequently postponed. Public systems are expected to preserve old outputs while operating under increasingly thin margins. The system continues, but it continues with less room for error.

This framing also matters for the energy transition. A serious net-energy perspective does not imply nostalgia for fossil fuels. It implies analytical sobriety about the material overhead of transition itself. H. Sahin and collaborators argue in “Systemwide Energy Return on Investment in a Sustainable Transition towards Net Zero Power Systems” (2024) that transitions should be evaluated in systemwide net-energy terms. This matters because replacing one energetic regime with another is not just a matter of swapping generators. It requires transmission systems, storage, balancing, construction, extraction of materials, and enabling infrastructures that all impose real energetic costs.

The question, then, is not simply whether a lower-carbon system can be built. It is also what kind of surplus remains available while and after it is built. That is a harder question, but it is the right one.

7. The low-yield internet

The digital sphere is where this larger contraction becomes especially legible because digital life presents itself as weightless while resting on dense material infrastructures and increasingly extractive institutional logics.

Early digital culture emerged in a context of relatively generous surplus. That does not mean it was utopian, but it did mean the online environment could support more open-ended forms of exploration. Personal sites, forums, niche archives, long-form blogs, amateur scholarship, idiosyncratic directories, and hobbyist communities flourished in a setting where not every interaction had yet been optimized for capture. Discovery often felt high-yield. A modest amount of time online could produce real encounter, deep novelty, and durable learning.

That changed with platformization.

Thomas Poell argues in “Three Challenges for Media Studies in the Age of Platforms” (2020) that platforms do not merely host content; they restructure how content is produced, distributed, and monetized. José van Dijck extends this in “Governing Platforms and Societies” (2024) by treating platforms as governance infrastructures rather than neutral intermediaries. These are decisive shifts. Once the digital sphere is reorganized around platform logics, its basic function changes. The system no longer exists primarily to facilitate exploration. It exists to maximize engagement, circulation, and monetizable attention.

This is why the online environment now so often feels low-yield. The problem is not simply “too much information.” The problem is a throughput regime. Users are routed through systems designed to keep them moving, reacting, and remaining available for capture. Recommendation engines, ad-tech incentives, industrial content production, and platform competition all push toward higher volume and faster circulation. The result is an environment in which informational abundance often coincides with experiential depletion.

“Fracking for meaning” is a useful phrase because it names a specific structure: increasing effort applied to a field whose easy returns have already been harvested. More clicks, more content, more streams, more AI-generated text, more aggregated commentary, more endless feed. And yet, for many users, less durable comprehension, less surprise, less depth, less orientation.

The internet did not simply become noisy. It became extractive.

8. Cognitive fatigue under extraction

The subjective consequences of this shift are now widespread enough to feel ordinary: doomscrolling, flattening attention, compulsive checking, diffuse agitation, and the sense that enormous informational throughput yields very little that settles into understanding.

Research on information overload helps ground this perception. Miriam Arnold, Mascha Goldschmitt, and Thomas Rigotti argue in “Dealing with Information Overload: A Comprehensive Review” (2023) that digitalization and expanding information and communication technologies intensify overload in work and organizational settings. Tara Behrend and her coauthors, in “Implications of Social Media for a Changing Work Landscape” (2024), show that social media’s incorporation into work environments changes expectations, interruptions, boundary conditions, and demands. These literatures do not justify a total explanation of exhaustion, burnout, or polarization. But they do support a structural claim: many contemporary environments demand rising cognitive input for uncertain return.

That distinction matters. Fatigue in such contexts is often psychologized as an individual failure of discipline. But a throughput environment systematically generates interruption, fragmentation, attentional switching, and low-grade emotional taxation. Under those conditions, the private feeling of not being able to think clearly may have a public infrastructure.

This is where the broader argument returns. The digital environment is not floating free from material history. It is one site where thinning surplus and extractive optimization meet. As civilizational margins tighten, institutions seek to preserve output by intensifying capture. The result is a world in which the cost of attention rises while the quality of return falls.

The lights are still on, but cognition flickers.

9. What forms remain viable under lower surplus?

Once diagnosis is clear, the strategic question changes. The issue is no longer how to keep extending the expansionary logic of the high-surplus era. The issue is what kinds of systems remain coherent under thinner margins.

Before naming particular traditions or preferences, it helps to define the traits of low-surplus coherence.

Such systems tend to privilege maintenance over expansion. They value repair over replacement. They keep feedback loops shorter, so that consequence remains legible to use. They prefer boundedness to indefinite externalization. They rely more on local knowledge and less on very long opaque chains of command or supply. They aim for resilience and sufficiency rather than maximal throughput.

The literature on commons governance is useful here because it gives these intuitions institutional form. Elinor Ostrom argues in “Coping with Tragedies of the Commons” (1999) that common-pool resources are not doomed to failure simply because they are shared. Under specific conditions, users can develop workable rules, monitoring practices, sanctions, and norms for sustainable governance. Arun Agrawal, in “Sustainable Governance of Common-Pool Resources” (2003), extends and qualifies this tradition by emphasizing context, politics, and the complexity of governance arrangements. The common thread is not romantic localism. It is the value of systems in which monitoring, use, and consequence remain tightly related.

Repair and maintenance studies deepen this point. Javier Ureta argues in “On the Challenges (and Limits) of Repairing Infrastructures” (2014) that repair is neither simple nor heroic. It is constrained, uneven, and political. Repair often normalizes fragility rather than abolishing it. That is important because it keeps the low-surplus discussion realistic. Maintenance is not salvation. It is the difficult work of holding systems together when replacement and expansion become more expensive.

Michael Buser and Kate Boyer add another crucial layer in “Care Goes Underground: Thinking through Relations of Care in the Maintenance and Repair of Urban Water Infrastructures” (2021). Their argument shows that maintenance is not only technical. It is also care. Keeping water systems functional is not just a matter of pipes and budgets. It is a matter of sustaining the material conditions of collective life. That linkage matters well beyond water. Under lower surplus, maintenance work becomes more visible as the hidden substrate of social continuity.

The question is no longer what can expand furthest. It is what can endure.

10. Indigenous, care, and degrowth frameworks

Only after those criteria are clear does it make sense to ask which traditions and frameworks already organize themselves around them.

Some Indigenous land-based practices, some feminist and social-reproduction approaches to care, and some degrowth traditions matter here not because they are morally fashionable but because they often preserve organizational logics better suited to bounded material life. Reciprocity, stewardship, sufficiency, attentiveness to feedback, and reluctance toward endless extraction are not decorative values under lower-surplus conditions. They are practical virtues.

Eduardo Brondizio and his coauthors argue in “Locally Based, Regionally Manifested, and Globally Relevant: Indigenous and Local Knowledge, Values, and Practices for Nature” (2021) that Indigenous peoples and local communities preserve knowledge systems and governance practices of major importance for social-environmental management. The point is not to romanticize such systems or erase their internal diversity. It is to note that some of them retain closer couplings among land, use, memory, and consequence than high-surplus modern systems typically do.

Feminist and social-reproduction traditions matter for a parallel reason. They center the labor of maintaining life rather than the spectacle of expansion. That includes care work, household reproduction, support labor, and the forms of social sustenance without which no productive order could continue. Even when such traditions are not explicitly framed in energy terms, they often begin from a truth that the surplus age tried to obscure: maintenance is prior to growth because life must be reproduced before it can be intensified.

Degrowth gives this a macro-political language. Giorgos Kallis and his coauthors define the field in “Research on Degrowth” (2018) as a political-economic reorganization aimed at reducing resource and energy use while preserving or improving the conditions for collective well-being. Properly understood, degrowth is not simple contraction and not involuntary recession. It is a critique of the demand that every social good be subordinated to the expansion of throughput.

These traditions are not identical, and they should not be forced into a synthetic purity. But they do converge on a crucial point: a viable order cannot be built on the permanent assumption that extraction can intensify without consequence. What makes them newly legible is not fashion. It is the return of material constraint.

11. Degrowth as recalibration of perception

The adjustment required by lower surplus is not only institutional. It is also perceptual.

A civilization habituated to high surplus learns to evaluate life in terms of speed, scale, range, and convenience. It learns to mistake expansion for value. It learns to read intensity as richness. Under tighter margins, those evaluative habits become maladaptive. The problem is not simply that they are ecologically costly. It is that they often mismeasure what is actually worth having.

This is where the discussion moves from systems to sensibility. A lower-surplus politics needs different metrics of the good. A. Jansen and collaborators argue in “Beyond GDP: A Review and Conceptual Framework for Measuring Sustainable and Inclusive Wellbeing” (2024) that GDP is an inadequate proxy for human flourishing and that broader frameworks are needed to evaluate collective well-being. That point matters because it opens space for a different civilizational calculus.

Once value is no longer reduced to throughput, some goods appear in a new light. Friendship is high-yield. Proximity is high-yield. Repair is high-yield. Shared competence is high-yield. Silence can be high-yield. So can boredom, slowness, and time not already colonized by alerts and optimization. These things are not “free” in an absolute sense; they still depend on institutions, safety, and material support. But they often require far less energetic intensity than the systems that taught modern subjects to overlook them.

Attention restoration research offers a modest empirical foothold here. H. Ohly and coauthors argue in “Attention Restoration Theory: A Systematic Review of the Attention Restoration Potential of Exposure to Natural Environments” (2016) that exposure to certain environments can aid attentional recovery. This does not prove a general politics of slowness, but it does support a limited and important claim: low-intensity environments can sometimes produce disproportionately valuable cognitive returns.

The deeper point is evaluative. The future may reward lower-input, higher-yield forms of life more than the surplus age ever learned to notice. Under those conditions, density matters more than scale. Nearness matters more than reach. Return matters more than spectacle.

12. Conclusion

EROEI should not be treated as a master key, and this paper has not argued that it explains everything. The stronger and narrower claim is that high net energy built a civilization with unusually large material and cognitive range, and that the erosion of easy surplus is now narrowing that range while digital systems compensate by extracting harder for less return.

That claim helps reinterpret the modern period. The age of extraordinary surplus did not merely run factories and vehicles. It financed long supply chains, managerial hierarchies, institutional specialization, expanded education, broad communications systems, and the discretionary time through which cognition could extend beyond subsistence. It also supported ideological styles suited to buffered worlds: abstraction, confidence, universalization, and the assumption that scale itself signified maturity.

As that surplus erodes, the effects appear as a squeeze rather than a singular crash. Systems remain operational, but on thinner margins. Maintenance becomes harder. Redundancy becomes more expensive. Institutions become more brittle. The internet becomes an especially vivid theater of this process because it combines heavy infrastructure with low experiential yield, turning attention into a site of intensified extraction.

What follows is not a single political program. It is a selection criterion. Under lower-surplus conditions, the forms of life and system design most likely to remain coherent will be those organized around maintenance, repair, boundedness, reciprocity, and local feedback. They will be less committed to endless reach and more committed to durable return.

The task, then, is not to preserve the fantasies of the high-surplus age. It is to learn what still yields dignity, relation, and thought under tighter margins.

The problem is no longer how to stay at altitude. It is how to live well closer to the ground.
