# Plan: Extending the EROEI-Cognition Coupling Through Deep History

## Why this matters for the argument

The current paper starts at fossil fuels. This makes the coupling look like a modern phenomenon — an artifact of the oil age. But the data says it's been operating across every major energy transition in human history. Extending the history does three things:

1. **Strengthens the coupling hypothesis** by showing it operates across multiple independent energy transitions, not just one. If the same pattern repeats (energy surplus → cognitive infrastructure → reflexive depth), the argument stops looking like a one-time correlation and starts looking like a law.

2. **Reveals a missing variable: extractability.** Agriculture had LOW per-capita EROEI (~1.1-1.6, worse than foraging) but HIGH extractability — grain is visible, storable, taxable. The surplus that funded scribes and writing was not per-capita surplus but *state-extracted* surplus. This distinction matters for the modern argument: digital platforms extract cognitive surplus even when they don't produce it. Extractability is the mechanism by which surplus gets converted into scaffolding — AND the mechanism by which it gets captured.

3. **Puts the current crisis in deep perspective.** This is not the first time EROEI has declined and cognitive infrastructure has been threatened (Rome's decline, England's timber crisis). But it may be the first time the compensatory infrastructure is actively *inverting* reflexivity rather than just failing to support it. That's what makes Regime III new in human history, not the decline itself.

## The deep history in five transitions

### Transition 1: Fire → Cooking (~1 Mya - 300 kya)

**The energy shift:** Raw food → cooked food. Cooking increases caloric availability by 12-50% depending on the food (starch gelatinization, protein denaturation, reduced cost of digestion). Effectively raises the EROEI of the food system.

**The cognitive consequence:** The human brain consumes ~25% of the body's total energy — by far the most metabolically expensive organ per unit mass. A larger brain requires a higher caloric base. Wrangham (2009) argues cooking made it possible to sustain the brain that makes reflexive thought possible at all.

**What the data actually says:** Brain expansion across Homo follows a roughly linear trajectory over ~2 million years (600 cc → 1400 cc). Secure archaeological evidence for controlled fire begins at Wonderwerk Cave (~1 Mya) and becomes widespread only by ~400-300 kya. Large-brained H. erectus specimens exist from periods with no evidence of fire control.

**Honest assessment:** The coupling at this level is biologically plausible (the brain's energy demands need explaining, and cooking is the best candidate) but the timeline is messy. Cooking likely *enabled and maintained* brain expansion rather than *triggered* it. The paper should present this as: the very first energy transition — from raw to cooked food — established the metabolic base that makes reflexive cognition biologically possible. But the evidence for clean causation is contested.

**Sources:** Wrangham 2009, Carmody & Wrangham 2009, Wonderwerk Cave (Berna et al. 2012), Gesher Benot Ya'aqov (Goren-Inbar et al. 2004), PMC 2016 critique of fire-brain timing

### Transition 2: Foraging → Agriculture (~10 kya)

**The energy shift:** This is the counterintuitive case. Agriculture was NOT a per-capita surplus gain. Foragers often had better nutrition, more leisure, and less disease. Agricultural EROEI was ~1.1-1.6 depending on place and time (vs. significantly higher returns for foraging). Cohen & Armelagos (1984) documented health deterioration in 19 of 21 societies undergoing the transition. Stature declined. Disease increased.

**So how did agriculture produce cognitive infrastructure?** Not through per-capita surplus but through **extractable surplus**. James C. Scott (2017): "Only the cereal grains can serve as a basis for taxation." Grain is visible, countable, storable, divisible, and transportable. States could extract it. The surplus that funded scribes, priests, and administrators was not voluntary — it was coerced from farmers who were individually worse off.

**The cognitive consequence:** Writing literally emerged from grain accounting. The earliest cuneiform (~3000 BC) consists of pictographs of grain heads, inventories, and transactions. Writing was used exclusively for accounting until it expanded to other purposes centuries later. The "division between manual and intellectual labor" — the first cognitive specialist class — was funded by grain taxation.

**What this tells us about the coupling:** The coupling is not just EROEI → surplus → scaffolding. It's EROEI → *extractable* surplus → scaffolding. A society can have high per-capita EROEI (foragers) and no cognitive infrastructure, or low per-capita EROEI (farmers) and writing, mathematics, and specialist knowledge classes — if the surplus is extractable. **Extractability is the mediating variable the current paper is missing.**

This also reveals the dark side of the coupling from the very beginning: the scaffolding for cognitive infrastructure was built on coerced labor and reduced well-being for the majority. Scribes existed because farmers were taxed. The relationship between energy surplus and cognitive infrastructure has always passed through power, extraction, and inequality.

**Sources:** Scott 2017 (*Against the Grain*), Cohen & Armelagos 1984, PNAS 2022 on Neolithic stature, Schmandt-Besserat on token systems and early writing, Resilience.org on pre-industrial agricultural EROEI

### Transition 3: The 6,000-year plateau (5000 BC - 1700 AD)

**The energy shift:** Essentially none at the per-capita level. Ian Morris's data: energy capture rose from ~17,000 kcal/cap/day (2000 BC) to ~31,000 kcal/cap/day (Roman peak) and then barely moved until ~1700 (still ~30,000 kcal/cap/day). The same basic energy converters — human muscle, animal muscle, wood/charcoal, water, wind — for six millennia. Malanima: "The energy system of the ancient Mediterranean civilisations was the same as that of all agrarian societies between the 5th millennium B.C. and the 19th century."

**Cognitive infrastructure during the plateau:** Libraries (Alexandria, Pergamon, imperial Chinese collections), philosophy (Athens, the Islamic Golden Age, medieval scholasticism), legal codes, schools, translation movements. These were real. But they were funded by *scale* (more people contributing to surplus) and *extraction efficiency* (better state apparatus), not by per-capita EROEI gains. The Roman Empire's cognitive achievements were the product of extracting surplus from millions of farmers and enslaved people, concentrated through an administrative apparatus, and allocated to specialist classes.

**What this tells us about the coupling:** When per-capita EROEI is flat, cognitive infrastructure can still grow — but only through better extraction from a larger population. This has a ceiling. And when the extraction apparatus collapses (fall of Rome, various dark ages), the cognitive infrastructure collapses with it — libraries burned, literacy rates crashed, specialist knowledge lost. Tainter (1988): "Societies collapse when their investments in social complexity and their energy subsidies reach a point of diminishing marginal returns."

**The honest gap:** No EROEI estimates exist for ancient energy converters as discrete systems. We have per-capita consumption (Morris) but not energy *returns* in the EROEI sense.

**Sources:** Morris 2010, 2013, 2015; Malanima on Roman energy; Tainter 1988; Library of Alexandria funding via Ptolemaic agricultural surplus

### Transition 4: Wood → Coal (1500-1800)

**The energy shift:** England's timber crisis. Population doubled (16th century), timber demand for building, shipbuilding, smelting, and domestic fuel exceeded supply. Elizabeth I's Parliament introduced 30+ bills to address the fuel crisis. By the 1660s-1670s, coal supply surpassed firewood. By 1800, coal provided energy equivalent to 11 million acres of woodland — over a third of England's total land area.

**The cognitive consequence:** Coal surplus directly funded the infrastructure of mass cognition. Steam-powered printing (1814: The Times at 5,000 copies/hour, circulation rose from 5,000 to 50,000). Compulsory education (Prussia 1760s-80s, Britain 1870). Commercial lending libraries → free public libraries. Mass literacy rose "dramatically" in the late 18th and early 19th centuries. The postal system, newspapers, the novel as a form, public lectures, mechanics' institutes — all funded by coal surplus.

**What this tells us about the coupling:** The wood → coal transition is the clearest historical example of an EROEI crisis resolved by a new energy source, with cognitive infrastructure expanding immediately after. It also shows that the coupling operates on transition timescales: the timber crisis was ~150-200 years, and England's intellectual life (Shakespeare, Bacon, Newton, the Royal Society) flourished during exactly the period of crisis — suggesting that institutional inertia carried cognitive infrastructure through the transition. **Scaffolding degrades slower than surplus, because buildings and curricula persist.** This matches Prediction 5 in the outline (temporal lag).

**Sources:** Parliamentary records on Elizabethan timber crisis, Cambridge Population Group on coal transition, history of printing and mass literacy, Hall & Klitgaard on EROEI thresholds

---

### Transition 5: Coal → Oil → Decline (1900-present)

**The energy shift:** The paper's existing territory. Oil peaked at ~100:1 EROEI. Morris: per-capita energy went from 92,000 to 230,000 kcal/day in the 20th century alone — more than doubling in one century after being nearly flat for six millennia. Now declining: global fossil fuels at useful stage ~3.5:1 (Brockway et al. 2024).

**The cognitive consequence:** Universal education, research universities, mass media, the internet, the 20th century cognitive expansion documented in the Flynn effect. And now: the reversal.

**What's new about this transition:** Every previous EROEI decline was resolved by a transition to a new, higher-EROEI energy source (wood → coal, coal → oil). The current decline has no obvious successor at comparable EROEI. Renewables are real but their systemwide EROEI (Capellan-Perez et al. 2024) does not reproduce the ~100:1 surplus of peak oil. AND — uniquely in human history — the compensatory infrastructure (digital platforms) is not just failing to scaffold reflexivity but actively extracting it. Previous transitions had no equivalent of Regime III. The cognitive infrastructure of previous eras might degrade and collapse (fall of Rome, dark ages), but it was never actively inverted by a system designed to capture the very capacity it was supposed to support.

## What this means for the paper's structure

The paper currently has Steps 1-10 in the outline. The deep history should enter as a new step between Step 5 (the coupling) and Step 6 (three regimes). Call it **Step 5b: Historical evidence for the coupling**.

It serves two purposes:

1. **Inductive support.** If the coupling operated across fire → cooking → brain expansion, agriculture → grain taxation → writing, coal → mass literacy, AND oil → 20th century cognitive expansion, then the claim that it also operates in the present (and in reverse) is much stronger than if it's only argued from the fossil era.

2. **Introduces extractability as a variable.** The agricultural transition shows that EROEI alone is insufficient — you need *extractable* surplus. This refines the coupling hypothesis: EROEI constrains total surplus; extractability determines how much of that surplus reaches cognitive infrastructure; and the extracting agent (state, market, platform) shapes what KIND of cognitive infrastructure gets built. In the agricultural case, the extracting agent was the state, and the scaffolding it built served state purposes (accounting, law, administration). In the present case, the extracting agent is the platform, and the "scaffolding" it builds serves platform purposes (engagement, capture, monetization) — which is why it doesn't scaffold reflexivity.

3. **Makes Regime III historically specific.** Previous EROEI declines led to Regime II (scaffolding degradation, complexity collapse — Rome, dark ages). Regime III is new. The difference is the compensatory infrastructure. Rome didn't have an equivalent of digital platforms actively harvesting cognitive capacity during its decline. The collapses of the past were failures of support. The current crisis is an active inversion.

## Honest gaps to flag in the paper

- Fire-brain timeline doesn't line up cleanly. Present as "metabolic enabling condition" not "direct cause."
- No EROEI estimates for ancient energy converters. Use Morris's per-capita energy capture data instead.
- No scholar has measured institutional complexity against wood EROEI decline in pre-coal Europe. The coal transition may have been too fast for a clean test.
- The full EROEI-cognition argument across deep history has NOT been published as a unified thesis. The pieces are all there (White, Cottrell, Smil, Morris, Tainter, Wrangham, Hagens). Nobody has assembled them. This paper would be the first.

## Key scholars for the deep history

- **Leslie White** (1943): White's Law. Culture evolves with energy harnessed per capita. The foundational statement.
- **Fred Cottrell** (1955): *Energy and Society*. First book-length treatment of energy-culture coupling.
- **Vaclav Smil** (2017): *Energy and Civilization*. Most comprehensive empirical treatment across 10,000 years.
- **Ian Morris** (2010, 2013, 2015): Energy capture index from 14,000 BC to present. The best quantitative dataset.
- **Joseph Tainter** (1988): Complexity collapses when energy returns diminish. The decline side of the coupling.
- **Richard Wrangham** (2009): Cooking hypothesis. Extends the coupling to biology.
- **James C. Scott** (2017): *Against the Grain*. Reveals extractability as the key variable in agricultural surplus.
- **Nate Hagens**: Synthesizes Hall, Tainter, and ecological economics. Closest to the full argument.
- **David LePoire** (Argonne National Lab): Energy transitions as complexity thresholds across deep history.
