Tonic immobility is an involuntary, reversible state of profound motor inhibition — muscular rigidity, analgesia, and markedly reduced responsiveness — first characterized in animals as elicited by physical restraint combined with inescapable threat. The foundational animal literature is Gallup's 1977 special issue treatment in *The Psychological Record*; that source's journal, volume, and page range are corroborated by later citations (including a 2025 review by Woodruff), but its primary abstract text was not independently verified here, since the issue predates consistent PubMed indexing.

In humans, tonic immobility has been operationalized entirely through retrospective self-report scales, not through laboratory-induced or physiologically measured immobility. Fusé and colleagues (2007, *Journal of Anxiety Disorders*) developed and validated the Tonic Immobility Scale (TIS) across two samples of female sexual-assault survivors — an exploratory factor analysis (N=88) and a confirmatory factor analysis on an independent sample (N=191) — finding that "the TIS is comprised of two independent factors: physical immobility and fear." Abrams and colleagues (2009, *Depression and Anxiety*), using a new Tonic Immobility Questionnaire (TIQ) in 78 undergraduate students who reported a TI experience during a traumatic event, found a three-factor solution (physical immobility, fear, dissociation) in which "peritraumatic dissociation scores alone accounted for 51% of the variance in TI scores," with no difference in TI frequency or severity across trauma types; the authors conclude that "TI may represent an extreme behavioral expression of trauma-induced peritraumatic dissociation." Bovin and colleagues (2008, *Journal of Traumatic Stress*), in a cross-sectional questionnaire study of 176 female undergraduates with a sexual-assault history, found that "tonic immobility fully mediated relations between perceived inescapability and overall PTSD symptom severity, as well as reexperiencing and avoidance/numbing symptom clusters," concluding that "tonic immobility could be one path through which trauma survivors develop PTSD symptoms."

All three human studies are retrospective self-report questionnaire studies in convenience or self-selected samples — undergraduates, self-selected sexual-assault survivors — not laboratory-induced or physiologically measured TI, and not longitudinal designs that establish causal direction: the mediation models reported are cross-sectional statistical mediation, not causal proof. None of them measure a switch out of immobility into an activated defensive or aggressive state; they characterize the immobility endpoint itself and its correlation with later distress. None involve mood-disorder populations — the convenience and self-selected trauma samples reviewed here are not equivalent to a clinical depression or bipolar population.

Tonic immobility should be distinguished from the dorsal vagal state described in polyvagal-theory accounts of parasympathetic collapse. Both name an immobility endpoint, but they come from separate evidence bases and separate anatomical claims: tonic immobility is defined and measured through the rodent predator-defense ethology tradition (Gallup) and human trauma self-report psychometrics (TIS, TIQ) reviewed above, while the dorsal vagal state rests on Porges's contested claim of an anatomically distinct dorsal vagal branch of the parasympathetic nervous system. Neither literature reviewed here establishes that the two name the same underlying mechanism, and a clinician or researcher should not assume equivalence merely because both describe immobility.

The consequence for clinical assessment and research design is that, because human tonic immobility is measured through retrospective self-report, any claim about a switch out of immobility into active defense rests on scales that capture the immobility endpoint and its correlation with distress or PTSD severity after the fact, not a measured behavioral or physiological transition. A design intended to study the transition itself — rather than its retrospective correlates — would require prospective, in-the-moment physiological or behavioral measurement, and cross-sectional mediation results should not be read as establishing which variable causes which.
