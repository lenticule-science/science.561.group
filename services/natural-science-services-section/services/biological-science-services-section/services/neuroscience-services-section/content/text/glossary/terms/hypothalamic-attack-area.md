The hypothalamic attack area is the ventrolateral subdivision of the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMHvl) in mice, identified by Lin, Boyle, Dollar, Lee, Lein, Perona, and Anderson (2011, *Nature*) as a locus whose activity is bidirectionally, causally linked to inter-male attack behavior. Optogenetic — but not electrical — stimulation of VMHvl neurons causes male mice to attack females and inanimate objects as well as males, and pharmacogenetic silencing of VMHvl reversibly inhibits inter-male aggression. This bidirectional design, activation causing attack and silencing blocking it, is specifically what licenses a causal rather than merely correlational claim about this node, confirmed against the paper's own PubMed abstract.

Immediate-early-gene mapping and single-unit recording during social interaction, reported in the same study, reveal overlapping but distinct neuronal subpopulations for fighting and mating within VMHvl, with attack-active neurons suppressed during mating. The authors frame this as a substrate for competition between the two behaviors — an opponent-process account — rather than as one shared circuit explaining a general overlap between sex and aggression. Exact quantitative results from this study — the number of mice per condition, attack-latency statistics, and the percentage of animals attacking — are not given in the paper's own abstract and remain unverified at the level of exact numbers.

The hypothalamic attack area is categorically distinct from the periaqueductal gray's columnar organization of defensive output. VMHvl is a hypothalamic node whose rodent-causal role is to drive offensive attack initiated toward a conspecific or object; the periaqueductal gray's dorsolateral, lateral, and ventrolateral columns instead organize an animal's defensive response — active escape or flight versus immobility — to an external threat. Both are subcortical nodes with rodent bidirectional causal evidence for high-arousal behavior, but one produces initiated attack and the other organizes defense against attack, and the studies establishing each used different manipulations (optogenetic activation and pharmacogenetic silencing of VMHvl, versus kainic-acid microinjection and electrical or chemical stimulation of PAG columns).

This is causal evidence in mice only. It licenses a mechanistic claim about a specific hypothalamic node driving stereotyped attack behavior in rodents; it does not license a claim that any homologous human hypothalamic circuit "causes rage," since no equivalent causal — optogenetic or lesion — manipulation exists, and could not ethically exist, in humans. Any bridge from VMHvl to a claimed human "rage switch" is extrapolation, not evidence.

The consequence for research design is that because the only causal evidence for this node comes from rodent optogenetic and pharmacogenetic manipulation — an intervention class that cannot ethically be performed in humans — any human study invoking a hypothalamic attack area can at most report correlational imaging or lesion-case evidence, which is a categorically weaker evidential class than the bidirectional rodent design described here. A report on human data should state that distinction explicitly rather than importing the rodent causal language of triggering or necessity into a description of a human correlational finding.

## Related terms

- Periaqueductal gray — the midbrain structure organizing defensive rather than offensive-attack output, established by a separate rodent stimulation literature
- Circa-strike defense — the active-defense mode organized by PAG columns, distinct from this hypothalamic attack-initiation node
