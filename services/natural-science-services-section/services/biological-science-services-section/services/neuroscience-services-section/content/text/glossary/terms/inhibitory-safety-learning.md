Inhibitory safety learning is the associative-learning process by which an organism learns that a specific cue or context predicts the non-occurrence of an expected aversive outcome, forming a new inhibitory association that competes with, rather than erases, the original fear association.

The concept has its roots in the classical extinction-learning tradition and was given its influential clinical formulation by Michelle Craske and colleagues as the "inhibitory learning approach" to exposure therapy. This approach was framed explicitly against an older habituation model, in which repeated exposure to a feared stimulus was thought to work by simply reducing fear response through familiarity. Instead, the inhibitory learning account holds that exposure works by maximizing violation of the person's negative expectancy — arranging exposures so that the feared outcome is clearly disconfirmed — and by building a new, safety-based association strong and flexible enough to compete successfully with the old threat association at the moment of retrieval. Practical strategies derived from this model include removing safety behaviors that would otherwise prevent full expectancy violation, deepening extinction by combining multiple feared cues, and deliberately varying the contexts of exposure to promote generalization; a 2022 update reframed the approach around inhibitory retrieval specifically, organizing strategies by whether they promote new learning, generalize it, or aid its retrieval later.

A key and clinically important limitation, established in the broader extinction literature associated with Mark Bouton's work, is that inhibitory safety associations are strongly context-dependent: fear that has been successfully extinguished in one context can return when the person encounters the original threat cue in a new context (the renewal effect), even though nothing has changed in what the person explicitly believes. This means safety, once learned, is not a permanent erasure of the underlying fear memory but a competing, context-sensitive association that must be actively generalized to hold up outside the setting in which it was built.

For the relational framework, inhibitory safety learning is significant because it shows that the resolution of fear is itself a manufactured, maintained relation rather than a subtraction from a stock. Safety is not the removal of a threat association's contents; it is a new relation — between a cue and non-occurrence — built alongside the old one and capable of losing its hold when the context that supports it changes. This is structurally close to the standing/spotlight distinction used elsewhere in the relational framework for grace: safety, like grace-as-standing, must be actively constructed and generalized rather than simply checked once, and it can lapse when the structure that sustains it is not carried into a new context.
