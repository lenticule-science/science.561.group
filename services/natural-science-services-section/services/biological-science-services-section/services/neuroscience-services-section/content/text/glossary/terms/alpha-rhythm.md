The alpha rhythm is an 8–12 Hz [oscillation](neural-oscillation.md), largest over the occipital lobes at the back of the head. It is strongest when a person is awake, relaxed and has their eyes closed. It was the first human brain rhythm recorded: Hans Berger reported it in 1929, which is why it is sometimes called Berger's wave [@berger1929].

Opening the eyes or starting a demanding task suppresses it, and so does anxious arousal. It is now understood less as idling than as active gating: alpha tends to rise over brain regions whose input is being held back [@buzsaki2006].

Because it rises with relaxed wakefulness, alpha has long served as a marker of calm. Increased alpha is reported in meditation and slow breathing practices, including forms of [qigong](../../../../../medical-science-services-section/services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/qigong.md). It was also the original hypothesis behind [cranial electrotherapy stimulation](../../../../../medical-science-services-section/services/medical-technology-services-section/services/electrotherapy-devices-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/cranial-electrotherapy-stimulation.md), whose makers claimed their devices shift the EEG toward alpha. An alpha increase shows a change of state; it is not by itself evidence that anxiety or insomnia improved.
