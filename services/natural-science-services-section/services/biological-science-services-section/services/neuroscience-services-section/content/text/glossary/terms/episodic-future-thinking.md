---
name: "episodic future thinking"
description: "The capacity to mentally simulate specific personal future events by recombining details from past experience, understood via Schacter and Addis's constructive episodic simulation hypothesis to share its core machinery with episodic memory."
date-created: 2026-08-26
authors:

---

Episodic future thinking is the capacity to mentally simulate specific, personal future events by flexibly recombining details drawn from past experience. It is the future-facing counterpart of episodic memory, and the two are now understood to share much of the same underlying cognitive and neural machinery rather than being separate faculties.

The concept builds on Endel Tulving's earlier proposal that episodic memory depends on a distinctive form of self-knowing consciousness — "mental time travel" — that allows a person to re-experience the past subjectively rather than merely knowing facts about it. Daniel Schacter and Donna Rose Addis extended this insight into prospection with the constructive episodic simulation hypothesis, arguing that imagining the future draws on the same constructive, recombinatorial memory system used to reconstruct the past, which is why both remembering and imagining are error-prone and detail-flexible in similar ways, and why damage to episodic memory tends to impair future simulation as well. R. Nathan Spreng and colleagues mapped this shared process onto the brain's default mode network — medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate and precuneus, angular gyrus, and medial temporal lobe structures — establishing it as the common core network recruited by both remembering and future-oriented simulation.

More recent work has shown this network is not perfectly undifferentiated: it fractionates into a subsystem that tracks how vivid and detailed a simulated event is, largely independent of its emotional tone, and a separate subsystem, including ventromedial prefrontal cortex, that tracks the value and emotional valence of what is imagined. This refinement matters directly for understanding hope and fear as forms of future thinking: individual-differences research has found that anxiety is not generally associated with reduced vividness or specificity of imagined future events, but is associated with more detailed negative-future simulation and less detailed positive-future simulation, suggesting a shift in valence allocation rather than a change in overall simulation capacity.

For the relational framework, episodic future thinking shows that hope and fear are not distinct faculties but the same relational simulation machinery run under different valence weightings. The future, on this account, is not perceived directly but continuously constructed out of the same stock of relational, episodic materials — memories, associations, learned contingencies — redirected toward positive or negative outcomes depending on which valence policy currently governs the simulation.
