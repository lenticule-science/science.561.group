The locus coeruleus is a small cluster of noradrenergic neurons in the pons, containing only a few thousand cells in humans, yet projecting extraordinarily broadly to nearly the entire forebrain and cerebellum — it is among the most widely projecting neural systems in the brain. The locus coeruleus does not carry specific sensory or motor content; instead, it carries a neuromodulatory signal: noradrenaline released into widespread terminal fields adjusts the gain, or sensitivity, of local neural processing across receiving regions, an effect formalized in the Aston-Jones and Cohen adaptive-gain model. That model distinguishes between two firing modes of locus coeruleus neurons: a tonic, baseline firing pattern that sets a steady level of forebrain noradrenergic tone, and phasic bursting that occurs in response to significant stimuli or attentional events, with the balance between them varying as a function of task demands and environmental context. The Aston-Jones and Cohen framework proposes that this tonic-phasic balance instantiates a choice between exploitation and exploration: higher tonic firing favors exploiting a well-learned policy, while lower tonic firing and higher phasic burst responsiveness favors flexible, exploratory sampling of the task space when learned strategies are failing.

A crucial point of confusion arises from the shared name "noradrenaline" across two distinct systems. The locus coeruleus is the source of central noradrenaline, a neuromodulator that adjusts cortical gain and affects processing throughout the brain; the sympathetic nervous system, by contrast, releases noradrenaline as a neurotransmitter at the adrenal medulla and in peripheral tissues, and this peripheral catecholamine acts on vasculature and cardiac tissue. They are the same molecule but operate in entirely different systems with different receptors, different time courses, and different physiological consequences. Conflating central and peripheral noradrenaline is a persistent source of error in the literature, especially in studies of arousal and orthostatic challenge.

Because the locus coeruleus sets the gain on processing across the entire forebrain and cerebellum as a unified system, sustained elevation of locus coeruleus output produces a global change in the character of all ongoing processing. This is why arousal states — characterized by high sympathetic activity, elevated heart rate, dilated pupils, and heightened vigilance — are experienced and measured globally, as a unified change in responsiveness across domains, rather than as an isolated symptom or a collection of independent components. A person in a state of high physiological arousal does not experience faster processing in vision only, or tremor in the hands only; they experience a global shift in the speed, salience, and readiness of everything at once. This global, non-specific nature of locus coeruleus gain modulation explains why studies looking for domain-specific cognitive deficits in high-arousal states often find instead a broadening of attention and a slowing of executive function across the board.

## Related terms

- Arousal — a global physiological and behavioral state generated by locus coeruleus-mediated gain modulation
- Noradrenaline — the neurotransmitter released by the locus coeruleus into the central nervous system
- Sympathoadrenal system — the peripheral system releasing the same molecule peripherally
- Adaptive gain — the framework relating locus coeruleus firing patterns to exploitation versus exploration
