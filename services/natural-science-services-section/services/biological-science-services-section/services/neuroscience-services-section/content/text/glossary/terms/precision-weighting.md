Precision-weighting is the mechanism, within predictive-processing and active-inference accounts of the brain, by which attention is reconceived as the optimization of confidence assigned to a prediction error, rather than as a spotlight moving over a scene. In these models the nervous system encodes beliefs about the causes of its sensory input as probability distributions, and precision is the inverse of the variance of such a distribution — a formal measure of how much confidence the system currently assigns to a given prediction error or signal. Precision-weighting is the process of adjusting how strongly a given prediction error is allowed to update the brain's ongoing model of the world.

The framework derives from Karl Friston's free-energy formulation of brain function, in which perception and action jointly minimize prediction error (or "surprise") across a hierarchy of generative models. Harriet Feldman and Karl Friston gave the account its clearest early statement, showing in simulations of directed spatial attention and biased competition that raising the precision assigned to a particular sensory channel or location functions exactly like attending to it: it increases the gain of matching prediction errors and their influence on inference, without invoking any separate "attention" mechanism outside of precision estimation itself. Precision-weighting is thought to be implemented biologically through neuromodulation — acetylcholine and norepinephrine are the leading candidates for the systems that set the gain on prediction-error units — which is significant because those neuromodulatory systems have their own real time constants, adaptation, and habituation dynamics, distinct from the formal precision variable itself. Later work in the 2020s extended the account to preparatory and selective attention paradigms (such as the cocktail-party effect) and to psychiatric conditions understood as disorders of precision estimation, though critics have argued that attention involves computations — such as active sampling and competition for limited representational resources — that are not fully reducible to precision-weighting alone.

For the relational framework, precision-weighting is significant because it locates "spending attention" in a reweighting of relationships — between a prediction and the signal that might contradict it, between one hierarchical level of a model and another — rather than in the expenditure of a substance. On this account, allocating attention toward a hopeful or fearful simulation is a routing operation: a redistribution of which relations are currently trusted to drive inference, adjustable in principle without cost in the core formalism. This complicates any simple resource-stock account of attention, while leaving open — through the real dynamics of the neuromodulatory systems that implement precision assignment in the brain — a distinct and slower sense in which sustained precision-weighting still has a genuine physiological cost.
