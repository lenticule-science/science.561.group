The defense cascade is a clinical synthesis, proposed by Kozlowska, Walker, McLean, and Carrive (2015, *Harvard Review of Psychiatry*), that arranges arousal, active defense, freezing, tonic and collapsed immobility, and quiescent immobility as one continuous sequence sharing a common neural substrate. The paper's own abstract states the sequence directly: "Arousal is the first step in activating the defense cascade; flight or fight is an active defense response for dealing with threat; freezing is a flight-or-fight response put on hold; tonic immobility and collapsed immobility are responses of last resort to inescapable threat, when active defense responses have failed; and quiescent immobility is a state of quiescence that promotes rest and healing. Each of these defense reactions has a distinctive neural pattern mediated by a common neural pathway: activation and inhibition of particular functional components in the amygdala, hypothalamus, periaqueductal gray, and sympathetic and vagal nuclei. Unlike animals, which generally are able to restore their standard mode of functioning once the danger is past, humans often are not, and they may find themselves locked into the same, recurring pattern of response tied in with the original danger or trauma."

This is a narrative clinical review and synthesis, not a primary empirical study: it reports no patient sample and no numbers of its own, instead integrating rodent neurophysiology — including the periaqueductal-gray columnar stimulation work reviewed under periaqueductal-gray, on which co-author Carrive is himself a primary investigator — with clinical case description and treatment recommendations. Its own abstract frames the defense cascade as endowed in all humans by evolution, and its clinical vignettes span children, adolescents, and adults; its stated target presentations are PTSD, peritraumatic reactions, complex trauma, borderline personality disorder, and states of intense distress potentially leading to self-harm. It discusses the catatonic spectrum explicitly, noting that some clinicians have proposed catatonia as a fear response phylogenetically related to tonic immobility, but it does not address bipolar or unipolar depressive presentations by name.

The distinction that must be stated plainly is that the paper's mapping of specific clinical presentations onto specific PAG-column and amygdala-node activation patterns is an explicit theoretical bridging exercise, extrapolated by its own authors from rodent circuit data, not a report of new human physiological or imaging data localizing those clinical states to those rodent-defined nodes. No PAG-column-level imaging or intracranial recording in a human trauma patient — establishing, for example, that a given patient's "fright" state maps onto human ventrolateral-PAG activation the way rat kainic-acid injection does in rodents — was located in the review underlying this term. The "distinctive neural pattern" claims are extended by analogy from the rodent PAG-columnar and amygdala circuit-mapping literature and from parallel amygdala-hypothalamus-PAG circuit reviews, not from new human circuit-localization findings.

A separate, six-term lineage of a related mnemonic — "freeze, flight, fight, fright, flag, faint" — is distinct from Kozlowska and colleagues' own five-step arousal, flight-or-fight, freeze, tonic/collapsed-immobility, quiescent-immobility sequence, and is most commonly attributed in the secondary literature to Schauer and Elbert (2010). That paper sits outside the PubMed/Europe PMC index used elsewhere in this review, and its primary abstract text was not independently fetched; what secondary sources consistently attribute to it, reported here as the authors' framing rather than independently verified primary text, is that the six-stage cascade depends on the organism's appraisal of its own capacity to act and the perceived characteristics of the threat, with the "fright," "flag," and "faint" stages specifically involving parasympathetic-dominant shutdown.

The consequence for clinical observation and research design is that a claim such as "this patient's dissociation reflects ventrolateral-PAG-type quiescence" should be treated as a clinically useful organizing hypothesis for treatment planning, not as an established neural fact about that patient's brain. Testing the hypothesis at the level the paper's own language invites would require the kind of human circuit-level localization — column-resolving imaging or comparable direct measurement — that neither this synthesis nor the rodent literature it draws on currently supplies; absent that, the defense cascade should be used as a treatment-organizing framework and not cited as evidence of a demonstrated human circuit mapping.
