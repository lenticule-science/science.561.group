Cerebral hypoperfusion is a reduction in cerebral blood flow — either globally (affecting the whole brain) or regionally (affecting specific vascular territories). Blood flow to the brain is measured clinically and in research through several methods, each with different spatial resolution, temporal sensitivity, and interpretive challenges. Transcranial Doppler ultrasonography measures flow velocity in large intracranial vessels (the middle cerebral artery, anterior cerebral artery, and others) non-invasively through the skull, providing good temporal resolution and quantifiable flow velocity, but does not directly measure blood flow volume and relies on assumptions about vessel diameter. Arterial spin labeling MRI uses radio-frequency labeling of blood water in the neck arteries to track blood arrival in the brain, providing maps of regional perfusion with whole-brain coverage, though with limited temporal resolution. Extracranial Doppler of the common carotid and vertebral arteries in the neck measures blood flow entering the brain, avoiding the skull completely and allowing direct measurement of flow volume, but samples only the main arterial input and misses intracranial redistribution. Each method answers slightly different questions and has different sensitivity to different kinds of perfusion failure.

A critical distinction, often muddled in research on myalgic encephalomyelitis and orthostatic intolerance, separates resting cerebral hypoperfusion from dynamic failure of perfusion maintenance. A normal supine resting measurement of cerebral blood flow does not exclude the possibility that flow fails to be maintained when the patient stands or changes posture. Orthostatic cerebral hypoperfusion is a specific failure: in the supine position, blood flow is adequate and measured values are normal, but when the person becomes upright, autoregulatory mechanisms fail to maintain intracranial perfusion, and blood flow falls. This is mechanistically distinct from a chronic reduction in resting flow, and it has profound consequences for research design: a patient with normal supine transcranial Doppler or arterial spin labeling might nonetheless have severe orthostatic cerebral hypoperfusion that would not be detected by a single supine measurement. Some studies of ME/CFS have indeed found reduced resting flow by transcranial Doppler or arterial spin labeling, but others have found resting flow in the normal range while documenting failure of that flow to be maintained during tilt or standing. The distinction matters for mechanism: orthostatic failure implicates autonomic dysfunction specifically, whereas global resting hypoperfusion implicates either chronic reduction in cardiac output or structural narrowing of vessels.

The practical consequence for clinical research and individual assessment is decisive. A single measurement of cerebral perfusion — whether transcranial Doppler, arterial spin labeling, or extracranial Doppler — taken in the supine position cannot establish the presence or absence of orthostatic cerebral hypoperfusion. Any claim that a patient's brain is adequately perfused, based on normal supine flow measurement alone, misses the distinct possibility that flow is adequate lying down but fails upon standing. Similarly, any study comparing brain perfusion between ME/CFS patients and healthy controls must specify whether measurements were taken supine, upright, or during a provocation like tilt; a group difference in supine flow does not imply equivalent differences in orthostatic flow, and absence of difference supine does not exclude important differences under challenge. The posture during measurement determines what physiology is being interrogated, and posture is rarely adequately specified in the published literature.

## Related terms

- Cerebral blood flow — the rate of blood delivery to the brain; hypoperfusion is its reduction
- Orthostatic intolerance — a condition in which standing or postural change produces symptoms; may or may not involve cerebral hypoperfusion
- Cerebral autoregulation — the system that maintains cerebral blood flow despite changes in systemic blood pressure
- Transcranial Doppler ultrasonography — a method for measuring flow velocity in intracranial vessels
- Arterial spin labeling — an MRI method for mapping regional cerebral blood flow
