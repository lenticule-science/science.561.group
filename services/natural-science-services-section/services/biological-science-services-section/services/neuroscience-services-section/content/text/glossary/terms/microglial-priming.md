Microglia are the resident macrophages of the central nervous system, derived embryologically from the yolk sac and establishing themselves in the brain and spinal cord before the blood-brain barrier is even fully formed. Microglial priming is a phenomenon in which microglia, following an initial immune insult or inflammatory challenge, remain in a sensitized state — their baseline morphology and gene expression may appear near-normal, yet they retain an altered responsiveness to future stimuli. When a second inflammatory stimulus is then presented, a primed microglial cell mounts an exaggerated and prolonged inflammatory response — higher cytokine production, more extensive activation, longer duration — compared to what an unprimed cell would produce in response to the same stimulus.

This distinction between priming and activation is important and sometimes overlooked. Activation is an overt shift in morphology and function; activated microglia assume an amoeboid shape and rapidly upregulate pro-inflammatory genes and cytokine production. Priming, by contrast, is a change in the microglial cell's state of readiness that may not be visible at the level of morphology or baseline gene expression alone — the cell looks normal until challenged. Perry and Holmes's work on microglial priming in the context of neurodegeneration and aging established that a prior insult can leave microglia in a state of heightened reactivity for weeks or months, such that an otherwise mild subsequent challenge triggers an amplified response.

The application of microglial priming to myalgic encephalomyelitis and post-exertional malaise is a hypothesis with significant biological plausibility, but the current evidence is thin and must be stated plainly. The Nakatomi et al. 2014 positron emission tomography study is one of the most frequently cited pieces of evidence, but it is a small-sample study with approximately 15 cases and 13 controls, and the finding has not cleanly replicated in subsequent work. TSPO positron emission tomography ligand interpretation itself remains contested, so a TSPO signal cannot be unambiguously attributed to microglial activation. In the absence of larger, well-controlled studies with mechanistic specificity, microglial priming in ME/CFS remains a well-motivated working hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism.

The consequence of understanding microglial priming as the operating hypothesis is testable. If brain immune cells are primed by a prior insult and then amplify their response to a subsequent challenge, the system predicts a delayed activation: the initial trigger does not immediately produce the full inflammatory response, because microglial priming itself is the intermediate state established after the first hit. The flare in symptoms characteristic of post-exertional malaise — the delayed worsening by 24 to 72 hours rather than immediate collapse — would be explained by the time course of priming plus the subsequent amplified response to the triggering challenge. This temporal signature is a prediction that distinguishes microglial priming from immediate, non-amplified inflammation, and it is a signature that can be tested against alternative models.

## Related terms

- Microglial activation — the overt shift in morphology and function; priming is the sensitized state preceding it
- Neuroinflammation — the broader category of immune activation in the brain
- Allostatic load — the cumulative physiological cost of which repeated microglial priming may be one mechanism
- Post-exertional malaise — a condition in which delayed, prolonged symptom flare is consistent with amplified microglial response to challenge
