A priority map is a topographic neural representation in which the strength of activity at each location encodes not what is present at that location but how behaviorally important it currently is — a combined ranking of everything competing for attention and action, rather than a picture of the world.

The concept was formalized by James Bisley and Michael Goldberg, who showed that neurons in the lateral intraparietal area (LIP) of monkey parietal cortex respond in proportion to a location's behavioral priority rather than its raw sensory salience: activity there sums a fast, largely automatic bottom-up response to stimulus onset with slower, top-down signals reflecting task relevance, reward history, and current goals. The peak of this combined map is read out by the oculomotor system to target eye movements and, more generally, is treated as marking whatever the visual system will attend to next. The framework sits inside the older biased-competition tradition (Robert Desimone and John Duncan), which established that multiple candidate stimulus representations mutually suppress one another's gain in visual cortex, so that only a subset can drive behavior at a given moment; priority maps can be read as a specific, spatially organized instantiation of that competition, computed jointly across several parietal and frontal regions rather than in any single area alone.

Priority maps make a specific claim that distinguishes them from simple resource or spotlight metaphors of attention: what gets attended is not an intrinsic property of a stimulus, and not the possession of some independent attentional faculty, but the outcome of a continuously recomputed comparison against everything else currently in competition. Moving a stimulus from low to high priority does nothing in isolation; its priority is always relative to the rest of the map at that instant. Subsequent work has extended the account beyond spatial vision to value-based and goal-based competition more generally, treating priority computation as a general mechanism by which nervous systems arbitrate among multiple simultaneous, unequal claims on limited processing and motor output.

For the relational framework, priority maps are useful precisely because they show attention being allocated by comparison rather than by possession: a location or object is never "attended" in itself, only ever attended-relative-to what else is competing for the same map at the same moment. This supports treating attention economically as a rivalrous allocation at the point of selection — the map is always fully occupied, and raising one claimant's standing is definitionally a lowering of every other claimant's — while leaving open, as a separate question, whether any slower resource is being consumed across the interval during which many such momentary competitions are resolved in sequence.
