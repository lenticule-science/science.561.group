Circa-strike defense is the terminal mode of Fanselow's threat-imminence continuum: the vigorous, active-defense behavior — escape, attack, explosive flight — that rodent studies show is elicited specifically by imminent or actual physical contact with a threat, rather than by threat at a distance. Fanselow's 1994 statement is explicit: "Extremely high levels of fear, such as those provoked by physical contact, elicit the vigorous active defenses that compose the circa-strike mode. Midbrain structures such as the dorsolateral periaqueductal gray and the superior colliculus play a crucial role in organizing this mode of defense." This mode is proposed to be switched into, rather than escalated toward, through mutually inhibitory interaction with the forebrain-gated freezing circuitry that dominates the preceding post-encounter mode — a disinhibition event, in Fanselow's rodent model, not a graded ramp of a single circuit.

Rodent circuit-mapping work supports a topographically organized substrate for this mode within the periaqueductal gray. Depaulis, Bandler, and Vergnes (1989) used dose-response kainic-acid microinjection (4, 20, 40, 100, 200 pmol) in rat PAG and found dose-dependent defensive-posture and escape-jumping output, confirmed not secondary to seizure activity via EEG recording at cortical, hippocampal, amygdala, and PAG electrodes. Depaulis, Keay, and Bandler's 1992 follow-up mapping found that caudal and lateral PAG sites evoke forward avoidance and escape behavior plus reactive freezing, while more rostral and intermediate sites evoke backward defensive movements — a gradient of active-defense subtypes within the dorsolateral and lateral column, separately organized from the ventrolateral quiescence zone that instead produces immobility (see periaqueductal-gray, tonic immobility).

Circa-strike defense is distinguished from tonic immobility by both its rodent circuit substrate and its behavioral topography: circa-strike is an active, sympathetically dominated escape-or-attack output organized by the dorsolateral and lateral PAG columns and superior colliculus, while tonic immobility is a passive, reduced-responsiveness output organized (in the rodent stimulation literature) by the ventrolateral PAG column. The two are proposed as mutually inhibitory rather than as points on one shared intensity scale.

In humans, fMRI shows a correlated shift toward midbrain involvement as threat imminence increases, though the evidence is correlational and the paradigm never involves an immobility state. Mobbs and colleagues (2007, *Science*, N=14) found that "as the virtual predator grew closer, brain activity shifted from the ventromedial prefrontal cortex to the periaqueductal gray... imminence-driven periaqueductal gray activity correlated with increased subjective degree of dread and decreased confidence of escape." Mobbs and colleagues (2009, *Journal of Neuroscience*, N=24, 12 male, mean age 27.0 ± 4.7) reported that "active avoidance during circa-strike threat increased activity in mid-dorsal ACC and midbrain areas... increased coupling between the midbrain and mid-dorsal ACC and decreased coupling with the sgACC, amygdala, and hippocampus... panic-related locomotor errors were correlated with midbrain activity."

This human evidence does not, on its own, establish causality: no lesion, TMS, or pharmacological perturbation of the human PAG was performed in these studies to show the PAG shift produces the behavioral or affective shift rather than merely co-occurring with it. Nor does it resolve column-level specificity, since standard-resolution human PAG fMRI cannot reliably separate the rodent-defined dorsolateral and ventrolateral columns. Most importantly for the consequence stated below, the human paradigm is an active-avoidance task throughout — participants are always moving or deciding to flee — so it speaks to escalation of active defense under increasing imminence, not to a switch out of immobility or freezing into attack. No study located in the underlying review scanned participants transitioning from a freezing or immobile behavioral state into active flight-or-fight within a single session, in either humans or animals, with neuroimaging or intracranial recording.

The consequence for research design is that a claim about "the switch from freezing into circa-strike defense" is licensed, at the level of rodent circuit-manipulation evidence, only as a disinhibition account between two distinct PAG-column systems; at the level of human evidence, only a correlated forebrain-to-midbrain shift during escalating active avoidance has been shown, and this is a distinct and narrower claim. A study designed to test the switch itself — rather than escalation within one mode — would need to capture a freezing or immobile baseline state and its transition into active defense within the same experimental session, which the reviewed literature does not yet provide.

## Related terms

- Threat-imminence continuum — the larger three-mode framework of which circa-strike defense is the terminal stage
- Periaqueductal gray — the midbrain structure whose dorsolateral and lateral columns organize this mode
- Tonic immobility — the passive, ventrolateral-PAG-associated state this mode is proposed to be mutually inhibitory with
