Cerebral autoregulation is a system-level homeostatic process by which the brain maintains blood flow within a relatively narrow range despite substantial changes in systemic arterial blood pressure. Under normal conditions, cerebral blood flow (CBF) remains approximately constant — around 50 milliliters per 100 grams of brain tissue per minute — across a mean arterial pressure range of roughly 50 to 150 millimeters of mercury. Outside this autoregulatory range, CBF becomes pressure-passive: at pressures below 50 mmHg, CBF drops and the brain risks hypoperfusion and ischemia; above 150 mmHg, CBF rises and the blood-brain barrier can fail, producing cerebral edema and hypertensive encephalopathy. Autoregulation is the system that holds flow constant within the pressure range where neural function requires it.

Three partially independent mechanisms contribute to cerebral autoregulation, each operating on different timescales and through different physical principles. The myogenic mechanism is the fastest, operating within seconds: when arterial pressure increases, the smooth muscle cells of cerebral arterioles sense the increased distending pressure and contract, reducing vessel diameter and therefore resisting flow, maintaining constant flow through the narrower vessel — this is purely physical and does not require neural signaling. Metabolic regulation operates on seconds to minutes: local neural activity consumes oxygen and glucose, producing metabolic byproducts (adenosine, H+, K+) that accumulate in the extracellular space, dilate local vessels, and increase flow to that region. This is related to neurovascular coupling but operates at a whole-brain level rather than region-by-region. Neurogenic mechanisms involve the sympathetic nervous system: when blood pressure is very high, sympathetic constriction of larger cerebral vessels protects smaller downstream vessels from excessive pressure; when blood pressure is low, reduced sympathetic tone permits vasodilation and preserves flow. Each mechanism is imperfect alone but together they provide robust autoregulation across a wide pressure range.

Autoregulation is measured through two approaches, yielding different physiological information. Static autoregulation measures the steady-state relationship between blood pressure and blood flow, typically over 5–10 minutes, and is quantified as the slope of the CBF versus blood-pressure curve — zero slope indicates perfect autoregulation (CBF unchanged despite pressure change), while a steeper slope indicates impaired autoregulation. Dynamic autoregulation measures the transient response of flow to a sudden blood-pressure change, quantifying how quickly flow recovers to baseline after a perturbation; it is measured via transfer-function analysis or impulse-response methods. Static autoregulation can be relatively preserved while dynamic autoregulation is impaired, or the reverse, so both need to be assessed if the full picture of autoregulatory capacity is sought. Laboratory methods for measuring autoregulation include transcranial Doppler ultrasonography (measures flow velocity in large intracranial vessels), arterial spin labeling MRI (maps regional perfusion), and invasive arterial and intracranial-pressure monitoring in research or clinical settings.

Cerebral autoregulation is mechanistically and functionally distinct from neurovascular coupling, and the distinction is essential for interpreting research on cerebral hypoperfusion. Neurovascular coupling is the system that *matches local blood flow to local neural activity*: when neurons in a region increase their firing, that region's blood flow increases to provide the needed metabolic substrate. This is a spatial coupling system — the blood goes where the neurons are firing — operating on the seconds-level timescale. Cerebral autoregulation, by contrast, is a system that *defends overall cerebral blood flow against systemic pressure changes*, independent of where neurons are firing. The two systems operate at different scales (local versus global), have different mechanisms (metabolic signaling from neurons versus mechanical and neurogenic vascular responses), and can fail independently. A person can have intact neurovascular coupling but impaired autoregulation, producing normal local flow response to neural activity but failure to maintain overall perfusion when blood pressure drops (as in orthostatic intolerance). Conversely, a person can have relatively intact autoregulation but impaired coupling (as might occur with endothelial or astrocytic dysfunction), maintaining overall average blood flow despite pressure changes but with reduced ability to redirect flow to active regions, producing reduced BOLD signal during tasks despite adequate overall perfusion. The two require different assessment approaches: neurovascular coupling is tested via functional imaging during neural activation (task fMRI, checking for BOLD response), while autoregulation is tested by imposing blood-pressure changes (passive leg raise, lower-body negative pressure, tilt testing) and measuring the flow response.

For research on myalgic encephalomyelitis and orthostatic intolerance, the failure to distinguish autoregulation from coupling has muddied the mechanistic literature. A report of "reduced cerebral blood flow" in a patient group does not specify whether the deficit is failure to maintain global flow (autoregulation failure), failure to direct flow to active regions (coupling failure), or absolute reduction in resting flow from chronic pathology. A study finding reduced BOLD fMRI activation could reflect either reduced actual neural activity or impaired coupling despite intact neural activity. And a patient with orthostatic symptoms and normal supine autoregulation might nonetheless have impaired dynamic autoregulation that fails specifically during postural change, making upright measurement essential. Resolving these distinctions requires explicit measurement protocols: pressure-change challenges (tilt, lower-body negative pressure) with direct flow measurement for autoregulation; neural-activation tasks with flow measurement for coupling; supine and upright measures to distinguish dynamic failure from global insufficiency.

## Related terms

- Neurovascular coupling — the system matching local blood flow to local neural activity; distinguished from autoregulation
- Cerebral blood flow — the rate of blood delivery to the brain; autoregulation maintains it
- Cerebral hypoperfusion — reduction in CBF that may result from autoregulation failure
- Blood pressure — the driving force for CBF; autoregulation resists pressure changes
- Orthostatic intolerance — a symptom that can result from dynamic autoregulation failure upon postural change
- Myogenic mechanism — the physical basis of the fastest autoregulatory response
- Transcranial Doppler ultrasonography — a method for measuring cerebral blood flow velocity to assess autoregulation
- Arterial spin labeling — an MRI method for measuring regional cerebral blood flow
