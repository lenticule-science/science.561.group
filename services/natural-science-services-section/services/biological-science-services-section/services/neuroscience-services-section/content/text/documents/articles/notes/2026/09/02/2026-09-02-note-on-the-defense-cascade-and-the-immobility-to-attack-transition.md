## What this argues

The literature on defensive behavior gives a well-characterized rodent circuit that produces two opposite outputs — passive immobility and active, explosive defense — from opposite sites within the same midbrain structure, and a well-characterized human imaging finding that the balance of forebrain and midbrain control shifts as a threat becomes more imminent. What it does not give, in either species, is a single study that catches the switch between immobility and attack itself in progress. The rodent work establishes that each state can be produced in isolation by direct stimulation of its own circuit node; the human work establishes that, under an externally paced, escalating active-defense task, control shifts from cortex toward midbrain as capture becomes more likely. Between those two facts sits a widely used clinical synthesis — the "defense cascade" — that narrates a continuous sequence running from arousal through active defense to freezing to collapse, and that maps each stage onto the rodent circuitry by extension. That synthesis is its own authors' theoretical bridge, built by analogy from animal data, not a report of new human circuit-localization findings. This note lays out what the rodent circuit evidence actually shows, what the human imaging evidence actually shows, where the clinical formulation goes beyond both, and states plainly what nobody has yet measured.

## The threat-imminence continuum: a rodent behavior-systems account

Michael Fanselow's threat-imminence continuum organizes rodent defensive behavior into three modes tied to the spatial, temporal, and psychological distance from a predator or threat: pre-encounter, post-encounter, and circa-strike. The continuum is not a report of one new experiment but a synthesis of Fanselow's own and others' rat lesion, stimulation, and pharmacology data into a behavior-systems framework (Fanselow, 1994). In the paper's own words, "low levels of fear promote pre-encounter defenses, such as meal-pattern reorganization. Moderate levels of fear activate post-encounter defenses. For the rat, freezing is the dominant post-encounter defensive response... Projections from the amygdala to the ventral periaqueductal gray activate freezing. Extremely high levels of fear, such as those provoked by physical contact, elicit the vigorous active defenses that compose the circa-strike mode. Midbrain structures such as the dorsolateral periaqueductal gray and the superior colliculus play a crucial role in organizing this mode of defense." The paper's proposed mechanism for the switch is specific: "inhibitory interactions between the structures mediating circa-strike and post-encounter defense allow for the rapid switching between defensive modes as the threatening situation varies" — a hierarchically organized, mutually inhibitory relationship between forebrain-gated freezing circuitry and midbrain circa-strike circuitry, so that the switch is a disinhibition event rather than a graded escalation of one circuit.

This is a rat behavior-systems model built from lesion and stimulation data in rodents confronting live or model predators and shock. Fanselow (1994) frames the continuum explicitly in terms of predatory threat and shock/pain paradigms; it contains no human data and makes no claim about what triggers a switch in a human outside a live threat-detection context. The [threat-imminence continuum](../../../../../../glossary/terms/threat-imminence-continuum.md) and [circa-strike defense](../../../../../../glossary/terms/circa-strike-defense.md) terms carry the concept and its sourcing in full; it is linked here rather than restated.

The continuum's original naming traces to a book chapter, Fanselow & Lester (1988), in the edited volume *Evolution and Learning*. That chapter is not PubMed- or Europe-PMC-indexed, and its primary text was not independently fetched in the underlying literature search for this note; its content is reported here only as it is cited and summarized in later PubMed-indexed papers, including Fanselow (1994) itself — unverified against a primary electronic source. The parallel rodent-ethology tradition that named and quantified the same behavior categories (flight, freezing, defensive threat, defensive attack, risk assessment) independently of Fanselow's computational framing is Robert and Caroline Blanchard's ethoexperimental program, most often cited to Blanchard & Blanchard (1988) in the *Annual Review of Psychology*. That chapter's existence and general content are corroborated by multiple independent modern citations — for instance a 2015 paper (Pearson, Defensor, Blanchard & Blanchard, PMID 26066729) that cites and extends the Blanchards' framework in a mouse genetic model — but its own primary abstract text was not retrieved and quoted in this search; it is flagged here, as in the source dossier, as unverified against a primary abstract.

## The periaqueductal gray's columns: opposite outputs from opposite sites

The midbrain [periaqueductal gray](../../../../../../glossary/terms/periaqueductal-gray.md) (PAG) is organized into longitudinal columns — dorsomedial, dorsolateral, lateral, and ventrolateral — and rodent stimulation and microinjection studies show that these columns produce genuinely opposite defensive outputs. Activation of the dorsolateral or lateral column drives active, sympathetically dominated escape and attack; activation of the ventrolateral column (vlPAG) drives quiescence, immobility, and parasympathetically dominated hyporeactivity.

The reviewing paper most often cited for the "columns as modules for opposite defense strategies" framing is Bandler & Shipley (1994), which states: "different classes of threatening or nociceptive stimuli trigger distinct co-ordinated patterns of skeletal, autonomic and antinociceptive adjustments by selectively targeting specific PAG columnar circuits." Carrive (1993), the review proposing the four-column scheme itself, states the opposition directly: "Reactions evoked from the lateral PAG column are associated with somatomotor and autonomic activation and are characteristic of an organism's response to superficial or cutaneous noxious stimuli, whereas reactions evoked from the ventrolateral PAG column are associated with somatomotor and autonomic inhibition."

The primary evidence behind both reviews is a series of single-lab rat microinjection studies. Depaulis, Keay & Bandler (1994) microinjected kainic acid (40 pmol) into rat vlPAG in freely-moving rats tested in a social pairing with an untreated, weight-matched partner; the paper reports, quoted directly, that "injected rats become quiescent, i.e., there was a cessation of all ongoing spontaneous activity," and also hyporeactive — "the investigative approaches of the partner failed to evoke orientation, startle reactions, or vocalization" — with the effect confirmed not secondary to seizure activity. A companion paper, Depaulis, Keay & Bandler (1992), mapped kainic-acid injection sites across the intermediate and caudal thirds of rat PAG and found that caudal/lateral sites evoke forward avoidance/escape behavior plus reactive freezing, while more rostral/intermediate sites evoke backward defensive movements — a topographic gradient of active-defense subtypes within the dorsolateral/lateral column, distinct from the separately mapped ventrolateral quiescence zone. An earlier dose-response study, Depaulis, Bandler & Vergnes (1989), used graded kainic-acid doses (4, 20, 40, 100, and 200 pmol) in rat PAG and found dose-dependent defensive-posture and escape-jumping output, confirmed via EEG at cortical, hippocampal, amygdalar, and PAG electrodes not to be secondary to seizure.

All four of these primary studies are single-lab rat electrical- or chemical-stimulation/microinjection studies. None involve human subjects. None measure spontaneous, endogenously triggered switching between the two states in an awake, freely behaving animal outside an experimenter-imposed threat or injection. That is the precise limit of what this evidence base shows: the two PAG columns can produce opposite outputs when independently activated by an experimenter. It does not show that the switch between the two states in a natural setting is driven by a single identified triggering signal, and it does not show that this columnar antagonism is the mechanism of any human affective-state transition.

A related paper worth naming without full independent verification: Tovote et al. (2016), an optogenetic and electrophysiological mouse study reporting a central-amygdala-to-vlPAG inhibitory pathway that produces freezing via disinhibition of vlPAG outputs to medullary premotor targets, with anatomical and functional interaction between this pathway and flight circuits. Its title, existence, and general finding were confirmed through the paper's abstract page and secondary summaries; its exact abstract text was not independently pulled and quoted through the same verification channel used for the other sources in this section, and it is flagged here, as in the source dossier, as not fully re-verified.

## Tonic immobility: a well-measured endpoint, not a captured transition

[Tonic immobility](../../../../../../glossary/terms/tonic-immobility.md) (TI) is an involuntary, reversible state of profound motor inhibition — muscular rigidity, analgesia, reduced responsiveness — that in animals is elicited by physical restraint plus inescapable threat, foundationally characterized by Gallup (1977). That 1977 paper's journal, volume, and page range were located through search and are corroborated by multiple independent modern citations, including a 2025 review (Woodruff, *Frontiers in Psychology*), but its own primary abstract text was not independently fetched through PubMed or Europe PMC in this search — this journal's older issues are not consistently PMID-indexed — and it is flagged as unverified against a primary electronic abstract.

In humans, TI has been operationalized by self-report scales built and validated in trauma populations. Fusé, Forsyth, Marx, Gallup & Weaver (2007) ran an exploratory factor analysis (Study 1, N=88 female sexual-assault survivors) and a confirmatory factor analysis (Study 2, N=191 independent female sexual-assault survivors) on the Tonic Immobility Scale, concluding, quoted directly: "the TIS is comprised of two independent factors: physical immobility and fear." Abrams, Carleton, Taylor & Asmundson (2009) developed a separate self-report instrument, the Tonic Immobility Questionnaire, in N=78 undergraduate students who reported a TI experience during a traumatic event; exploratory factor analysis yielded a three-factor solution (physical immobility, fear, dissociation), and "peritraumatic dissociation scores alone accounted for 51% of the variance in TI scores," with no difference in TI frequency or severity found across trauma types. The authors' own conclusion, quoted: "TI may represent an extreme behavioral expression of trauma-induced peritraumatic dissociation." Bovin, Jager-Hyman, Gold, Marx & Sloan (2008), in a cross-sectional questionnaire study of N=176 female undergraduates with a history of sexual assault, found that "tonic immobility fully mediated relations between perceived inescapability and overall PTSD symptom severity, as well as reexperiencing and avoidance/numbing symptom clusters," and partially mediated the relation between fear and overall PTSD severity — concluding that "tonic immobility could be one path through which trauma survivors develop PTSD symptoms."

All three human studies are retrospective self-report questionnaire studies in convenience samples — undergraduates, self-selected sexual-assault survivors — not laboratory-induced or physiologically measured TI, and not longitudinal designs that establish causal direction; the mediation models are cross-sectional statistical mediation, not causal proof. None of them measure or address a switch out of immobility into anger, rage, or attack: they characterize the immobility endpoint and its correlation with later distress, not a transition to an activated defensive state. None involve mood-disorder populations; the convenience and self-selected trauma samples used here are not equivalent to a clinical depression or bipolar population.

## The amygdala-hypothalamus-PAG hierarchy: many paths, not one fear circuit

Gross & Canteras (2012) reviews rodent lesion, tract-tracing, and optogenetic/pharmacogenetic circuit-mapping work to argue that fear and defense are not implemented by one linear amygdala-to-PAG "fear circuit," but by several anatomically and functionally distinct amygdala-hypothalamus-PAG/brainstem circuits, each dedicated to a different class of threat. The review's own framing, quoted from its abstract: "fear of painful stimuli, predators and aggressive members of the same species are processed in independent neural circuits that involve the amygdala and downstream hypothalamic and brainstem circuits." The review builds on, rather than independently re-derives, the PAG columnar physiology established by the stimulation and lesion studies above: it describes the PAG itself as split along the same dorsal/ventral line, with dorsal regions implicated in active freezing/flight-type output and sympathetic activation, and ventral regions implicated in immobility-type output and parasympathetic activation. This is a review article synthesizing others' rodent data, not a primary dataset with its own sample size, and it contains no human data; it supports the general architectural claim that an amygdala-hypothalamus-PAG hierarchy exists and computes both threat-class-specific and imminence-specific outputs, but it does not itself address a switch between low-arousal immobility and high-arousal rage as a discrete clinical or psychiatric phenomenon.

One specific node within this hierarchy has been isolated with bidirectional causal manipulation in mice rather than only correlational or lesion evidence: the [hypothalamic attack area](../../../../../../glossary/terms/hypothalamic-attack-area.md), the ventrolateral part of the ventromedial hypothalamus identified by Lin and colleagues, whose optogenetic activation is sufficient to trigger attack and whose pharmacogenetic silencing reversibly blocks it. That term carries its own sourcing and its own caveat — that no equivalent causal manipulation of a homologous human hypothalamic region exists — and is linked here rather than restated; it stands as the sharpest available example of just how far rodent circuit precision currently outruns anything available for the human hierarchy discussed next.

## The clinical defense-cascade formulation: a stated theoretical bridge

Kozlowska, Walker, McLean & Carrive (2015), in the *Harvard Review of Psychiatry*, proposes a "defense cascade": arousal as the first step, then flight-or-fight as an active defense response, then freezing as a flight-or-fight response put on hold, then tonic immobility and collapsed immobility as responses of last resort to inescapable threat when active defense has failed, and finally quiescent immobility as a state of rest and healing. The paper's own words: "Each of these defense reactions has a distinctive neural pattern mediated by a common neural pathway: activation and inhibition of particular functional components in the amygdala, hypothalamus, periaqueductal gray, and sympathetic and vagal nuclei. Unlike animals, which generally are able to restore their standard mode of functioning once the danger is past, humans often are not, and they may find themselves locked into the same, recurring pattern of response tied in with the original danger or trauma."

This is a narrative clinical review and synthesis, not a primary empirical study — it reports no patient sample and no numbers, and it integrates rodent neurophysiology (the PAG columnar work and the amygdala-hypothalamus-PAG hierarchy work covered above) with clinical case description and treatment recommendations for psychiatric patients with trauma histories. The paper's mapping of specific clinical presentations — rage, withdrawal, dissociation, fainting — onto specific PAG-column and amygdala-node activation patterns is, by its own account, a theoretical bridging exercise: the abstract does not claim new human physiological or imaging data localizing those clinical states to those rodent-defined nodes. No PAG-column-level imaging or intracranial recording in a human trauma patient establishing that a specific patient's presentation maps onto human vlPAG activation the way rat kainic-acid injection does was located anywhere in the underlying search. Kozlowska holds a joint appointment in psychiatry and paediatrics, but the paper is not scoped to children and adolescents specifically: its abstract states that evolution has endowed all humans with this defense-cascade continuum, its clinical vignettes span children, adolescents, and adults, and its target presentations are PTSD, peritraumatic reactions, complex trauma, borderline personality disorder, and states of intense distress potentially leading to self-harm. It does not name bipolar or unipolar depressive presentations, though it does discuss the catatonic spectrum, noting that some clinicians have proposed catatonia is a fear response phylogenetically related to tonic immobility. The [defense cascade](../../../../../../glossary/terms/defense-cascade.md) term carries this sourcing and this same flag in full.

A separate, more specific six-term mnemonic — "freeze, flight, fight, fright, flag, faint" — circulates in the secondary literature and is distinct from Kozlowska et al.'s five-step list; it is most commonly attributed to Schauer & Elbert (2010) in the *Zeitschrift für Psychologie*. That paper sits outside the PubMed/Europe PMC index used elsewhere in this note; a publisher page was located but its primary abstract was not fetched and quoted, and it is flagged, as in the source dossier, as unverified. What secondary sources consistently attribute to it — reported here as the paper's framing, not as independently verified primary text — is that the six-stage cascade depends on the organism's appraisal of its own capacity to act (age, physical power) and the perceived characteristics of the threat, and that the "fright," "flag," and "faint" stages specifically involve parasympathetic-dominant shutdown.

## Human imaging of the threat-imminence shift: correlation, not causation

The human evidence that most directly parallels the rodent post-encounter-to-circa-strike transition is a small body of fMRI work by Dean Mobbs and colleagues, using virtual-predator paradigms. Mobbs et al. (2007), in *Science*, scanned N=14 healthy participants while a virtual predator chased them through a maze under a capture-and-shock contingency. The finding, quoted directly: "as the virtual predator grew closer, brain activity shifted from the ventromedial prefrontal cortex to the periaqueductal gray. This shift showed maximal expression when a high degree of pain was anticipated. Moreover, imminence-driven periaqueductal gray activity correlated with increased subjective degree of dread and decreased confidence of escape." The authors' own framing of the implications, quoted: "Our findings cast light on the neural dynamics of threat anticipation and have implications for the neurobiology of human anxiety-related disorders."

A follow-up, Mobbs et al. (2009) in the *Journal of Neuroscience*, scanned N=24 healthy subjects (12 male, mean age 27.0 ± 4.7 years) in an active-avoidance paradigm with analogous "postencounter" and "circa-strike" phases and high- versus low-capture-probability conditions. The finding: "postencounter threat elicited activity in forebrain areas, including subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC), hippocampus, and amygdala. Conversely, active avoidance during circa-strike threat increased activity in mid-dorsal ACC and midbrain areas... increased coupling between the midbrain and mid-dorsal ACC and decreased coupling with the sgACC, amygdala, and hippocampus... locomotor errors correlated with subjective reports of panic for the high compared with low probability of capture during the circa-strike threat, and these panic-related locomotor errors were correlated with midbrain activity." The authors' conclusion: "higher forebrain areas are involved in early-threat responses, including the assignment and control of fear, whereas imminent danger results in fast, likely 'hard-wired,' defensive reactions mediated by the midbrain." A later extension of the same escape paradigm, Qi, Hassabis, Sun, Guo, Daw & Mobbs (2018) in *PNAS*, reports that "rapid escape decisions rely on 'reactive fear' circuits in the periaqueductal gray and midcingulate cortex (MCC), while protracted escape decisions... were associated with 'cognitive fear' circuits, which include posterior cingulate cortex, hippocampus, and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex."

What this body of work does, and does not, settle about state switching has to be stated with the same precision the rodent sections above got:

- It establishes, with fMRI — a hemodynamic, seconds-scale, spatially coarse measure, not single-neuron or optogenetic causal manipulation — a correlation between subjective threat imminence and a shift in the relative balance of forebrain (vmPFC, sgACC, amygdala, hippocampus) versus midbrain (PAG, mid-dorsal ACC, MCC) BOLD activity, and a correlation between midbrain activity and self-reported dread, panic, and behavioral (locomotor) errors.
- It does not establish causality: no lesion, TMS, or pharmacological perturbation of the human PAG was performed in any of these studies to show that the PAG shift produces the behavioral or affective shift, rather than merely co-occurring with it.
- It does not resolve column-level specificity in humans. Standard-resolution human PAG fMRI cannot reliably separate the rodent-defined dorsolateral and ventrolateral sub-columns; the "PAG" activation reported in Mobbs et al. is not shown to be specifically dorsolateral (active-defense) rather than a mixture that could include ventrolateral signal. A 7-Tesla human PAG columnar-organization study exists in the broader literature by title — a working-memory study, not a defensive-behavior study, and a preprint at the time it was located — and is named here only to say it was not treated as evidence for column-level specificity in the Mobbs et al. findings.
- It does not involve an immobility or freezing behavioral state at the low-arousal end. The human paradigm is an active-avoidance task throughout — participants are always moving or deciding whether to flee — so it speaks to escalation of active defense under increasing imminence, not to a switch out of immobility or freezing into attack. No human study located in the underlying search scanned participants transitioning from a freezing or immobile behavioral state into active flight-or-fight.
- It does not involve a clinical population. All three Mobbs et al./Qi et al. paradigms used healthy volunteer samples in a virtual-predator paradigm, not psychiatric patients, and none measured mood-state or affect switching as a spontaneous, endogenous event — the threat was always experimenter-imposed and externally paced (the approaching virtual predator), not an internally generated affective transition.

## What has not been shown

Putting the rodent and human evidence together makes the actual gap precise rather than impressionistic. The rodent literature establishes the endpoints in isolation: the vlPAG column produces quiescence and hyporeactivity when directly stimulated (Depaulis, Keay & Bandler, 1994); the dorsolateral/lateral column and superior colliculus produce active escape and attack when directly stimulated (Depaulis, Bandler & Vergnes, 1989; Depaulis, Keay & Bandler, 1992); and Fanselow's (1994) theoretical account proposes that the two are held in a mutually inhibitory relationship such that disinhibition, not graded escalation, drives the switch between them. None of the underlying rodent studies measure a spontaneous, endogenously triggered switch between the two states in an awake, freely behaving animal outside an experimenter-imposed injection or stimulus. The human literature establishes a correlational, graded shift in cortical-versus-midbrain BOLD balance as an externally imposed, continuously escalating active-defense threat becomes more imminent (Mobbs et al., 2007, 2009; Qi et al., 2018) — but every human paradigm located here keeps its subjects in active avoidance throughout, so none of it touches the low-arousal, immobile end of the continuum at all, let alone a switch out of it. No study, animal or human, located anywhere in the underlying search directly measures a subject transitioning from a behaviorally confirmed freezing or tonic-immobility state into an active flight-or-fight or rage state within a single experimental session with neuroimaging or intracranial recording. This absence is reported here as a finding, not smoothed over: the defense cascade's clinical use of "immobility can flip to attack" is a plausible extrapolation from two circuits shown, separately, to be mutually inhibitory and individually capable of producing opposite outputs — it is not yet a captured transition, in either species.

## Relation to the section's other threat and arousal material

This note's circuitry sits alongside, and should not be conflated with, several concepts already established elsewhere in this section. The [locus coeruleus](../../../../../../glossary/terms/locus-coeruleus.md)'s tonic/phasic noradrenergic firing implements a general adaptive-gain model of arousal and exploration-versus-exploitation; it is a different system from the PAG-columnar switch described here, which is specific to defensive behavioral mode rather than a general arousal dial, though the two systems are not shown here to be independent of one another. [Precision-weighting](../../../../../../glossary/terms/precision-weighting.md) reconceives attention allocation under threat as a gain parameter on prediction error within a predictive-processing account — a different level of description from the autonomic and motor gain shifts the PAG columns implement, and no source used in this note attempts to unify the two frameworks. [Perseverative cognition](../../../../../../glossary/terms/perseverative-cognition.md) and [inhibitory safety learning](../../../../../../glossary/terms/inhibitory-safety-learning.md) both describe how a human sustains or discharges a threat representation cognitively — through worry and rumination in the first case, through new associative learning in the second — which is a different question from the circuit-level, largely non-cognitive switch between defensive behavioral modes this note covers. All four terms are linked here rather than restated; none of them supply, and none of the sources used to build them supply, the missing immobility-to-attack transition data named in the section above.
