The periaqueductal gray (PAG) is a midbrain structure surrounding the cerebral aqueduct, organized — per rodent stimulation and microinjection studies — into longitudinal functional columns (dorsomedial, dorsolateral, lateral, ventrolateral) rather than acting as a single undifferentiated structure. Carrive's 1993 review proposed the four-column scheme from rat lesion, stimulation, and pharmacology work, and Bandler and Shipley's 1994 synthesis in *Trends in Neurosciences* stated the governing principle: "different classes of threatening or nociceptive stimuli trigger distinct co-ordinated patterns of skeletal, autonomic and antinociceptive adjustments by selectively targeting specific PAG columnar circuits." The columns are not redundant channels for a single "fear" signal; they are separately addressable modules, each coordinating a distinct package of motor, autonomic, and pain-modulation output.

The dorsolateral and lateral columns and the ventrolateral column produce opposite defensive outputs in rodents, established by kainic-acid microinjection studies in rats. Depaulis, Keay, and Bandler (1994) injected kainic acid (40 pmol) into rat ventrolateral PAG (vlPAG) and reported that "injected rats become quiescent, i.e., there was a cessation of all ongoing spontaneous activity... also hyporeactive: the investigative approaches of the partner failed to evoke orientation, startle reactions, or vocalization," confirmed as not secondary to seizure activity by EEG monitoring. By contrast, Depaulis, Keay, and Bandler's 1992 mapping study found that caudal and lateral PAG sites evoke forward avoidance and escape behavior plus reactive freezing, while more rostral and intermediate sites evoke backward defensive movements — a topographic gradient of active-defense subtypes, separately organized from the ventrolateral quiescence zone and confirmed non-seizure-related by cortical, hippocampal, amygdala, and PAG EEG recording in a related dose-response study (Depaulis, Bandler, and Vergnes, 1989).

This columnar antagonism is a distinction, not a demonstrated mechanism of natural switching: the rodent stimulation and microinjection studies establish that the two column systems *can produce* opposite outputs when independently activated by direct experimental manipulation, not that an animal's spontaneous, endogenously triggered transition between active defense and immobility in a natural setting is driven by a single identified triggering signal acting on this antagonism. None of the rodent studies measure spontaneous switching in an awake, freely behaving animal outside an experimenter-imposed threat or injection.

Human evidence is imaging-correlational rather than causal, and it does not resolve column-level anatomy. Mobbs and colleagues (2007, *Science*, N=14 healthy participants navigating a virtual-predator maze with a capture-and-shock contingency) reported that "as the virtual predator grew closer, brain activity shifted from the ventromedial prefrontal cortex to the periaqueductal gray... imminence-driven periaqueductal gray activity correlated with increased subjective degree of dread and decreased confidence of escape." A follow-up study (Mobbs et al. 2009, *Journal of Neuroscience*, N=24 healthy subjects, 12 male, mean age 27.0 ± 4.7 years) found that "postencounter threat elicited activity in forebrain areas, including subgenual anterior cingulate cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala," while "active avoidance during circa-strike threat increased activity in mid-dorsal ACC and midbrain areas," with "panic-related locomotor errors... correlated with midbrain activity." Both studies are fMRI — a hemodynamic, seconds-scale, spatially coarse measure — with no lesion, TMS, or pharmacological perturbation of the human PAG performed to show that the PAG shift produces the behavioral or affective shift rather than merely co-occurring with it. Standard-resolution human PAG fMRI also cannot reliably separate the rodent-defined columns: the "PAG" activation reported in these studies is not shown to be specifically dorsolateral (active-defense) rather than a mixture that includes ventrolateral signal.

The consequence for research design is direct: a study reporting increased human PAG BOLD signal under threat has not, by that measurement alone, specified whether the signal reflects active-defense circuitry, quiescence-immobility circuitry, or some mixture of both, because the two rodent-defined columns are architecturally opposite and standard fMRI resolution cannot separate them. Causal claims about a specific column's contribution to a human affective or behavioral state remain unlicensed by fMRI correlation alone and require either column-resolving methods or direct perturbation, neither of which the imaging literature reviewed here provides.

## Related terms

- Circa-strike defense — the active-defense output organized by the dorsolateral and lateral PAG columns
- Tonic immobility — a state whose animal and human characterization sits alongside, though not identically with, ventrolateral-PAG-type quiescence
- Hypothalamic attack area — a separate, hypothalamic (not midbrain) rodent-causal node driving offensive attack rather than defensive escape or immobility
- Threat-imminence continuum — the rodent behavior-systems framework in which the circa-strike mode is organized by dorsolateral PAG and superior colliculus
