The Universal Edibility Test is a sequential procedure — originating in the
US Army survival manual, ATP 3-50.21 ("Survival," formerly FM 3-05.70 and
originally FM 21-76) — intended to let a person in a survival emergency
judge whether an unidentified wild plant is safe to eat, in the absence of
positive identification. It is one of the most widely circulated pieces of
[foraging](./foraging.md)-adjacent practitioner guidance, and one of the
most widely criticized by experienced foragers, for reasons documented
below. The manual's lineage (ATP 3-50.21 as the current successor to FM
3-05.70 and FM 21-76) is confirmed via Army Publishing Directorate listings
and retail/archival copies of each edition; the test's presence in the
current manual is confirmed via multiple independent secondary
sources that reproduce it as sourced to the Army manual (see Sources), not
via a direct read of the ATP 3-50.21 PDF itself, which could not be
fetched during this revision.

## The protocol as commonly presented

The test as popularly summarized proceeds through stages: separate the
plant into its parts (roots, leaves, stems, flowers) and test only one part
at a time; smell the plant for strong or acrid odors; before ingesting
anything, rub a piece of the plant on the inner wrist or inner elbow and
wait 15 minutes for a skin reaction; if no reaction, hold a small piece to
the lips, then to the tongue, then chew it without swallowing, waiting
several minutes at each step for burning, stinging, or numbness; only after
all prior steps pass does the protocol call for swallowing a small amount
and waiting several hours, observing for illness, before eating a modest
quantity. Full execution of the described sequence takes many hours.

## Documented criticisms

Practitioner-authority literature raises several specific problems with the
test:

**It is routinely mispresented.** Paul Kirtley — a UK-based wilderness
bushcraft instructor whose writing specifically catalogs the test's
problems — states that many popular presentations frame it as "how to know
if any plant is edible," which he characterizes as categorically wrong:
the test only applies within the survival-emergency context it was
designed for, is described incompletely in most secondary retellings (in
particular, the requirement to test each plant part separately is
frequently dropped), and its wide circulation leads inexperienced people to
treat it as an "easy solution" to their lack of genuine plant knowledge,
especially when presented online with undue authority.

**It does not apply to fungi.** Fungal toxins do not reliably produce the
taste or skin-contact warning signs the plant-oriented protocol depends on;
survival and foraging sources are consistent — across the independent
outlets cited below (Backpacker, RECOIL OFFGRID, TruePrepper, Texas
Bushcraft) — that the test should never be applied to mushrooms, though
this is a point of broad practitioner-literature consensus rather than a
single named authority's finding. This is consistent with the toxicological pattern documented
at [poisonous-lookalike](./poisonous-lookalike.md), where amatoxin
poisoning from *Amanita* species can be asymptomatic for many hours before
severe, often irreversible organ damage appears.

**It misses slow-acting and dose-dependent toxins.** Not every toxic plant
produces an adverse reaction within the test's short observation windows;
some toxins take hours or days to produce symptoms, and some plants are not
harmful in the small test quantities used but become dangerous at the
larger quantities a hungry forager would actually eat.

**It is impractical as actually specified.** Kirtley notes the full,
correctly executed protocol — separating and independently testing each
plant part, with waiting periods at each of several steps — consumes many
hours, in tension with the time pressure of the emergency situations in
which it is meant to be used. (This specific point appears in Kirtley's
reply to a reader comment on the article rather than in the article's main
text, which discusses time-consuming as a fact but a commenter and
Kirtley's own reply are what pin the multi-hour figure down; still a
directly attributable, published statement by Kirtley on his own site.)

## Relation to sustainable and routine foraging practice

The Universal Edibility Test is an emergency-survival protocol, not a
substitute for positive identification in ordinary foraging practice. It
addresses a different problem than the poisonous-lookalike safety issue
documented at [poisonous-lookalike](./poisonous-lookalike.md): the test is
for situations where no identification is possible at all, whereas the
lookalike problem concerns misidentifying one known species for another.
Practitioner-canon field guides (Samuel Thayer, John Kallas — see
[foraging](./foraging.md)) are widely known, including by reputation among
foragers, for insisting on complete positive identification of every part
of a plant rather than on rule-of-thumb tests; this characterization is
standard within foraging practitioner culture but is not verified here
against specific passages in Thayer's or Kallas's books, and no specific
published statement by either author criticizing the Universal Edibility
Test by name was located during this revision.
