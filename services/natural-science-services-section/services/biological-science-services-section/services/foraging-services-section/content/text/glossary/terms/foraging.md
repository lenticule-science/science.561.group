Foraging is the deliberate gathering of wild plants, fungi, and other
non-cultivated organisms for food, medicine, or material use. As a
discipline of practice it is genuinely cross-disciplinary: it draws
identification and taxonomic knowledge from botany and mycology,
seasonality knowledge from ecological phenology, safety knowledge from
clinical toxicology, and increasingly draws field-identification tooling
from citizen-science platforms such as iNaturalist. No single constituent
science is "more specific" than the union of the practice, which is why
this library seats foraging as its own discipline rather than as a branch
of botany or mycology — the same reasoning by which agricultural science
sits as a sibling to botany, mycology, and ecology rather than inside any
one of them.

## Two evidentiary tiers

The foraging literature splits cleanly into a peer-reviewed academic tier
and a practitioner-canon tier, and the two should not be conflated.

**Peer-reviewed core.** The academic study of wild food use is carried out
primarily within ethnobotany (see [ethnobotany](./ethnobotany.md)), with
supporting literatures in clinical toxicology (poisoning case series and
surveillance data), population ecology (sustainable-harvest studies), and
legal scholarship. Łukasz Łuczaj is the most prolific contemporary
peer-reviewed author on European wild food ethnobotany, e.g. Łuczaj &
Szymański (2007), "Wild vascular plants gathered for consumption in the
Polish countryside: a review," *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine*
3:17 (documenting 112 species, 3.7% of the Polish flora, from 42
ethnographic/botanical sources). Nancy J. Turner (University of Victoria)
is the leading peer-reviewed ethnobotanist of North American Indigenous
wild food systems, with 50+ years of fieldwork; her *Food Plants of
Coastal First Peoples* and *Food Plants of Interior First Peoples*
document 100–150+ traditionally harvested species with harvest and
preparation methods, and her Kwakwaka'wakw protocol work documents
selective-harvest management of 100–200 food and utility species.

**Practitioner canon.** The field-guide and identification literature that
most working foragers actually use is authored outside the peer-reviewed
system. Samuel Thayer (self-taught, three decades of teaching; *The
Forager's Harvest*, *Nature's Garden*, *Incredible Wild Edibles*, and the
2023 *Field Guide to Edible Wild Plants of Eastern and Central North
America*, winner of the National Outdoor Book Award) and John Kallas, PhD
(nutrition; founder of the Institute for the Study of Edible Wild Plants,
Portland, OR, est. 1993; author of the two-volume *Edible Wild Plants*
series) are the dominant contemporary authors in this tier. Kallas holds a
doctorate in nutrition, making his practitioner writing unusually adjacent
to peer-reviewed nutrition science, but the books themselves are not
peer-reviewed. This distinction matters for a foraging library because
identification-safety claims (see
[poisonous-lookalike](./poisonous-lookalike.md)) carry direct physical
stakes and the two tiers differ in evidentiary standard.

## Constituent disciplines and this section's boundary

Foraging practice draws on, but does not own:

- **Botany** — plant taxonomy, morphology, and species-level identification
  remain canonically owned by botany (e.g. *Allium tricoccum*'s botanical
  record lives in `botany-services-section`).
- **Mycology** — fungal taxonomy and biology remain canonically owned by
  mycology; as of this section's founding, mycology's glossary carries no
  toxicology or lookalike-identification content, a documented gap.
- **Ecology** — phenology (seasonal timing of life-cycle events) and
  harvest-population dynamics remain ecology's concepts; this section's
  [foraging-calendar](./foraging-calendar.md) applies phenology to harvest
  timing without owning the underlying phenological science.
- **iNaturalist-style observation** — field identification via
  photograph-based citizen science is a tooling layer this section relates
  to (`inaturalist-services-section`) rather than owns.

What this section owns canonically: the practice-level synthesis —
edibility and lookalike safety, harvest ethics and sustainability, law and
access, and seasonality practice — that draws on but is not reducible to
any one constituent science.
