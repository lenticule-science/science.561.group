Sustainable harvest is the practice of gathering wild plants (and by
extension fungi) at a rate and pattern their populations can replace, so
that a foraged site remains productive rather than being depleted. It
combines two distinct evidentiary strands: population ecology, which
supplies quantitative harvest-rate thresholds for specific species, and
Indigenous ethical frameworks, which supply a reciprocity-based practice
model that in some documented cases produces measurable ecological
benefit rather than merely limiting harm.

## Population-ecology evidence

**Ramps (*Allium tricoccum*).** The best-documented sustainable-harvest
study in the foraging literature is Rock, Beckage & Gross (2004),
"Population recovery following differential harvesting of Allium
tricoccum Ait. in the southern Appalachians," *Biological Conservation*.
Multi-year experimental harvests in Great Smoky Mountains National Park at
0/25/50/75/100% removal rates found roughly 2.5-year recovery at 5%
harvest versus roughly 150-year recovery following a whole-population
(near-100%) harvest; a 25% harvest required roughly 20 years to recover.
This finding underlies the widely cited "10% once per decade"
sustainable-harvest guideline, attributed to a University of Tennessee
seven-year study (cited via United Plant Savers). Population growth in
ramps is driven mainly by slow clonal (asexual) reproduction — plants take
5–8 years to reach reproductive maturity — rather than by seed
reproduction, which is why overharvest recovery is so slow relative to
faster-reproducing species. A more recent agroforestry study (2022,
*Agroforestry Systems*) found that total ramp plant/bulb weight increases
250–400% from early to late season, suggesting that later-season harvest
can reduce the plant-count impact of taking the same total yield weight.
The species' botanical and conservation-status record is canonically owned
by botany; see
[`allium-tricoccum.md`](../../../../../botany-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/allium-tricoccum.md).

**American ginseng (*Panax quinquefolius*).** Roughly 300 years of
wild-harvest export pressure underlie its CITES Appendix II listing.
Herbarium-record analysis documents an apparent 150-year decline in wild
American ginseng abundance across 19 US states. A simulation study
("Consequences of harvesting for genetic diversity in American ginseng,"
*Biodiversity and Conservation*) found that random harvest at legal-limit
levels, and harvest restricted to mature plants specifically, significantly
reduces within-population genetic diversity (notably allelic richness).
USGS harvest-trend analysis of state-level export data (1978–2014, 18
eastern states) examines harvest patterns as a decline indicator. Current
threat framing treats harvest, deer herbivory, and climate change as
interacting pressures rather than harvest alone.

## The honorable harvest and Indigenous reciprocity frameworks

Robin Wall Kimmerer (SUNY-ESF; enrolled member, Citizen Potawatomi Nation)
articulates the "honorable harvest" in *Braiding Sweetgrass: Indigenous
Wisdom, Scientific Knowledge and the Teachings of Plants* (Milkweed
Editions, 2013) — a trade/practitioner book, not a peer-reviewed
publication. The honorable harvest is a reciprocity-based ethic: take only
what is given; ask permission before taking; use everything taken; never
take the first plant found or the last one present; and sustain the ones
who sustain you. Kimmerer gives the empirical example that traditional
sweetgrass-harvesting techniques measurably increase, rather than reduce,
subsequent plant vigor — i.e., a properly practiced harvest can improve
population outcomes relative to no harvest at all, not merely limit damage
relative to unrestrained harvest. [This claim, as reported in *Braiding
Sweetgrass*, is Indigenous-knowledge/practitioner literature rather than an
independently verified peer-reviewed finding in the source literature
review, though it is increasingly cited within peer-reviewed conservation
science as a traditional-ecological-knowledge (TEK) framework.]

The closest peer-reviewed academic analogue to the honorable harvest is
Nancy Turner's documented Kwakwaka'wakw harvesting-protocol research
(interviews and harvesting demonstrations with 18 knowledge holders),
which shows selective and rotational harvest techniques that historically
sustained, and in some cases enhanced, populations of 100–200 species
across northwestern North America. See [ethnobotany](./ethnobotany.md).
