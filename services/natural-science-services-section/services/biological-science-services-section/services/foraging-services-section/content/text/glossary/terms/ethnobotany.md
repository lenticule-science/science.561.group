Ethnobotany is the academic discipline concerned with the documented
relationship between people and plants. Within it, a specific sub-literature
on "wild food plants" (WFPs) sits between ethnobotany proper, nutrition
science, and conservation biology, and supplies most of
[foraging](./foraging.md)'s peer-reviewed evidentiary base — as distinct
from the larger practitioner-authored field-guide literature the practice
also relies on.

## Key contemporary authors

**Łukasz Łuczaj** is the most prolific contemporary peer-reviewed author on
European wild food ethnobotany. His core papers include:

- Łuczaj, Ł. & Szymański, W.M. (2007). "Wild vascular plants gathered for
  consumption in the Polish countryside: a review." *Journal of
  Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine*, 3:17. Reviews 42 ethnographic/botanical
  sources and records 112 species (3.7% of the Polish flora) used
  historically as food.
- Łuczaj, Ł. (2008). "Archival data on wild food plants used in Poland in
  1948." *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine*, 4:4.
- Łuczaj, Ł. (2012). "Ethnobotanical review of wild edible plants of
  Slovakia." *Acta Societatis Botanicorum Poloniae*, 81(4). Documents 106
  species (>3% of the Slovak flora) drawn from 24 sources.
- Łuczaj, Ł. et al. (2012). "Wild food plant use in 21st century Europe:
  the disappearance of old traditions and the search for new cuisines
  involving wild edibles." *Acta Societatis Botanicorum Poloniae*, 81(4).

**Nancy J. Turner** (Distinguished Professor Emerita, School of
Environmental Studies, University of Victoria) is the leading
peer-reviewed ethnobotanist of North American Indigenous wild food
systems, with 50+ years of fieldwork alongside First Nations elders. Key
works include *Ancient Pathways, Ancestral Knowledge: Ethnobotany and
Ecological Wisdom of Indigenous Peoples of Northwestern North America*
(2 vols., McGill-Queen's University Press, 2014) and *Food Plants of
Coastal First Peoples* / *Food Plants of Interior First Peoples*
(UBC Press/Royal BC Museum), which document 100–150+ traditionally
harvested species along with harvest and preparation methods. Turner's
peer-reviewed work on Kwakwaka'wakw traditional harvesting protocols
(interviews with 18 knowledge holders plus harvesting demonstrations)
documents selective-harvest management of 100–200 food and utility
species — the closest peer-reviewed academic analogue to the Indigenous
"honorable harvest" framing discussed at
[sustainable-harvest](./sustainable-harvest.md).

## Wild-edible nutrition literature

A substantial and growing body of nutritional-composition research on wild
edible plants exists, but it is dominated by regional case studies from
Africa and Asia rather than forming a unified temperate-North-American or
European corpus. Representative work: Rumicha et al. (2025), "Food, Feed,
and Phytochemical Uses of Wild Edible Plants: A Systematic Review," *Food
Science & Nutrition*; and district-level Ethiopian studies (Tach Gaint,
Simada, Dibatie, Southwest Ethiopia) reporting protein content of
4.0–21.7%, fat 0.7–6.1%, fiber 8.9–22.3%, and micronutrient ranges (Ca
3.7–594.8 mg/100g; K 440.6–1487.8 mg/100g; Fe 0.8–38.5 mg/100g) generally
comparable to, or exceeding, cultivated analogues. [UNVERIFIED as a general
claim beyond the cited regional studies: whether these nutritional patterns
generalize to temperate wild edibles has not been established by a
comparable body of literature.]

## Boundary with practitioner canon

Ethnobotany's peer-reviewed literature is a documentation and analysis
discipline; it is distinct from the practitioner field-guide literature
(Samuel Thayer, John Kallas) that most working foragers rely on for
identification and preparation instruction. See [foraging](./foraging.md)
for the fuller discussion of this two-tier evidentiary structure.
