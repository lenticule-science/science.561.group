Foraging in the United States is governed by a patchwork of federal, state,
and municipal rules rather than any single statute, and — until a 2018 law
review article — this patchwork had received essentially no sustained
legal-scholarship attention despite foraging's growing popularity.

## Foundational legal scholarship

Linnekin, B.J. (2018), "Food Law Gone Wild: The Law of Foraging," *Fordham
Urban Law Journal*, 45(4), pp. 995–1044, is described in the source
literature review as the first comprehensive legal treatment of National
Park Service regulations, state foraging statutes, and municipal foraging
ordinances. The article documents that, as of publication, legal and
social-science scholarship on foraging was "virtually nonexistent" despite
its growing popularity and the wide, often-conflicting variation across
federal, state, and local rules. It also traces a three-pronged history of
foraging restrictions historically targeted at Indigenous peoples, formerly
enslaved Black Americans, and poor rural white communities, and argues for
stronger legal protection of foraging access.

## Federal regulatory landscape

No single federal "foraging law" exists; regulation is layered across
National Park Service (NPS), US Forest Service (USFS), and Bureau of Land
Management (BLM) rules, themselves layered under property, resource
management, and criminal law. [The specifics below are drawn from secondary
and practitioner sources that corroborate Linnekin's descriptive claims but
are not themselves peer-reviewed.]

**National Park Service.** Foraging — of plants, fungi, wildlife, and even
natural objects such as rocks or antlers — is prohibited in nearly all
national parks and monuments by default. Individual park superintendents
may designate limited personal-consumption exceptions (for example,
specific berries or mushrooms) through each park's site-specific
"Compendium." Penalties can reach $5,000 in fines and up to six months'
imprisonment for a first offense.

**National Forests (USFS).** Generally more permissive than NPS land:
personal-use gathering of berries, mushrooms, and similar resources is
typically allowed without a permit on most forest land. Harvesting certain
resources (firewood, Christmas trees) may require a free or low-cost
permit. Commercial-quantity collection requires a paid permit.

**BLM lands.** Similarly lenient for personal use; commercial harvesting
requires authorization.

**State and municipal law.** Varies widely and is documented in
state-by-state secondary compilations rather than a single authoritative
source; Linnekin's law-review article is the primary academic work that
actually catalogs this variation systematically rather than merely
restating it.

## Relation to sustainable harvest and land access

Foraging law intersects with, but is analytically distinct from, the
ecological sustainable-harvest question documented at
[sustainable-harvest.md](./sustainable-harvest.md): a harvest can be
legal but ecologically unsustainable, or ecologically sustainable but
illegal on a given parcel of land. Linnekin's historical argument — that
foraging restriction has functioned as a tool of exclusion against
Indigenous, Black, and poor rural communities specifically — gives the
legal-access question its own independent ethical weight distinct from
the conservation rationale usually cited for restricting foraging.
