This is a study of foraging as a subject of formal research, read for
one purpose: to establish, for each of five sub-literatures —
ethnobotany and wild-food-plant science, wild-food nutrition,
phenology/seasonality science, sustainable-harvest science, and US
foraging law — which claims trace to peer-reviewed sources and which
trace to practitioner canon (field guides, trade books, extension
bulletins), so that later terms and notes in this section can cite the
right kind of source for the right kind of claim. Method: a literature
search compiled into a research report (`report-1.md`, 2026-08-22),
read here for named authors, years, and DOIs/PubMed IDs rather than
restated as a topic summary; every load-bearing claim below carries
its source, and every claim the underlying report itself could not
verify to the primary-source level is carried forward marked
**[UNVERIFIED]** rather than laundered into fact. This section already
defines the terms *foraging*, *ethnobotany*, *poisonous-lookalike*,
*universal-edibility-test*, *sustainable-harvest*,
*foraging-law-united-states*, and *foraging-calendar*; this note does
not redefine them, only supplies the literature that stands behind
them.

## 1. Ethnobotany and the wild-food-plant sub-literature

Wild food use is studied academically within ethnobotany, with a
specific sub-literature on "wild food plants" (WFPs) sitting between
ethnobotany, nutrition science, and conservation biology. Two authors
anchor the peer-reviewed core of this sub-literature for different
regions.

**Łukasz Łuczaj** is the most prolific contemporary peer-reviewed
author on European wild-food ethnobotany. His method across the core
papers is consistent: systematic review of historical/ethnographic
sources cross-checked against field botany, reported as species counts
against the regional flora. Łuczaj & Szymański (2007), "Wild vascular
plants gathered for consumption in the Polish countryside: a review,"
*Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine* 3:17 (DOI:
[10.1186/1746-4269-3-17](https://ethnobiomed.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1746-4269-3-17)),
reviews 42 ethnographic/botanical sources and records 112 species
(3.7% of Polish flora) historically used as food. Łuczaj (2008),
"Archival data on wild food plants used in Poland in 1948," *Journal
of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine* 4:4
([PubMed 18218132](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18218132/)), and
Łuczaj (2012), "Ethnobotanical review of wild edible plants of
Slovakia," *Acta Societatis Botanicorum Poloniae* 81(4)
([pbsociety.org.pl](https://pbsociety.org.pl/journals/index.php/asbp/article/view/asbp.2012.030/0)),
apply the same method to Poland's 1948 wartime-era foraging and to
Slovakia (106 species, >3% of the flora, from 24 sources). Łuczaj et
al. (2012), "Wild food plant use in 21st century Europe: the
disappearance of old traditions and the search for new cuisines
involving wild edibles," *Acta Societatis Botanicorum Poloniae* 81(4)
([pbsociety.org.pl](https://pbsociety.org.pl/journals/index.php/asbp/article/view/asbp.2012.031)),
extends the method to a continental synthesis and documents both
decline in traditional practice and a countervailing revival in new
foraging-driven cuisine.

**Nancy J. Turner** (Distinguished Professor Emerita, School of
Environmental Studies, University of Victoria) is the leading
peer-reviewed ethnobotanist of North American Indigenous wild-food
systems, with 50+ years of fieldwork alongside First Nations elders.
*Ancient Pathways, Ancestral Knowledge: Ethnobotany and Ecological
Wisdom of Indigenous Peoples of Northwestern North America* (2 vols.,
McGill-Queen's University Press, 2014) and *Food Plants of Coastal
First Peoples* / *Food Plants of Interior First Peoples* (UBC
Press/Royal BC Museum) document 100–150+ traditionally harvested
species with harvest and preparation methods. Turner's peer-reviewed
work on Kwakwaka'wakw traditional harvesting protocols — interviews
with 18 knowledge holders plus harvesting demonstrations — documents
selective-harvest management of 100–200 food/utility species; this is
the closest peer-reviewed academic analogue to the Indigenous
"honorable harvest" ethic discussed in §4 below (summarized via
[ResearchGate: "To Get More Harvest"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380394199_To_Get_More_Harvest_Natural_Systems_Cultural_Values_and_Indigenous_Resource_Management_in_Northwestern_North_America)).

**Practitioner canon, distinct in method and evidentiary standard.**
Samuel Thayer, a self-taught forager-author with three decades of
teaching, is the field's most widely cited practitioner author: *The
Forager's Harvest* (2006), *Nature's Garden* (2010), *Incredible Wild
Edibles* (2017), and the 2023 *Field Guide to Edible Wild Plants of
Eastern and Central North America* (National Outdoor Book Award
winner) ([foragersharvest.com](https://www.foragersharvest.com/sams-books.html)).
John Kallas, PhD (nutrition), founder of the Institute for the Study
of Edible Wild Plants and Wild Food Adventures (Portland, OR, est.
1993), occupies an intermediate position: his doctorate in nutrition
makes his practitioner writing unusually adjacent to peer-reviewed
nutrition science, but the books (*Edible Wild Plants*, vols. 1–2) are
themselves field guides grounded in personal field experience and
secondary literature review, not peer-reviewed publications. This
report's own summary table names the distinction plainly: the
peer-reviewed core is Łuczaj (*J. Ethnobiology & Ethnomedicine*, *Acta
Soc. Bot. Pol.*) and Turner (academic monographs, peer-reviewed TEK
papers); the practitioner canon is Thayer and Kallas (field guides).

## 2. Wild-food nutrition literature

A growing peer-reviewed body of nutritional-composition studies
exists, but it is structurally lopsided: it is mostly regional case
studies from Africa and Asia rather than a unified corpus for the
temperate North American/European species most relevant to US
foraging. Rumicha et al. (2025), "Food, Feed, and Phytochemical Uses of
Wild Edible Plants: A Systematic Review," *Food Science & Nutrition*
(DOI: [10.1002/fsn3.70454](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/fsn3.70454);
also [PMC12177425](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12177425/))
is the closest thing to a synthesis found. Regional studies from
Ethiopia (Tach Gaint district, [PMC10593547](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10593547/);
Simada district, [PMC12061528](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12061528/);
Dibatie district, [PMC12643722](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12643722/);
Southwest Ethiopia, [PMC10220415](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10220415/))
report protein 4.0–21.7%, fat 0.7–6.1%, fiber 8.9–22.3%, and
micronutrient ranges (Ca 3.7–594.8 mg/100g; K 440.6–1487.8 mg/100g; Fe
0.8–38.5 mg/100g) generally comparable to or exceeding cultivated
analogues — a real finding, but one whose generalization to
temperate-zone wild edibles (ramps, nettles, lamb's quarters, etc.) is
not itself established in the literature surveyed here. "Wild edible
plants of Northeast India: A tripartite review of nutritional,
pharmacological and market value" ([Ethnobotany Research and
Applications](https://ethnobotanyjournal.org/index.php/era/article/view/8243))
is the same pattern again: real peer-reviewed nutrition science, wrong
biogeography for direct application to this section's likely species
list.

## 3. Phenology and seasonality science

The USA National Phenology Network (USA-NPN) is the principal US
infrastructure for phenological observation — aggregating volunteer
(citizen-science) and professional data into predictive models of
species' life-stage timing, which is directly relevant to
harvest-window prediction for foraged species. Its foundational
peer-reviewed paper, Crimmins, Crimmins, Gerst, Rosemartin & Weltzin
(2017), "USA National Phenology Network's volunteer-contributed
observations yield predictive models of phenological transitions,"
*PLOS ONE* 12(8):e0182919 (DOI:
[10.1371/journal.pone.0182919](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0182919);
[PMC5568737](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568737/)), is
built on a dataset of 10+ million observations.

The literature also establishes, at climatology scale, why any fixed
foraging calendar degrades over time: Schwartz et al.'s spring-index
climatology (cited via
[NOAA Climate.gov](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/event-tracker/how-spring-plant-milestones-are-shifting-response-changing-climate))
found Northern Hemisphere first-leaf and first-bloom dates advancing
1.2 and 1.0 days/decade respectively over 1955–2002, with western-US
leaf-out advancing ~1.5 days/decade over 1950–2005; IPCC AR5
synthesizes this to spring events shifting ~1–3 days earlier per
decade Northern-Hemisphere-wide, species-dependent. A second,
independent mechanism compounds the calendar-reliability problem
rather than simply shifting it: Allstadt et al. and related work on
"false springs," *Environmental Research Letters* 10:104008
([IOPscience](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/10/10/104008)),
documents increasing risk of early leaf-out followed by killing frost
across the conterminous US during the 21st century — meaning a
foraging calendar that simply shifts dates earlier to track mean
climate change is not sufficient; variance around the mean is also
increasing. Recent downscaled projections extend this work
regionally ([PMC11694835](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11694835/)).
Documented pollen-season lengthening — 1990–2020 onset advancing 9–18
days (hazel), 5–13 days (oak), 8–25 days (grasses), 6–25 days
(nettle), with hazel's season lengthening 21–104% — is cited in the
underlying report as a phenology proxy evidencing the same mechanism,
not itself a foraging metric.

## 4. Sustainable-harvest science

This is the sub-literature with the most directly load-bearing
numbers for a `sustainable-harvest` term, because it contains
controlled or quasi-controlled recovery experiments rather than only
observational correlation.

**Ramps (*Allium tricoccum*).** Rock, Beckage & Gross (2004),
"Population recovery following differential harvesting of Allium
tricoccum Ait. in the southern Appalachians," *Biological Conservation*
([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0006320703001939);
summarized via [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228407707_Population_recovery_following_differential_harvesting_of_Allium_tricoccum_Ait_in_the_southern_Appalachians)),
is a multi-year experimental-harvest study in Great Smoky Mountains
National Park at 0/25/50/75/100% harvest fractions. Its finding is the
mechanism, not just a rule of thumb: population recovery time scales
sharply nonlinearly with harvest fraction — roughly 2.5 years to
recover from a 5% harvest, but roughly 150 years to recover from
~95–100% harvest, with a 25% harvest requiring roughly 20 years. The
widely repeated "10% harvest, once per decade" guideline is attributed
by [United Plant Savers](https://unitedplantsavers.org/ramps-allium-tricoccum/)
to a separate University of Tennessee 7-year study, not directly to
Rock/Beckage/Gross, so the two figures should not be conflated as one
finding. The mechanism behind the slow recovery is demographic: ramp
population growth is driven mainly by slow asexual (clonal)
reproduction (5–8 years to reproductive maturity) rather than by seed
reproduction, per the demographic study cited at United Plant Savers;
*A. tricoccum* is listed "Special Concern" in Maine and Rhode Island
and "Commercially Exploited" in Tennessee. A 2022 *Agroforestry
Systems* paper (DOI via
[Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10457-022-00790-3))
adds a harvest-timing lever independent of harvest fraction: total
plant/bulb weight increases 250%/400% from early to late season,
implying later-season harvest can reduce plant-count impact for the
same harvested weight.

**American ginseng (*Panax quinquefolius*).** CITES Appendix II
listing reflects roughly 300 years of wild-harvest export pressure. A
herbarium-record decline study documents an apparent 150-year decline
in wild abundance across 19 US states from historical specimen data
([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0006320706003120)).
A simulation study, "Consequences of harvesting for genetic diversity
in American ginseng (Panax quinquefolius L.)," *Biodiversity and
Conservation* (DOI via
[Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10531-004-7308-7)),
found that random harvest at legal-limit levels, and harvest
restricted to mature plants specifically, both significantly reduce
within-population genetic diversity (notably allelic richness) — a
mechanism distinct from simple population-count decline, since a
population can persist in raw numbers while losing genetic diversity.
USGS harvest-trend analysis of state-level export data (1978–2014,
18 eastern states,
[usgs.gov](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/explaining-harvests-wild-harvested-herbaceous-plants-american-ginseng-a-case-study))
examines harvest patterns as a decline indicator; current framing per
United Plant Savers treats harvest, deer herbivory, and climate change
as interacting threats rather than harvest alone
([unitedplantsavers.org](https://unitedplantsavers.org/population-distribution-and-threats-of-american-ginseng-panax-quinquefolius-l-in-indiana-and-illinois/)).

**Indigenous harvesting protocols and "honorable harvest."** Robin
Wall Kimmerer (SUNY-ESF; enrolled member, Citizen Potawatomi Nation),
*Braiding Sweetgrass: Indigenous Wisdom, Scientific Knowledge and the
Teachings of Plants* (Milkweed Editions, 2013), is practitioner/trade
literature, not peer-reviewed, whose "Honorable Harvest" chapter
articulates a reciprocity ethic — take only what is given, ask
permission, use everything taken, never take the first or the last,
sustain the ones who sustain you — and reports the empirical claim
that traditional sweetgrass harvesting techniques measurably increase,
rather than reduce, subsequent plant vigor. This claim is reported
here as Kimmerer states it; the underlying report did not independently
verify the sweetgrass-vigor finding against a peer-reviewed source, so
it is carried as a practitioner-literature claim, not confirmed
science. Its peer-reviewed analogue is Turner's Kwakwaka'wakw
harvesting-protocol research (§1 above): selective/rotational
techniques shown, via 18 knowledge-holder interviews and harvesting
demonstrations, to have historically sustained or enhanced populations
of 100–200 species. *Braiding Sweetgrass* is nonetheless treated in
the underlying report as foundational to the field's applied
literature, on the same footing as Thayer and Kallas in §1 — a
practitioner canon this section's terms should cite as such, not as a
substitute for the peer-reviewed TEK literature it parallels.

## 5. US foraging law

Linnekin, B.J. (2018), "Food Law Gone Wild: The Law of Foraging,"
*Fordham Urban Law Journal* 45(4), pp. 995–1044
([Fordham LawNet full text](https://ir.lawnet.fordham.edu/ulj/vol45/iss4/3/)),
is the foundational and, per the underlying report, the *only*
identified peer-reviewed/law-review-level legal-scholarship treatment
of foraging law — described in the article itself as the first
comprehensive legal treatment of National Park Service regulations,
state foraging statutes, and municipal foraging ordinances, noting
that legal/social-science scholarship on foraging was, at the time of
publication, "virtually nonexistent" despite foraging's growing
popularity and the wide, often-conflicting variation across
federal/state/local rules. The article also traces a three-pronged
history of foraging restrictions historically targeted at Indigenous
peoples, formerly enslaved Black Americans, and poor rural white
communities, and argues for stronger legal protection of foraging
access.

The federal regulatory landscape Linnekin describes is corroborated,
but not independently established, by practitioner/secondary sources:
no single federal "foraging law" exists; regulation is a patchwork
under National Park Service (NPS), US Forest Service (USFS), and
Bureau of Land Management (BLM) rules layered under property,
resource-management, and criminal law. NPS foraging (plants, fungi,
wildlife, even natural objects such as rocks or antlers) is prohibited
by default in nearly all national parks and monuments, with individual
superintendents able to designate limited personal-consumption
exceptions via each park's site-specific "Compendium"; penalties can
reach $5,000 and six months' imprisonment for a first offense
([The Counter](https://thecounter.org/the-case-for-legalizing-foraging-in-national-parks/)).
USFS national-forest land is generally more permissive for personal-use
gathering without a permit, and BLM land is similarly lenient for
personal use, with commercial-quantity collection requiring
authorization on both. State and municipal law varies widely and is
catalogued mainly in secondary state-by-state compilations rather than
in further legal scholarship — Linnekin's article is, per the
underlying report, the primary academic source that actually catalogs
this variation rather than merely restating it.

## What this study could not verify

Carried forward directly from the underlying report's own gaps
section, unresolved by this note:

- No peer-reviewed clinical case-report literature on moonseed
  (*Menispermum canadense*) poisoning was located; identification and
  toxicity claims for that species rest on extension/horticultural
  secondary sources rather than a clinical case series. (This matters
  for the `poisonous-lookalike` term specifically — see the companion
  note on identification safety.)
- Exact current-year AAPCC/NPDS plant- and mushroom-exposure counts
  were not extracted from full-text data tables of a specific annual
  report; only aggregate/summary figures were available via abstracts,
  and the annual reports themselves are paywalled behind *Clinical
  Toxicology*.
- A unified peer-reviewed nutritional-composition literature specific
  to temperate North American or European wild edible plants — the
  species most relevant to US foraging — is thin; most located
  nutrition studies are regional case studies from Ethiopia, India, and
  other non-temperate regions, and their quantitative findings should
  not be assumed to transfer directly to temperate-zone species.
- Pokeweed (*Phytolacca americana*) poisoning documentation located in
  the underlying report is grounded in state extension and
  poison-center clinical guidance rather than an identified
  peer-reviewed clinical case series.
- The underlying report could not fetch the SSRN copy of Linnekin's
  article directly (403 error); its citation details were
  cross-confirmed via the Fordham LawNet repository instead, which is
  the authoritative host copy, so this gap does not weaken the
  citation itself.
- Kimmerer's empirical claim that traditional sweetgrass-harvesting
  technique increases subsequent plant vigor is reported as stated in
  *Braiding Sweetgrass* and was not independently checked against a
  peer-reviewed source in the underlying report; it should be treated
  as a practitioner-literature claim pending that check.
