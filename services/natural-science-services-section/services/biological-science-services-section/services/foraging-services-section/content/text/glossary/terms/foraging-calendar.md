A foraging calendar is a schedule, formal or informal, of when a given wild
species is in its harvestable state — leaf-out, flowering, fruiting, or
root maturity, depending on the species and the plant part sought. Foraging
calendars are an applied use of phenology, the ecological study of the
timing of life-cycle events, and so are canonically dependent on
phenology's science while being themselves a practice concept: this section
owns the harvest-timing application, ecology owns the underlying
phenological science.

## Phenological infrastructure

The USA National Phenology Network (USA-NPN) is the principal US
infrastructure for phenological observation, aggregating volunteer
(citizen-science) and professional data to build predictive models of
species' life-stage timing — directly relevant to harvest-window
prediction for foraged species. The foundational peer-reviewed paper is
Crimmins, T.M., Crimmins, M.A., Gerst, K.L., Rosemartin, A.H., & Weltzin,
J.F. (2017), "USA National Phenology Network's volunteer-contributed
observations yield predictive models of phenological transitions," *PLOS
ONE*, 12(8):e0182919, built on a dataset of over 10 million observations. A
USGS fact sheet, "The USA National Phenology Network; taking the pulse of
our planet," summarizes the program's design.

## Climate-driven erosion of calendar reliability

A body of peer-reviewed climatology documents that phenological timing is
shifting under climate change, which directly undermines the reliability
of any fixed foraging calendar over multi-decade timescales:

- Foundational spring-phenology climatology (Schwartz, M.D. et al., cited
  via a NOAA Climate.gov synthesis) found first-leaf and first-bloom dates
  in the Northern Hemisphere advanced 1.2 and 1.0 days per decade
  respectively over 1955–2002; in the western US specifically, leaf-out
  advanced roughly 1.5 days per decade over 1950–2005.
- The IPCC Fifth Assessment Report's synthesis found spring events shifting
  roughly 1–3 days earlier per decade across the Northern Hemisphere, with
  magnitude depending on species.
- Research on "false springs" (Allstadt et al. and related work, "Spring
  plant phenology and false springs in the conterminous US during the 21st
  century," *Environmental Research Letters* 10:104008) documents
  increasing risk of early leaf-out followed by damaging frost — directly
  relevant to foraging-calendar reliability, since an early-triggered
  harvest window can be followed by a frost event that damages or kills the
  expected harvest.
- Recent downscaled climate projections extend this analysis to future
  phenological change across the contiguous United States.
- As a phenology proxy illustrating the same climate-shift mechanism (not
  itself a foraging metric), documented pollen-season lengthening over
  1990–2020 found pollen onset advancing 9–18 days (hazel), 5–13 days
  (oak), 8–25 days (grasses), and 6–25 days (nettle), with hazel's overall
  pollen season lengthening 21–104%.

## Practical implication

Together, this literature indicates that a static foraging calendar — a
fixed "ramps come up in early April" rule, for instance — degrades in
accuracy over time as climate shifts advance or destabilize spring timing.
Phenological-network data (USA-NPN's "Nature's Notebook" observational
tools) functions as a hybrid citizen-science/research infrastructure that
can, in principle, replace fixed calendar dates with locally current
observation, an approach structurally similar to the citizen-science field
identification this section relates to via `inaturalist-services-section`.
