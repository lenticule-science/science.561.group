A poisonous lookalike is a toxic plant or fungal species that a forager can
mistake for an edible species because the two share gross field
characters — overall habit, leaf shape, root form, or fruiting structure —
even though they differ taxonomically and in toxicity. Misidentification of
lookalikes is the central safety hazard of [foraging](./foraging.md), and
the clinical/toxicological literature documenting it is uneven: some
pairings rest on peer-reviewed case series and national surveillance data,
others rest only on extension-service or horticultural secondary sources.
This entry documents both tiers and flags the gap explicitly, per house
practice on carrying forward unverified claims rather than laundering them
into fact.

## Documented pairings

**Poison hemlock (*Conium maculatum*) vs. wild carrot, parsnip, and
celery.** *Conium maculatum* is repeatedly documented in clinical
literature as confusable with cultivated and wild members of Apiaceae due
to shared family resemblance and a carrot-like taproot. A 2019 literature
review and case report ("Conium maculatum intoxication," PubMed 30594904)
and a *Cureus* case report ("Acute Intoxication With Poison Hemlock,"
PMC12005235) document severe neurotoxicosis — tremor, ataxia,
hypersalivation, muscle/respiratory paralysis, rhabdomyolysis, acute renal
failure — that can begin within 15 minutes of ingestion; one documented
case involved a 6-year-old child hospitalized after ingestion.
Extension-service guidance corroborates the botanical confusability
(University of Illinois Extension, 2024; USDA-ARS poisonous-plant research
profile).

**Amanita species vs. edible mushrooms.** This is the best-surveilled
pairing in the literature. Diaz (2005), "Syndromic diagnosis and management
of confirmed mushroom poisonings," *Critical Care Medicine* 33(2):427-436,
reviews 1959–2002 data describing 28,018 mushroom poisonings recorded since
1951 and classifies poisoning syndromes including accelerated
nephrotoxicity, rhabdomyolysis, erythromelalgia, and delayed neurotoxicity.
CDC MMWR has published repeated outbreak reports on *Amanita phalloides*
(death cap) poisoning in Northern California; the most recent documented
outbreak (November 2025–March 2026) recorded 39 cases, 3 liver
transplants, and 4 deaths in the initial MMWR report, later tallied by the
California Poison Control System at 47 cases / 4 deaths / 4 transplants
through May 2026 across Humboldt-to-San Luis Obispo counties. Amatoxin
mushrooms cause roughly 50 deaths per year in Europe and Asia but only 1–2
per year on average in the US, a difference attributed to foraging
prevalence rather than differential toxicity (as cited in an UpToDate
summary of the clinical literature, a secondary source);
*Amanita phalloides* is non-native to North America, believed introduced
via contaminated nursery soil, and is now established on the US west
coast, mid-Atlantic, and northeast. National-scale morbidity data (CDC
MMWR 70(10), 2021) found approximately 1,328 emergency-department visits
and 100 hospitalizations in 2016 alone for accidental poisonous-mushroom
ingestion, with roughly 7,500 poisonous-mushroom ingestions reported
annually to US poison control centers, and severe poisonings from foraged
mushrooms rising over 1999–2016.

**Death camas (*Toxicoscordion venenosum*) vs. wild onion (*Allium
canadense*).** A case series documents seven family members (5 children, 2
adults) poisoned after misidentifying death camas bulbs as wild onion,
with onset 30–60 minutes and a progression from nausea/vomiting/abdominal
pain to bradycardia and hypotension caused by the alkaloid zygacine
(alpha-blockade, sodium-channel activation, vagal stimulation); zygacine is
heat-stable and not destroyed by cooking. The same literature reports the
identification differentiator: wild onion has a distinct onion odor and
flat leaves, while death camas lacks onion smell, tastes bitter, and has a
V-shaped leaf cross-section.

**Pokeweed (*Phytolacca americana*) vs. parsnip/horseradish.** Only very
young spring shoots (pre-red/purple coloration, under 6 inches, before
branching) are traditionally regarded as edible after preparation;
poisoning most often results from harvesting mature stalks, leaves, or
root fragments (the most toxic part) mistaken for parsnip or horseradish
root. [This pairing's documentation is grounded mainly in state
agricultural-extension and poison-center clinical guidance — West Virginia
Department of Agriculture, Vanderbilt/Tennessee Poison Center — rather than
a peer-reviewed clinical case series located in the source literature
review; flagged accordingly.]

**Moonseed (*Menispermum canadense*) vs. wild grape.** *Menispermum
canadense* fruit closely resembles wild grape (*Vitis* spp.) but contains
isoquinoline alkaloids (notably dauricine) throughout the plant, reported
to cause seizures, abdominal cramps, nausea, and cardiac arrhythmia,
potentially fatal especially in children. [UNVERIFIED at the peer-reviewed
level: no peer-reviewed clinical case report on moonseed poisoning was
located in the source literature review. The toxicological and
identification claims here — a single crescent-shaped seed versus grape's
round seeds, smooth versus toothed leaf margins, absence versus presence of
tendrils — rest on horticultural and extension-service secondary sources
(Missouri Department of Conservation field guide; Birds & Blooms) and
should be treated as less certain than the hemlock, Amanita, and death-camas
entries above.]

## Surveillance infrastructure

The American Association of Poison Control Centers (AAPCC) National
Poison Data System (NPDS) is the standard US surveillance instrument for
plant and mushroom exposure counts, published annually in *Clinical
Toxicology* (e.g. the 38th Annual Report, for 2020). All roughly 55 US
poison centers upload case data to NPDS automatically. [Exact current-year
plant/mushroom exposure subtotals were not extracted from full-text NPDS
