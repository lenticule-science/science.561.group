**Gua sha** (刮痧, guāshā) is a traditional East Asian surface treatment in which a practitioner uses a smooth-edged instrument to press-stroke lubricated skin in repeated, usually unidirectional passes. In many body-treatment forms, pressure is sufficient to raise visible **sha**: red to purple petechiae and ecchymosis that fade over several days.

The name joins **gua** (刮, to scrape or rub) with **sha** (痧), a historically broader medical term. Sha is not simply the modern biomedical word for bruising. In Chinese medical history it named a family of signs and acute illness presentations as well as the cutaneous response elicited or interpreted during treatment.

## Within Chinese medicine

In Chinese medical practice, gua sha is used within pattern diagnosis rather than as an isolated mechanical procedure. It is commonly associated with moving constrained Qi and Blood, opening the channels, releasing exterior pathogenic influence, and addressing pain or stiffness where obstruction is clinically inferred.

Traditional interpretation may attend to where sha appears, how readily it appears, its color and distribution, the patient's constitution, and the presenting pattern. These claims belong to Chinese medicine's own diagnostic vocabulary and should not be treated as direct translations of biomedical anatomy or physiology.

## Material procedure

A typical body-treatment sequence uses a lubricant and a rounded tool made from horn, ceramic, stone, metal, wood, or another smooth material. Contemporary commercial tools are often jade or quartz, but those materials are not what defines the therapy. Pressure, direction, treatment region, duration, and the decision whether to raise visible sha vary by lineage, indication, setting, and practitioner.

The modern cosmetic practice usually called **facial gua sha** is materially related but clinically distinct. Facial practice typically uses much lighter pressure and ordinarily avoids petechiae. Claims about sculpting, lymphatic drainage, wrinkles, or collagen therefore require separate evaluation rather than inheriting the evidence base for traditional body gua sha.

## Biomedical description

The visible marks intentionally produced in body gua sha are petechiae and ecchymosis representing **extravasation of blood in superficial tissue**. A small 2007 laser-Doppler study in 11 healthy participants documented a marked short-term increase in surface microcirculation after treatment [@nielsen2007]. That establishes a physiological response to the mechanical procedure; it does not establish a particular disease mechanism or validate every traditional indication.

Clinical research remains limited. Small randomized trials have reported short-term benefit for chronic neck and low-back pain, while reviews note small samples, difficult blinding, heterogeneous protocols, and uncertain long-term effects.

## Safety

Because treatment can intentionally extravasate blood, reusable tools can become contaminated even when the skin looks intact. Infection-control literature therefore recommends appropriate disinfection for reusable gua sha tools [@nielsen2012]. Treatment should avoid broken, infected, severely inflamed, or otherwise vulnerable skin.
