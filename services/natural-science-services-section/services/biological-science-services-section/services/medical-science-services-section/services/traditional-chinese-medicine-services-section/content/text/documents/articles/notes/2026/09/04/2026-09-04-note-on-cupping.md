Cupping (ba guan 拔罐, literally "pulling cups") is a therapeutic method in Traditional Chinese Medicine that draws stagnant [Qi](../../../../../../glossary/terms/qi.md) and [Blood](../../../../../../glossary/terms/xue.md) to the body's surface using suction created inside a sealed cup. Like [acupuncture](../../../acupuncture.md) and tui na, cupping works within the [meridian](../../../../../../glossary/terms/meridians.md) and organ-function system of TCM. The method is applied according to pattern diagnosis that has been established through the practitioner's assessment.

## Methods of cupping

Four distinct methods are used, each producing different therapeutic effects:

**Fire cupping (huo guan 火罐)** — The traditional method. A flame is briefly held inside the cup to create suction, then the cup is applied to the skin. The heat warms the area and opens the pores, allowing Qi and Blood to surface. Used for Cold patterns and to tonify Yang Qi.

**Wet cupping (ci guan 刺罐)** — After fire cupping, the skin is lightly scraped or pricked with a needle to bring a small amount of blood to the surface, then the cup is reapplied. This draws stagnant Blood more forcefully than dry cupping alone. Used primarily for severe Blood stasis patterns, acute pain, and conditions requiring aggressive drainage.

**Sliding cupping (tui guan 推罐)** — Oil is first applied to the skin, then a fire-cupped cup is moved across the surface of the skin along meridian lines or muscle groups. This combines the penetrating effect of cupping with the fascial release of massage. Used for muscular tension, meridian blockage, and extended regions of stagnation.

**Flash cupping (zhu guan 走罐)** — Rapid repeated application and removal of cups over the same area. The repeated stimulation creates a stronger effect than static cupping. Used for acute muscular pain and conditions requiring intensive stimulation in a brief period.

## Classical foundations

Cupping appears in the earliest foundational texts of Chinese medicine:

- The *Huang Di Nei Jing* (Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic, *Su Wen* and *Ling Shu*) discusses methods of drawing out pathogens and stagnation from the superficial tissues
- The *Zhenjiu Jiayi Jing* (Systematic Classic of Acupuncture and Moxibustion), compiled by Huangfu Mi in 259 CE, provides detailed cupping applications
- The *Minjian Bencao* (Materia Medica of Folk Medicine) and other Ming Dynasty texts standardized cupping techniques and applications

The practice remained central through the Qing Dynasty and remains standard in contemporary TCM practice across acupuncture schools.

## How cupping works in TCM terms

Cupping addresses stagnation — the condition in which Qi or Blood is no longer flowing smoothly through a meridian or region. Stagnation produces pain, swelling, and palpable tension. When Qi stagnates, Blood stagnates alongside it; when Blood stagnates, pain is prominent and bruising may appear. Cupping draws this stagnation upward toward the skin's surface where it can be resolved:

- The suction of the cup opens the tissue and pulls stagnant material toward the superficial layers
- The warmth of fire cupping expands the pores and relaxes the constrictive patterns that trap Qi
- The practitioner assesses the color and texture of the skin under the cup — deep purple, dark red, or raised welts indicate the degree of stagnation being drawn
- The *sha* (sha 痧) — the petechiae or red/purple discoloration — represents stagnant Qi and Blood being brought to where the body can clear it

The discoloration fades over days to weeks as the body reabsorbs and processes the mobilized Qi and Blood. The practitioner's skill includes knowing how long to leave the cup in place, which cupping method matches the pattern, and how to read the *sha* to assess whether the treatment is producing the desired effect.

## Ecchymosis mechanism

The visible bruising under cups (the *sha*) has been examined in biomedical research as a physiological phenomenon. Cupping creates a negative pressure gradient that draws blood toward the skin surface. The capillaries dilate in response to the suction, bringing red blood cells to the dermis and creating the characteristic discoloration. The darker the bruising, the greater the volume of blood drawn. This is not vessel rupture in the damaging sense but a controlled vasodilation and extravasation — the body's inflammatory response to the suction stimulus, which triggers healing mechanisms.

Biomedical research examining cupping's effects (Cao et al. 2012; Kim et al. 2011; Lauche et al. 2012) has documented that cupping increases local blood flow, produces measurable anti-inflammatory effects through increased prostaglandin and other signaling molecules, and in pain conditions shows efficacy approaching or exceeding other manual therapies in controlled trials. The mechanism from this perspective involves local tissue trauma triggering healing processes and autonomic effects that reduce pain perception. This is external commentary on the physiological processes underlying the TCM model, not an alternate explanation of how cupping works — the TCM model is one carving of the body's functional organization, and the biomedical model is another. Where they describe the same phenomenon (stagnant blood being mobilized, inflammation being cleared, tissue healing being accelerated), the descriptions are complementary readings of the same event from different theoretical frames.
