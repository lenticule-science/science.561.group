Ying Qi (營氣, Nutritive Qi or Construction Qi) is the refined, clear portion of the Qi extracted from food and drink that circulates inside the blood vessels, nourishing the internal organs and the whole body, and contributing directly to the formation of Blood. It is paired throughout TCM literature with Wei Qi (衛氣, Defensive Qi), the coarser, more mobile portion of the same source Qi that circulates outside the vessels and at the body's surface — the two together forming a single system distinguished by location and function rather than substance.

## Classical basis

The foundational description of both Ying Qi and Wei Qi, and of their shared origin and contrasting circulation, is given in the *Huang Di Nei Jing, Ling Shu*, Chapter 18, 營衛生會 (*Ying Wei Sheng Hui*, "The Meeting of Nutritive and Defensive Qi"): "人受氣於穀，穀入於胃，以傳與肺，五藏六府，皆以受氣，其清者為營，濁者為衛。營在脈中，衛在脈外" — a person receives Qi from grain; grain enters the stomach and is transmitted to the Lung; the five Zang and six Fu organs all receive Qi from it; its clear portion becomes Ying, its turbid portion becomes Wei; Ying travels inside the vessels, Wei travels outside the vessels. The same chapter gives Ying Qi's specific function: "營氣者，泌其津液，注之於脈，化以為血，以榮四末，內注五臟六腑" — Nutritive Qi secretes body fluid, pours it into the vessels, transforms it into Blood, nourishes the four limbs, and flows inward to irrigate the five Zang and six Fu organs.

## Relationship to Blood

Ying Qi's defining clinical significance is its direct participation in the formation of Blood — the *Ling Shu* passage above states explicitly that Ying Qi "transforms into Blood" (化以為血). TCM textbooks accordingly describe Ying Qi and Blood as closely related, near-interchangeable substances circulating together within the same vessels: where Qi in general is said to move Blood, and Blood is said to nourish Qi, Ying Qi in particular is described as inseparable from Blood in circulation, to the point that clinical patterns are sometimes named jointly as "Ying-Blood level" disorders, especially in the Four Levels (衛氣營血) framework used to track the progression of febrile disease from the body's surface to its interior.

## Circulation

Ying Qi circulates in a fixed, cyclical pathway through the twelve regular meridians, moving in sequence from one meridian to the next and completing a full circuit of the body in coordination with Wei Qi's separate, day-night-alternating circulation pattern. This orderly, unceasing circulation is described in the *Ling Shu* passage's closing image: "營周不休，五十而復大會，陰陽相貫，如環無端" — nutritive Qi circulates without cease, completing fifty circuits before the great reunion [of Ying and Wei], yin and yang penetrating each other continuously, like a ring without end.

## Clinical significance

Ying Qi deficiency presents similarly to Blood deficiency, given the two substances' close relationship: pallor, fatigue, numbness of the limbs, and poor nourishment of tissue generally. Because Ying Qi is drawn from the same source (Gu Qi, extracted by Spleen transformation) as Wei Qi, chronic Spleen dysfunction that impairs Yun Hua reduces both Ying and Wei Qi together, though a patient's presentation may emphasize one deficiency pattern over the other — poor surface defense and susceptibility to colds pointing toward Wei Qi weakness, versus pallor and numbness pointing toward Ying Qi/Blood weakness.

## Biomedical convergence, honestly graded

The Ying/Wei division — one portion of a shared source circulating within vessels and nourishing tissue, the other circulating outside vessels in a surface-defensive role — bears a loose structural resemblance to the biomedical distinction between the intravascular compartment (plasma proteins, circulating nutrients, and cells confined to the bloodstream) and tissue-level or surface-level immune and barrier function (skin, mucosa, innate surface defenses). This is, at most, a resonance of structure: TCM arrived at the Ying/Wei division from the observation that some qualities of vitality track with circulation-in-the-vessels and others track with the body's exterior resilience against invasion, not from any anatomical distinction between plasma and interstitial or surface compartments. The two frameworks are not describing the same substances by different names; they are two independent systems that both found it useful, for their own diagnostic purposes, to separate an "inside the vessel" function from an "at the surface" function.
