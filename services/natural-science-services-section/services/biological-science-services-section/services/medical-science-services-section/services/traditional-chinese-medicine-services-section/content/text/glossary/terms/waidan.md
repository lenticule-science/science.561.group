Waidan (外丹), external alchemy, is the Chinese laboratory art of making elixirs. It is "external" in contrast to [neidan](./neidan.md), inner alchemy. Its practitioners heated and transformed minerals in sealed crucibles in search of an elixir (dān, 丹, also "cinnabar") that would confer long life or immortality. The chief minerals were cinnabar (mercuric sulphide), mercury, lead, sulphur, and arsenic compounds.

Its classic early statement is Ge Hong's fourth-century *Baopuzi*, which ranks the elixir above every other method of transcendence. The art flourished through the Tang dynasty [@pregadio2008]. It was also dangerous. Several Tang emperors are believed to have died of elixir poisoning, and the toxicity of mercurial and arsenical preparations was part of why the language of the furnace, the ingredients, and the firing times turned inward. From the Song dynasty onward, neidan read that same language as a description of breath, attention, and the body's own [Three Treasures](./san-bao.md).

Waidan also left practical chemistry in Chinese pharmacy and technology. The early recipe for gunpowder is recorded in an alchemical context.
