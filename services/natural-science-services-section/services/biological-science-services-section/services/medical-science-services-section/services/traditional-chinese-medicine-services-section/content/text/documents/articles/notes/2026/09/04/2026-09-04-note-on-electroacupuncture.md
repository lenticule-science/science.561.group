Electroacupuncture (dian zhen 電針) applies electrical current through acupuncture needles to enhance and direct the therapeutic effect. In clinical practice, fine wires connect to the inserted needles, and a battery-powered device produces an electrical pulse. The frequency, amplitude, and pattern of stimulation can be adjusted to produce specific therapeutic effects. Electroacupuncture is a modern refinement of traditional acupuncture, building on the foundational TCM understanding of how to regulate [Qi](../../../../../../glossary/terms/qi.md) flow through [meridian](../../../../../../glossary/terms/meridians.md) stimulation.

## Frequency-dependent effects

Biomedical research has revealed that electroacupuncture produces frequency-dependent neurochemical effects. Han Jisheng's foundational work (Han 2003) demonstrated that different frequencies of electrical stimulation produce selective release of different endogenous opioid peptides:

- **Low frequency (2-15 Hz)** stimulation releases primarily enkephalins and beta-endorphin
- **High frequency (50-100 Hz)** stimulation releases primarily dynorphins
- **Mixed or alternating frequencies** produce release of multiple opioid peptide families

This frequency-specificity explains why adjusting the stimulation frequency produces different analgesic profiles and why electroacupuncture can be tuned to specific pain conditions. The clinical implication is that the frequency chosen during treatment directly determines the neurochemical cascade it triggers.

Zhao's systematic review (Zhao 2008) integrated findings on the broader mechanism: electroacupuncture activates opioid-sensitive brainstem regions including the periaqueductal gray (PAG), rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM), and locus coeruleus. These regions modulate spinal nociceptive transmission through descending pathways. The electrical stimulation of needles in the tissue increases afferent input, which is then integrated in these brainstem centers to produce descending inhibition of pain signals.

## Clinical electroacupuncture through inserted needles

In clinical practice, electroacupuncture requires:

1. **Needle insertion** at selected acupoints according to TCM pattern diagnosis
2. **Connection** via thin wires to a device that generates electrical pulses
3. **Parameter adjustment** — frequency, pulse width, amplitude, and waveform are set based on the condition being treated and the desired effect
4. **Stimulation duration** — typically 15-30 minutes per session

The tissue around the needle carries the current, and the patient experiences a rhythmic sensation of stimulation (not pain, but a strong sensory input that TCM practitioners call "de qi" — obtaining Qi). The combination of needle placement (TCM point selection) and electrical stimulation (biomedically quantifiable frequency effects) produces a synergistic therapeutic effect.

## Consumer point-stimulator pens: a distinct application

Consumer electroacupuncture devices — handheld "point stimulator" pens or mats — differ substantially from clinical electroacupuncture:

**Surface contact vs. needle insertion** — Consumer devices apply electrical stimulation through surface electrodes or contact points that rest against the skin. Clinical electroacupuncture delivers stimulation through needles inserted into tissue. The insertion of needles creates deeper penetration and engages muscle and connective tissue layers that surface contact does not reach.

**Current pathway** — Needles inserted at depth create a current pathway through tissue layers, activating deeper nerve fibers and producing different stimulation characteristics than surface current. Surface devices primarily stimulate superficial mechanoreceptors and cutaneous nerve fibers.

**Point precision** — Clinical electroacupuncture requires precise point location and needle placement according to TCM anatomy. Consumer pens may offer preset points or general area stimulation, which may or may not correspond to classical acupuncture anatomy.

**Frequency control** — Clinical devices offer precise control of frequency, pulse width, and amplitude. Consumer devices typically offer preset patterns or limited adjustment.

**Therapeutic intent** — Clinical electroacupuncture is ordered as part of a TCM diagnostic and treatment plan. Consumer devices are marketed for general wellness, pain relief, or symptom management without the framework of pattern diagnosis.

Both surface and needle-based electrical stimulation can produce analgesic effects. The consumer devices may be effective for localized pain or muscle tension through the activation of cutaneous and superficial nerve fibers. However, the therapeutic depth and specificity achieved through clinical electroacupuncture — with its combination of precise needle placement, tissue-layer penetration, and frequency-dependent neurochemical effects — is not replicated by surface contact devices. This is not a judgment of efficacy but a distinction of mechanism and therapeutic scope.
