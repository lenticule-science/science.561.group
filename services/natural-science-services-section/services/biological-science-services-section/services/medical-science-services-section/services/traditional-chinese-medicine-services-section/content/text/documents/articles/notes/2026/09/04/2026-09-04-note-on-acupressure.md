Acupressure (zhi ya 指壓, literally "finger pressure") is the application of sustained or rhythmic manual pressure to acupuncture points without the use of needles. Within TCM, acupressure operates through the same [meridian](../../../../../../glossary/terms/meridians.md) system and point functions as needle acupuncture. It is distinct from the general therapeutic massage of tui na, though both are manual methods within TCM's repertoire. Acupressure serves as an accessible tool for self-treatment and for patients who cannot or prefer not to receive needling.

## Acupressure within tui na

In traditional practice, acupressure techniques form part of the broader tui na repertoire. The tui na practitioner uses finger pressure (an rou 按揉, pressing and rolling motions), pinching (nie 捏), and percussion (pai 拍) techniques at specific points as well as along meridian pathways and muscle groups. The sustained pressure technique used at acupoints is zhi ya — maintained pressure, usually with the thumb or index finger, held at one point for a specified duration.

The key distinction within TCM is that acupressure, like needle acupuncture, targets specific points for their regulated functions, whereas tui na's broader massage techniques work along fascial lines and muscle groups. A tui na practitioner applying finger pressure to the point P6 (Neiguan) with the intention of treating nausea is performing acupressure. The same practitioner working along the forearm's flexor surface to release tension is performing a broader tui na technique.

## P6 (Neiguan) and nausea

The point P6 — Pericardium 6, Neiguan (內關, literally "inner gate") — lies on the ventral forearm, between the palmaris longus and flexor carpi radialis tendons, about two cun (finger-widths) above the wrist crease. In TCM terms, P6 governs the Pericardium's protective function and regulates the Heart's Shen (spirit/consciousness). Clinically, P6 is one of the most widely used points for nausea of any cause: motion sickness, medication-induced nausea, nausea from cancer treatment, post-operative nausea, pregnancy-related nausea, and functional nausea.

Biomedical research on P6 acupressure and acupuncture for nausea has found consistent effects. A Cochrane systematic review examined acupoint stimulation across multiple applications and found that stimulation of P6 reduces nausea incidence and severity. The mechanism from a biomedical perspective involves vagal afferent stimulation altering brainstem centers that regulate nausea and vomiting, though the specificity of P6 versus non-specific pressure remains somewhat debated in the research literature. From the TCM perspective, the point's traditional function — to calm the Heart-Pericardium and restore harmony — produces its clinical effect.

Acupressure at P6 is readily self-administered and is commonly recommended for patients with chronic or recurrent nausea. The stimulation can be continuous (steady finger pressure) or alternating (pressing and releasing in rhythm), applied for 2-5 minutes per session, multiple times daily if needed.

## The Kuznetsov applicator: a distinct device

The Kuznetsov applicator (also spelled Kuznetsov mat or Applicator Kuznetsova) is a Soviet-era invention: a mat or textile device with thousands of short plastic or metal spikes arranged in a grid. It was developed in the 1970s as a self-care tool for general pain and tension, based on the principle of stimulating the skin and triggering local reflexes.

The Kuznetsov applicator is sometimes marketed as an "acupressure mat" and associated with Traditional Chinese Medicine acupressure, but it is not part of TCM. It is a mechanical device intended to apply diffuse pressure stimulation to large skin areas (back, legs) in the practitioner's absence. Its mechanism is closer to that of a massage tool or a reflex stimulus than to TCM acupressure, which targets specific points with specific therapeutic intentions.

The distinction matters because:

1. **Specificity** — TCM acupressure targets individual points chosen through pattern diagnosis. The Kuznetsov applicator applies undifferentiated pressure to whatever body area is in contact with it.

2. **Duration and technique** — TCM acupressure employs sustained, often rhythmic pressure (steady or pulsing) with awareness of the point's response. The Kuznetsov applicator is typically used for 5-15 minutes per session with the body's weight providing the pressure intensity.

3. **Context** — TCM acupressure is one tool within a diagnostic and therapeutic framework. The Kuznetsov applicator is marketed as a self-care device for general wellness or pain reduction.

Both may produce therapeutic effects through stimulating the skin and triggering local and systemic responses. From a biomedical perspective, both create mechanical pressure stimulation that can reduce muscle tension, increase local blood flow, and trigger reflex pathways. The Kuznetsov applicator's effects are not invalidated by its distinction from TCM acupressure; it is simply a different tool with different indications and a different theoretical context.
