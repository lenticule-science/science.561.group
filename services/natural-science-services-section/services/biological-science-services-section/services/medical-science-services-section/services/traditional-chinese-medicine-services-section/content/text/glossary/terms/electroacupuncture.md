Electroacupuncture (dian zhen, electrical needling) applies electrical current through acupuncture needles to enhance and direct therapeutic effect. In clinical practice, fine wires connect inserted needles to a battery-powered device producing adjustable electrical pulses in frequency, amplitude, and pattern. Electroacupuncture is a modern refinement of traditional acupuncture, building on TCM's foundational understanding of how needle stimulation regulates Qi flow through meridians. Biomedical research has revealed that electroacupuncture produces frequency-dependent neurochemical effects. Han Jisheng's foundational work demonstrated that different frequencies produce selective release of different endogenous opioid peptides: low frequency (2-15 Hz) releases primarily enkephalins and beta-endorphin; high frequency (50-100 Hz) releases primarily dynorphins; mixed or alternating frequencies produce release of multiple opioid peptide families. This frequency-specificity means adjusting stimulation frequency produces different analgesic profiles, allowing electroacupuncture to be tuned to specific pain conditions. Zhao's systematic review integrated findings on the broader mechanism: electroacupuncture activates opioid-sensitive brainstem regions including the periaqueductal gray, rostral ventromedial medulla, and locus coeruleus. These regions modulate spinal nociceptive transmission through descending pathways. In clinical practice, electroacupuncture requires needle insertion at selected acupoints according to TCM pattern diagnosis; connection via thin wires to a device generating electrical pulses; parameter adjustment based on condition and desired effect; and stimulation for typically 15-30 minutes per session. The tissue around the needle carries current, and patients experience a rhythmic sensation of stimulation (de qi—obtaining Qi). Consumer electroacupuncture devices—handheld point-stimulator pens or mats—differ substantially from clinical electroacupuncture. Consumer devices apply electrical stimulation through surface electrodes against skin (not needle insertion at depth), creating shallower current pathways. Needles inserted at depth create current through tissue layers, activating deeper nerve fibers and producing different stimulation characteristics than surface contact. Clinical electroacupuncture requires precise point location and needle placement according to TCM anatomy; consumer pens may offer preset points or general area stimulation not corresponding to classical anatomy. Both surface and needle-based electrical stimulation can produce analgesic effects; consumer devices may be effective for localized pain through cutaneous and superficial nerve fiber activation, but the therapeutic depth and specificity of clinical electroacupuncture with its precise point placement, tissue-layer penetration, and frequency-dependent neurochemical effects is not replicated by surface contact devices.
