This note reads the biomedical trials of [qigong](../../../../../../glossary/terms/qigong.md) and [taijiquan](../../../../../../../../../medical-services-section/services/health-services-section/services/fitness-services-section/services/martial-arts-services-section/services/tai-chi-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/taijiquan.md) as the section's charter asks: as external commentary on the practices, not as a verdict on the tradition's own account of them. It records what is well supported, what is suggestive, and why the evidence resists becoming stronger.

## Where the evidence is strongest

**Balance and falls in older adults.** This is the most consistent finding. Taijiquan trains continuous single-leg weight transfer, which is balance training in biomechanical terms. The Cochrane review of exercise for preventing falls in community-dwelling older people found that tai chi reduced the rate of falls by about a fifth compared with control. It graded that estimate lower in certainty than the one for combined balance and functional exercise [@sherrington2019].

**Postural stability in Parkinson's disease.** One trial randomized 195 people with mild-to-moderate Parkinson's disease to tai chi, resistance training, or stretching for 24 weeks. The tai chi group did better than both on measures of postural stability, and fell less often than the stretching group [@li2012].

**Fibromyalgia.** A small trial found tai chi improved fibromyalgia symptoms and quality of life more than wellness education with stretching [@wang2010]. A larger comparative-effectiveness trial (226 participants) found tai chi improved symptom scores as much as or more than aerobic exercise, with greater benefit from longer practice [@wang2018].

## Where it is suggestive

A broad review of qigong and tai chi trials reported favourable results across many outcomes [@jahnke2010]:

- bone health;
- cardiopulmonary fitness and blood pressure;
- physical function;
- quality of life and self-efficacy;
- depression and anxiety;
- immune markers.

The breadth is itself the caution. Many of the trials were small, short, and at unclear risk of bias. The review establishes that the practices are safe, acceptable, and worth studying, not that each benefit is secure.

Studies of Baduanjin specifically, often run in China with older adults or people with chronic disease, follow the same pattern. The direction of effect is consistently favourable. The certainty is limited by trial quality and heterogeneity.

The breathing component has the clearest physiology. [Slow-paced breathing](../../../../../../../../../../../physiology-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/slow-paced-breathing.md) near six breaths per minute reliably raises heart-rate variability and is associated with reduced arousal [@zaccaro2018]. This gives a plausible mechanism for the calming, blood-pressure, and mood findings. It is plausible, not demonstrated, because trials of whole forms rarely isolate the breath.

## Why the evidence resists becoming stronger

**The intervention is several things at once.** A qigong or tai chi class combines movement, slowed breathing, trained attention, social contact, a teacher, a ritual setting, and the expectations each brings. What a trial tests depends on its control [@wayne2008]:
- against wait-list, it tests the whole package;
- against stretching, it tests roughly everything but the stretching;
- against aerobic exercise, it asks whether the mind-and-breath components add anything to movement.

The [three regulations](../../../../../../glossary/terms/san-tiao.md) name exactly these components. A well-designed trial says which regulation it is testing.

**Blinding is impossible.** Participants know they are doing qigong. Expectancy and the ritual of practice cannot be removed, only compared.

**"Qigong" is not one intervention.** Still standing, Baduanjin, and emitted-qi healing share a name and little else. Pooling them in meta-analysis mixes interventions, and a finding for one form does not transfer to another.

**Dose and fidelity are rarely reported well.** How often, how long, taught how, and practised at home or not all vary widely. The standardized forms of 2003 make fidelity easier, one reason they dominate recent trials.

## How to use this in resources

When writing a resource on qigong or taiji for a reader deciding whether to practise:

- Name the form and the population. "Tai chi reduces falls in community-dwelling older adults" is supported. "Qigong heals" is not a claim.
- Keep the tradition's account in its own terms, and give the biomedical reading beside it as a second reading.
- State safety plainly. These are low-intensity practices, generally safe and adaptable, including seated forms. For people with conditions where exertion causes post-exertional crashes, such as ME and long COVID, slow standing and seated forms and slow breathing are the gentler end, and pacing governs dose. Those sections are [myalgic encephalomyelitis](../../../../../../../../../myalgic-encephalomyelitis-services-section/README.md) and [long COVID](../../../../../../../../../long-covid-services-section/README.md).

Open questions for the ledger:
- The evidence for qigong in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hypertension, and cancer-related fatigue needs its own reading of the specific reviews.
- No trial here tests one regulation against the others.
