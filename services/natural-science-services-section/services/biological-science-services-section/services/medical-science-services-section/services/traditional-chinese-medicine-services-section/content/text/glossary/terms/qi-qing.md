Qi Qing (七情, the Seven Affects) are Traditional Chinese Medicine's category of internal (as opposed to external or dietary) causes of disease: joy (喜, xi), anger (怒, nu), worry (憂, you), pensiveness (思, si), grief (悲, bei), fear (恐, kong), and fright (驚, jing). Each affect is associated with a specific Zang organ and a characteristic direction of effect on Qi, and each becomes pathogenic not by occurring — normal emotional response is not disease — but by being excessive, prolonged, or chronically suppressed.

## Classical basis

Individual emotion-organ correspondences appear scattered through the *Huang Di Nei Jing*, most systematically in *Su Wen* Chapter 5, 陰陽應象大論 (*Yin Yang Ying Xiang Da Lun*), which embeds each affect within the Five Phase correspondence tables — for instance "在志為思，思傷脾，怒勝思" (in the emotions [Earth] is Pensiveness; Pensiveness injures the Spleen; Anger overcomes Pensiveness), giving each affect both an organ target and a place in the Five Phase restraining cycle. The systematic collection and formalization of the seven affects specifically as a category of internal causation, distinguished from external and dietary causes, is credited to the Song-dynasty physician Chen Yan (陳言, courtesy name Wuze, 陳無擇) in his *San Yin Ji Yi Bing Zheng Fang Lun* (《三因極一病證方論》, c. 1174 CE, "Treatise on the Three Categories of Causes, Symptoms, and Formulas Extending to Unity"). Chen Yan's tripartite etiology divides all disease causation into external causes (外因, the Six Excesses of wind, cold, heat, dampness, dryness, and summer heat), internal causes (內因, the seven affects), and causes neither internal nor external (不內外因, such as dietary irregularity or parasitic damage) — establishing the seven affects as medicine's first systematically named internal-cause category.

## The seven affects and their organs

| Affect | Character | Organ | Effect on Qi |
|---|---|---|---|
| Joy | 喜 (xi) | Heart | Slows and scatters Qi |
| Anger | 怒 (nu) | Liver | Makes Qi rise |
| Worry | 憂 (you) | Lung | Knots and depletes Qi |
| Pensiveness | 思 (si) | Spleen | Knots Qi |
| Grief | 悲 (bei) | Lung | Dissolves Qi |
| Fear | 恐 (kong) | Kidney | Makes Qi descend |
| Fright | 驚 (jing) | Heart/Kidney | Scatters Qi |

Worry and grief are both assigned to the Lung but are classically distinguished — worry as a knotting, anxious anticipation, grief as a dissolving, depleting sorrow — even though they share an organ target and their clinical presentations (chest tightness, shallow breathing, fatigue for worry; shortness of breath, weak voice, pale complexion for grief) overlap considerably.

## The pathogenicity criterion

The seven affects' defining clinical principle is that emotion itself is not disease. Anger felt and expressed appropriately is a normal physiological response; anger that is chronic, suppressed, or grossly disproportionate produces the pathological pattern of Liver Qi stagnation. Grief appropriate to genuine loss is a normal process; grief that remains prolonged and unresolved depletes Lung Qi and produces chronic fatigue and immune vulnerability. This criterion — magnitude, duration, and whether the affect is expressed or bottled — rather than the mere presence of feeling, is what makes the seven affects a medical category rather than a moral judgment about emotional life.

## Clinical significance

Because each affect targets a specific organ, a patient's emotional history is, in TCM diagnostic practice, direct clinical data pointing toward which organ system is likely disturbed — chronic unresolved anger toward Liver patterns, chronic anxious rumination toward Spleen patterns, chronic unprocessed fear toward Kidney patterns. This is not a claim that emotion is the only or even the primary cause of any given organ pattern (diet, overwork, and external pathogenic factors all contribute independently), but the seven affects give TCM a structured way to ask about a patient's emotional life as part of the same diagnostic inquiry used to ask about digestion, sleep, and pain — rather than treating psychological history as a separate domain outside the medical interview.

## Biomedical convergence, honestly graded

The seven affects' organizing principle — that the pathogenic ingredient is duration and suppression, not the emotion's mere occurrence — resonates with the psychoneuroimmunological finding that chronic, unresolved stress states (not acute emotional response) drive the sustained sympathetic and inflammatory signaling documented in the modern social-genomics literature (the Conserved Transcriptional Response to Adversity, chronic social-defeat paradigms in animal models, and related work). That resonance is at the level of a shared clinical principle — chronicity and non-resolution matter more than acute intensity — and does not extend, on current evidence, to the specific organ assignments: biomedicine has not established that worry-predominant chronic stress produces a Spleen-congruent physiological signature distinguishable from anger-predominant or fear-predominant chronic stress (see Hypothesis 3 in "Marrow, Spleen, and the Transport of Substances" for a concrete, falsifiable version of this open question). The seven affects' organ-specificity is a testable hypothesis that current biomedical research is positioned to examine, not yet a confirmed finding.
