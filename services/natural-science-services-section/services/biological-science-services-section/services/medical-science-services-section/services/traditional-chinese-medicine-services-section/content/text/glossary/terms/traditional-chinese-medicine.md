Traditional Chinese Medicine (中醫, zhōngyī, "Chinese medicine") is the system of diagnosis and treatment developed in China from the classical canons: the *Huangdi Neijing* (in two parts, *Suwen* and *Lingshu*), the *Nanjing*, and the *Shanghan lun*. It was elaborated by two millennia of physicians and schools.

The physiology it reasons in is functional. The body is described through:

- the substances of [qi](./qi.md), [blood](./xue.md), [body fluids](./jin-ye.md), and [essence](./jing.md);
- the organ-function systems of [zang-fu](./zang-fu.md);
- the [channels](./meridians.md) that connect them;
- the organizing frames of [yin-yang](./yin-yang.md) and the [five phases](./wu-xing.md).

Clinical reasoning identifies a pattern (證, zhèng), for which the [eight principles](./eight-principles.md) are one frame. It treats the pattern through acupuncture and moxibustion, herbal prescription, tuina massage, dietetics, and [qigong](./qigong.md) [@kaptchuk2000].

"TCM" in the narrow sense names the standardized, institutional form of this medicine created in the People's Republic of China from the 1950s. It was systematized into textbooks and colleges and integrated with biomedicine in state hospitals [@scheid2002]. The older, plural practice it drew from is often called Chinese medicine or classical Chinese medicine. This section uses "traditional Chinese medicine" for the system as a whole and marks the distinction where it matters [@unschuld1985].

This section studies the system in its own terms, as the [section README](../../../../README.md) states. Its concepts are recorded as a coherent carving of the body with its own criteria of success. Biomedical findings are recorded as external commentary on it, not as its adjudication.
