**Sha** (痧) is a Chinese medical term whose history is broader than the red marks now popularly associated with gua sha. It can refer to a family of acute illness presentations, to a diagnostic interpretation of superficial signs, and to the petechial or ecchymotic manifestation deliberately raised during gua sha and related techniques.

Treating sha as merely "bruising" collapses several centuries of changing medical usage into one biomedical observation. Treating it as a mysterious toxin is equally misleading. The term belongs first to a historical diagnostic and therapeutic vocabulary.

## Historical anchor

A major textual anchor is Guo Zhisui's **Sha Zhang Yu Heng** (《痧脹玉衡》), written in 1675 during the early Qing period [@guo1675]. Guo describes sha as a clinically important category that could present with varied and rapidly changing symptoms. His work systematizes several ways of treating it, including scraping, releasing blood by needling, and medicines.

The preface is especially important for the social history of the practice. Guo reports that country people used coins dipped in oil to scrape cases attributed to sha and remarks that such methods were commonly performed by women and therefore neglected by famous physicians. This is evidence for the coexistence of household knowledge and elite medical writing, not evidence that Guo invented gua sha in 1675.

Historical claims should distinguish the history of scraping and rubbing techniques, the history of **sha** as a disease/sign category, the history of gua sha as an explicitly named and systematized therapy, and modern professional and commercial forms of gua sha.

## Sha in treatment

In traditional practice, the appearance of sha may be read as clinically meaningful. Color, density, distribution, ease of appearance, tenderness, and location can be interpreted in relation to patterns involving Qi, Blood, Heat, Cold, Wind, or channel obstruction. The treatment aim is not conventionally to create a bruise for its own sake but to alter a pattern understood through the larger diagnostic system.

This interpretation should be recorded as a **traditional medical claim**. It should not be converted into claims such as "dark sha means more toxins" without independent support.

## Biomedical observation

When gua sha raises visible red or purple marks, the immediate biomedical description is **petechiae and ecchymosis caused by superficial microvascular blood extravasation**. Laser-Doppler work also documents increased local surface perfusion after treatment [@nielsen2007].

Those findings and the Chinese category of sha concern the same treated body but answer different questions. Biomedical observation asks what tissue and vascular events occurred. Traditional diagnosis asks what those signs mean within a pattern of illness and treatment. Similarity between the observations does not make the categories equivalent.

## Epistemic status

Claims about sha should be marked by type whenever possible: historical attestation, traditional medical claim, observed physiology, clinical evidence, mechanistic hypothesis, phenomenological or cultural account, or commercial claim.
