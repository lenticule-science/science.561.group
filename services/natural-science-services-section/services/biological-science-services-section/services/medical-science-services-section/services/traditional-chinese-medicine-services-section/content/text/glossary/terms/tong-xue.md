Tong Xue (統血, "governing" or "holding" the Blood) names the Spleen's function of keeping Blood contained within the vessels and channels rather than extravasating — leaking out of its proper pathway. It is distinguished from Yun Hua (運化, the Spleen's transformation-and-transportation function that supplies Blood's raw material) as a separate, later-articulated Spleen function specifically concerned with containment rather than production.

## Classical basis

Tong Xue's clearest classical statement is not from the *Huang Di Nei Jing* but from the Ming-dynasty physician Xue Ji (薛己), in his case records (*Xue Shi Yi An*, 《薛氏醫案》): "心主血，肝藏血，脾能統攝於血" — the Heart governs Blood, the Liver stores Blood, and the Spleen is able to hold and govern Blood. This sentence divides labor among three organ systems with respect to Blood: the Heart circulates it, the Liver stores a reserve of it, and the Spleen keeps it inside its vessels. Later Qing-dynasty commentary extended and generalized the doctrine — Shen Mingzong (沈明宗), in his annotations on the *Jin Gui Yao Lue*, wrote "五臟六腑之血，全賴脾氣統攝" (the blood of all five Zang and six Fu organs relies entirely on Spleen Qi for its containment) — indicating the doctrine had become settled clinical consensus by the Qing period rather than remaining one physician's idiosyncratic formulation.

## Mechanism, in TCM's own terms

TCM attributes Tong Xue to the "holding" or "consolidating" quality of Spleen Qi generally — the same quality by which Qi is said to hold organs in their proper position (preventing prolapse) and hold fluids where they belong (preventing spontaneous sweating and other fluid leakage). Blood containment is one instance of a broader Qi function: where Qi is abundant and functioning normally, it exerts a centripetal, holding effect on the substances and structures it accompanies. Where Spleen Qi is deficient, this holding effect weakens, and Blood — no longer adequately contained — escapes its vessels.

## Clinical presentation and treatment

Tong Xue failure (脾不統血) presents as chronic, low-grade, or recurrent bleeding that is characteristically pale or watery in color (as opposed to the dark, thick bleeding associated with Heat forcing Blood recklessly, a different pathomechanism) accompanied by the general signs of Spleen Qi deficiency: fatigue, pale complexion, poor appetite, loose stools, a pale tongue, and a weak pulse. Typical presentations include easy bruising, prolonged or heavy menstrual bleeding, blood in the stool or urine that is pale rather than bright red, and bleeding under the skin (purpura) in more severe or chronic cases.

The treatment principle follows directly from the mechanism: because the bleeding results from insufficient Qi to hold Blood in place, treatment tonifies Spleen Qi rather than relying on cooling or purely astringent (styptic) methods, which would be indicated instead for Heat-type bleeding. The classical formula most associated with this pattern is Gui Pi Tang (歸脾湯, "Restore the Spleen Decoction"), which combines Spleen-Qi-tonifying herbs with Blood-nourishing and Shen-calming herbs, reflecting the clinical observation that chronic Blood loss from Spleen deficiency typically co-occurs with Blood deficiency and its associated Shen disturbance (insomnia, palpitations, poor memory).

## Biomedical convergence, honestly graded

TCM's diagnostic separation of pale, chronic, easily-bruised bleeding (attributed to Spleen failing to hold Blood) from bright-red, forceful, hot-presentation bleeding (attributed to Heat recklessly forcing Blood) has an intuitive resemblance to a biomedical distinction between bleeding from structural/coagulation-factor deficiency or capillary fragility versus bleeding from acute inflammatory or vascular-injury processes — but this resemblance has not, to this note's knowledge, been formally tested by comparing TCM Tong-Xue-failure diagnosis against specific coagulation, platelet, or vascular-fragility biomarkers in controlled studies. The clinical picture TCM describes (chronic, pale, low-grade bleeding with generalized fatigue) is real and recognizable; whether it corresponds to a single identifiable biomedical mechanism, or is a functional category spanning several distinct biomedical causes of mild chronic bleeding, remains an open and testable question rather than an established convergence.
