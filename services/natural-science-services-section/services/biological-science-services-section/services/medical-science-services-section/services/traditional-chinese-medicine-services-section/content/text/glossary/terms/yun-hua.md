Yun Hua (運化) names the Spleen's core physiological function in Traditional Chinese Medicine: the transformation (化, hua) of ingested food and drink into usable substance, and the transportation (運, yun) of that substance to where the body needs it. The two characters are conventionally paired as a single compound term, but they name two distinct operations that the Spleen performs together — extraction and distribution — and TCM clinicians distinguish failures of one from failures of the other when a patient's presentation calls for it.

## Classical basis

The physiological sequence Yun Hua describes is given directly in the *Huang Di Nei Jing, Su Wen*, Chapter 21, 經脈別論 (*Jing Mai Bie Lun*): "飲入於胃，游溢精氣，上輸於脾，脾氣散精，上歸於肺" — drink enters the stomach; its essential qi overflows and is transmitted upward to the Spleen; the Spleen's qi disperses that essence upward to the Lung. This single sentence is the standard textbook citation for the mechanism, and later passages in the same chapter extend the same logic to solid food. The Spleen and Stomach's joint role as the digestive seat of the body is given its classical administrative metaphor in *Su Wen* Chapter 8, 靈蘭秘典論 (*Ling Lan Mi Dian Lun*): "脾胃者，倉廩之官，五味出焉" — the Spleen-Stomach is the Granary Official, from which the five flavors emerge. The formula that elevates this function to the center of TCM physiology as a whole is Li Dongyuan's (李東垣) *Pi Wei Lun* (《脾胃論》, *Treatise on the Spleen and Stomach*, Jin-Yuan dynasty): "脾胃為後天之本，氣血生化之源" — the Spleen-Stomach is the root of postnatal life, the source from which Qi and Blood are engendered and transformed.

## The two operations

**Transformation (化)**: The Spleen extracts the essential, usable portion of ingested food and drink — termed Gu Qi (穀氣, grain qi) — separating it from what cannot be used. This extracted essence is the raw material for Qi, Blood, and Body Fluids (Jin Ye); without adequate transformation, the raw material for all three is insufficient regardless of how much food is eaten.

**Transportation (運)**: The Spleen distributes the transformed essence to the Lung (which further disperses it to the skin and body surface), to the Heart (which further transforms Food Qi into Blood proper), and to the muscles and limbs, which the Spleen is separately said to govern. Transportation also includes the Spleen's role in moving fluids as part of the body's overall water metabolism, working alongside the Lung and Kidney.

The Spleen's transformation and transportation together are also credited with keeping fluids and blood moving in their proper channels rather than pooling or leaking — a function closely related to, but classically distinguished from, the Spleen's separately named function of Tong Xue (統血, holding Blood within the vessels).

## Clinical significance

Yun Hua failure is one of the most common findings in TCM clinical practice, presenting as poor appetite, bloating and distension after eating, loose stools, fatigue, a heavy or foggy sensation in the head and limbs, and a pale, possibly swollen tongue with a thin white coating. Because Yun Hua supplies the raw material for Qi, Blood, and Jin Ye, its failure cascades: inadequate transformation produces Qi deficiency and Blood deficiency downstream, while inadequate transportation of fluids produces Dampness, and — if the Dampness thickens and congeals over time — Phlegm. The saying "the Spleen is the source of Phlegm" describes exactly this progression: a Spleen that fails at Yun Hua does not merely under-produce nourishment, it also fails to move the fluids it cannot use, leaving them to accumulate pathologically.

Because Yun Hua is functionally so close to digestion and absorption in the biomedical sense, TCM treatment for its failure — tonify Spleen Qi, dry Dampness, use warming and aromatic herbs that support digestive transformation — overlaps substantially with dietary and digestive management in a biomedical frame, even though the underlying theoretical accounts (Qi transformation versus enzymatic digestion and intestinal absorption) describe the process at entirely different levels.

## Biomedical convergence, honestly graded

Yun Hua's clinical picture — poor appetite, bloating, loose stools, fatigue following meals — overlaps substantially with what biomedicine would investigate as malabsorption, dysbiosis, or functional dyspepsia, and TCM's historical anatomical category 脾 is documented to have spanned both the biomedical spleen and the pancreas (see the *Nan Jing*, Difficulty 42, and Wang Qingren's *Yi Lin Gai Cuo* on this history), which makes a functional resemblance to pancreatic exocrine digestion unsurprising rather than mysterious. This is a resonance of clinical picture and historical anatomy, not of mechanism: Yun Hua as TCM describes it is a single unified process of extraction-and-distribution attributed to one organ-function system, where biomedicine distributes the same territory across gastric digestion, pancreatic exocrine enzyme secretion, small-intestinal absorption, and hepatic/portal processing — several separately regulated systems rather than one. The overlap is real and clinically useful to notice; it is not evidence that TCM's single-organ account was secretly a biomedical account in disguise.
