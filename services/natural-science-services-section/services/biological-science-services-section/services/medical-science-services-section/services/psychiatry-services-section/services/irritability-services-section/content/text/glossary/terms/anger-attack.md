An anger attack is a sudden, out-of-proportion spell of anger accompanied by autonomic or panic-like features — tachycardia, sweating, flushing, a subjective sense of loss of control — first characterized by Fava, Rosenbaum, and colleagues in outpatients being treated for major depressive disorder at the Massachusetts General Hospital Depression Research Program. Across their case series, anger attacks were reported at rates depending on sample and definition: 44% of 127 medication-free depressed outpatients in the 1993 report (PMID 7848377), and, in an earlier, smaller subgroup comparison, 48% of 31 younger depressed patients versus 21% of 29 age-matched healthy controls (PMID 1775598, p = .048).

An anger attack is not a DSM-III-R, DSM-IV, or DSM-5 diagnostic criterion for major depressive disorder — it is a research construct, defined and measured by the Anger Attacks Questionnaire developed within this one research program. It should also be distinguished from irritability as a trait or mood state: irritability names a standing proneness to anger or a period of elevated angry mood, while an anger attack is a bounded, panic-attack-like spell with its own autonomic signature, closer in phenomenology to a discrete panic attack than to a mood state. Fava and Rosenbaum's own 1999 review describes the construct as "sudden intense spells of anger that resemble panic attacks but lack the predominant affects of fear and anxiety associated with panic attacks" (PMID 10418810).

Every quantitative finding about anger attacks in this literature comes from the same research program's open-label, uncontrolled case series (no placebo arm in either the 1991 or 1993 reports), conducted at a single U.S. academic center in adult depressed outpatients already seeking treatment — so the reported resolution of anger attacks in 71% of patients who had them, after 8 weeks of open-label fluoxetine in the 1993 report, cannot be separated from a general antidepressant response, a placebo response, or regression to the mean, and the 1999 summary review restates the same program's own accumulated observations rather than an independent replication. A neuroendocrine pilot finding — blunted prolactin response to TRH in patients with anger attacks (n=12) versus without (n=13) — is likewise a small mechanistic study whose authors frame their own conclusion as suggestive ("may have... greater central serotonergic dysregulation"), not an established mechanism, and blunted TRH-prolactin response is a nonspecific finding reported across other psychiatric conditions. Any use of the anger-attack construct in current research or practice should therefore treat its treatment-response and mechanism findings as hypothesis-generating from one program's uncontrolled series, not as replicated causal evidence.
