Rapid cycling names a bipolar-disorder course specifier: four or more mood episodes (manic, hypomanic, or depressive) within twelve months. The definition originates with Dunner and Fieve (1974), who proposed it as a predictor of lithium-prophylaxis treatment failure in a retrospective clinical-factor analysis of lithium non-responders; secondary sources describe roughly 44 of 96 lithium-treated patients as meeting the definition, though this could not be independently verified against the original abstract. The same four-episodes-per-year threshold was later adopted essentially unchanged as a DSM course specifier.

Rapid cycling is a course specifier, not a separate diagnosis or a fixed subtype: DSM-5 states it can wax and wane over the course of the illness rather than marking a stable, permanent illness form. This distinguishes it from proposed sub-thresholds inside the same spectrum — "ultra-rapid" cycling (roughly 48-hour cycles) and "ultradian" cycling (polarity changes faster than once every 24 hours) — which some researchers describe as the outer edge of the rapid-cycling spectrum but which have never themselves been adopted into DSM or ICD (see ultradian-cycling).

A 2023 mixed-method systematic meta-review aggregating 22 reviews (30 underlying studies, 13,698 bipolar-disorder patients) found 27.6% (3,777 patients) met rapid-cycling criteria, with rapid-cycling patients showing greater illness severity, lower functioning, and higher rates of suicide attempts, hypothyroidism, and substance use than non-rapid-cycling patients. A cross-sectional cohort of the first 500 enrollees in the Systematic Treatment Enhancement Program for Bipolar Disorder (STEP-BD) found 20% met rapid-cycling criteria in the preceding year; rapid cyclers were disproportionately female, had earlier illness onset, presented with more depressive symptoms, had poorer functioning, and had significantly more depressive and hypomanic/manic episodes in the preceding year than non-rapid-cyclers.

For research design, the consequence is that these prevalence and correlate figures come from help-seeking, largely tertiary-referral or research-registry samples (STEP-BD, and reviews aggregating similarly ascertained cohorts) — they are cross-sectional estimates in clinical populations, not population-representative incidence figures, and because rapid cycling is a course specifier that can wax and wane, a single cross-sectional assessment characterizes a patient's status during the assessed year, not a permanent trait.
