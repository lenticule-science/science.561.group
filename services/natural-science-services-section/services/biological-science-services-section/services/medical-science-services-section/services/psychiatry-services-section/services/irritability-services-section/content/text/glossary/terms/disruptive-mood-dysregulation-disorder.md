Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (DMDD) is a DSM-5 diagnosis, introduced in 2013, for children who show chronic, non-episodic irritable or angry mood present most days plus frequent temper outbursts grossly out of proportion to the situation. It was adapted from severe mood dysregulation (SMD), a research construct proposed in 2003 by Leibenluft and colleagues as a "broad phenotype" of pediatric bipolar disorder, distinguished from narrow-phenotype, episodic mania; the DSM-5 workgroup dropped SMD's hyperarousal requirement when converting the research construct into a diagnostic category.

DMDD should be distinguished from intermittent explosive disorder, the discrete-episode construct documented in adult general-population surveys: DMDD requires a chronic, non-episodic irritable or angry background mood present most days, plus frequent outbursts, whereas intermittent explosive disorder is defined by recurrent discrete episodes without a required chronic mood disturbance between them, and is documented predominantly in adults rather than children. DMDD should also be distinguished from its own precursor, severe mood dysregulation, since the DSM-5 workgroup dropped SMD's hyperarousal criterion in defining DMDD — the two are related but not identical constructs.

In pooled community samples of 7,881 observations of 3,258 children and adolescents ages 2-17, three-month DMDD prevalence ranged 0.8%-3.3%, highest among preschoolers, and dropped only slightly under strict exclusion-criterion application (Copeland, Angold, Costello, and Egger, 2013). DMDD co-occurred with nearly every other psychiatric disorder assessed in these children — odds ratios of 9.9-23.5 with depressive disorders and 52.9-103.0 with oppositional defiant disorder (ODD) — occurring alongside another diagnosis 62%-92% of the time; that near-total overlap with ODD is itself part of the nosological criticism below, not evidence that DMDD identifies a population distinct from ODD. In one hospitalized pediatric sample (children ages 5-12, n=82), the picture looked different by informant: 30.5% met DMDD criteria by parent report versus only 15.9% by direct inpatient-unit observation (Margulies, Weintraub, Basile, Grover, and Carlson, 2012) — a discrepancy the authors treat as the central finding, not a general "~30% of children" prevalence claim, since it is a single-informant estimate in one inpatient sample far above the community rate.

DMDD's own diagnostic reliability is a documented weak point. In the DSM-5 field trials — paired, independent clinician interviews of the same patients on separate occasions — DMDD's test-retest kappa was reported as 0.25, in the "questionable" range, with substantial site-level variability (roughly 0.49-0.50 at one predominantly inpatient site, "unacceptable" at others). This kappa figure is verified here against a secondary peer-reviewed review's citation of Regier and colleagues' 2013 field-trials report, not against the primary paper's own reported table directly. Partly on the strength of this reliability picture and the near-total ODD overlap described above, the ICD-11 working group (Lochman, Evans, Burke, and colleagues, 2015) declined to adopt an equivalent stand-alone DMDD category, recommending instead a chronic-irritability/anger specifier on oppositional defiant disorder. That recommendation is a nosological and classification judgment by one expert panel about DMDD as a bounded diagnostic category — it is not, on its own terms, a finding that chronic childhood irritability is not a real or impairing clinical target; the community-prevalence and inpatient-overlap findings above speak to validity and diagnostic overlap, the kappa figure speaks only to inter-rater reliability, and the three should not be collapsed into a single verdict on the construct.
