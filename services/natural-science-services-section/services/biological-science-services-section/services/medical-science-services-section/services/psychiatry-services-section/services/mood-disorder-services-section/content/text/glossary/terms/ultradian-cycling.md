Ultradian cycling names reported bipolar mood-polarity changes occurring faster than once every 24 hours — an extension, past "ultra-rapid" cycling (cycles of roughly 48 hours), of the rapid-cycling course specifier (four or more episodes per year) to its outer, sub-daily edge. It has never been admitted into DSM-5 or ICD-11 as a recognized diagnostic specifier or subtype.

The primary descriptive source is a case series of five hospitalized bipolar patients studied by retrospective life-charting and prospective daily mood ratings during extended inpatient evaluation, reporting a spectrum of cycle frequencies from annual down to distinct, abrupt mood shifts under 24 hours in duration in patients without evidence of personality disorder — offered by the authors as evidence against a simple reinterpretation of the pattern as borderline personality disorder. As a case series of five patients, this establishes that such patterns are observable and documentable in careful inpatient study, not their population prevalence or their status as a distinct nosological entity; the authors themselves frame it as an open descriptive question about the outer edge of the rapid-cycling spectrum, not a settled category.

A critical secondary commentary argues the construct lacks demonstrated content and discriminant validity relative to other conditions with intrinsic moment-to-moment mood variability — ordinary affective lability seen in borderline personality disorder or ADHD-associated mood reactivity — and warns of the risk that a diagnostically fashionable label gets over-applied to non-bipolar mood instability once an "ultra-rapid" specifier is available for clinicians to reach for; this critique was read in summarized secondary form, not quoted directly from its primary text.

For clinical observation and research design, the consequence is that a report of sub-24-hour mood-polarity change should not be treated as an established, DSM/ICD-recognized bipolar course specifier: because DSM-5 and ICD-11 have never admitted ultradian cycling, and because its discriminant validity against other sources of rapid mood variability is disputed rather than demonstrated, distinguishing it from borderline personality disorder-associated lability or other non-bipolar mood instability requires an independent structured assessment, not the presence of fast mood swings alone.
