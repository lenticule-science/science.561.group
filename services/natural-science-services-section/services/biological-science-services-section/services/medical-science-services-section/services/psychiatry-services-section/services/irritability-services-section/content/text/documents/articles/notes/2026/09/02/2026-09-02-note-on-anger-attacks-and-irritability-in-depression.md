## What this argues

Depressive illness carries a documented, high-arousal, angry face alongside
its low-arousal, anhedonic one, and the research literature treats that
angry face as several distinct clinical objects rather than one. In adults,
Fava, Rosenbaum, and colleagues described discrete "anger attacks" —
sudden, out-of-proportion, autonomically marked spells of anger — in
roughly 30-48% of depressed outpatients depending on the sample, resolving
in the majority of patients given open-label antidepressant treatment; the
STAR*D effectiveness trial separately found that 40-46% of depressed
outpatients report irritability present at least half the preceding week,
tracking with severity, suicide risk, and (in the confirmatory sample)
explicitly *not* with bipolar-spectrum features. In children and
adolescents, a different and much larger research program — Leibenluft,
Stringaris, and Brotman's dimensional-irritability program — has built a
measurement instrument (the Affective Reactivity Index), traced genetic and
longitudinal links between irritability and depressed mood, proposed a
two-mechanism neurobiological model, and fed a new DSM-5 diagnostic
category (disruptive mood dysregulation disorder) whose own field-trial
reliability was rated "questionable" and which the ICD-11 working group
declined to adopt as a stand-alone diagnosis. Separately again, intermittent
explosive disorder names a discrete-episode adult construct found in a
national U.S. survey to affect a lifetime 7.3% of adults, and the wider
aggression literature distinguishes reactive (provoked, anger-driven) from
proactive (planned, instrumental) aggression as two correlated but
separable dimensions.

The weight of citation traffic in this area sits overwhelmingly in
children and adolescents: most of what follows in Sections 3 and 4 below
is developmental research, built on child/adolescent samples, and it says
nothing directly about how irritability behaves in adults with first
onset in adulthood. The adult literature — anger attacks, STAR*D
irritability, intermittent explosive disorder — is real but thinner, older,
and in the anger-attacks case built almost entirely on one research
program's own open-label, uncontrolled case series. This note keeps those
two literatures, and the populations each was measured in, explicitly
separate throughout; nowhere below should a child/adolescent finding be
read as if it had been shown in adults, or an adult finding as if it had
been shown in children.

## Anger attacks in major depression (Fava, Rosenbaum, and colleagues)

### First description and prevalence

Fava, Rosenbaum, and colleagues, working out of the Massachusetts General
Hospital Depression Research Program, described a subgroup of depressed
outpatients who report discrete "anger attacks" — sudden, situationally
disproportionate spells of anger with autonomic or panic-like features
(tachycardia, sweating, flushing, a subjective sense of loss of control).
The construct and its treatment response are already given a full
independent entry at
[anger-attack](../../../../../../glossary/terms/anger-attack.md); the
numbers below are given here because the note's argument depends on
seeing them against the STAR*D and dimensional-irritability numbers that
follow.

In the earlier 1991 report (verified against the PubMed abstract, PMID
1775598), the Anger Attacks Questionnaire was given to 79 consecutive
adult patients (25 men, 54 women; mean age 38.8 ± 10.3 years) diagnosed
with major depression by structured interview (SCID, DSM-III-R). Thirty-four
of the 79 (13 men, 21 women) met the study's anger-attack criteria. In a
smaller subgroup comparison — 31 younger depressed patients against 29
age-matched normal controls — 48% of the depressed group reported anger
attacks versus 21% of controls (p = .048). Of the 79 depressed patients,
19 were treated openly with fluoxetine 20 mg/day for at least 8 weeks: 9
(47%) had anger attacks at baseline, and only 3 (16%) still reported them
after treatment (p < .05).

In the 1993 report (Part 1; verified against the PubMed abstract, PMID
7848377), a larger cohort of 127 medication-free adult outpatients with
major depression was assessed with the Anger Attacks Questionnaire, the
Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression, the Symptom Questionnaire, and the
Cook-Medley Hostility Scale; 85 were reassessed after 8 weeks of
open-label fixed-dose fluoxetine (20 mg/day). At baseline, 44% reported
anger attacks, and this subgroup scored significantly higher on anxiety,
somatization, and state/trait hostility measures than depressed patients
without attacks. After treatment, anger attacks resolved in 71% of those
who had reported them at baseline, alongside a (not statistically
tested-as-significant) trend toward greater overall clinical improvement
in the anger-attack subgroup.

Both reports are open-label and uncontrolled — neither had a placebo arm
— in adult outpatients already seeking depression treatment at a single
U.S. academic center. The resolution of self-reported anger attacks after
8 weeks of open fluoxetine cannot, on this design, be separated from a
general antidepressant response, a placebo response, or regression to the
mean. Anger attacks are not, and have never been, a DSM-III-R, DSM-IV, or
DSM-5 criterion for major depressive disorder; the construct and its
measure (the Anger Attacks Questionnaire) belong to this one research
program.

### A later summary review

Fava and Rosenbaum's 1999 review (*Journal of Clinical Psychiatry*,
60(Suppl 15):21-24, PMID 10418810) restates the program's accumulated
findings, giving a summary prevalence of roughly 30-40% of depressed
patients and a resolution rate of 53-71% across patients treated with
fluoxetine, sertraline, or imipramine, pooled across the program's studies.
This dossier's writer verified the review's existence and its framing of
the construct against the PubMed abstract directly — the review describes
anger attacks as "sudden intense spells of anger that resemble panic
attacks but lack the predominant affects of fear and anxiety associated
with panic attacks" — but did not independently re-derive the 30-40%/
53-71% summary figures from each constituent primary study; they are
reported here as the review's own summary of its own program's work, not
as freshly extracted primary-study numbers. A narrative review by the
same investigators summarizing their own prior case series is not an
independent replication; it should not be read as a meta-analysis by
outside investigators.

### A neuroendocrine correlate

A companion 1993 report (Part 2; verified against the PubMed abstract,
PMID 8328558) gave the TRH stimulation test to 25 adult patients with
major depression (12 with anger attacks, 13 without) at the same program;
22 were retested after 8 weeks of fluoxetine. Patients with anger attacks
showed a blunted prolactin response to TRH relative to those without;
fluoxetine treatment was followed by an increase in the prolactin response
among the anger-attack group, and the prolactin response "tended to
predict" degree of treatment response, with no effect size reported in
the abstract. The authors' own framing is explicitly hypothesis-level:
"these results suggest that the subset of depressed patients with anger
attacks may have a greater central serotonergic dysregulation than
depressed patients without such attacks." An n of 25 (12 versus 13) is a
small mechanistic pilot, and a blunted prolactin response to TRH is a
nonspecific neuroendocrine finding reported across other psychiatric
conditions in the wider literature — this does not establish a specific
serotonergic pathway for anger attacks.

## Irritability prevalence in STAR*D — adult major depressive disorder

Perlis and colleagues examined irritability directly, and separately from
the anger-attack construct, in the Sequenced Treatment Alternatives to
Relieve Depression (STAR*D) effectiveness trial — a large U.S. cohort of
treatment-seeking adults with major depressive disorder, not a
general-population sample.

### Preliminary report (2005)

In the first 1,456 evaluable adult outpatients with nonpsychotic major
depressive disorder entering STAR*D (verified against the PubMed
abstract, PMID 15705000), irritability was defined as present at least
half of the preceding week. 582 of 1,456 (40%) met this threshold.
Irritable participants were more likely female, younger, unemployed, and
to report a history of one or more suicide attempts; functional status
and quality of life were poorer. Irritability correlated with overall
depressive severity, which "accounted for nearly all of the clinical
differences noted" between irritable and non-irritable patients, with one
exception: an association between irritability and vascular disease that
persisted after adjustment for age, sex, and depression severity. The
authors' own conclusion, quoted from the abstract: irritability "is
correlated with overall depression severity and thus may not represent a
distinct depressive subtype per se." This is a cross-sectional
description within one adult treatment-seeking cohort; it establishes
correlation with severity and suicide-attempt history, not a longitudinal
or causal claim.

### Confirmatory report (2009)

A later, larger STAR*D sample — 2,307 adult participants with major
depressive disorder (verified against the PubMed abstract, PMID
19207123) — used the clinician-rated Inventory of Depressive
Symptomatology to define irritability by the same threshold (present at
least half the preceding week). 1,067 of 2,307 (46%) met it. Irritable
participants were again more likely female and younger, with greater
depression severity and anxiety, poorer quality of life, and more prior
suicide attempts or suicidal ideation. Logistic regression found that
bipolar-spectrum features were *not* more common among those with
irritability. The authors' own conclusion, quoted: "irritable depression
is not a distinct subtype of MDD, but irritability is associated with
greater overall severity, anxiety comorbidity and suicidality." This
explicit negative finding — no association with bipolar-spectrum features
in this adult sample — argues against reading irritability in unipolar
depression as a marker of hidden bipolarity in this population. It says
nothing about irritability's meaning in child or adolescent samples, which
is a separate literature and population, covered next.

## Irritability as a transdiagnostic dimension — child and adolescent literature

Everything in this section is child or adolescent research. The program
associated with Leibenluft, Stringaris, and Brotman treats irritability
not as a symptom specific to one diagnosis but as a dimensional trait
cutting across depressive, anxiety, and disruptive-behavior presentations
in youth — a different framing from either the anger-attack construct or
the STAR*D findings above, both of which describe adults.

### The Affective Reactivity Index

The program's standard measurement instrument, the Affective Reactivity
Index (ARI), already has its own entry at
[affective-reactivity-index](../../../../../../glossary/terms/affective-reactivity-index.md):
a brief, 7-item (6 scored plus 1 impairment item) parent- and self-report
scale, validated in a U.S. sample of 218 children and adolescents recruited
at the National Institute of Mental Health (bipolar disorder n=39, severe
mood dysregulation n=67, children at familial risk for bipolar disorder
n=35, healthy volunteers n=77) and a U.K. sample of 88 drawn from a
general child mental-health setting and from schools. Internal consistency
is described in the original validation abstract only qualitatively as
"excellent"; exact alpha and test-retest coefficients were not given in
that abstract and were not independently re-derived from the paper's
full-text tables for this note — a gap flagged rather than filled with a
number from elsewhere, since later cross-cultural replication alphas (for
example, an Italian-sample study reporting 0.79 and 0.78) describe those
later samples, not the founding validation study's own figures.

### Genetic overlap with depressed mood — adolescent twins

Stringaris, Zavos, Leibenluft, Maughan, and Eley (2012; verified against
the PubMed abstract, PMID 22193524) analyzed a UK adolescent twin/sibling
sample of 2,651 and found the genetic correlation (rA) between
irritability and depressed mood was 0.70 (95% CI 0.59-0.82) — markedly
higher than the genetic correlation between "headstrong/hurtful" oppositional
behavior and depression, 0.46 (95% CI 0.36-0.57). The reverse pattern held
for delinquency: headstrong/hurtful behavior correlated more strongly with
delinquency (rA = 0.80, 95% CI 0.72-0.86) than irritability did (rA = 0.57,
95% CI 0.45-0.69). The authors read this, via the "generalist genes"
hypothesis, as evidence that genes of broad effect underlie the
irritability-depression overlap specifically, as distinct from the
oppositional-behavior-delinquency overlap. This is a twin design in one UK
adolescent cohort: a genetic correlation of 0.70 describes shared genetic
liability between two phenotypes as measured in this sample, not a
specific gene, a specific neurobiological mechanism, or a claim about
adult-onset presentations, and it does not by itself show that
irritability converts into depression in an individual case.

### Longitudinal prediction — a child-to-young-adult twin cohort

Savage, Verhulst, Copeland, Althoff, Lichtenstein, and Roberson-Nay (2015;
verified against the PubMed abstract, PMID 25901774) used the Swedish
Twin Study of Child and Adolescent Development — 1,348 twin pairs, parent-
report measures at four waves from ages 8-9 to 19-20 — to fit genetically
informed cross-lagged models. Irritability more strongly predicted later
anxious/depressed symptoms than the reverse path, and this cross-lagged
relationship reached significance only in late childhood/early
adolescence. Both additive genetic and unique environmental factors
contributed significantly to both constructs; shared/common environmental
factors linked the two as well, but contributed less variance than
genetic or unique-environmental factors. The authors describe this as
supporting "a causal role of irritability on anxiety/depression at older
ages" — their own phrase for a cross-lagged statistical pattern under
their model's assumptions, not evidence from an experimental
manipulation. This is child-to-young-adult data (ages 8-9 through 19-20);
it says nothing directly about irritability-depression dynamics in adults
with first onset in adulthood.

### A translational mechanism review

Brotman, Kircanski, Stringaris, Pine, and Leibenluft (2017; verified
against the PubMed abstract, PMID 28103715) integrate clinical,
experimental-psychology, and translational neuroscience findings into a
two-mechanism model of pathological irritability in youth: aberrant
responding to frustrative non-reward (reward-system dysfunction, see the
house's [frustrative nonreward](../../../../../../../../../../../psychology-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/frustrative-nonreward.md)
entry for the underlying RDoC construct) and aberrant approach-response to
threat, each linked to prefrontal, striatal, and amygdalar function, with
reward and threat processing said to "potentiate one another." This is
explicitly a review and model paper proposing hypotheses for future
testing — the abstract itself says "future work should test
pathophysiological hypotheses" — not a single study reporting its own
sample size or effect size, and it should not be cited as if it
independently demonstrated a new empirical finding. The house's
[reactive-aggression](../../../../../../../../../../../psychology-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/reactive-aggression.md)
entry documents a live, unresolved directional conflict adjacent to this
model: adults with intermittent explosive disorder show *exaggerated*
amygdala reactivity to social threat, while children with severe mood
dysregulation show *reduced* amygdala and striatal activation to a
frustrating, non-social feedback task — two different paradigms in two
different populations, not one unified circuit story, and that entry's
own caution against collapsing them applies here as well.

### Developmental course and heritability — an unverified summary figure

A frequently repeated secondary claim states that irritability's
heritability runs "around 30-40%," comparable to depression and anxiety.
This figure recurred across review articles and commentary found in the
search behind this note, but was not traced to one named primary twin
study with its own quoted heritability estimate and confidence interval.
It is reported here, as it was in the search, as an unverified summary
figure — not a confirmed primary-source number alongside the two named
twin studies above.

## Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder

Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (DMDD) already has a full entry at
[disruptive-mood-dysregulation-disorder](../../../../../../glossary/terms/disruptive-mood-dysregulation-disorder.md).
It is a DSM-5 diagnosis, introduced in 2013, exclusively for children:
chronic, non-episodic irritable or angry mood present most days, plus
frequent temper outbursts grossly out of proportion to the situation. What
follows summarizes the contested parts of its evidence base, because the
note's larger argument — that irritability names several things the
citation traffic tends to blur together — turns on exactly this
disorder's internal tensions.

### Precursor: severe mood dysregulation

DMDD was adapted from severe mood dysregulation (SMD), a research
construct Leibenluft, Charney, Towbin, Bhangoo, and Pine proposed in 2003
(PMID 12611821) as a "broad phenotype" of pediatric bipolar disorder,
distinguished from narrow-phenotype, episodic mania. Per secondary
characterization of the broader research program (not independently
re-verified against the 2003 paper's own methods section for this note),
SMD's operational criteria included onset before age 12, symptoms present
12 or more months, abnormal mood most days noticeable to others,
hyperarousal, and marked reactivity to negative emotional stimuli (e.g.,
tantrums or rages) at least three times a week. SMD was never itself a
DSM diagnostic category, and the DSM-5 workgroup dropped its hyperarousal
requirement when converting it into DMDD — related but not identical
constructs.

### Community prevalence and comorbidity — children ages 2-17

Copeland, Angold, Costello, and Egger (2013; verified against the PubMed
abstract, PMID 23377638) pooled three community studies — 7,881
observations of 3,258 children and adolescents ages 2-17, using structured
psychiatric interviews. Three-month DMDD prevalence ranged 0.8%-3.3%
across samples, highest in preschoolers, and dropped only slightly under
strict application of the exclusion criterion. DMDD co-occurred with every
common psychiatric disorder assessed: odds ratios with depressive
disorders ranged 9.9-23.5, and with oppositional defiant disorder (ODD)
52.9-103.0. DMDD occurred alongside another diagnosis 62%-92% of the time,
and alongside both an emotional and a behavioral disorder simultaneously
32%-68% of the time. Affected children showed elevated social impairment,
school suspension, service use, and poverty. The authors' own conclusion,
quoted: "disruptive mood dysregulation disorder is relatively uncommon
after early childhood, frequently co-occurs with other psychiatric
disorders, and meets common standards for psychiatric 'caseness.'" The
very high ODD odds ratios (52.9-103.0) reflect near-total diagnostic
overlap in this data and belong to the criticism discussed below — they
are not evidence that DMDD identifies a population distinct from ODD. A
companion editorial (Axelson, 2013, PMID 23377631) states, per secondary
summaries of its content (PubMed lists no structured abstract for this
comment piece, so its exact wording was not independently confirmed from
primary text for this note), that the scientific support for DMDD at
DSM-5's publication came primarily from studies of the related SMD
construct, and that Copeland's paper was among the first to apply the
actual DMDD criteria empirically.

### Clinical and inpatient rates, and an informant discrepancy — hospitalized children ages 5-12

Margulies, Weintraub, Basile, Grover, and Carlson (2012; verified against
the PubMed abstract, PMID 22713098) studied 82 hospitalized children ages
5-12. 30.5% met DMDD criteria by parent report, but only 15.9% by direct
inpatient-unit observation. Among the 56% of children with reported manic
symptoms, 45.7% met DMDD criteria by parental history versus 17.4% by
direct unit observation. The authors treat this history-versus-observation
discrepancy as the central finding, not a general "about 30% of children"
prevalence claim — it is a single-informant estimate in one inpatient
sample, far above the Copeland community estimate of 0.8%-3.3%, and the
gap between what parents report and what clinicians observe on the ward
is itself the thing this study establishes.

### Reliability, and the ICD-11 refusal

DMDD's diagnostic reliability is a documented weak point. In the DSM-5
field trials — paired, independent clinician interviews of the same
patients on separate occasions (Regier and colleagues, 2013, PMID
23111466) — DMDD's test-retest kappa was reported as 0.25, in the
"questionable" range, with substantial site-level variability: roughly
0.49-0.50 at one predominantly inpatient site, "unacceptable" at other,
more outpatient, sites. Stated plainly, per the house's own verification
rule: **the kappa = 0.25 figure is verified here against a secondary
peer-reviewed review's citation of the Regier et al. report, not directly
against the primary paper's own reported table** — this dossier's writer
did not independently pull the per-diagnosis kappa table from the primary
article.

Partly on the strength of this reliability picture and the near-total ODD
overlap documented above, the ICD-11 working group (Lochman, Evans,
Burke, and colleagues, 2015, PMID 25655147) declined to adopt an
equivalent stand-alone DMDD category. Per search-derived summaries of the
paper (the abstract fetch for this PMID returned no text, so this
characterization was not independently confirmed against primary text for
this note), the working group recommended instead that ICD-11 use a
chronic-irritability/anger specifier on oppositional defiant disorder,
rather than a separate diagnosis. This is a nosological and classification
judgment by one expert panel about DMDD as a bounded diagnostic category —
it is not, on its own terms, a finding that chronic childhood irritability
is not a real or impairing clinical phenomenon. The community-prevalence
and inpatient-overlap findings above speak to validity and diagnostic
overlap; the kappa figure speaks only to inter-rater reliability; and none
of the three should be collapsed into one verdict on the underlying
construct.

## Intermittent explosive disorder — adult epidemiology

Intermittent explosive disorder (IED) already has a full entry at
[intermittent-explosive-disorder](../../../../../../glossary/terms/intermittent-explosive-disorder.md).
It is defined by recurrent, discrete episodes of impulsive aggression
grossly out of proportion to any precipitating stressor, without DMDD's
required chronic between-episode mood disturbance, and its epidemiology —
unlike the dimensional-irritability program above — was measured in
adults. Kessler, Coccaro, Fava, Jaeger, Jin, and Walters (2006; verified
against the PubMed abstract, PMID 16754840) used the National Comorbidity
Survey Replication (NCS-R), a nationally representative, face-to-face
household survey of 9,282 U.S. adults age 18+ conducted 2001-2003 with
the WHO Composite International Diagnostic Interview. Lifetime IED
prevalence was 7.3%; 12-month prevalence 3.9%. Cases reported a mean of
43 lifetime attacks and a mean of $1,359 in lifetime property damage;
injuries occurred at a rate of 180 per 100 lifetime cases; mean age at
onset was 14. Sociodemographic correlates were "uniformly weak." IED was
significantly comorbid with most DSM-IV mood, anxiety, and substance-use
disorders. Despite the early onset and heavy burden, treatment specific to
anger was rare: 60.3% of people with IED had at some point sought
treatment for some emotional or substance problem, but only 28.8% had
ever received anger-specific treatment, and only 11.7% of 12-month cases
had received anger-specific treatment in the past year — a gap the
authors call a target for early detection and outreach.

A secondary, frequently repeated claim holds that the 7.3% lifetime figure
reflects a "broad" definition (three or more lifetime impulsive-anger
attacks), against a narrower 5.4% figure requiring three or more attacks
within a single year. This distinction did not appear in the primary
abstract's own wording as obtained for this note — only the single
lifetime (7.3%) and 12-month (3.9%) figures were directly confirmed there
— so the broad/narrow framing is reported as an unverified secondary
characterization, not a confirmed primary-source figure.

Methodologically, these are retrospective self-report estimates from a
single national survey using structured, lay-administered diagnostic
interviews rather than clinician-confirmed diagnoses; the reported mean of
43 lifetime attacks and the dollar-figure property-damage estimate are
recall-dependent and should not be read as precise counts. The finding
describes the U.S. adult general population circa 2001-2003; it says
nothing about child or adolescent IED prevalence, non-U.S. populations, or
the relationship between IED (a discrete-episode construct) and DMDD's
chronic, non-episodic irritability — related but explicitly distinct
constructs that this survey does not equate.

## Reactive versus proactive aggression

A separate strand of the aggression literature distinguishes reactive
(impulsive, provoked, anger-driven) from proactive (planned, instrumental,
unprovoked) aggression as two correlated but psychometrically and
developmentally separable dimensions. The house's
[reactive-aggression](../../../../../../../../../../../psychology-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/reactive-aggression.md)
entry already covers the neurobiological side of this distinction — the
rodent-only causal evidence for a hypothalamic aggression node, and the
conflicting directions of amygdala findings in adult IED versus irritable
children, both noted above. The instrument most commonly used to separate
the two dimensions behaviorally is the Reactive-Proactive Aggression
Questionnaire (RPQ).

Raine, Dodge, Loeber, Gatzke-Kopp, Lynam, Reynolds, Stouthamer-Loeber, and
Liu (2006; verified against the PubMed abstract, PMID 20798781) — a
longitudinal study of schoolboys, this is child/adolescent research —
gave the 23-item RPQ (11 proactive items, 12 reactive items) to 334 boys
at age 16, with antisocial, psychosocial, and personality measures
collected at ages 7 and 16. Confirmatory factor analysis supported a
two-factor proactive/reactive structure that replicated across
independent subsamples. Proactive aggression at age 7 was uniquely
characterized by fight initiation, "strong-arm" tactics, delinquency, poor
school motivation, poor peer relationships, single-parent status,
psychosocial adversity, substance-abusing parents, and hyperactivity; at
age 16, by psychopathic personality traits, blunted affect, delinquency,
and serious violent offending. Reactive aggression at age 16 was uniquely
characterized by impulsivity, hostility, social anxiety, lack of close
friends, unusual perceptual experiences, and ideas of reference. The
authors describe the RPQ as "a brief but reliable and valid self-report
instrument" for assessing the two dimensions "in child and adolescent
samples" — their own population claim, stated as such.

This is a study of adolescent and pre-adolescent boys only; the abstract
does not mention girls, and this original validation is specific to that
population, though the RPQ has since been used more broadly across ages
and both sexes in other validation work not detailed or verified in this
note. The reactive/proactive distinction, as established here, is a
descriptive-correlational structure — two dimensions separated by
self-report and file/interview correlates in one longitudinal design — not
a claim about distinct neurobiological causal pathways; a neurobiological
version of that distinction is the separate claim covered in the
reactive-aggression entry linked above, with its own, still-unresolved,
directional conflict.

## What remains unresolved

Several disagreements above are live, not settled, and should not be
smoothed into a single narrative:

- **Whether STAR*D irritability marks a distinct depressive subtype.**
  Both Perlis reports (2005, adult n=1,456; 2009, adult n=2,307) answer
  this directly: no, irritability tracks overall severity and does not
  mark bipolarity. This is the one clean, internally consistent finding
  in the set, replicated across two STAR*D samples of different size.
- **Whether DMDD identifies a population distinct from oppositional
  defiant disorder.** The near-total odds-ratio overlap (52.9-103.0,
  Copeland et al., children ages 2-17) and the ICD-11 working group's
  preference for an ODD specifier over a stand-alone category both cut
  against a clean separation; DMDD's own advocates frame the diagnosis as
  identifying children with combined emotional and behavioral
  dysregulation regardless.
- **How prevalent DMDD actually is in children.** Community estimates
  (0.8%-3.3%, ages 2-17) and one hospitalized-sample parent-report
  estimate (30.5%, ages 5-12) differ by roughly an order of magnitude,
  and even within that hospitalized sample, parent report and direct
  clinical observation disagreed by close to a factor of two (30.5% vs
  15.9% overall; 45.7% vs 17.4% among children with manic symptoms).
  Nothing above resolves which informant or setting gives the "true" rate;
  the disagreement is itself part of the record.
- **The direction of amygdala involvement in anger-driven aggression.**
  Adults with intermittent explosive disorder show exaggerated amygdala
  reactivity to social-threat faces (Coccaro et al., adults, n=10 versus
  10 controls, per the reactive-aggression entry); children with severe
  mood dysregulation show reduced amygdala and striatal activation to a
  frustrating, non-social feedback task (Deveney et al., children, n=42).
  Different populations, different paradigms, opposite directions — not
  one circuit story.
- **Whether anger attacks resolve because of antidepressant
  pharmacology specifically.** Every quantitative treatment-response
  figure in Section 2 above (71% resolution, 1993; 84% reduction in
  reporting, 1991) comes from open-label case series with no placebo arm,
  at one U.S. academic center, in adults already seeking depression
  treatment — a real, repeatedly observed pattern within that one
  research program, and not yet an independently controlled finding.
