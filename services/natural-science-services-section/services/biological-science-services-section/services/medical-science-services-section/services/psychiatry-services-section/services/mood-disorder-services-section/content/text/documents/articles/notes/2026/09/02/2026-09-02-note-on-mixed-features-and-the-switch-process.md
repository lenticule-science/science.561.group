## What this argues

Psychiatry has built several distinct instruments for describing a
depressive state that also carries excitatory features — agitation,
irritability, racing thoughts, mood lability — and several distinct
instruments for describing the transition between depressive and manic
poles. None of them measure the same thing, and several of them
disagree with each other on basic questions: how the excitatory
symptoms should be counted, how common mixed presentations are, whether
antidepressants cause switches, and whether recurrence itself
accelerates over an illness course. This note lays out what each
instrument was built to measure, what it actually found, and where a
live disagreement between primary sources remains unresolved rather
than settled by a later study. The unifying observation, stated once at
the end rather than smoothed into every section: every construct
reviewed here — a symptom checklist, a rating scale, an episode count,
a registry hazard ratio — measures how depressed, agitated, or
symptomatic a person currently is, or how many discrete episodes they
have already had. None of them outputs a number for how close the
system is, right now, to flipping into the other pole. The one
framework built explicitly to measure proximity to a transition rather
than severity of the current state exists, has been tested, and by its
own most recent systematic review does not yet work.

## The DSM-5 mixed-features specifier: what it admits, what it excludes

The [mixed features specifier](../../../../../../glossary/terms/mixed-features-specifier.md)
is DSM-5's course specifier for a major depressive, manic, or hypomanic
episode: at least three of seven listed manic/hypomanic symptoms
present nearly every day for the majority of the episode. Its defining
methodological choice — the crux this whole literature turns on — is
what it deliberately leaves out. The DSM-5 task force excluded
psychomotor agitation, irritability, and distractibility from the
admitted symptom list because these three were judged to overlap with
core depressive and anxiety symptoms and unable to reliably signal
manic rather than depressive polarity. Malhi and colleagues (2017)
argued in print that this exclusion may have stripped out core features
of mixed states rather than merely overlapping noise, proposing a
return to a Kraepelinian framework scoring mood, ideation, and activity
as three semi-independent axes instead of a symptom checklist — a
published critique and argument, not an empirical result that has
displaced the DSM-5 criteria as the operative standard.

Because the specifier's admitted list excludes the three most commonly
reported excitatory features of depression, its measured prevalence is
acutely sensitive to which instrument is used to apply it. Zimmerman
and Mackin (2023) assessed 309 depressed patients against seven
different operational definitions — two derived from a dedicated DSM-5
mixed-features interview, five from Young Mania Rating Scale-derived
algorithms — and found prevalence varying roughly 8-fold across
definitions, with poor agreement between the interview-based and
YMRS-based approaches despite each showing some independent validity.
A later Zimmerman and Mackin study (2026; 459 psychiatric outpatients)
tested lowering the required symptom count from three to two: prevalence
rose from 3.9% to 13.1%, roughly a 3.4-fold increase, but the newly
captured two-criteria-only group showed no statistically meaningful
differences from the below-threshold group on bipolar family history,
functional impairment, comorbidity, or suicide risk — arguing against
loosening the threshold, though the study only tested the 2-versus-3
question, not whether 3 is itself well-calibrated against non-DSM
frameworks. A separate large multi-site sample, the International Mood
Disorders Collaborative Project (McIntyre et al., 2015; 982 individuals
in a current mood episode — 506 MDD, 216 bipolar I, 130 bipolar II),
found the DSM-5 specifier met by 26.0% (n=149) of the MDD group, 34.0%
(n=65) of the bipolar-I group, and 33.8% (n=49) of the bipolar-II
group — a materially different rate from either Zimmerman figure above,
which is exactly the instrument-dependence problem the Zimmerman 2023
comparison documents: these three prevalence figures should not be
compared as if identical methodology produced them.

## Koukopoulos's mixed depression and agitated depression: the competing constructs

Where the DSM-5 specifier is built on excluding psychomotor agitation,
irritability, and distractibility, Athanasios Koukopoulos's
[mixed depression](../../../../../../glossary/terms/mixed-depression.md)
construct is built on the opposite decision: it foregrounds psychic
agitation, marked irritability, and mood lability as core, defining
features of a bipolar mixed state occurring alongside a full depressive
syndrome. In Koukopoulos's own later scheme, this construct further
splits into [agitated depression](../../../../../../glossary/terms/agitated-depression.md) —
mixed depression with psychomotor agitation present — versus mixed
depression proper, defined by the absence of psychomotor agitation. The
two labels are not synonyms in Koukopoulos's own usage, even though
different authors elsewhere in the literature have used them
inconsistently.

A validation study of Koukopoulos's criteria in 435 clinic-referred
patients with a major depressive episode (Sani et al., 2014,
International Mood Network sites) found the most prevalent individual
criteria were absence of psychomotor retardation (84%), mood lability
or marked reactivity (78%), and psychic agitation or inner tension
(75%); the overall diagnostic model achieved 76.4% sensitivity and
86.3% specificity against clinical correlates (CGI severity, rapid
cycling, substance-abuse history) used as validators — concurrent or
construct validity against clinical correlates, not diagnostic accuracy
against an independent structured-interview gold standard. When
Koukopoulos's criteria (KMxD) and the DSM-5 mixed-features specifier
(MFS) were compared head-to-head in 300 patients with a major
depressive episode (155 unipolar, 145 bipolar; Mineo et al., 2022), both
discriminated bipolar from unipolar depression with similar overall
accuracy by ROC-AUC — KMxD 71.9% (95% CI 66.2–77.7%) versus DSM-5 MFS
77.0% (95% CI 72.0–82.1%) — with KMxD more sensitive and DSM-5 MFS more
specific. Neither framework dominates the other in this single Italian
clinical sample, and the comparably-sized effect should not be read as
settling which construct is "correct."

Koukopoulos's criteria have also been operationalized as a dedicated
instrument, the Koukopoulos Mixed Depression Rating Scale, validated in
350 patients (Sani et al., 2018) with internal consistency Cronbach's
α = 0.76, inter-rater reliability κ = 0.73, a two-factor structure
(anger, psychomotor excitation) accounting for 80% of variance, and
predictive-validity C-statistic 0.82 — an instrument built on a rival,
non-DSM construct, whose own authors flag the validation as
cross-sectional. A study or clinician reporting "mixed depression"
therefore has to specify which framework was used: the DSM-5 specifier
and Koukopoulos's criteria are built on opposite decisions about which
symptoms count, and yield materially different sensitivity/specificity
trade-offs on the same patients.

The historical lineage runs further back than either modern framework.
Kraepelin described "excited depression" as one of his mixed states of
manic-depressive insanity; his student Weygandt coined "agitated
depression" for the same combination of depressed mood, psychomotor
agitation, and flight of ideas. Koukopoulos revived and extended this
lineage in his own scheme. The primary validation papers for the
agitated-depression reframing (Koukopoulos et al., 2005; Koukopoulos et
al., 2007) are conceptual and clinical-validation papers proposing and
defending a nosological distinction, not large epidemiological studies,
and their full numeric results were not independently verified beyond
what a secondary review (Verdolini et al., 2015) reports.

## How common this is in patients presenting with depression: BRIDGE and BRIDGE-II-MIX

The BRIDGE study (Angst et al., 2011) assessed 5,635 adults with a
current major depressive episode, clinic-referred across 521 treatment
centers in 18 countries, at a single psychiatrist-administered
consultation. 16.0% met DSM-IV-TR bipolar I/II criteria outright; 47.0%
met a broader, separately validated "bipolarity specifier" built from
family history of mania/hypomania, multiple past mood episodes,
antidepressant-induced hypomania, current mixed symptoms, and comorbid
substance use — an additional 31 percentage points identified beyond
DSM-IV-TR alone. This 47.0% is the rate meeting a broadened, non-DSM
specifier the authors themselves designed and validated, not a rate
confirmed by independent structured diagnostic follow-up in the same
study.

A second, independent multinational cohort, BRIDGE-II-MIX (Perugi et
al., 2015; 2,811 adults with a DSM-IV-TR major depressive episode
across 239 centers in 8 countries), sharpened the same point using
mixed features specifically: 29.1% met a broad, research-based
"depressive mixed state" definition permitting overlapping symptoms,
versus only 7.5% meeting the strict, non-overlapping-symptom DSM-5
specifier. The most common individual manic/hypomanic symptoms in the
whole sample were irritable mood (32.6%), emotional/mood lability
(29.8%), and distractibility (24.4%) — dominated by exactly the three
symptoms the DSM-5 specifier excludes; euphoria and grandiosity were
comparatively rare. Both BRIDGE cohorts sampled patients who had
already presented for psychiatric care, which skews toward more severe
or complex presentations than a general-population sample of depressed
people — these rates should not be generalized to a non-clinical
population.

Within the same BRIDGE-II-MIX cohort, a substantial minority — 475 of
2,811 patients (16.90%) — had a history of antidepressant-induced
hypomania or mania (Barbuti et al., 2017), and this subgroup resembled
the bipolar-disorder comparison group more than the remaining MDD group
on family history of bipolar disorder and rates of atypical features,
comorbid anxiety, eating, and borderline personality disorders. The
causal direction — unmasking a pre-existing bipolarity versus a
drug-induced state in someone who would not otherwise have switched — is
not settled by this cross-sectional, retrospective design.

## Irritability, anger, and anger attacks as markers

[Irritability](../../../../../../../../../irritability-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/irritability.md)
is named as a core feature in Koukopoulos's mixed-depression criteria
and among the most common individual symptoms in BRIDGE-II-MIX, but the
irritability construct itself covers a trait-level disposition and a
bounded episode-level presentation that are measured differently across
studies — a distinction that matters for reading any single prevalence
figure attached to the word. A related but narrower construct,
[anger attacks](../../../../../../../../../irritability-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/anger-attack.md) —
sudden, situationally disproportionate spells of anger with autonomic
or panic-like features — were reported by Fava and colleagues (1993) in
44% of 127 medication-free depressed outpatients at baseline, resolving
in 71% of the 85 patients who completed 8 weeks of open-label
fluoxetine treatment and had reported attacks. This is a single research
program's uncontrolled, open-label case series with no placebo arm; the
resolution rate cannot be separated from a general antidepressant
response, a placebo response, or regression to the mean.

Anger attacks during a depressive episode were reported as markedly
more common in bipolar than unipolar depression by Perlis and colleagues
(2004): 62% of a 29-patient bipolar-depression group versus 26% of a
50-patient unipolar-MDD group, with anger attacks an independent
predictor of bipolarity in multiple logistic regression. The authors
themselves describe this as a preliminary, small-sample finding needing
confirmation — a 29-patient subsample should not be read as a stable
population estimate.

## The switch process: the NIMH research-ward studies and what survived them

The foundational descriptive work on abrupt mood-polarity transitions is
a 1972 three-part NIMH intramural research-ward series (Bunney, Murphy,
Goodwin, and colleagues). Patients with manic-depressive illness were
followed through spontaneous switches on a specialized inpatient ward,
with investigators recording behavioral ratings, sleep (including
EEG/REM), and urinary and CSF monoamine metabolites across the
transition. This is longitudinal, intensive inpatient observation, not a
randomized or population-based study, and the exact patient count for
Paper I could not be retrieved from the available abstract record — its
cohort size is unverified pending direct access to the full text.

What has recurred essentially unchanged in the modern literature is the
purely descriptive finding: switches from depression into mania can
occur within minutes to a couple of days, and marked insomnia with
reduced REM sleep often precedes the switch. This is the direct
ancestor of Wehr's later sleep-deprivation work (below). The
mechanistic claim — a specific, switch-linked shift in catecholamine
metabolism as the proximate cause — has not been confirmed as a clean,
singular mechanism; a 2010 narrative review (Salvadore et al.) frames
catecholamine abnormalities as one of several candidate mechanisms,
alongside neurotrophic and neuroplastic factors, HPA-axis hyperactivity,
and circadian-rhythm disruption, rather than a confirmed, isolated
pathway. No direct experimental replication attempting to falsify the
monoamine-switch hypothesis specifically was found. Fifty years on, this
body of work does not establish a validated biomarker or lab test for an
impending switch, and it does not distinguish whether sleep loss
precedes the switch as cause or as an early symptom of the same
underlying process — the 1972 series itself treats this as an open,
correlational question.

The clearest direct descendant is Wehr's sleep-deprivation work. Wehr
and colleagues (1982) monitored wrist activity and sleep longitudinally
in 15 rapidly cycling and 52 non-rapidly cycling manic-depressive
patients; among 9 rapidly cycling, drug-free, depressed patients given
one night of total sleep deprivation (40 hours awake) to simulate a
spontaneous 48-hour sleep-wake cycle, 8 switched out of depression and 7
were rated manic or hypomanic. Colombo and colleagues (1999) later gave
up to three cycles of therapeutic sleep deprivation to 206 bipolar
depressed patients and found switch into mania in 4.85% of cycles and
into hypomania in 5.83% — rates the authors describe as comparable to
those seen with antidepressant drug treatment. These studies were
conducted specifically in bipolar, often rapid-cycling, drug-free
1980s-90s cohorts; they do not show the same mania risk holds in
unipolar depression or in patients on modern combination
pharmacotherapy. Wehr's "final common pathway" framing — sleep-wake
disruption as the convergent route to mania regardless of inciting
cause — is his own theoretical interpretation of convergent evidence,
not a finding established by any single study.

## Rapid and ultradian cycling: what a course specifier can and cannot bear

[Rapid cycling](../../../../../../glossary/terms/rapid-cycling.md) —
four or more mood episodes within twelve months, a definition
originating with Dunner and Fieve (1974) as a predictor of
lithium-prophylaxis failure — is a course specifier, not a fixed
subtype: DSM-5 states it can wax and wane over the illness course. A
2023 mixed-method systematic meta-review (Miola et al.) aggregating 22
reviews (30 underlying studies, 13,698 bipolar-disorder patients) found
27.6% (3,777 patients) met rapid-cycling criteria; a cross-sectional
cohort of the first 500 STEP-BD enrollees (Schneck et al., 2004) found
20% met the criteria in the preceding year. Both figures come from
help-seeking, tertiary-referral or research-registry samples — they are
cross-sectional estimates in clinical populations, not
population-representative incidence.

[Ultradian cycling](../../../../../../glossary/terms/ultradian-cycling.md)
pushes the same episode-counting logic to its outer, sub-daily edge —
mood-polarity changes reported faster than once every 24 hours — and
has never been adopted into DSM or ICD as a recognized specifier. Its
primary descriptive source is a case series of five hospitalized
bipolar patients (Kramlinger & Post, 1996) studied by retrospective
life-charting and prospective daily mood ratings, reporting cycle
frequencies down to distinct, abrupt shifts under 24 hours in patients
without evidence of personality disorder. A critical secondary
commentary (Goldberg, 2011) argues the construct lacks demonstrated
content and discriminant validity relative to other sources of
intrinsic moment-to-moment mood variability — ordinary affective
lability in borderline personality disorder or ADHD-associated mood
reactivity — and warns of a fashionable label being over-applied to
non-bipolar mood instability once an "ultra-rapid" specifier is
available to reach for.

The instructive point for this note is what both specifiers share
structurally: they count completed episodes after the fact. Rapid
cycling is only established once four episodes have already occurred in
a year; it says nothing about when the fifth is coming. Ultradian
cycling extends the same after-the-fact counting logic to a sub-daily
timescale and, on that extension, loses discriminant validity against
other causes of fast mood variability entirely. Neither specifier is
built to output a measure of imminence — only a retrospective count.

## Antidepressant-emergent switch: trials and registries disagree

Whether antidepressants trigger a manic or hypomanic switch in bipolar
depression, and at what rate, is a genuinely contested question in
[treatment-emergent affective switch](../../../../../../glossary/terms/treatment-emergent-affective-switch.md),
where randomized-trial meta-analyses and large naturalistic cohorts
report different answers and neither side has displaced the other. A
2004 systematic review of 12 short-term RCTs (1,088 patients; Gijsman et
al.) found a switch rate of 3.8% on antidepressant versus 4.7% on
placebo (risk ratio 1.00, 95% CI 0.47–2.13), with the authors explicitly
noting too few manic events occurred to have adequate power to detect a
real difference — a null result under low power, not demonstrated
safety. A 2025 network meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (1,362 patients; Oliva
et al.) likewise found no individual antidepressant with a significantly
higher switch risk than placebo (venlafaxine's point estimate was
highest, RR 4.53, 95% CI 0.47–43.25, crossing 1), rating overall
confidence in the evidence as low.

Large naturalistic cohort and registry studies report meaningfully
elevated risk by contrast. Tondo, Vázquez, and Baldessarini (2010)
pooled 109 studies (114,521 patients total; 35 studies in the
random-effects pooled analysis) and found 12.5% mania/hypomania risk
with antidepressant exposure versus 7.5% without, with tricyclics
carrying higher risk than serotonergic agents and mood stabilizers
showing only a minor protective effect the authors judge is probably
confounded by preferential prescribing to patients already judged
mania-prone. Viktorin and colleagues (2014), using a Swedish national
registry of 3,240 bipolar patients starting antidepressant treatment
with no prior-year antidepressant exposure, found antidepressant
monotherapy carried a hazard ratio of 2.83 for mania, while an
antidepressant added to an existing mood stabilizer carried no
increased — and, over the longer period studied, apparently
protective — risk. The RCT literature is short in duration, frequently
requires or permits mood-stabilizer co-treatment, often excludes
patients judged at high switch risk, and has too few manic events for
adequate power; the registry literature captures real-world monotherapy
use over longer follow-up with larger event counts, but is subject to
confounding by indication that none of these cohort studies can fully
rule out. Neither literature shows antidepressants cause switch in a
given individual case, and the two are answering different practical
questions rather than simply contradicting each other.

A post-hoc analysis of the CO-MED randomized trial (Olgiati & Serretti,
2023; 425 MDD outpatients, 28-week follow-up) complicates the picture
further. Rates of antidepressant-emergent mood switch varied hugely by
definition used: 48% (204/425) by a liberal ASRM-based definition, 35.8%
(152/425) by a 12-week-window definition, 20.7% (88/425) by a strict
sustained-threshold-crossing definition. Baseline mixed depressive
episodes were more common among switchers than non-switchers (11.7% vs.
5.9%, p=0.032), but self-reported irritability did not differ
significantly between the two groups (0.75±0.44 vs. 0.70±0.56, p=0.269),
nor did psychomotor agitation (p=0.490); increased talkativeness
independently predicted switch. Switchers had lower acute suicidal
ideation (residual ideation 20.8% vs. 41.3%, p<0.0001) but worse
lifetime suicidal-behavior severity (2.40±2.47 vs. 1.85±2.12, p=0.014),
and higher remission rates (41.2% vs. 18.4%, p<0.0001) than
non-switchers. This is a genuine internal complication for the
irritability-as-marker story told above: irritability is repeatedly
named a hallmark of mixed states in the conceptual literature, but in
this specific dataset it did not behave as a differentiator of
treatment-emergent switch — the switch association ran through
categorical "mixed episode" status and talkativeness, not through
irritability itself.

This finding sits in direct tension with a separate, contrarian result
from the STAR*D dataset (Perlis, Cusin & Fava, 2014; 2,397 MDD
outpatients). There, proposed DSM-5-like mixed-features symptoms —
operationalized from 6 self-report items of a mania subscale, not the
DSM-5 clinician interview — were associated with a greater, not lesser,
likelihood of remission across up to four sequential antidepressant
trials: 449 of 2,397 patients (18.7%) reported at least two mixed
symptoms, and their presence carried an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.16
(95% CI 1.03–1.28) for remission, with expansive mood and cheerfulness
the strongest individual drivers. This does not contradict the Olgiati
and Serretti finding above so much as measure a different outcome:
remission of the index depressive episode versus emergence of a new
hypomanic or manic episode. The two literatures should not be conflated
as though they answered the same question, and this note does not
resolve which outcome matters more for any given patient — only that
mixed features have been shown, in different cohorts and by different
measures, to predict both a better index-episode outcome and a higher
switch rate.

A smaller, explicitly low-powered data point: a 99-patient,
single-center conference abstract (Charaa et al., 2022; 89 MDD, 10
bipolar) reported mixed features (by Koukopoulos criteria) in 19.5%
overall — 80% of the 10-patient bipolar subgroup (8 patients) versus
12.3% of the MDD subgroup — with more substance abuse (p=0.005) and
suicide attempts (p=0.01) among mixed-features-positive patients, and
antipsychotic (but not antidepressant) treatment associated with lower
rates of mixed features (p<0.001). The bipolar-subgroup n of 10 is far
too small to support the 80% figure as a stable estimate, and this is a
conference abstract, not a full peer-reviewed methods paper.

## Kindling and episode sensitization: the statistical critique aimed at its core

Robert Post proposed, by analogy to electrophysiological kindling in
epilepsy, that mood episodes become progressively more frequent and less
dependent on external stressors as illness recurs. Register-based
cohort studies support the underlying correlation: a Danish national
case register of all hospital admissions for primary affective disorder
1971–1993 (Kessing et al., 1998; 20,350 first-admission patients) found
recurrence rate increasing with the number of previous episodes in both
unipolar and bipolar patients, and a Zurich cohort of 406 patients (186
unipolar, 220 bipolar; admitted 1959–1963, followed through 1997;
Kessing, Hansen, Andersen & Angst, 2004) found the same pattern held
even after using frailty models to control for individual predisposition
to recurrence.

A specific, statistically grounded critique challenges the stronger
claim central to the kindling metaphor — that each additional episode
itself causes progressively shorter subsequent intervals within a
person — rather than the underlying correlation. Anderson, Monroe,
Rohde, and Lewinsohn (2016) identify "Slater's fallacy": as episodes
accumulate, individuals who happen to have consistently short
intermorbid intervals become a progressively larger share of whoever is
still left to be observed for a next episode, which manufactures the
appearance of accelerating intervals across the sample even if no
individual patient's own risk is truly accelerating. In their own
304-participant sample (drawn from the Oregon Adolescent Depression
Project, all with at least two lifetime major depressive episodes), the
"accelerating cycle" pattern disappeared once this artifact was
corrected for; instead, high-recurrence individuals showed consistently
short intermorbid intervals from their very first episode onward — a
between-person trait difference in vulnerability, not a within-person,
episode-caused sensitization process.

These two literatures are not measuring quite the same claim, and this
is an unresolved dispute between two explanations for the same
population-level correlation, not a case where one side has been shown
false and the other confirmed. The Kessing-type finding — recurrence
risk correlates with episode count — is not directly contradicted by
Anderson and colleagues; what is directly challenged is the specific,
stronger prediction that recurrence genuinely accelerates within an
individual over time. Neither literature constitutes a randomized or
causally interventional test of kindling, and none of these studies used
molecular or electrophysiological data from mood-disorder patients
paralleling actual epilepsy-kindling protocols — "kindling" remains an
analogy imported from a different literature, not a demonstrated shared
mechanism.

## The unifying point: measuring how low, not how close to flipping

Laid side by side, every construct in this note shares a structural
limitation the field has not addressed. The DSM-5 mixed-features
specifier counts symptom-checklist items present nearly every day across
a majority of an already-underway episode — a composition-and-severity
measure of a state the person is already in. Koukopoulos's mixed
depression and its rating scale are richer descriptively, foregrounding
agitation, irritability, and lability as core features rather than
excluded noise, but they are still cross-sectional symptom counts taken
during an episode, not a measure of how close that episode is to
flipping polarity. Rapid cycling and ultradian cycling count completed
episodes after the fact; rapid cycling is established only once four
episodes have already happened in a year, and pushing the same counting
logic down to sub-24-hour changes (ultradian cycling) loses discriminant
validity against ordinary mood lability rather than gaining any
predictive proximity measure. The NIMH switch-process studies describe
what a switch looks like once it is underway — minutes to days, often
preceded by insomnia and reduced REM sleep — but more than fifty years
later, no validated biomarker or lab test for an impending switch has
emerged from that line of work, only a widening set of candidate
mechanisms that have not converged on one confirmed pathway. Kindling
proposes that recurrence risk itself changes with episode count, but its
most specific, quantitative version — that risk accelerates within a
person, not merely differs between people — is directly challenged as a
statistical artifact by the sharpest critique aimed at it. And
antidepressant-emergent switch, measured either in short RCTs or large
registries, is a rate computed after enough people have already
switched to count them — not a signal computed on any one person before
their own switch.

One thread in the wider mood-disorder literature has tried directly to
build the missing instrument: a measure of proximity to a transition
rather than severity of the current state. [Critical slowing
down](../../../../../../glossary/terms/critical-slowing-down.md) and
the [early warning signal](../../../../../../glossary/terms/early-warning-signal.md)
framework it motivates propose using rising autocorrelation and variance
in repeated momentary mood ratings as a leading indicator of an
approaching mood-state transition, borrowed from the same
dynamical-systems logic used to anticipate ecosystem collapse and
climate tipping points. It exists, it has been tested prospectively
within single monitored individuals, and — per the most recent
systematic review of every published within-person study (Helmich et
al., 2024) — it does not yet work at a standard adequate for clinical
use: pooled sensitivity for detecting an actual transition was commonly
well under 50% and was 0% in one 42-patient remitted-MDD cohort, no
published study has yet attempted a fully prospective test free of the
false-positive inflation that a genuinely blind, repeated-testing design
would require, and a parallel meta-analysis of 126 ecological systems
found early warning signals detected before only 13% of transitions.
[Mood instability](../../../../../../glossary/terms/mood-instability.md),
the simpler and more established measure of how much affect swings in
magnitude, is a distinct construct from critical slowing down's claim
about the changing rate and structure of those swings, and neither
substitutes for the other.

The consequence, stated plainly rather than as a call to action: this
literature has built extensive, empirically grounded ways to measure how
depressed, agitated, mixed, or symptomatically labile a person is at a
given moment, and extensive ways to count how many discrete episodes or
switches a person has already had. It has not yet built a working way to
measure how close a currently depressed or currently stable person is to
their next flip. The one attempt built explicitly for that purpose is
tested, published, and — by its own most recent review — not yet
adequate to the task.

- [Early warning signal](../../../../../../glossary/terms/early-warning-signal.md) —
  the applied, tested, and currently inadequate proximity-to-transition

  distinguishes from proximity-to-transition

  the trait/state construct named as a core feature by Koukopoulos's

- [Anger attack](../../../../../../../../../irritability-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/anger-attack.md) —
  the narrower, autonomically-marked construct distinguished from
