Intermittent explosive disorder (IED) is defined by recurrent, discrete episodes of impulsive aggression — verbal or physical outbursts grossly out of proportion to whatever provoked them — rather than by a standing, between-episode mood disturbance. In the National Comorbidity Survey Replication (NCS-R), a nationally representative, face-to-face household survey of 9,282 U.S. adults age 18+ conducted 2001-2003 using the WHO Composite International Diagnostic Interview, Kessler, Coccaro, Fava, and colleagues (2006) found a lifetime IED prevalence of 7.3% and a 12-month prevalence of 3.9% — far higher than had previously been recognized. Cases reported a mean of 43 lifetime attacks and a mean of $1,359 in lifetime property damage, injuries occurred at a rate of 180 per 100 lifetime cases, and mean age at onset was 14.

IED should be distinguished from disruptive mood dysregulation disorder, the pediatric diagnosis covering chronic irritability. Where DMDD requires a chronic, non-episodic irritable or angry mood present most days between outbursts, IED is defined purely by the discrete episodes themselves, with no required background mood disturbance, and its epidemiology here describes the adult general population rather than children — the two are related but explicitly distinct constructs, and this survey's findings say nothing about IED's prevalence or presentation in children or adolescents.

Despite the disorder's early onset (mean age 14) and heavy associated burden, treatment contact specific to anger was rare: of people meeting lifetime IED criteria, 60.3% had at some point sought treatment for some emotional or substance problem, but only 28.8% had ever received anger-specific treatment, and only 11.7% of 12-month cases had received anger-specific treatment in the past year — a gap the authors call a target for early detection and outreach. Methodologically, these are retrospective self-report estimates from a single national survey using structured, lay-administered diagnostic interviews rather than clinician-confirmed diagnoses; the reported mean of 43 lifetime attacks and the dollar-figure property-damage estimate are recall-dependent and should not be read as precise counts. A widely repeated secondary claim that the 7.3% lifetime figure reflects a broad definition (three or more lifetime impulsive-anger attacks) against a narrower 5.4% figure (three or more attacks within one year) could not be traced to the primary abstract's own wording and is reported here only as an unverified secondary characterization, not a confirmed primary-source distinction.
