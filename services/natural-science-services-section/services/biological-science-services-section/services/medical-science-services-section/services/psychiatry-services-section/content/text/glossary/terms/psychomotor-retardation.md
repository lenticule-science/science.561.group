Psychomotor retardation is an objectively measurable slowing of movement and speech that occurs in depressive illness, distinct from a patient's subjective report of feeling slowed down. Per Bennabi and colleagues' systematic review, which synthesizes a large body of prior work (the review's own methods describe drawing on 116 papers, a scope reported by the review itself rather than independently re-derived from its full text for this entry), psychomotor abnormalities in depressed patients have been instrumented with actigraphy (wrist-worn accelerometry of spontaneous movement), cinematographic and kinematic analysis of movement, and ground-reaction-force platforms measuring gait and posture, in addition to the retardation subscales carried by standard clinician-rated depression scales. The review explicitly notes the field's lack of one standardized measurement approach across these methods: actigraphic counts, kinematic movement-time parameters, and force-platform indices are not interchangeable operationalizations of the same underlying slowing, and a study reporting "psychomotor retardation" without naming its measurement method is reporting one of several only loosely comparable things.

The review associates psychomotor retardation with abnormalities of prefrontal cortex function and of dopaminergic transmission specifically — a synthesis claim of the review, not a single controlled study demonstrating a causal prefrontal-dopamine-to-retardation chain. This places psychomotor retardation in the same neighborhood as, but not the same construct as, motivational anergia: the reduced willingness to exert effort for reward that is operationalized behaviorally by effort-discounting tasks such as the Effort Expenditure for Rewards Task, and argued by Treadway and Zald's reconsideration of anhedonia to be dissociable from consummatory pleasure and traceable to the same mesolimbic dopamine literature. Psychomotor retardation is a directly observed or instrumented slowing of actual movement and speech; motivational anergia is an effort-allocation computation inferred from choice behavior on a task. The two are read against the same accumbens-dopamine and prefrontal literature and plausibly share mechanism, but psychomotor retardation is not itself measured by, and does not reduce to, an effort-discounting task.

The consequence for research design is that treatment-response evidence for psychomotor retardation is genuinely mixed rather than settled, and should be reported as such rather than smoothed into one rule. In a cohort of 122 patients with unipolar psychotic depression, presence of psychomotor retardation at baseline predicted lower remission rates and greater persistence of delusions; baseline prevalence was 73%, falling to 35% after six weeks of treatment (Bennabi et al. 2020). Yet a more recent secondary analysis pooling nine randomized controlled trials states, in its own framing, that it remains unclear whether psychomotor retardation is independently associated with poorer prognosis — the authors of the largest pooled analysis located for this entry do not claim a settled answer. The 2013 review additionally reports a split by drug class: baseline retardation predicted response to milnacipran, a serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor, in at least one cited trial, while similarly designed studies found psychomotor retardation did not predict response to SSRIs. A research design that treats "psychomotor retardation predicts poor antidepressant response" as one general, drug-class-independent finding is not supported by this literature; a study should specify measurement method and antidepressant class before drawing a prognostic claim from baseline retardation.
