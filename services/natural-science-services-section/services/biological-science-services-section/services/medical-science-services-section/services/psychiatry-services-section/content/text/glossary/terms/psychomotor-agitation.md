Psychomotor agitation is excessive, non-goal-directed motor activity — pacing, restlessness, an inability to remain still — that accompanies mental tension and is tracked as a named, separate symptom across several distinct clinical contexts. The U.S. FDA's black-box warning language for antidepressants, issued in 2004, lists anxiety, agitation, panic attacks, insomnia, irritability, hostility, aggressiveness, impulsivity, akathisia, hypomania, and mania as reported symptoms following antidepressant initiation (regulatory text reported here as characterized by search results for this entry, not independently re-fetched from FDA.gov). The clinical literature groups much of this presentation under the label "activation syndrome" (Amitai et al. 2015), most documented in children and adolescents and most concentrated in the early weeks of treatment or after a dose change. Agitation is also part of the neuroglycopenic symptom cluster of hypoglycemia, in which brain glucose deprivation produces confusion, difficulty concentrating, irritability, and a combative or agitated presentation sometimes mistaken for intoxication — a cluster distinguished, in standard endocrinology teaching, from the earlier-warning autonomic symptoms of hypoglycemia (sweating, tremor, palpitations, hunger) that are mediated by catecholamine and cholinergic release rather than by glucose deprivation of the brain itself.

The FDA's own black-box list is itself evidence for the distinction this term exists to hold: it names "agitation" and "akathisia" as separate items in the same symptom list, rather than treating one as a subtype of the other. Crowner and colleagues' 1990 observational study makes the same distinction operational: a closed-circuit television camera was installed in each corner of the dayroom of a locked unit for violent psychiatric patients, and incidents of assault, together with the five minutes preceding each one, were recorded on videotape; all parties present were then rated specifically for akathisia's motor component, separately from any global judgment of how agitated the ward was. Psychomotor agitation is the nonspecific clinical sign — motor restlessness from any cause, including the underlying psychiatric illness itself — while akathisia is a specific, drug-induced motor state that can produce or mimic that sign and can be rated as its own variable, as Crowner and colleagues did.

The clinical and research consequence is that psychomotor agitation is a nonspecific sign that cannot be attributed to illness severity, or to any single mechanism, without first considering reversible medical causes and medication effects. The hypoglycemic neuroglycopenic pattern is reported in standard clinical teaching as an established pattern, but no primary study located for this entry gives a specific numeric prevalence of irritability or agitation as a hypoglycemia symptom; it remains, nonetheless, a reversible cause to exclude before agitation is read as psychiatric. And because a specific, ratable motor state — akathisia — can itself produce or be mistaken for agitation, an assessment or research design that records "agitation" as one undifferentiated sign, without separately rating for akathisia's motor component, risks treating a medication effect as evidence of worsening illness.
