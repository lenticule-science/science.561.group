Akathisia is a movement disorder of subjective inner restlessness and a compulsion to move — pacing, rocking, shifting weight from foot to foot, an inability to sit or stand still — associated with dopamine-receptor-blocking and other psychotropic medications. It is named, alongside agitation, hostility, aggressiveness, impulsivity, hypomania, and mania, as a reported symptom following antidepressant initiation in the U.S. FDA's black-box warning language issued in 2004 (regulatory text characterized via search results for this entry, not independently re-fetched from FDA.gov) — evidence that akathisia is treated, even by regulators, as its own distinct entity where it co-occurs with, and can be confused with, generalized agitation.

The clearest evidence that akathisia is separable from ordinary agitation, rather than a synonym for it, comes from Crowner and colleagues' 1990 observational study. A closed-circuit television camera was installed in each corner of the dayroom of a locked unit for violent psychiatric patients; incidents of assault, together with the five minutes preceding each assault, were recorded on videotape, and all parties present were rated for akathisia's motor component. The finding — "both victims and assailants were akathisic before about half of all incidents; bystanders rarely were" — is described as the first observational study of the akathisia-violence relationship, and its design is what makes the distinction operational: akathisia was scored as its own rateable variable, not inferred backward from the presence of agitation or violence. This is a single small-unit observational study, not a controlled trial, and the causal evidence linking akathisia to violence more broadly is limited, even in favorable secondary descriptions, to case reports and small case series. A 2021 systematic review, characterized via search result and not independently fetched for this entry, is reported as concluding that akathisia cannot be reliably linked to suicidal behavior in patients treated with antipsychotic medication — a more recent, systematic look at an adjacent question that found the evidence insufficient for a reliable causal link.

The practical consequence, and the reason this term is entered separately from psychomotor agitation rather than folded into it, is that akathisia is routinely mistaken for a worsening of the underlying psychiatric illness rather than recognized as an adverse effect of its treatment. Because akathisia presents as restlessness and motor agitation, and can co-occur with irritability, the same surface phenotype as a genuine psychiatric agitation or activation state, a clinician who does not separately assess for akathisia's specific motor component — as Crowner and colleagues' rating protocol did — risks reading a medication-induced state as evidence that the underlying illness, or the dose of an antipsychotic or antidepressant already given, needs to be increased rather than reconsidered. The evidence base for treating akathisia as a validated general cause of aggression remains thin — case reports and small case series, not controlled studies — so the correct clinical inference from observed agitation is not that akathisia is present, but that akathisia is one alternative explanation that must be actively assessed for and ruled in or out before attributing new agitation to illness progression.
