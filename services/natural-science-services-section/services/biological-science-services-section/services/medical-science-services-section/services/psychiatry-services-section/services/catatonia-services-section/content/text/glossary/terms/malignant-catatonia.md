Malignant catatonia is a severe variant of the catatonic syndrome marked by hyperthermia, autonomic instability, and severe rigidity, historically described in the world literature as "lethal catatonia" because of its substantial risk of death if untreated. A 1986 world-literature review characterized it as "a syndrome rather than a specific disease," arising from both functional psychiatric illness and organic (general-medical or neurological) disease, and reported that its prevalence appeared to have declined with modern psychopharmacologic treatment while cases continued to occur.

Malignant catatonia is clinically difficult to separate from neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS), the rigidity-and-hyperthermia syndrome associated with antipsychotic exposure, and the relationship between the two is contested rather than settled: a 1994 review proposing a revised malignant/nonmalignant catatonia nomenclature argued that malignant catatonia has causes beyond neuroleptic exposure, meaning NMS is one route into a broader malignant-catatonia picture rather than a categorically separate illness. Clinical reviews consistently describe a differential pattern — malignant catatonia tending to show waxy flexibility and catalepsy together with a preceding prodrome of excitement or agitation before rigidity sets in, versus NMS classically beginning with "lead-pipe" rigidity without such a prodrome, tied temporally to antipsychotic exposure — but no single primary study establishing this differential with reported sensitivity or specificity was located; it is best treated as clinical teaching rather than a validated discriminating instrument.

The consequence for clinical observation and research design is twofold. Quantified mortality figures for malignant catatonia circulate widely in secondary teaching, but the primary 1986 world-literature review itself reports no quantified mortality percentage, so any specific mortality figure asserted for malignant catatonia should be treated as unverified against that primary source rather than repeated as established fact. And because the excitement-before-rigidity prodrome pattern is descriptive teaching rather than a quantified, prospectively validated finding, establishing it as a genuine discriminator from NMS would require a head-to-head comparison with defined sensitivity and specificity — not yet available in the literature reviewed here — before the pattern can be used as a diagnostic instrument rather than a clinical impression.

## Related terms

- Catatonia — the broader syndrome of which this is the hyperthermic, high-mortality-risk variant
- Waxy flexibility — one of the signs whose presence, alongside catalepsy, features in the clinical differential from neuroleptic malignant syndrome
- Bush-Francis catatonia rating scale — the instrument used to rate severity and track treatment response
