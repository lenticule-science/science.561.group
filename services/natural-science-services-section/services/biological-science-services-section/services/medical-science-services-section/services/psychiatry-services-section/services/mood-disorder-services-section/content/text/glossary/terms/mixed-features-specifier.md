The DSM-5 (2013) "with mixed features" specifier applies to a major depressive episode — in either major depressive disorder or bipolar disorder — or to a manic/hypomanic episode, and requires at least three of seven listed manic/hypomanic symptoms present nearly every day for the majority of the days of the episode: elevated or expansive mood, inflated self-esteem or grandiosity, more talkative than usual or pressure to keep talking, flight of ideas or racing thoughts, increase in energy or goal-directed activity, increased or excessive involvement in risky activities, and decreased need for sleep.

The crux of the specifier is what it deliberately excludes. The DSM-5 task force excluded psychomotor agitation, irritability, and distractibility from Criterion B because these symptoms overlap with core depressive and anxiety symptoms and were judged unable to reliably distinguish manic from depressive polarity. This exclusion is the direct point of departure from Koukopoulos's non-DSM mixed-depression construct, whose criteria foreground psychic agitation, irritability, and mood lability as core features rather than excluded ones — the inverse choice from the DSM-5 task force's. Malhi and colleagues have argued in print that removing these three overlapping symptoms may strip out what are in fact core features of mixed states, proposing a return to a Kraepelinian framework that scores mood, ideation, and activity as three semi-independent axes rather than a symptom checklist; this is a published critique and argument, not an empirical demonstration that has displaced the DSM-5 criteria as the operative diagnostic standard.

Because the specifier's symptom list excludes the three most commonly reported excitatory features of depression, its measured prevalence depends heavily on the operational definition and instrument used. Zimmerman and Mackin (2023), assessing 309 depressed patients against seven different operational definitions (two derived from a dedicated DSM-5 mixed-features interview, five from Young Mania Rating Scale-derived algorithms), found prevalence varying roughly 8-fold across definitions, with poor agreement between the interview-based and YMRS-based approaches despite each showing some independent validity. In the BRIDGE-II-MIX study (Perugi et al. 2015; 2,811 patients with a major depressive episode across 239 centers in 8 countries), only 7.5% met the strict DSM-5 specifier, versus 29.1% meeting a broader research-based "depressive mixed state" definition that permitted overlapping symptoms; the most common individual manic/hypomanic symptoms in the whole sample were irritable mood (32.6%), emotional/mood lability (29.8%), and distractibility (24.4%) — precisely the symptoms the DSM-5 specifier excludes. A later Zimmerman and Mackin study (459 psychiatric outpatients) tested lowering the required count from 3 to 2 criteria: prevalence rose from 3.9% to 13.1%, but the newly captured 2-criteria-only group did not differ meaningfully from the below-threshold group on bipolar family history, functional impairment, comorbidity, or suicide risk, arguing against loosening the threshold. None of this settles whether the 3-criterion threshold is itself well-calibrated relative to non-DSM frameworks, which flag substantially higher rates using different logics (see mixed-depression). For research design, the practical consequence is that a reported prevalence for "mixed features in depression" is not interpretable without naming the exact instrument and criterion count used to generate it.
