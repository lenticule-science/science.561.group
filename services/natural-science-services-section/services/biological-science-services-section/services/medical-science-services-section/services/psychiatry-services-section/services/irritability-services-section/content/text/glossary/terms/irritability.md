Irritability is a low threshold for anger, frustration, or annoyance in response to provocation, but the single word covers two distinguishable phenomena in the clinical and research literature. In its trait sense, irritability is a dimensional, relatively stable disposition — a proneness to anger that a person carries across situations and over time, the sense used when Leibenluft, Stringaris, and Brotman describe irritability as a transdiagnostic dimension cutting across depressive, anxiety, and disruptive-behavior presentations in youth, and the sense the Affective Reactivity Index is built to quantify. In its episode or state sense, irritability names a bounded period during which the trait manifests — the "present at least half the preceding week" operationalization used in the STAR*D depression trial (Perlis and colleagues), or the chronic, "most days" mood criterion in disruptive mood dysregulation disorder, which itself couples that tonic-mood requirement to a separate, phasic criterion of discrete temper outbursts. That DSM-5 category requiring both a chronic mood state and frequent discrete outbursts under one label is itself evidence that the literature has not settled on one word for one phenomenon.

Irritability, in either sense, should be distinguished from an anger attack, a more narrowly defined research construct: a discrete, situationally disproportionate episode of anger with autonomic or panic-like features (tachycardia, sweating, flushing, subjective loss of control) studied as a subgroup phenomenon within major depressive disorder. An anger attack is always episodic and always autonomically marked; irritability, by contrast, can be a stable trait with no discrete autonomic episode at all, and even in its episode sense (an outburst, a period of elevated irritable mood) need not carry the panic-attack-like somatic signature that defines an anger attack.

The consequence for research design is that prevalence and correlational findings reported under the single word "irritability" are not directly comparable across studies unless the sense being measured is specified. STAR*D's approximately 40-46% adult prevalence figures (Perlis and colleagues, 2005 and 2009) describe a state-level threshold — present at least half of the preceding week — in a treatment-seeking depressed adult cohort, cross-sectional and not a claim about stable trait level. DMDD's community prevalence of well under 5% after early childhood (Copeland, Angold, Costello, and Egger, 2013, in children and adolescents ages 2-17) describes categorical diagnostic caseness combining the tonic-mood and phasic-outburst criteria. And a genetic correlation of 0.70 between irritability and depressed mood found in a UK twin/sibling sample of adolescents (Stringaris, Zavos, Leibenluft, Maughan, and Eley, 2012) describes the dimensional trait as captured by parent-report instruments in youth. Treating these as one number describing one phenomenon would conflate a self-reported symptom-week threshold in depressed adults, a categorical pediatric diagnosis, and a dimensional trait score in adolescent twins — three different measurement operations that happen to share a name.
