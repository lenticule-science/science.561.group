## What this argues

[Catatonia](../../../../../../glossary/terms/catatonia.md) is the
clinical syndrome where the transition between a low-arousal and a
high-arousal affective-motor state is least deniable: the same
diagnostic threshold, the same rating instrument, and — in a
substantial minority to a slight majority of patients depending on
which pole is used as the starting point — the same episode admit both
a stuporous, immobile, hypokinetic presentation and a purposeless,
agitated, hyperkinetic one. This note lays out that evidence: the
DSM-5-TR's unification of catatonia as a transdiagnostic sign-count
syndrome rather than a schizophrenia subtype; Kahlbaum's 1874
description of a cyclic course through both poles in the same
patients; a 1,456-patient cohort showing the two poles co-occurring
within episodes far more often than a mutually-exclusive-subtype model
would predict; the Bush-Francis Catatonia Rating Scale and its
interrater reliability; the lorazepam challenge test and its treatment
response rates, with ECT as a second-line option; the GABA-A
hypofunction hypothesis, marked throughout as a hypothesis resting on
small and largely unverified imaging samples; and the evidence that,
contrary to the historical Kraepelinian association, catatonia occurs
more often in affective illness than in schizophrenia. It ends
where the literature ends: a targeted search for any primary study
measuring the transition itself — its speed, its triggers, or a
biomarker of it — returned nothing. That absence is the open question
this section exists to hold, not a gap to paper over.

## Catatonia as a unified, transdiagnostic syndrome

DSM-5 removed the standalone "catatonic schizophrenia" subtype that
DSM-IV carried and replaced it with one syndrome, diagnosed by three or
more of twelve psychomotor signs — stupor, catalepsy, waxy flexibility,
mutism, negativism, posturing, mannerism, stereotypy, agitation not
influenced by external stimuli, grimacing, echolalia, and echopraxia —
applied through three routes: catatonia associated with another mental
disorder (a specifier attachable to mood, psychotic, neurodevelopmental,
or other disorders), catatonic disorder due to another medical
condition, and unspecified catatonia. DSM-5-TR, published in 2022, made
no substantive change to this definition.

This is verified against Rogers, Wilson & Oldham (2025), *BMC
Psychiatry*, DOI 10.1186/s12888-025-06857-6 — a narrative/comparative
review of ICD-11 against DSM-5-TR classification, not a primary data
study, cited here as the clearest secondary statement of the DSM-5-TR
criteria structure reached. The article states DSM-5-TR uses twelve
eligible clinical features (against ICD-11's fifteen) and that
"DSM-5-TR has not made substantive changes to the definition of
catatonia" relative to DSM-5. The exact twelve-item list and the
three-of-twelve threshold were cross-checked against a ResearchGate-
hosted figure caption and the emedicine/Medscape catatonia overview —
both secondary renderings of the same DSM-5-TR text, not the APA manual
itself, which sits behind no open-access route reached in this search.

What this threshold does not license: treating "three of twelve signs
present" as equivalent to "catatonia is present and etiologically
understood." The DSM-5-TR criteria are a phenomenological, sign-count
threshold, agnostic to mechanism and agnostic to which pole — retarded,
excited, or mixed — the qualifying signs come from.

A related terminological point: "catatonic depression" is not itself a
DSM diagnostic entity. The DSM-5/DSM-5-TR mechanism is a catatonia
specifier attached to a major depressive or bipolar diagnosis. Multiple
secondary sources converge on this without disagreement — the Rogers
et al. review above, and a case-series/review discussing catatonia and
melancholia as overlapping but formally distinct specifiers (catatonia
a psychomotor specifier; "with melancholic features" a separate
depressive-episode specifier), reached here only through search
synthesis rather than a fetched full text. This is well-supported by
convergent secondary description, not directly read off the DSM-5-TR
primary text. It licenses saying the *term* "catatonic depression" is
informal — not that the underlying clinical phenomenon (depression with
catatonic signs) is fringe; a substantial case and cohort literature,
discussed below, addresses exactly that phenomenon under the
specifier's clinical mechanism.

## Kahlbaum's original description: a cyclic course, not a static entity

Karl Ludwig Kahlbaum's 1874 monograph, *Die Katatonie oder das
Spannungsirresein*, described catatonia not as a disease entity but as
a syndrome with a cyclic, alternating course running through
melancholic (retarded), manic (excited), stuporous, confused, and — in
a subset — dementing phases in the same patients. Kahlbaum's own cases
already showed patients moving between low-arousal stuporous states and
high-arousal excited or agitated states within one clinical course, and
he explicitly did not treat catatonia as tied to one underlying disease.

This is 1874 German-language material, predating PubMed, and reached
here only through secondary historical accounts: Goldar, Starkstein &
Hodgkiss (1995), *History of Psychiatry*, DOI
10.1177/0957154X9500602205, PMID 11639692 — its abstract was read in
full, and it proposes a Wernicke/Kretschmer-derived dopaminergic
mechanistic hypothesis distinguishing two catatonic types ("akinetic
motality psychosis" and "catatonia sensu strictu"), but it does not
itself report Kahlbaum's case count or discuss whether he treated
catatonia as one disease entity — those specific historical claims rest
on a second source, "The Syndrome of Catatonia" (PMC4695780, full text
read), which states Kahlbaum's cases numbered around 25 to 26 and that
he "did not consider its presentation as indicative of a single disease
entity," describing it across depression, mania, and psychosis, with
long periods of immobility alternating with agitation or excitement in
the same patients, and a good-prognosis majority alongside a subset
progressing to poor or lethal outcome.

Kahlbaum's original report is an unsystematic case series — no formal N
reported consistently across secondary sources, cited variously as
around 25 or 26 cases — predating modern diagnostic methodology and not
independently re-verifiable here from the primary German text. What the
secondary sources report Kahlbaum as concluding: catatonia is a
syndrome cutting across affective and psychotic illness, definitionally
including both a stuporous/retarded pole and an excited pole, with
transition between them as part of the original clinical picture rather
than a modern reinterpretation. Treating the 1874 case count or
Kahlbaum's specific clinical narrative as independently confirmed
against the primary German text is not licensed by this search — only
English-language secondary historical accounts were read.

## The poles co-occur: cohort evidence against mutually exclusive subtypes

The clinical distinction between
[retarded catatonia](../../../../../../glossary/terms/retarded-catatonia.md)
and
[excited catatonia](../../../../../../glossary/terms/excited-catatonia.md)
is one of direction, not of which sign set is present: both draw on the
same twelve-item DSM-5-TR list and the same three-of-twelve threshold.
The strongest modern evidence on whether these are genuinely separate
subtypes or two ends of one alternating syndrome comes from Dawkins,
Cruden-Smith, Carter, Amad, Zandi, Lewis, David & Rogers (2022),
*Frontiers in Psychiatry*, DOI 10.3389/fpsyt.2022.886662, PMID
35677876, verified against the full text (PMC9168075).

The design is a retrospective cross-sectional study using electronic
health records from a large London mental health trust: 1,456 validated
catatonia diagnoses across 2,130 catatonic episodes. The exact numbers,
reported per *patient* rather than per episode: stupor/immobility was
present in 925 patients, of whom 105 (11.4%) also exhibited excitement;
of 196 patients with excitement, 105 (53.6%) also exhibited
immobility/stupor. These are the same 105 co-occurring patients viewed
from each denominator — a slight majority of patients with excited
catatonia also had stupor, while only a minority of patients with
stuporous catatonia also had co-occurring excitement. The 2,130-episode
total is a separate count (total episodes across the 1,456-patient
cohort) that the paper does not use as a denominator for the
925/196/105 figures, and no per-episode percentage should be computed
against it.

The authors' own conclusion: that stupor and excitement, though
"seemingly opposite states," frequently co-occur within the same
episode, which they interpret as supporting "the recent concept of
psychomotor 'alternation'" — that retarded and excited catatonia are
not cleanly separable subtypes but points or oscillations on a single
syndrome, consistent with Kahlbaum's original framing.

This does not license any claim about the time-course, direction,
frequency-per-hour or -day, or triggers of alternation between poles.
This is a cross-sectional EHR co-occurrence analysis — same-episode
flagging in the medical record — not a prospective, moment-to-moment
observational study of switching. It cannot establish how abrupt a
transition is, what precedes it, or whether an excited phase is a
direct causal continuation of a retarded phase versus an independent
fluctuation. No prospective cohort study directly quantifying switch
latency or triggers was located in this search.

The consequence for research and clinical design, stated once here: a
single-timepoint assessment protocol that treats retarded and excited
presentations as mutually exclusive intake categories will misclassify
a substantial share of catatonic presentations — roughly half of those
showing excited signs, and about one in nine of those showing stuporous
signs, in the cohort above.

## Bush-Francis Catatonia Rating Scale

The [Bush-Francis Catatonia Rating
Scale](../../../../../../glossary/terms/bush-francis-catatonia-rating-scale.md)
(BFCRS) and its embedded 14-item Catatonia Screening Instrument (BFCSI)
were developed for systematic, operationalized description of catatonic
signs. This is verified against Bush, Fink, Petrides, Dowling & Francis
(1996), "Catatonia. I. Rating scale and standardized examination," *Acta
Psychiatrica Scandinavica*, DOI 10.1111/j.1600-0447.1996.tb09814.x, PMID
8686483 — the opening methodology text, confirmed via a fetched Europe
PMC abstract, states: "we developed a catatonia rating examination,
rating scale and screening instrument... a 23-item rating scale and a
truncated 14-item screening instrument using operationalized
definitions of signs ascribed to catatonia in published sources."

The design is an instrument-development and reliability study:
interrater reliability was assessed via 44 simultaneous ratings of 28
cases, each defined by presence of two or more signs on the 14-item
screen — the screening cutoff used operationally in this validation.
The exact numbers, confirmed against the full abstract text fetched via
NCBI E-utils after pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov itself returned only a
cookie-consent page to this search's fetch tool: interrater reliability
for the 23-item rating scale total score was 0.93, with mean per-item
agreement 88.2% (SD 9.9); for the 14-item screening instrument, total
score reliability was 0.95, with mean per-item agreement 92.7% (SD
4.9).

The authors conclude that catatonic signs can be reliably rated by
independent examiners using operationalized definitions, supporting the
scale's use for both screening (the two-of-fourteen threshold) and
severity/treatment-response tracking (the 23-item total score) — the
second role exercised directly in the companion treatment paper below.
This does not license treating the BFCRS as validated against excited
versus retarded subtypes specifically: it rates presence and severity
of individual catatonic signs, which include both hypokinetic items
(stupor, waxy flexibility) and hyperkinetic items (agitation), but it
was not designed or validated as a discriminator between "retarded" and
"excited" catatonia as diagnostic categories. A study wanting to
characterize a patient's pole must derive that classification itself
from the scale's item-level scores; the instrument does not output a
pole label.

## Lorazepam challenge and ECT response

The [lorazepam challenge
test](../../../../../../glossary/terms/lorazepam-challenge-test.md)
and full lorazepam treatment trial resolve catatonic signs in a
majority of systematically treated patients, with ECT effective in most
who fail lorazepam. This is verified against the companion paper, Bush,
Fink, Petrides, Dowling & Francis (1996), "Catatonia. II. Treatment
with lorazepam and electroconvulsive therapy," *Acta Psychiatrica
Scandinavica*, DOI 10.1111/j.1600-0447.1996.tb09815.x, PMID 8686484,
verified against the abstract fetched via NCBI E-utils.

The design is an open, systematic treatment study: 28 catatonic
patients were treated with parenteral and/or oral lorazepam for up to
five days, 11 of them receiving an initial intravenous lorazepam
challenge, progressing to ECT for lorazepam failures, with outcome
tracked quantitatively using the BFCRS. The exact numbers: 16 of 21
patients (76%) who completed a full lorazepam trial had resolution of
catatonic signs. Four patients who failed lorazepam responded promptly
to ECT. A positive response to the initial parenteral lorazepam
challenge predicted eventual lorazepam response, as did shorter
duration of catatonic symptoms before treatment — meaning the
challenge's value is specifically prognostic, not merely confirmatory
of the diagnosis in isolation.

The authors conclude that lorazepam is an effective first-line
treatment for catatonia in most patients, with ECT an effective
second-line option for lorazepam nonresponders — both response and
challenge-test response graded and probabilistic, not universal. This
does not license generalizing the 76% figure as a population base rate
for "catatonia responds to lorazepam" outside systematically treated
inpatient samples of this size (n=21 completers, n=28 enrolled), nor as
evidence about which pole — excited or retarded — responds better; this
study does not stratify response by pole.

A second, larger body of evidence on ECT specifically is contested
rather than settled. Leroy, Naudet, Vaiva, Francis, Thomas & Amad
(2018), "Is electroconvulsive therapy an evidence-based treatment for
catatonia? A systematic review and meta-analysis," *European Archives
of Psychiatry and Clinical Neuroscience*, DOI
10.1007/s00406-017-0819-5, PMID 28639007, verified against the full
abstract fetched via Europe PMC, reviewed the literature through
October 2016 across PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Embase,
ClinicalTrials.gov, and Current Controlled Trials, pooling pre-post
catatonia/clinical-improvement rating-scale changes with a
random-effects model where feasible. The exact numbers: 564 patients
from 28 studies were included; ten studies were poolable for pre-post
improvement, giving a standardized mean difference of −3.14, 95% CI
[−3.95, −2.34] — a very large effect size on catatonia/clinical rating
scales. Adverse effects (mental confusion, memory loss, headache,
anesthesia-related effects) were reported in seven studies.

The authors' own conclusion, in substance: "the literature consistently
describes improvement in catatonic symptoms after ECT. However, the
published studies fail to demonstrate efficacy and effectiveness" by
rigorous trial standards — the identified RCTs were "of low quality and
were heterogeneous," precluding pooling of RCT-only results, and the
authors call for a properly designed RCT. This is reported here as
contested and unresolved rather than smoothed into a consensus: a large
observational effect size stands beside an explicit authorial caveat
that this does not equal RCT-grade efficacy proof. Citing an "80-100%
ECT response rate" — a range that circulates in narrative reviews but
was not verified here against one primary study reporting exactly that
range — as an RCT-established figure is not licensed by this evidence;
per Leroy et al., no such RCT-grade effectiveness demonstration
currently exists, only large observational pre-post effects.

## Malignant catatonia and the NMS differential

[Malignant
catatonia](../../../../../../glossary/terms/malignant-catatonia.md) is
a hyperthermic, autonomically unstable, severely rigid variant of
catatonia carrying substantial mortality risk if untreated, clinically
overlapping with but arguably distinct from neuroleptic malignant
syndrome (NMS). Mann, Caroff, Bleier, Welz, Kling & Hayashida (1986),
"Lethal catatonia," *American Journal of Psychiatry*, DOI
10.1176/ajp.143.11.1374, PMID 3777225, verified against the abstract
fetched via Europe PMC, is a world-literature review stating lethal
catatonia was widely reported before modern psychopharmacologic
treatment, that its prevalence "may have declined" but it continues to
occur, and that it is "a syndrome rather than a specific disease,"
arising from both functional and organic illness. **The abstract itself
gives no quantified mortality percentage.** Commonly repeated figures —
"up to 50-75% mortality historically," "~10-20% with modern
treatment" — could not be verified against this primary abstract and
are not asserted here as established numbers.

Philbrick & Rummans (1994), "Malignant catatonia," *Journal of
Neuropsychiatry and Clinical Neurosciences*, PMID 7908547, reached here
at abstract/description level via search synthesis rather than a
fetched full primary text, reviews 1986-1991 English-language reports
plus five institutional cases, proposes a revised nomenclature for
malignant/nonmalignant catatonia, and argues malignant catatonia has
causes beyond neuroleptic exposure — that neuroleptic-associated NMS is
one route into a broader malignant-catatonia picture, not a
categorically separate illness.

Differential features reported consistently across secondary clinical
reviews, though not independently verified against one primary
comparative study: malignant catatonia tends to show waxy flexibility
and catalepsy together with a prodrome of excitement or agitation
before rigidity sets in, whereas NMS classically begins with
"lead-pipe" rigidity without a preceding excited catatonic prodrome and
is tied temporally to antipsychotic exposure. No head-to-head primary
study establishing these differentiating features with reported
sensitivity or specificity was located; this differential is stated
consistently across reviews but is clinical teaching, not a validated
discriminating instrument. It does not establish that malignant
catatonia is reached only via a prior excited phase — the "prodrome of
excitement" claim is a commonly stated clinical pattern, not a
quantified finding from a primary cohort verified here.

## Catatonia's prevalence: more affective than schizophrenic

Contrary to the historical Kraepelinian association of catatonia with
schizophrenia, prospective clinical samples find catatonic signs occur
more often in patients with affective — particularly manic — episodes.
Abrams & Taylor (1976), "Catatonia: a prospective clinical study,"
*Archives of General Psychiatry*, PMID 1267574, was reached via search
synthesis of the abstract and summary; a persistent cookie-wall issue
on pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov prevented an independent re-fetch of the raw
abstract text, and the citation, PMID, and headline numbers were
cross-confirmed across two independent search-result renderings — not
the same confidence as reading the primary abstract text directly.

The design is a prospective clinical study of 55 patients admitted over
14 months to two inpatient psychiatric units of a municipal hospital,
enrolled for exhibiting one or more of eight catatonic signs (mutism,
stereotypy, posturing, catalepsy, automatic obedience, negativism,
echolalia/echopraxia, stupor). The exact numbers: only 4 of 55 patients
(about 7%) met research criteria for schizophrenia; over two-thirds had
diagnosable affective disorders, usually mania. The eight catatonic
motor signs were distributed similarly across diagnostic groups — sign
pattern did not discriminate diagnosis — and were unrelated to
short-term treatment outcome; about two-thirds of the whole catatonic
sample were markedly improved or in remission at discharge.

The authors conclude that catatonia is not specifically or
predominantly a schizophrenia phenomenon; in this sample it was
substantially more common in mania and affective illness, challenging
the Kraepelinian catatonia-schizophrenia linkage. This is the study
later writing by Taylor and Fink repeatedly cites as founding evidence
for the broader argument that catatonia belongs primarily to
mood-disorder nosology. A separate review on the same general topic,
Krüger & Bräunig, "Catatonia in Affective Disorder: New Findings and a
Review of the Literature," *CNS Spectrums* 2000;5(7):48-53, reports
data on catatonia frequency in mixed mania but sits behind a Cambridge
Core paywall past its abstract and was not read here.

A corroborating but less precisely sourced figure: Penland, Weder &
Tampi (2006), "The catatonic dilemma expanded," *Annals of General
Psychiatry*, PMID 16959040, full text verified, states catatonic
symptoms have been found "at rates ranging from 13% to 31%" in
affective-disorder inpatient groups (chiefly manic-depressive samples)
across multiple cited studies, and gives historical acute-psychotic-
admission catatonia prevalence of "6% to 38%," with more recent
estimates of "2% to 8%" of acute admissions — a wide, study-dependent
range this review does not itself resolve into one number and is
reported here as unresolved rather than smoothed into a single "true"
prevalence.

This does not license treating "catatonia is more affective than
schizophrenic" as settled epidemiology across all modern settings: the
Abrams & Taylor figure is one 1976 municipal-hospital sample (n=55);
the Penland et al. review shows the reported prevalence range is wide
and setting-dependent (2%-38%) across the literature it surveys, and
does not itself adjudicate a single correct rate. Contemporary
catatonia cohorts drawn from different populations — autism-spectrum,
delirium, general-medical, first-episode-psychosis samples — were not
systematically reviewed here for their diagnostic-category breakdown.

## GABA-A hypofunction: a hypothesis, not an established mechanism

A hypothesis associates catatonia with reduced GABA-A receptor
binding/activity in right-hemisphere orbitofrontal and posterior
parietal cortex, with benzodiazepine (lorazepam) treatment partially
normalizing abnormal orbitofrontal activation and orbitofrontal-medial-
prefrontal connectivity. A complementary hypothesis attributes akinetic
catatonia to glutamatergic/NMDA-receptor hyperactivity overriding
GABAergic tone, with some clinical response reported to NMDA antagonists
such as amantadine and memantine. Both are stated here as hypotheses,
not established mechanisms, because of how thinly the imaging evidence
behind them was reachable in this search.

The bibliographic anchor is Northoff, "Brain imaging in catatonia:
current findings and a pathophysiologic model," *CNS Spectrums*
2000;5(7):34-46 — its bibliographic details were verified via a
Cambridge Core listing and cited secondary reviews, but the full
primary text sits behind a Cambridge Core paywall and was not fetched;
this entire claim rests on secondary description of Northoff's model,
not on a direct reading of his primary imaging papers or their effect
sizes. Secondary reviews converge in describing: a right-hemisphere
network spanning medial/lateral orbitofrontal and posterior parietal
cortex as structurally and functionally implicated; reduced GABA-A
receptor density reported in some cortical regions (for example, left
sensorimotor cortex in one cited study); and a small functional-imaging
study — cited as involving six patients studied while asymptomatic
after a recent catatonic episode, using an emotional-processing task —
reporting that abnormal orbitofrontal activation during the task was
reduced or normalized following lorazepam.

Without the primary text, the design behind these claims can only be
reconstructed loosely: a set of small case-control functional
(fMRI, and separately MEG) neuroimaging studies, plus at least one
PET/SPECT-type receptor-binding study, synthesized by Northoff into a
"top-down/bottom-up" dysregulation model. Individual sample sizes,
exact effect sizes, and p-values for any single imaging finding beyond
the roughly six-patient figure reported secondarily could not be
verified, and every number in this section is flagged as unverified
against primary text. Northoff separately reported, per secondary
description, successful treatment of three acute akinetic catatonic
patients with the NMDA antagonist amantadine, offered as support for a
glutamatergic contribution to the akinetic/retarded pole specifically.

A complementary motor-network account — described in secondary sources
including a *Journal of Neuropsychiatry and Clinical Neurosciences*
review and more recent systematic imaging reviews (indexed at
PMC11006573 and via ScienceDirect, none fetched in full here) —
describes converging structural and functional imaging findings in
motor and premotor cortex, the supplementary motor area, and basal
ganglia circuits in catatonia, particularly catatonia within
schizophrenia, framed as complementary to, not a refutation of, the
orbitofrontal/GABA-A model.

None of this licenses treating the GABA-A hypofunction hypothesis, the
glutamatergic/NMDA account, or the motor-network account as an
established mechanism for catatonia in general, still less for
retarded-to-excited transition specifically. Every imaging claim here
traces to small, largely uncontrolled, non-replicated samples described
secondarily; sample sizes, statistical thresholds, and replication
status could not be verified against primary imaging papers, and no
imaging study located addresses the transition between poles directly.

## What the mechanism literature does not establish about transitions between poles

Gathering the negative findings scattered through the sections above,
stated plainly:

- No imaging, receptor-binding, or biomarker study located in this
  search scans a patient *during* a retarded-to-excited or
  excited-to-retarded switch. All imaging evidence describes patients
  in one state — often post-episode/asymptomatic, or in an unspecified
  mixed sample — not a within-subject before/during/after comparison
  across a switch.
- The GABA-A hypofunction and glutamatergic/NMDA hyperactivity
  hypotheses are each offered, in the secondary literature reached
  here, as accounts of catatonia broadly or of the akinetic/retarded
  pole specifically (per Northoff's amantadine cases). No primary
  mechanistic hypothesis located in this search specifically predicts
  or explains why or when a given patient would flip from retarded to
  excited catatonia, as opposed to simply having catatonia with mixed
  or fluctuating signs. "Top-down/bottom-up dysregulation" is described
  secondarily as Northoff's frame for different clinical presentations,
  not as a specified switching mechanism with directional predictions
  that could be verified here.
- The best transition-relevant data found — the Dawkins et al. 2022
  cohort discussed above — is cross-sectional co-occurrence within
  EHR-coded episodes, not a time-resolved or prospectively observed
  switch; it establishes that stupor and excitement often coexist or
  both appear somewhere in the same episode, not the direction, speed,
  or trigger of a change from one to the other.
- No study located here reports a validated predictor — clinical,
  biochemical, or imaging — of which catatonic patients will show
  excited features, retarded features, both, or an alternation over an
  episode.
- The malignant-catatonia literature's commonly cited "prodrome of
  excitement before rigidity/stupor" pattern is descriptive clinical
  teaching, not a quantified, prospectively validated transition
  finding.
- Nothing found here licenses extending this catatonia literature to
  non-catatonic switch-like affective/arousal phenomena — mixed
  affective states in mood disorder without psychomotor catatonic
  signs, or rapid cycling. The object of this literature is
  specifically the DSM/ICD-defined catatonic sign complex, not arousal
  or emotional-valence switching in general.

**A targeted search for a direct empirical study of the felt or
observed transition moment itself — its speed, its triggers, or any
biomarker of switching between retarded and excited catatonia — found
none.** This is reported as a null result, not an oversight: the search
covered the imaging/mechanism literature (section above), the
malignant-catatonia prodrome literature, and the cohort/EHR literature,
and none of them contains a study designed to catch a patient in the
act of switching poles. Catatonia gives the affective-switch research
program its clearest natural case — two poles under one diagnostic
threshold, a fast-acting challenge test, an episode short enough to
observe — and the literature still has not been asked, in a
prospective, time-resolved way, what happens in between. That is the
open question this section exists to hold.
