## What this argues

A person moving abruptly from a low-arousal, immobile, anhedonic state
into a high-arousal, irritable, mobilized one is describing a switch,
not a drift — and the medical literature has looked for organic and
pharmacological precipitants of exactly this kind of shift from several
independent directions: epilepsy research asking whether seizures
themselves produce aggression; psychopharmacology asking whether
antipsychotics and antidepressants produce agitation as a drug effect
rather than an illness effect; reproductive endocrinology asking why
premenstrual symptoms turn on and off abruptly rather than tracking
absolute hormone levels; general endocrinology asking whether thyroid
and glucose disturbance can mimic a psychiatric switch; and, more
recently and much more thinly, autism and ADHD research asking whether
their own characteristic dysregulated states meet the same evidentiary
bar. This note surveys all eight bodies of evidence as they actually
stand, not as they are usually summarized. Several of them are far
better established than they are usually credited with being — the
epilepsy-aggression link is the opposite of what "temporal lobe rage"
folk psychiatry assumes; the premenstrual-dysphoric-disorder mechanism
is a genuinely elegant piece of experimental endocrinology. Others are
far thinner than their clinical circulation implies — interictal
dysphoric disorder, rejection-sensitive dysphoria, and autistic
meltdown/shutdown all carry active researcher disagreement or a
peer-reviewed literature far smaller than the volume of clinical and
lay discussion built on top of them. Every claim below states the
population it was measured in, the exact numbers the source paper
reports, and whether the claim was independently verified against a
primary abstract or full text, verified only via a secondary
characterization, or not locatable at all. Nothing here diagnoses any
individual or any presentation; it maps what the organic-medicine and
pharmacology literatures have and have not established as causes of an
abrupt shift into irritability or agitation.

## Temporal lobe epilepsy: ictal, postictal, and peri-ictal states

Epilepsy is the oldest candidate explanation for episodic rage in
clinical folklore, and it is also the precipitant whose evidence most
directly contradicts the folklore. The 1981 review that settled the
question of ictal (during-seizure) aggression was itself prompted by
epilepsy having been invoked in at least 12 violent-crime
"diminished responsibility" defenses since 1977: Delgado-Escueta,
Mattson, King and colleagues, "The Nature of Aggression During
Epileptic Seizures" (*New England Journal of Medicine*, 1981). The
NEJM abstract itself could not be loaded in the course of this
research — the paper is paywalled, and PubMed and Europe PMC returned
only cookie-consent shells rather than content. The commonly cited
figures describing it — a video-EEG survey of "several thousand
seizures" with an aggression incidence of roughly 1 in 1000 — are
carried forward from a 2016 secondary review's characterization of the
1981 paper (below), not read directly from the original text. That
qualification stands on its own: this specific number is second-hand.
What the secondary source reports as the 1981 paper's conclusion is
that ictal violence is rare, poorly directed and disorganized, and
very unlikely to produce serious injury — apparent "aggression" during
a seizure is usually resistive behavior against restraint or an
intervening bystander, not a goal-directed attack. This finding is
specifically about aggression *during* the electrographic seizure. It
says nothing about the postictal or interictal periods, which the
literature treats as distinct phenomena with different rates.

Postictal aggression — occurring after the seizure discharge ends,
during the confusional recovery period — is different, and the direct
evidence for it is thinner still: a single-patient case study.
Yankovsky, Veilleux, Dubeau and Andermann, "Post-ictal rage and
aggression: a video-EEG study" (*Epileptic Disorders*, 2005), verified
directly against the Europe PMC abstract record (a channel distinct
from the Wiley/JLE publisher pages, which returned 403 errors to
automated fetching). The patient was a man with a lifelong seizure
history and intellectual disability, focal seizures secondarily
generalizing, and bilateral periventricular nodular heterotopia — a
developmental brain malformation the paper's own authors treat as
causally related to both his intellectual disability and his epilepsy.
This single case therefore does not establish postictal rage in
epilepsy patients generally; it establishes it in one patient with a
specific comorbid developmental brain abnormality. Immediately after
the ictal EEG discharge ended, the patient became, in the paper's own
words, "greatly agitated, with undirected aggression, loud screaming,
kicking and fighting against restraints." A separately titled paper,
"Subacute postictal aggression in patients with epilepsy" (*Epilepsy &
Behavior*, cited inside the 2016 review below), is characterized by
that review as finding this to be the *rarest* postictal category, at
roughly 0.5% — a figure reported here exactly as it appears inside the
2016 review, not independently verified against the original paper.

The best-verified synthesis of this whole area is Brodie, Besag,
Ettinger and colleagues, "Epilepsy, Antiepileptic Drugs, and
Aggression: An Evidence-Based Review" (*Pharmacological Reviews*,
2016), verified directly against the PMC full text (PMC4931873). Its
own bottom-line conclusion, quoted directly: "The available data
clearly indicate that aggressive behavior in epilepsy is most commonly
unrelated to seizures themselves" — occurring mainly interictally and
tied to psychiatric comorbidity rather than to seizure activity. The
review reports [reactive aggression](../../../../../../../../../psychology-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/reactive-aggression.md)
during postictal psychosis at 22.8% of cases (their characterization of
Kanemoto et al., 2010, not independently checked against Kanemoto's own
paper), and interictal base rates of aggressive behavior in unselected
epilepsy samples of 4.3% (Rodin, 1973) and up to 7% (Currie et al.,
1971) — both figures as cited inside Brodie et al., not independently
verified against the 1973 and 1971 originals.

A third claim — that peri-ictal (pre-ictal, or "prodromal") dysphoric
symptoms build over hours to days before a seizure and remit within a
few days after it — appears only as a search-engine synthesis
reporting a mean prodrome frequency of 21.9% across eight studies. No
primary study behind this figure could be identified or fetched in the
course of this research; it is reported here as an unverified claim
requiring further primary-source confirmation, not as an established
finding.

None of this licenses treating any single episode of anger or
irritability as probably epileptic, or treating temporal lobe epilepsy
as a common explanation for directed violence. The weight of the
best-verified synthesis (Brodie et al., 2016) runs the other way:
aggression in epilepsy is mostly not seizure-related, and where it is
tied to seizures, it is postictal — occurring during confusional
recovery, not during full alertness — rather than a "switch" happening
in a clear sensorium.

## Interictal dysphoric disorder: a proposed, contested entity

Interictal dysphoric disorder (IDD) was proposed by Blumer and
colleagues (cited across secondary reviews as 1995 and again 2004 for
an eight-symptom formulation; Blumer's own primary papers could not be
independently fetched in this research, so this description is a
multiply-cited secondary characterization) as a distinct
epilepsy-related affective syndrome, harking back to Kraepelin's
"Verstimmungszustand." The eight proposed affective-somatoform symptoms
are depressive mood, anergia, pain, insomnia, paroxysmal
[irritability](../../../../../../../../services/irritability-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/irritability.md)
with explosive affect, euphoric moods, fear, and anxiety — the
paroxysmal-irritability-with-explosive-affect component being the one
most relevant to abrupt switching, but it is one symptom inside a still
contested eight-symptom construct, not itself independently validated
as a switch-like phenomenon on its own.

The construct's current standing is, on the most directly verified
recent evidence, negative. Grzegorzewska, Wiglusz, Jakuszkowiak-Wojten,
Cubała, Włodarczyk and Szarmach, "Interictal dysphoric disorder in
epilepsy and its relationship with specific clinical and demographic
variables" (*Psychiatry and Clinical Psychopharmacology*, 2024),
verified against the full text: a post-hoc analysis of registry data
from a tertiary epilepsy center, 96 people with epilepsy assessed from
118 consecutive patients screened. 48.5% (n=47) met IDD criteria.
IDD-positive and IDD-negative groups did not differ on drug-resistant
epilepsy status (72.34% vs. 73.47%, p=.901) or sex (36.17% vs. 28.57%
male, p=.426); IDD-positive patients had later epilepsy onset (median
age 20 vs. 16, p=.033) and much higher rates of prior psychiatric
treatment (25.53% vs. 8.16%, p=.022). The authors' own conclusion,
quoted: their "data do not contribute to support evidence of IDD as
[a] stand-alone [phenomenon]" — that is, the 2024 study argues against
IDD's independence from ordinary psychiatric comorbidity, given the
large overlap with prior psychiatric diagnosis.

An earlier study reaches a more sympathetic but still divided
conclusion. Mula, Jauch, Cavanna, Gaus, Kretz, Collimedaglia, Barbagli,
Cantello, Monaco and Schmitz, "Interictal dysphoric disorder and
periictal dysphoric symptoms in patients with epilepsy" (*Epilepsia*,
2010), verified against the Europe PMC abstract record. 142 consecutive
adult epilepsy outpatients were assessed with the 38-item self-report
Interictal Dysphoric Disorder Inventory. IDD was diagnosed in 31 of the
142 subjects — 21.8% — but symptoms showed a clear-cut relationship
with seizures (that is, were periictal rather than "true" interictal)
in 54.8% of those 31 cases, which the authors use to argue for
splitting IDD from periictal dysphoric symptoms as two distinct
clinical endophenotypes rather than one. 21.8% is a markedly different
rate than the 48.5% reported above, and than the 34%–49% figures other
reviews attribute to Blumer's own original samples; a pattern across
multiple systematic reviews — titled, characteristically, "The
interictal dysphoric disorder of epilepsy: Legend or reality?" and
similar (titles verified by search only; full texts not fetched in this
research) — frames IDD's nosological status as an open, unresolved
debate, citing prevalence estimates spanning roughly 17%–49% across
studies and substantial symptom overlap with standard DSM mood and
anxiety diagnoses.

None of this licenses treating IDD as an accepted diagnostic category.
It is a proposed entity whose own most recent, most directly verified
literature argues it may not be separable from ordinary
depression/anxiety comorbid with epilepsy.

## Akathisia and antidepressant/antipsychotic activation syndromes

[Akathisia](../../../../../../glossary/terms/akathisia.md) — the
drug-induced movement disorder of inner restlessness and compulsion to
move — has a documented association with agitation, irritability, and,
in some case series, aggression, but the causal evidence for that
specific link is thin: case reports and one small observational study,
not controlled trials. Crowner, Douyon, Convit, Gaztanaga, Volavka and
Bakall, "Akathisia and violence" (*Psychopharmacology Bulletin*, 1990),
verified against the PubMed abstract record, installed a closed-circuit
television camera in each corner of the dayroom of a locked unit for
violent psychiatric patients; incidents of assault, plus the five
minutes preceding each assault, were recorded on videotape, and all
parties present were rated for akathisia's motor component. The
quoted finding: "Both victims and assailants were akathisic before
about half of all incidents; bystanders rarely were." This is
described as the first observational study of the
akathisia-violence relationship; it is a single small-unit
observational study, and its exact sample size and statistical
treatment were not independently confirmed in this research. An early
description (attributed to Van Putten, 1975, not independently
fetched) had proposed a link between akathisia and suicidality
specifically; a 2021 systematic review is characterized, via
search result only, as concluding that "akathisia cannot be reliably
linked to the presence of suicidal behavior in patients treated with
antipsychotic medication" — a more recent, systematic look at an
adjacent question (suicidality, not violence) that pushed back rather
than confirmed.

A separate, better-populated literature concerns SSRI/antidepressant
"activation syndrome" in children and adolescents — irritability,
[psychomotor agitation](../../../../../../glossary/terms/psychomotor-agitation.md),
aggression, and hostility — named directly in FDA black-box warning
language issued in 2004. A retrospective review of 82 children with
depression or OCD treated with SSRIs (Reinblatt et al., described
inside a 2015 review; neither independently fetched in this research)
found 22% experienced activation-related adverse events, broken down
as irritability 15%, anxiety 10%, mania 6%, aggression 1%, and insomnia
17% — percentages as reported inside the review's characterization,
not confirmed against the original data table. The review itself is
Amitai, Chen, Weizman and Apter, "SSRI-Induced Activation Syndrome in
Children and Adolescents — What Is Next?" (*Current Treatment Options
in Psychiatry*, 2015; title, authors, year and journal verified via
search, full text not independently fetched). The FDA's black-box
language (standard regulatory text, not re-fetched from FDA.gov in this
research) lists anxiety, agitation, panic attacks, insomnia,
irritability, hostility, aggressiveness, impulsivity, akathisia,
hypomania, and mania as reported symptoms following antidepressant
initiation. A wide prevalence range for activation syndrome in children
on SSRIs — 4%–65% — appears across search results; the range is wide
enough, and the underlying study heterogeneity unclear enough, that it
should be read as a rough summary of highly variable study designs and
definitions rather than a single well-defined rate.

None of this licenses treating antidepressant activation syndrome as
identical to, or evidence for, a broader depression-to-rage switch
phenomenon outside drug exposure. It is a medication-adverse-event
literature specifically about treatment-emergent activation, most
described in children and adolescents and most concentrated in the
early weeks of treatment or after a dose change. It is also a distinct
phenomenon from a genuine [treatment-emergent affective switch](../../../../../../../../services/mood-disorder-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/treatment-emergent-affective-switch.md)
into a full manic or hypomanic episode — the two get conflated
clinically, but activation syndrome and treatment-emergent affective
switch are separately named and separately evidenced constructs in this
literature, not one phenomenon under two labels.

## Premenstrual dysphoric disorder and ovarian-steroid withdrawal

[Premenstrual dysphoric disorder](../../../../../../glossary/terms/premenstrual-dysphoric-disorder.md)
(PMDD) has the most experimentally direct mechanism of any precipitant
in this note for *why* an affective shift would be abrupt rather than
gradual. DSM-5 criteria (standard nosology, cross-checked against
multiple independent descriptions, all concordant) require at least 5
of 11 symptoms present in most cycles over the past year, at least one
of which must be a core affective symptom — marked affective lability,
marked irritability/anger/increased interpersonal conflict, markedly
depressed mood, or marked anxiety/tension — with onset in the week
before menses, improvement within a few days after menses onset, and
absence or minimal presence in the week post-menses.
[Irritability](../../../../../../../../services/irritability-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/irritability.md)
is repeatedly described across secondary sources as the single most
commonly reported primary symptom.

The mechanistic claim — that onset is tied causally to *withdrawal
from*, not the absolute level of, ovarian steroids, specifically in
women with a prior history of the syndrome — comes from Schmidt,
Nieman, Danaceau, Adams and Rubinow, "Differential Behavioral Effects
of Gonadal Steroids in Women with and in Those without Premenstrual
Syndrome" (*New England Journal of Medicine*, 1998), verified against
the Europe PMC abstract record (NEJM.org itself returned a 403 to
automated fetching). The paper's own diagnostic label is premenstrual
syndrome (PMS) — DSM PMDD as a named category postdates this study, so
this is PMS-research-program evidence that the later PMDD literature
descends from, not a study that itself diagnosed PMDD. Twenty women
with PMS and comparison women without PMS were given the GnRH agonist
leuprolide or placebo. The 10 PMS women whose symptoms improved on
leuprolide, plus 5 further PMS women and 15 women without PMS
("normal" controls), then went through a double-blind, crossover
add-back phase in which estradiol and progesterone were each given for
four weeks during continued leuprolide administration. The 10
PMS-leuprolide women had a significant symptom decrease versus baseline
and versus the 10 PMS women given placebo; during add-back, this same
group had a significant recurrence of symptoms, while no mood changes
occurred in either the 15 normal women or the 5 PMS women given placebo
hormone during continued leuprolide. Recurrence tracked the *change* in
steroid level and required a prior history of PMS, not a specific
hormone or an abnormal absolute level. The authors' conclusion, quoted
directly from the verified abstract: "In women with premenstrual
syndrome, the occurrence of symptoms represents an abnormal response to
normal hormonal changes." The abstract does not give symptom-scale
scores or exact p-values for these comparisons beyond the word
"significant"; those specific statistics remain unverified in this
research. A 2017 replication ("Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder Symptoms
Following Ovarian Suppression: Triggered by Change in Ovarian Steroid
Levels But Not Continuous Stable Levels," *American Journal of
Psychiatry*) and a further 2025/2026 replication are referenced in
search results as extending this finding, suggesting it has been
revisited by the same or related research groups, but neither was
independently fetched in this research.

None of this licenses treating PMDD as evidence for switches outside
the menstrual cycle, or treating hormonal change generally as a model
for non-cyclical affective switching. This is a study of a specific,
cyclical, female-reproductive-axis phenomenon; it does not generalize
to non-cyclical or male populations.

## Thyroid dysfunction and hypoglycemia as reversible causes

Hyperthyroidism, especially Graves' disease, is commonly associated
with irritability and agitation that is reported as reversible with
treatment of the underlying thyroid disease. Stern, Robinson, Thorner,
Arruda, Prohaska and Prange, "A survey study of neuropsychiatric
complaints in patients with Graves' disease" (*Journal of
Neuropsychiatry and Clinical Neurosciences*, 1996), verified against
the Europe PMC abstract record: 137 patients with treated Graves'
disease completed a questionnaire on neuropsychiatric complaints.
Anxiety and irritability were common prior to treatment of the
hyperthyroidism, and these complaints were associated with delays in
seeking treatment and in receiving a correct diagnosis; patients also
reported significantly worse memory, attention, planning, and
productivity while hyperthyroid than beforehand, with some residual
cognitive deficits persisting even after becoming euthyroid. The
abstract does not contain a specific percentage figure for irritability
prevalence, and no such number could be confirmed against it — a
78.1%-type figure sometimes circulated for this study should not be
treated as established. A separately cited 2021 study (title and
authors not fully resolved in this research) is described as comparing
75 hyperthyroid patients to 75 euthyroid controls, finding 60% versus
34.7% prevalence of a psychiatric condition; this figure is reported
here as search-derived and not independently verified. The reported
clinical pattern across multiple concordant secondary sources —
standard endocrinology teaching, not individually fetched in this
research — is that psychiatric symptoms in hyperthyroidism typically
improve in parallel with treatment of the thyroid disease itself,
without needing a separate psychotropic medication in most cases. None
of this licenses treating irritability alone as sufficient evidence of
thyroid disease, or treating thyroid dysfunction as a common
explanation for episodic affective switching in the absence of other
signs of thyrotoxicosis — tachycardia, weight loss, heat intolerance,
tremor, goiter, or an abnormal TSH/free T4.

Hypoglycemia (specifically neuroglycopenia — the direct consequence of
brain glucose deprivation, as opposed to hypoglycemia's separate
autonomic symptom cluster) is standard, well-established endocrinology,
cross-checked across multiple concordant clinical descriptions
(Medscape's hypoglycemia clinical-presentation material and
neuroglycopenia topic overview, and the classic symptom-discrimination
literature — Towler and colleagues, "Mechanism of awareness of
hypoglycemia. Perception of neurogenic (predominantly cholinergic)
rather than neuroglycopenic symptoms," PMID 8243825, title and finding
direction confirmed via search but the full citation's exact journal,
year, and volume were not independently fetched in this research).
Neuroglycopenic symptoms include confusion, difficulty concentrating,
irritability, inappropriate behavior sometimes mistaken for
intoxication, and a combative or agitated presentation, escalating to
coma if severe; the separate neurogenic/autonomic cluster (sweating,
tremor, palpitations, hunger) is an earlier-warning set mediated by
catecholamine and cholinergic release. No primary study reporting a
specific numeric prevalence of irritability among hypoglycemic episodes
was located in this research — this is reported as an established
clinical pattern from textbook- and review-level sources, explicitly
not backed by a primary prevalence figure that could be verified.

## Autistic shutdown and meltdown

The peer-reviewed literature on meltdown and shutdown as distinct
constructs is small, recent, and almost entirely qualitative
(interview-based) rather than physiological or measurement-based. The
most directly relevant source, fetched in full: Phung, Penner, Pirlot
and Welch, "What I Wish You Knew: Insights on Burnout, Inertia,
Meltdown, and Shutdown From Autistic Youth" (*Frontiers in Psychology*,
2021). Eight autistic children and youth (ages 8–18, median 14)
completed two semi-structured interviews each via videoconference,
supported by visual aids, analyzed with reflexive thematic analysis.
Participants characterized "meltdown" as "feeling out of control," one
describing it as "You're a passenger on a ride of destruction… and
it's like hitting a bunch of stuff." "Shutdown" was characterized as
"feeling frozen" — physical exhaustion and heaviness, cognitive slowing
(difficulty deciding, focusing, communicating), and emotional
frustration at being unable to complete tasks; one participant: "I
don't have the energy to get up... I feel weak, not… like my body is
heavy." Meltdown was the more common presentation among these
specific youth; the authors suggest shutdown may be a response some
individuals develop later, such as in adulthood, as an alternative to
meltdown — but this is offered as interpretation from an eight-person
qualitative sample, not a developmental-trajectory finding from
longitudinal data. A measurement instrument exists for the adjacent but
distinct construct of autistic burnout: Mantzalas and colleagues,
"Measuring and validating autistic burnout" (*Autism Research*, 2024;
title and journal confirmed via search, full text not independently
fetched), whose Autistic Burnout Measure assesses decreased cognitive
ability, decreased emotion regulation, increased sensitivity, decreased
everyday functioning, increased avoidance/withdrawal, and increased
exhaustion — but this instrument targets burnout as a cumulative state,
not meltdown or shutdown as discrete, abrupt episodes.

None of this licenses a claim that meltdown and shutdown are validated,
measurable, distinct clinical constructs with established prevalence,
physiological correlates, or diagnostic criteria. What exists in the
peer-reviewed literature at this point is small-sample qualitative
description — this Phung et al. paper is n=8, and other cited work in
the same space is similarly small, including a related paper on
autistic burnout with only 8 participants — plus one emerging
self-report burnout measure. The much larger volume of detailed
phenomenological description of meltdown and shutdown found in autistic
community writing and advocacy material is not, on this search, matched
by an equivalent volume of peer-reviewed measurement; this asymmetry —
rich community description against a thin formal literature — is
reported here as the accurate state of the evidence, not a gap to
paper over. It should also be stated plainly that this is a separate
body of evidence from the house's own catatonia and mood-dynamical-
systems literature (linked below): shutdown's self-reported "frozen,"
"heavy" phenomenology and meltdown's "out of control" phenomenology
have not been cross-studied against retarded and excited catatonia or
against any dynamical-systems mood model in the peer-reviewed
literature located here, and no claim of shared mechanism between them
should be read into this note.

## ADHD emotional dysregulation and rejection-sensitive dysphoria

Emotional dysregulation is a well-documented, common feature of ADHD
across age groups, with meta-analytic evidence behind the general
claim. Beheshti, Chavanon and Christiansen, "Emotion dysregulation in
adults with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: a meta-analysis"
(*BMC Psychiatry*, 2020, volume 20, page 120; title, journal, and venue
confirmed via search, full methods and results tables not independently
fetched in this research), is reported as a systematic search yielding
13 qualifying studies, 10 of which were used for a between-group
meta-analytic comparison of ADHD versus healthy controls. A wider
prevalence range synthesized across the literature (not one single
study) puts significant emotional dysregulation at roughly 30%–70% of
adults and 25%–45% of children with ADHD; these ranges reflect
variation across studies using different definitions and measures and
could not be resolved to one number in this research, and are reported
honestly as a range rather than a single verified statistic.

"Rejection-sensitive dysphoria" (RSD) is a distinct and much thinner
case: a clinically popularized term with almost no dedicated primary
research literature behind it as a distinct construct. The term
"rejection sensitivity dysphoria"/"RSD" traces to 1960s depression
literature describing heightened rejection sensitivity contributing to
depressed mood, and was adopted and popularized specifically for ADHD
by William Dodson, MD, in clinical and popular writing (2016, 2023),
based on Dodson's own clinical observations rather than a primary
research program. Dodson's argument, as characterized in the writing
located by search, rests on five claims — extreme intensity of the
response relative to other populations, a genetic or neurological
basis, exclusive triggering by perceived rejection or criticism,
resistance to psychosocial treatment, and marked improvement with
guanfacine or clonidine — and none of these five claims was found, in
this research, to have been tested in a controlled primary study
specifically under the RSD label. The most directly relevant primary
literature located is a January 2026 qualitative study, "The lived
experience of rejection sensitivity in ADHD – a qualitative
exploration," posted on medRxiv and also appearing in PLOS ONE (title
and venue confirmed via search, authors not resolved and full text not
independently fetched in this research): a focus-group study of five
undergraduate students, using thematic analysis, finding themes of
withdrawal, masking, and bodily sensations in response to perceived
rejection. A synthesis point attributed to a Psychology Today piece
reviewing "the actual research" states that only five research studies
to date sufficiently address RSD as a construct, "all with small
samples (ranging from 4 to 43 participants) and all qualitative in
nature," and that no validated, reliable quantitative measure of RSD
yet exists; this "five studies total" claim could not be independently
verified against a systematic review in this research and is reported
here as a secondary characterization, not an independently confirmed
exhaustive count.

None of this licenses treating RSD as an established diagnostic or
research construct. The underlying phenomenon it points at — emotional
dysregulation in ADHD, and specifically heightened reactivity to
perceived rejection or criticism — has some qualitative support; the
specific named construct "rejection-sensitive dysphoria," as a
measurable, validated clinical entity with an evidence base independent
of one clinician's writing, does not appear to exist in the
peer-reviewed literature at any scale, per this research. This is a
case where a term is culturally very prominent while its formal primary
literature is genuinely thin, reported here plainly rather than
smoothed over — a different situation from
[disruptive mood dysregulation disorder](../../../../../../../../services/irritability-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/disruptive-mood-dysregulation-disorder.md),
which at least carries a DSM-5 diagnostic definition and field-trial
reliability data, however contested, or from
[intermittent explosive disorder](../../../../../../../../services/irritability-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/intermittent-explosive-disorder.md),
which has a national-survey prevalence estimate behind it. RSD, as
named, has neither.

## Traumatic brain injury and irritability

Irritability, anger, and aggression are common and often persistent
after traumatic brain injury (TBI), particularly with more severe
injury and comorbid PTSD symptoms. The best-verified source here, fetched
in full: Miles, Hammond, Neumann and colleagues, "Evolution of
Irritability, Anger, and Aggression after Traumatic Brain Injury:
Identifying and Predicting Subgroups" (*Journal of Neurotrauma*, 2021).
This was a prospective, multi-center longitudinal cohort of 346
veterans and service members (92% male, median age 28, 78% with severe
TBI) across five VA Polytrauma Rehabilitation Centers' post-acute
programs, 2010–2020, comparing irritability/anger/aggression (IAA)
status at admission versus discharge. Four trajectory subgroups
emerged: no IAA at either time point, 25.72% (n=89); resolved IAA
(present at admission, gone by discharge), 17.63% (n=61); delayed-onset
IAA (absent at admission, new at discharge), 8.96% (n=31); and
persistent IAA (present at both time points), 47.69% (n=165). Just
under half the sample — 47.69% — had persistent IAA, and over half —
56.65%, combining persistent and delayed-onset — had IAA present at
discharge. The key predictor, quoted directly: "Greater PTSD symptoms
were the only consistent predictor of belonging to all the subgroups
who had IAA compared with the no IAA subgroup"; each one-point increase
on the PTSD Checklist was associated with a 17%–19% increased risk of
IAA-subgroup membership. The authors' own stated limitations: the IAA
measure was a single item and "lacks reliability"; regression analyses
used a reduced sample (n=172) due to missing data; medication effects
were not accounted for; and the sample is limited to a VA
population — primarily young men with severe injuries — limiting
generalizability to civilian, older, or milder-TBI populations.

A second, older, frequently-cited longitudinal study is referenced in
search results but was not independently fetched in this research:
Brooks et al., described as following 42 TBI patients over five years,
finding persistent irritability in 67% at one year and 64% at five
years. A separate report, Baguley et al., is described as finding 25%
of patients from an Australian inpatient rehabilitation unit displaying
aggressive behavior, stable across 6, 24, and 60 months. Both figures
are reported here exactly as characterized in search results, flagged
as not verified against their primary abstracts.

None of this licenses treating all post-TBI irritability as a single
homogeneous phenomenon, or generalizing the veteran/severe-TBI cohort's
47.69%–56.65% figures to civilian or mild-TBI populations. The
single-item IAA measure and the authors' own reliability caveat mean
these are best read as a directional finding — irritability, anger, and
aggression are common and often persistent after TBI, and closely tied
to PTSD symptom burden — rather than a precise, generalizable
prevalence rate.

## What remains unverified in this literature

Stated plainly, as findings in their own right rather than gaps to
smooth over:

- The exact primary-text wording, sample size, and statistics of
  Delgado-Escueta et al. (1981, NEJM) could not be obtained; it is
  paywalled, and PubMed/Europe PMC returned only cookie-consent shells.
  The commonly cited "1 in 1000"/"several thousand seizures" figures
  come from a 2016 secondary review's characterization, not from the
  primary text.
- The within-paper symptom-scale scores and exact p-values inside
  Schmidt et al. (1998, NEJM) remain unconfirmed beyond the word
  "significant" — NEJM.org's full text returned a 403 error, and the
  Europe PMC abstract confirms sample sizes, group structure, and
  direction of findings but not exact statistics.
- The specific numeric figure sometimes circulated for irritability
  prevalence in Stern et al. (1996, Graves' disease) — a 78.1%-type
  figure — is not contained in the Europe PMC abstract record and
  should not be treated as established.
- No primary study establishing a specific numeric prevalence of
  irritability as a hypoglycemia symptom specifically (as opposed to
  the general clinical-teaching statement that it occurs) was located.
- Whether "five studies total, n=4–43" accurately counts the
  rejection-sensitive-dysphoria literature is unconfirmed; this claim
  traces to a single search-result characterization of a Psychology
  Today article, not an independently confirmed systematic review.
- The Brooks et al. and Baguley et al. TBI longitudinal figures (67%/64%
  irritability at one/five years; 25% aggression stable across
  6/24/60 months) are reported only via search-result characterization,
  not independently fetched from their primary abstracts.
- The Blumer 1995/2004 papers proposing IDD's eight-symptom
  formulation were not independently located; only secondary citations
  of them were available.
- No pediatric-EEG or physiological/objective measurement study of
  autistic meltdown or shutdown — as opposed to self-report or
  interview-based description — was located in this research. The
  absence itself is reported as the finding, not treated as an
  incidental gap.
