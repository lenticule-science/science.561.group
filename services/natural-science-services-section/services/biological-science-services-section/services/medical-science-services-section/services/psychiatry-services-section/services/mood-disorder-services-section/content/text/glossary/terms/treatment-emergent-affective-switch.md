Treatment-emergent affective switch names a manic or hypomanic episode that begins during or shortly after antidepressant treatment of a depressive episode. It is defined by its temporal relationship to antidepressant exposure rather than by a fixed rate or a settled mechanism — as the evidence below shows, its measured frequency depends heavily on study design. This distinguishes treatment-emergent affective switch from a baseline mixed depressive episode: the switch is defined by its temporal relationship to antidepressant exposure, not by excitatory symptoms already present before treatment began, even though the two are empirically associated, as below.

The estimated risk genuinely disagrees across designs, and neither side has displaced the other. Short randomized controlled trials of antidepressants in bipolar depression, pooled in meta-analysis, find switch rates that are low and not significantly different from placebo: a 2004 systematic review of 12 short-term RCTs (1,088 patients) found 3.8% switch on antidepressant versus 4.7% on placebo (risk ratio 1.00, 95% CI 0.47-2.13), and the authors explicitly note too few manic events occurred to have adequate power to detect a real difference — a null result under low power, not demonstrated safety. A 2025 network meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (1,362 patients) likewise found no individual antidepressant with a significantly higher switch risk than placebo, rating overall confidence in the evidence as low. Large naturalistic cohort and registry studies, by contrast, report meaningfully elevated risk: a comprehensive meta-analytic review pooling 109 studies (114,521 patients total, 35 in the pooled random-effects analysis) found 12.5% mania/hypomania risk with antidepressant exposure versus 7.5% without; a Swedish national-registry cohort of 3,240 bipolar patients found antidepressant monotherapy carried a hazard ratio of 2.83 for mania, while an antidepressant added to an existing mood stabilizer carried no increased — and, over the longer period studied, apparently protective — risk.

The two literatures are not simply contradictory; they answer different practical questions. The RCT literature is short in duration, frequently requires or permits mood-stabilizer co-treatment, often excludes patients judged at high switch risk, and has too few manic events for adequate statistical power. The registry/cohort literature captures real-world monotherapy use — which most current guidelines already discourage — over longer follow-up with larger event counts, but is subject to confounding by indication: patients placed on antidepressant monotherapy without a mood stabilizer may differ systematically (for example, in diagnostic certainty about bipolarity or in clinical monitoring) from those on combination treatment, a possibility none of the cohort studies can fully rule out. Neither literature shows that antidepressants cause switch in a given individual case.

A related complication concerns baseline mixed depression: a post-hoc analysis of the CO-MED trial (425 outpatients) found mixed depressive episodes more common among patients who later switched than among those who did not (11.7% vs. 5.9%, p=0.032), while self-reported irritability and psychomotor agitation showed no significant difference between switchers and non-switchers in that same dataset — a finding that complicates any simple claim that irritability itself predicts switch, even though irritability is repeatedly named a hallmark of mixed states in the conceptual literature (see mixed-depression). For clinical observation and research design, the consequence is that a reported switch rate is only interpretable alongside its study design and treatment condition: RCT-derived rates under mood-stabilizer co-treatment and registry-derived rates under real-world monotherapy are not measuring the same clinical scenario, and neither generalizes to the other's scenario.
