Retarded catatonia — also called stuporous or akinetic catatonia — names the hypokinetic pole of the catatonic syndrome: profound psychomotor slowing or arrest expressed as stupor, immobility, mutism, and waxy flexibility (a limb or posture that yields to passive repositioning and is then held in place). It is not a separately coded DSM-5-TR diagnosis; a patient meeting the syndrome's three-of-twelve-sign threshold predominantly through these hypokinetic signs and a patient meeting the identical threshold predominantly through hyperkinetic signs both receive the same diagnosis of catatonia, differentiated informally by clinicians as the retarded and excited poles.

The retarded and excited poles are distinguished by the direction of the motor abnormality, not by the presence or absence of catatonic signs: both draw on the same twelve-item sign list and the same diagnostic threshold, and often occur in the same patient. In a retrospective cohort of 1,456 patients across 2,130 catatonic episodes, 925 patients showed stupor/immobility, and of those, 105 (11.4%) also showed excitement within the same episode — a minority, but real overlap that the study's authors interpret as "alternation" between poles within one syndrome rather than two mutually exclusive subtypes. Kahlbaum's original 1874 description already framed catatonia this way, as a syndrome running a cyclic, alternating course through melancholic (retarded) and manic (excited) phases in the same patients, rather than as one static hypokinetic entity.

No primary study located in the literature reviewed for this term measures the transition between the retarded and excited poles directly: no imaging, receptor-binding, or biomarker study scans a patient during a retarded-to-excited switch, and the strongest available transition-relevant evidence — the 1,456-patient cohort above — is a cross-sectional, record-coded co-occurrence analysis, not a prospective or moment-to-moment observation of switching. It establishes that stupor and excitement can coexist within an episode, not the direction, speed, or trigger of a change from one to the other. The consequence for observation is that a single-timepoint rating cannot establish whether, or how, a given patient is moving between poles; only repeated ratings across the course of an episode could address that question, and none of the located literature reports having done so.
