Premenstrual dysphoric disorder is a DSM-5 diagnosis requiring at least five of eleven characteristic symptoms present in most menstrual cycles over the preceding year, at least one of which must be a core affective symptom: marked affective lability, marked irritability or anger or increased interpersonal conflict, markedly depressed mood, or marked anxiety and tension. Onset is specifically in the week before menses, with improvement within a few days after the onset of menses and symptoms absent or minimal in the week following menses; the symptoms must cause clinically significant distress or impairment. Irritability is repeatedly described, across independent secondary descriptions of the diagnostic criteria, as the single most commonly reported primary symptom. This criteria summary is standard nosology, cross-checked against multiple independent descriptions rather than a research claim requiring the usual primary-study verification.

What distinguishes premenstrual dysphoric disorder's abrupt, cyclical onset from a general model of hormone-driven mood change is that the trigger is withdrawal from — the change in — ovarian steroid levels, not their absolute level, and this trigger operates only in people with a prior history of the syndrome. Schmidt and colleagues' randomized, placebo-controlled study, published under the pre-DSM label "premenstrual syndrome" that the PMDD diagnostic literature descends from, gave the GnRH agonist leuprolide or placebo to 20 women with premenstrual syndrome and to comparison women without it. Ten of the premenstrual-syndrome women whose symptoms improved on leuprolide, plus 5 further premenstrual-syndrome women and 15 women without the syndrome, then entered a double-blind, crossover add-back phase receiving estradiol and progesterone, each for four weeks, during continued leuprolide administration. The 10 women given leuprolide showed a significant symptom decrease versus both their own baseline and the 10 comparison women given placebo; during hormone add-back, this same group had a significant recurrence of symptoms, while no mood changes occurred in either the 15 women without the syndrome or the 5 premenstrual-syndrome women given placebo hormone during continued leuprolide. Recurrence, in other words, tracked the change in steroid level and required a prior history of the syndrome — not a specific hormone and not an abnormal absolute level. The authors' own conclusion, quoted directly: "In women with premenstrual syndrome, the occurrence of symptoms represents an abnormal response to normal hormonal changes."

The consequence for research design is that this evidence supports an abnormal individual response to a normal, cyclical hormonal transition — not a general model in which hormone change itself produces mood switching in anyone exposed to it, and not evidence about non-cyclical or male populations. A study that treats hormone withdrawal as a general mechanism for affective switching outside this specific reproductive-axis, cyclical, female-specific context extrapolates beyond what the Schmidt and colleagues design established: women without the syndrome given the same leuprolide-and-add-back protocol showed no mood changes at all, so the finding is specifically about a prior-history-dependent vulnerability to change in ovarian steroid level, not a property of the hormones themselves. The original 1998 report available for this entry states these comparisons only as "significant," without exact effect sizes or p-values, which should be sought from the full text before this finding is used to support a specific quantitative claim; later replications have been referenced in the literature but were not independently read for this entry.
