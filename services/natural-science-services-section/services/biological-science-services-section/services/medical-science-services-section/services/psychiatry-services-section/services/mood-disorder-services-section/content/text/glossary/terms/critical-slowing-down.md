Critical slowing down names a general property of dynamical systems approaching a bifurcation: as a stable equilibrium loses its resilience, the system's return rate to equilibrium after small perturbations slows, which shows up statistically as rising temporal autocorrelation and rising variance in the system's fluctuations over time. The concept originates in dynamical-systems theory and was developed and empirically tested in ecology and climate science, where it has been studied as a candidate generic early-warning indicator for approaching tipping points in systems such as lake eutrophication and climate subsystems.

Van de Leemput and colleagues (2014) imported this framework into psychiatry, testing whether rising autocorrelation, rising variance, and rising cross-correlation between momentary emotion ratings (collected by Experience Sampling Method) were associated with a person's later transition into or out of a depressive episode. Across two independent samples — 535 women from a general-population twin cohort, of whom 13.5% went on to a clinical MDD diagnosis, and 93 patients with MDD tracked for treatment response — the subgroups with an upcoming mood transition showed a significant upward trend in baseline autocorrelation, variance, and cross-emotion correlation relative to the subgroups without (p < 0.05 for each indicator). The authors themselves described this as a first empirical bridge between dynamical-systems theory and psychopathology, not a validated predictive model, and cautioned in their own words that accurate dynamical models are hard to build even for physical systems such as lakes or the climate, let alone psychiatric disorders.

This finding is directly disputed on statistical grounds. Bos and de Jonge (2014) pointed out that van de Leemput et al.'s three indicators were each measured once per person — a single snapshot of that person's own autocorrelation, variance, and cross-correlation across one week of sampling — not repeatedly across time within each person. The published result is therefore a between-subject association (people who later transition have higher autocorrelation than people who do not), but it was written up, and widely read, as though it demonstrated a within-subject dynamical process — an individual's own autocorrelation rising as they approach their own tipping point — which the design cannot distinguish from a simple confound: the higher-risk subgroups' higher baseline mean levels of negative emotion could themselves produce higher measured autocorrelation and variance without any genuine loss of resilience. Van de Leemput and coauthors published a reply defending the between-subject design as informative, though its specific arguments were not accessible for this review. What critical slowing down is nearest to, and must be kept distinct from, is mood instability: mood instability describes how much a person's affect fluctuates in magnitude, while critical slowing down describes a specific pattern in the rate and structure of those fluctuations — their autocorrelation and variance trending upward — that is hypothesized, not established, to precede a discrete state transition.

The consequence for research design is that a cross-sectional or single-timepoint measurement of autocorrelation and variance across subjects cannot establish that any individual's own mood dynamics are slowing down before a transition; only genuinely within-subject, repeated-over-time monitoring of one person's own indicators, tracked up to and through an actual transition, can test the within-person claim. None of this literature identifies a neural, hormonal, or other physiological mechanism for a mood switch: every model here is a formal statistic or dynamical-systems description fitted to self-reported mood time-series data, not a demonstrated biological switch mechanism.
