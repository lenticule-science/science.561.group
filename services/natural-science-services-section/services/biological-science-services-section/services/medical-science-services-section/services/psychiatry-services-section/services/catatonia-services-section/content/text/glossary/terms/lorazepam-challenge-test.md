The lorazepam challenge test is a diagnostic-and-therapeutic probe for catatonia: an initial parenteral (typically intravenous) dose of lorazepam is given to a patient with suspected catatonic signs, and the response — commonly rated with an instrument such as the Bush-Francis Catatonia Rating Scale — is used both to support the diagnosis and to gauge whether a full lorazepam treatment trial is likely to succeed. In the study that formalized this practice, 28 catatonic patients were treated with parenteral and/or oral lorazepam for up to five days, 11 of them receiving an initial intravenous lorazepam challenge before the extended trial.

The challenge test is distinct from the full lorazepam treatment trial that may follow it: the challenge is a single dose used as a rapid diagnostic and prognostic probe, while the trial is the sustained course used to try to resolve catatonic signs. A positive response to the initial parenteral challenge predicted eventual response to the full lorazepam trial, as did a shorter duration of catatonic symptoms before treatment began — meaning the challenge's value is specifically prognostic, indicating who is likely to respond to a lorazepam trial, not merely confirmatory of the catatonia diagnosis in isolation.

Of 21 patients who completed a full lorazepam trial, 16 (76%) had resolution of catatonic signs, and of those who failed lorazepam, electroconvulsive therapy produced a prompt response in all 4 who received it — establishing lorazepam as an effective first-line approach with electroconvulsive therapy as an effective second-line option for nonresponders in this systematically treated sample. A separate systematic review and meta-analysis of electroconvulsive therapy for catatonia — 564 patients across 28 studies, 10 poolable for pre-post comparison — found a very large pooled improvement (standardized mean difference -3.14, 95% CI -3.95 to -2.34) but concluded that, because the identified randomized trials were low-quality and heterogeneous, the literature "fails to demonstrate efficacy and effectiveness" by rigorous trial standards despite consistently describing improvement — a large observational effect without an equivalent randomized-trial-grade demonstration. Neither study stratifies response by pole: the 76% response figure and the electroconvulsive-therapy effect size describe treated catatonia broadly, not whether the retarded or excited pole responds better, and neither figure should be generalized as a population base rate for catatonia response outside similarly sized, systematically treated inpatient samples.
