Catatonia is a psychomotor syndrome defined by the presence of three or more of twelve clinical signs: stupor, catalepsy, waxy flexibility, mutism, negativism, posturing, mannerism, stereotypy, agitation not influenced by external stimuli, grimacing, echolalia, and echopraxia. DSM-5 replaced DSM-IV's standalone "catatonic schizophrenia" subtype with this single, transdiagnostic sign-count threshold, applied through three diagnostic routes: catatonia associated with another mental disorder (a specifier attachable to mood, psychotic, neurodevelopmental, and other disorders), catatonic disorder due to another medical condition, and unspecified catatonia. DSM-5-TR, published in 2022, made no substantive change to this definition. In every route, catatonia is seated as a specifier or qualifier attached to an underlying diagnosis rather than coded as a stand-alone disease category.

This transdiagnostic framing reverses an older, narrower assumption that catatonia belongs chiefly to schizophrenia. Karl Ludwig Kahlbaum's 1874 monograph, *Die Katatonie oder das Spannungsirresein*, first described the syndrome as running a cyclic course across melancholic, manic, stuporous, and confused phases in the same patients, rather than as a fixed feature of one disease. A prospective study of 55 catatonic inpatients found only 4 (about 7%) met research criteria for schizophrenia, while over two-thirds had diagnosable affective disorders, usually mania — directly at odds with the historical catatonia-schizophrenia linkage. A separate review of the affective-disorder literature reports catatonic-sign prevalence at 13% to 31% of affective-disorder inpatient samples, and, across acute psychiatric admissions generally, a wide and study-dependent range running from 6% to 38% historically down to 2% to 8% in more recent estimates — a spread the review does not resolve into a single figure.

Catatonia's motor presentation spans two poles: a retarded (hypokinetic) pole of stupor, immobility, and mutism, and an excited (hyperkinetic) pole of purposeless agitation unresponsive to external stimuli. In a retrospective cohort of 1,456 validated catatonia diagnoses across 2,130 episodes, stupor/immobility was recorded in 925 patients, of whom 105 (11.4%) also showed excitement, while of 196 patients with excitement, 105 (53.6%) also showed stupor/immobility — the same 105 co-occurring patients counted from each side. The authors describe this as psychomotor "alternation": stupor and excitement, though seemingly opposite states, frequently co-occur within the same episode rather than marking two mutually exclusive subtypes.

Two consequences follow for clinical observation and research design. First, the DSM-5-TR sign-count threshold is phenomenological rather than mechanistic, and it is agnostic to which pole the qualifying signs come from — meeting "3 of 12" says nothing by itself about whether a patient's presentation is retarded, excited, or mixed; clinical assessment operationalizes the individual signs through instruments such as the Bush-Francis Catatonia Rating Scale rather than through a pole label. Second, because catatonia is coded as a specifier rather than a free-standing diagnosis, clinical and research records must separately track the disorder it attaches to; and because retarded and excited signs demonstrably co-occur within episodes, any study design that forces a single-timepoint classification into one pole risks misclassifying the substantial minority of patients — roughly half of those with excited signs, and about one in nine of those with stuporous signs, in the cohort above — who show both.

## Related terms

- Retarded catatonia — the hypokinetic pole of the syndrome
- Excited catatonia — the hyperkinetic pole of the syndrome
- Malignant catatonia — the hyperthermic, autonomically unstable variant carrying substantial risk of death if untreated
- Bush-Francis catatonia rating scale — the operationalized instrument used to rate catatonic signs
- Lorazepam challenge test — the diagnostic-and-therapeutic probe used to support the diagnosis and predict treatment response
