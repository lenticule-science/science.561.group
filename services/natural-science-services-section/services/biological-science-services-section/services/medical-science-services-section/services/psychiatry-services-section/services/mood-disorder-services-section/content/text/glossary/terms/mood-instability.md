Mood instability is defined, in a widely used clinical framing (Broome, Saunders, Harrison & Marwaha, 2015), as rapid oscillations of intense affect together with a difficulty regulating those oscillations or their behavioural consequences. It names the amplitude and frequency of change in momentary affect over short timescales, not the average intensity of that affect: two people can report the same mean level of negative affect across a week while differing sharply in how much that affect swings from one assessment to the next. Broome and colleagues report a population prevalence of 13.9% from the UK Adult Psychiatric Morbidity Survey 2007, higher in women and peaking in the 16–24 age range before declining with age, and argue mood instability has underappreciated potential as a transdiagnostic investigational and therapeutic target — their own interpretive framing rather than a finding derived from a single dataset.

An ecological momentary assessment study of 221 adults from a population-based Estonian sample (Sultson, Murd, Havik & Konstabel, 2024), sampling affect five times daily for a week, found that negative-affect instability predicted elevated depressive and generalized-anxiety symptoms even after statistically controlling for mean negative- and positive-affect intensity (depression odds ratio 2.15, 99% CI 1.08–4.30; generalized anxiety odds ratio 2.16, 99% CI 1.04–4.48) — roughly a doubling of odds per standard-deviation increase in instability, independent of how negative the person's affect was on average. The same predictors held for both depressive and anxiety symptoms, which the authors read as evidence the construct is transdiagnostic rather than disorder-specific, though 62% depression/anxiety symptom overlap in their sample limits how disorder-specific any interpretation can be. The authors' own explicit caution is that despite the word 'predicts' in their title, all of their analyses were cross-sectional: elevated instability and elevated symptoms were measured over the same assessment period, so the finding is an association, not a demonstration that instability precedes or causes later symptom elevation.

Mood instability is a descriptive measure of fluctuation magnitude, and must be kept distinct from early warning signal, the framework built on critical slowing down: mood instability describes how much affect swings, while an early warning signal is a claim about the changing rate and temporal structure of those swings — rising autocorrelation and variance — as a system nears a transition. A person's affect can be highly unstable, with large swings, without those swings showing the slowing-down pattern that early-warning-signal methods look for, and conversely a rising-autocorrelation signal can in principle occur alongside stable-magnitude fluctuation. None of the studies reviewed here identify a neural, hormonal, or other physiological mechanism producing mood instability; it is measured as a statistic over self-reported momentary ratings, not a physiological quantity. The consequence for measurement and research design is that a cross-sectional association between instability and symptom severity, however robust, cannot on its own establish that instability comes before or causes symptom elevation, and that any study using the word 'predicts' for a same-period cross-sectional design should be read for what the analysis actually tested, not for what its title implies.
