The Affective Reactivity Index (ARI) is a brief, 7-item parent- and self-report scale — 6 scored symptom items plus 1 impairment item — developed by Stringaris, Goodman, Leibenluft, Brotman, and colleagues (2012) to give a concise, dimensional measure of irritability usable in both highly specialized research settings and general child mental health settings. It was validated in two samples of children and adolescents: a U.S. sample of 218 recruited at the National Institute of Mental Health (bipolar disorder n=39, severe mood dysregulation n=67, children at familial risk for bipolar disorder n=35, healthy volunteers n=77), and a U.K. sample of 88 drawn from a general mental-health setting and from primary/secondary schools. In both samples, the parent- and self-report ARI scales loaded on a single factor and showed internal consistency the original validation study describes only as "excellent" — the exact alpha and test-retest coefficients are not given in the abstract text this dossier drew from, and were not independently re-derived from the paper's full-text tables.

The ARI measures irritability as a dimensional trait — a graded score, not a categorical diagnosis — and in the U.S. validation sample of children and adolescents, scores graded upward from healthy volunteers through children with severe mood dysregulation, with parent-rated (but not self-rated) irritability significantly higher in severe mood dysregulation than in bipolar disorder. This distinguishes the ARI from a diagnostic instrument like the criteria for disruptive mood dysregulation disorder: the ARI gives a continuous irritability score usable across clinical and non-clinical populations, while DMDD is a categorical yes/no diagnosis requiring specific duration, frequency, and setting criteria to be met.

Because the ARI was validated in children and adolescents only, using it to characterize irritability in adults — for example, alongside the STAR*D depression-trial irritability threshold or the adult anger-attack literature — extends the instrument beyond its validated population without established psychometric support in adults. And because the original 2012 validation paper's own abstract reports internal consistency only qualitatively, later cross-cultural replication studies that report specific alpha values (for example, an Italian-sample study reporting alphas of 0.79 and 0.78) describe those particular samples' psychometrics, not the original validation study's own numbers, and should not be substituted for them when citing the instrument's founding reliability figures.
