An antibody (immunoglobulin) is a Y-shaped protein with two functionally distinct regions: a constant region (Fc), which determines the antibody's class and effector behavior, and a variable region (Fab), which forms the antigen-binding site. The variable region's shape is generated by V(D)J recombination — random splicing of gene segments during B cell development — producing a repertoire of binding specificities large enough to cover essentially any antigen shape the body might encounter, without any single antibody being pre-specified for any single pathogen.

## Classes

- **IgM** — the first antibody produced in a primary response; forms pentamers, efficient at complement activation.
- **IgG** — the dominant antibody in blood and the main mediator of long-term humoral memory; crosses the placenta, conferring passive immunity to the fetus.
- **IgA** — secreted at mucosal surfaces (gut, airway, breast milk); the front line against pathogens at epithelial barriers.
- **IgE** — bound to mast cells; mediates allergic reactions and anti-parasite defense.
- **IgD** — present on naive B cell surfaces; role less fully characterized than the others.

## Affinity maturation

On first exposure to an antigen, B cells produce antibodies of modest affinity. In germinal centers — structures that form within lymph nodes and spleen during an active response — activated B cells undergo somatic hypermutation: their antigen-receptor genes accumulate point mutations at a rate far above the genomic background. Mutated B cell clones compete for antigen and for survival signals from helper T cells; clones whose mutated receptors bind antigen more tightly outcompete their neighbors and are selected to proliferate and differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells or long-lived memory B cells. Repeated over cycles, this variation-and-selection process raises the average binding affinity of the antibody population across an immune response — the molecular basis of why a second exposure to a pathogen produces a faster, stronger, better-fitting response than the first.

## Effector functions

Binding an antigen is rarely the whole story — what the antibody does once bound depends on its class and context:

- **Neutralization** — physically blocking a pathogen's ability to attach to or enter host cells.
- **Opsonization** — coating a pathogen so that phagocytes (which have receptors for antibody Fc regions) engulf it more efficiently.
- **Complement activation** — antibody binding can trigger the complement cascade, a sequence of plasma proteins that can punch holes directly in pathogen membranes.
