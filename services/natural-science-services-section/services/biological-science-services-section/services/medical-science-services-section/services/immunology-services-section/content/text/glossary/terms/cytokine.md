Cytokines are small secreted proteins produced by immune cells (primarily T cells, B cells, and macrophages) and other cell types (endothelial cells, fibroblasts, tissue-resident cells) that act as extracellular signalling molecules in immune responses, inflammation, hematopoiesis, and wound repair. The class includes interleukins (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10), tumor necrosis factors (TNF-α), interferons (IFN-γ, type I interferons), and chemokines (CXCL8, CCL2) — a functionally defined grouping rather than a single biochemical family. A cytokine is distinguished from a hormone by its source and mode of action: hormones are synthesized in specialized endocrine glands and transported via circulation to distant target tissues; cytokines are induced rapidly in response to local tissue damage or pathogen exposure and act primarily on nearby cells (paracrine signalling) or on the producing cell itself (autocrine signalling). The boundary blurs pragmatically — some cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) can reach systemic levels and produce distant effects — but the conceptual distinction holds: a hormone is constitutive and glandular; a cytokine is inducible and distributed.

The mechanistically essential feature of cytokine biology is context-dependence and pleiotropy: a single cytokine binds multiple receptor subtypes on different target cells, and the same cytokine can produce opposite effects depending on which receptor is engaged, which cell type expresses it, and the ambient concentration and presence of costimulatory signals. IL-6 exemplifies this sharply. When IL-6 binds membrane-bound IL-6 receptor (IL-6Rα) on hepatocytes and some immune cells, it promotes acute-phase protein synthesis and supports immune activation — broadly anti-inflammatory in functional consequence. When IL-6 is shed as soluble IL-6R (sIL-6R) in circulation and the IL-6/sIL-6R complex activates gp130 on neurons, astrocytes, and endothelial cells that express gp130 but not membrane IL-6Rα, it drives bloodbrain barrier disruption, synaptic loss, and depressive-like behavior — broadly pro-inflammatory and pathogenic. The same molecule, same ligand-receptor family, opposite functional outcomes determined by target-cell identity and receptor subtype. This context-dependence means cytokine redundancy — multiple cytokines can activate overlapping downstream pathways — is a fundamental feature, not a limitation: TNF-α and IL-1β both activate NF-κB and can each upregulate the other, creating amplification loops where a single cytokine trigger can recruit multiple mediators.

The consequence for biomarker work and clinical assessment is severe and underappreciated: a single serum-cytokine measurement is a weak and potentially misleading instrument because (1) cytokine concentrations are highly variable between individuals and across time, (2) the same measured level can reflect opposite functional states depending on which cytokine and which target-cell responses matter for the disease being studied, and (3) cytokine panels, despite producing two decades of ME/CFS and related-condition research, have shown inconsistent findings across studies — meta-analyses of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-8, and other cytokines in ME/CFS patients report positive associations in some cohorts, null findings or opposite directions in others, and the heterogeneity appears robust to reasonable corrections for measurement error and publication bias. The inconsistency is not primarily a failure of measurement technique or statistical power; it reflects that serum cytokine levels, untethered from the cellular and receptor context in which those cytokines exert effects, are epidemiologically and mechanistically underdetermined as markers of disease state. Functional assays — the ability of a patient's stimulated immune cells to suppress cytokine production when exposed to dexamethasone or glucocorticoid, for instance — predict clinically relevant outcomes more consistently than absolute cytokine concentration does, and are the appropriate experimental readout for testing mechanistic hypotheses about inflammation's role in symptom generation.
