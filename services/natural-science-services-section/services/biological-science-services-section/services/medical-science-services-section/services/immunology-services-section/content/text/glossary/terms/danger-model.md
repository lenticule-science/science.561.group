The danger model, proposed by Polly Matzinger in "Tolerance, Danger, and the Extended Family" (1994) [@matzinger1994], argues that the immune system's central question is not "is this foreign?" but "is this dangerous?" Under the classical self/non-self framework, immune cells should attack anything not recognized as self — but several stubborn observations resist that account: the immune system tolerates enormous quantities of non-self commensal bacteria in the gut, tolerates a genetically distinct fetus for the duration of pregnancy, and often tolerates transplanted tissue for a time before eventually rejecting it (rejection correlating with surgical trauma and inflammation at the graft site, not simply with the presence of foreign antigen). Matzinger's model explains all three by proposing that antigen-presenting cells are activated not by foreignness but by damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) — molecules released by cells dying abnormally (necrosis, injury) rather than dying in the routine, contained way (apoptosis).

## Consequences of the reframing

- **Commensal bacteria** don't trigger attack because they don't damage the tissue they inhabit — no danger signal, no response, regardless of how much non-self material is present.
- **The fetus** is tolerated because, absent complications, its presence does not generate tissue damage signals at the maternal-fetal interface.
- **Transplant rejection** is not simply "foreign tissue detected" but is potentiated by the surgical trauma of transplantation itself, which releases danger signals that activate antigen-presenting cells and prime the recipient's T cells against the graft's antigens presented in that inflamed context. A transplant introduced without associated tissue damage is, on this model, more likely to be tolerated than the same tissue introduced with it.
- **Tumor immunology** — many tumors evade immune attack in part because slow, well-contained tumor growth doesn't generate strong danger signals, even though tumor cells display abnormal (technically non-self, in the sense of mutated) antigens.

## Relationship to self/non-self and network theories

The danger model does not claim self/non-self discrimination is false — thymic negative selection against self-antigen is well established — but argues it is not sufficient on its own to explain when the immune system actually acts. Danger signaling functions as a second gate: an antigen-presenting cell encountering a foreign antigen without an accompanying danger signal tends to induce tolerance rather than activation, because presenting antigen without costimulation is one of the mechanisms of [peripheral tolerance](./immunological-tolerance.md). The two accounts are complementary rather than strictly rival, though they were often framed as competitors in the 1990s–2000s immunology literature, and Matzinger's model absorbed some of the explanatory territory that Jerne's [idiotypic network theory](./idiotypic-network.md) had also tried to cover — internal regulation independent of external antigen alone.

## A framing from outside immunology

The founding note for this section's theory arc reads Matzinger's danger model as "immunity keyed to witnessed damage rather than mere non-self — provenance over category" — an instance, on that reading, of a broader pattern where a classification system is described as tracking an evidentiary event (damage that occurred and was detected) rather than a static category membership (foreign vs. self).
