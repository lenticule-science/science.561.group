Interleukin-6 is a cytokine induced downstream of NF-κB activation that drives the hepatic acute-phase response, supports antibody production, and acts on the central nervous system to produce fever and sickness behaviour [tanaka2014]. Its gene is a canonical target of the NF-κB pathway [hayden2008], which is the relation this carrier asserts as **produced-by** (**established**).

It differs from most cytokines in having two signalling modes: classic signalling through membrane-bound IL-6 receptor, restricted to hepatocytes and some leukocytes, and trans-signalling through a soluble receptor complex that reaches any cell bearing gp130 [tanaka2014]. **Established**: the two modes and their different target populations are the organizing distinction of the modern IL-6 literature.

A serum concentration therefore does not determine the effect: the same level acts on a restricted or an unrestricted cell population depending on how much soluble receptor is present, so this cytokine cannot be read as a single-number index of inflammation. For research reading a person's inflammatory state over time, the consequence is that a stable concentration is compatible with a changing biological effect, and that comparisons across studies which measure only total concentration are comparing weaker quantities than they appear to.
