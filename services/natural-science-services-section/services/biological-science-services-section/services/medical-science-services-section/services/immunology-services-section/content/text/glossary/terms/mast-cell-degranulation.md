Mast cells are tissue-resident granulocytes distributed throughout the body at barrier surfaces (skin, gut, respiratory tract) and in neural-rich tissues. Their granules contain preformed mediators — histamine, tryptase, heparin, and other proteases — that are released rapidly when the cell is triggered, along with newly synthesized lipid mediators (prostaglandins and leukotrienes) generated within minutes of activation. Degranulation is the exocytotic discharge of these granules into the extracellular space, where the mediators act on local tissues and vasculature to produce immediate vascular permeability, smooth-muscle contraction, and pain. The classical understanding emphasizes IgE-dependent triggering: cross-linking of IgE molecules bound to high-affinity FcεRI receptors on the mast cell surface recruits Lyn and Syk kinases to the receptor, activating downstream phospholipase-C and calcium signalling, resulting in rapid degranulation. This is the pathway that dominates acute allergic reactions, where allergen-specific IgE on mast cells is cross-linked by allergen.

The IgE-independent pathways are equally important and are often underemphasized in standard accounts. Mast cells express MRGPRX2, a G-protein-coupled receptor activated by certain neuropeptides (substance P, calcitonin-gene-related peptide, neuropeptide Y), bacterial lipopolysaccharides, and various drugs (opioids, antibiotics), which triggers degranulation independently of IgE. The adrenergic nervous system can also trigger mast cell degranulation via β2-adrenergic receptor signalling and, under stress conditions involving norepinephrine surges, can mobilize mast cell mediators without any involvement of allergen or antibody. Complement (C5a and C3a) can directly activate mast cells through C3aR and C5aR receptors. These IgE-independent routes are mechanistically critical for understanding mast cell involvement in stress-related physiology, since a sympathetic surge can trigger central and peripheral mast cell degranulation without requiring prior IgE sensitization to an allergen.

Mast cell degranulation must be distinguished from mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS) as a clinical diagnosis. MCAS is a clinical entity characterized by recurrent symptoms attributed to mast cell-driven mediator release, typically involving flushing, urticaria, angioedema, gastrointestinal symptoms, and cardiovascular instability. However, diagnostic criteria for MCAS are genuinely contested: criteria vary between proposed schemas (WHO, international consensus proposals from 2012 onwards), and confirmation of a diagnosis typically requires serum tryptase levels sampled within the symptom episode (usually a matter of minutes to hours after symptom onset) compared against a personal baseline — a practically difficult requirement. Moreover, the diagnosis of MCAS in the absence of documented elevated tryptase remains controversial, since tryptase elevation is inconsistent across symptomatic episodes, and basal serum tryptase itself varies widely between individuals. The proposed involvement of mast cell degranulation in ME/CFS is a hypothesis with limited direct evidence: some ME/CFS patients report flushing, urticaria, and gastrointestinal symptoms superficially compatible with mast cell release, and small case series have documented elevated tryptase in some individuals, but systematic prospective studies correlating MCAS diagnostic criteria with ME/CFS diagnosis and with cytokine or symptom patterns are lacking.

The practical consequence of the degranulation mechanism is that tryptase, the most commonly measured mast cell mediator, is only detectable in serum at levels above baseline for a narrow window around the time of degranulation — typically a few hours. A blood sample must be drawn within hours of a suspected mast cell degranulation episode, and compared against that individual's own personal baseline tryptase level (measured at a quiescent time), to establish whether an elevation has occurred. Histamine is even more transient, with half-life measured in minutes, making it essentially impossible to measure retrospectively. This timing requirement means that establishing whether a particular clinical episode involved mast cell degranulation depends entirely on having drawn a timed blood sample during the event — a demand that makes epidemiological or clinical research on mast cell involvement in poorly-characterized conditions (such as ME/CFS) extraordinarily difficult, and explains why mast cell-mediator data in such conditions remain sparse.
