A mast cell is a tissue-resident granulocyte, seated at barrier surfaces and around blood vessels and peripheral nerves, that stores preformed mediators including histamine and tryptase in granules and releases them within seconds of activation [galli2020]. Activation is classically IgE-dependent through the high-affinity IgE receptor, but IgE-independent routes — including complement fragments, neuropeptides, and drugs acting at MRGPRX2 — are established, which is the **acts-through** relation to receptor asserted here.

Unlike circulating leukocytes recruited to a site after injury, it is already stationed in the tissue and can act before any recruitment occurs, which makes it an immediate rather than a recruited effector. **Established.**

Its position beside vessels and nerves means an activation event couples the immune, vascular, and sensory systems in one step, so a mast-cell mediated symptom cannot be assigned to any one of those systems on its own. Galli, Gaudenzio and Tsai's review states the ongoing concern directly: the cell's roles are established in allergic disease and host defence, while its proposed contributions to many other conditions rest on weaker evidence [galli2020]. **A note on the infection-associated chronic illness literature specifically**: mast-cell involvement in myalgic encephalomyelitis is a proposed mechanism, not an established finding — a targeted PubMed search on 2026-09-02 for mast-cell activation in that population returned clinical-overlap discussion and one small open treatment report, and no controlled demonstration of degranulation in a diagnosed cohort.
