Glucocorticoids, primarily cortisol in humans, are steroid hormones released from the adrenal cortex under hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis signalling. One of their major functions is to restrain inflammatory and immune responses after the acute phase of a threat or injury. Cortisol binds to the glucocorticoid receptor (GR), a ligand-activated transcription factor that translocates to the nucleus and modulates gene transcription. At inflammatory-response genes driven by NF-κB and AP-1 transcription factors — the canonical mediators of pro-inflammatory gene expression — GR tethers to these factors and competes for coactivators, directly repressing NF-κB/AP-1-driven transcription without necessarily recruiting corepressors; it also induces IκBα, which sequesters NF-κB in the cytoplasm and prevents its nuclear entry. Through these transrepression mechanisms, physiological cortisol levels suppress the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8) and upregulate anti-inflammatory mediators (IL-10, TGF-β). In a healthy acute stress response, this glucocorticoid brake acts as the termination signal for the inflammatory phase: adrenaline and sympathetic activation mobilize energy and heighten alertness in the short term, while cortisol gradually rises and shuts down that inflammatory amplification, allowing the system to return to quiescence.

Glucocorticoid resistance — also called glucocorticoid receptor resistance or GR desensitization — is the state in which cortisol is present at normal or elevated levels yet fails to exert its immunosuppressive effect. This is a distinctly different problem from absent cortisol (primary or secondary adrenal insufficiency), and the mechanisms are separable: cortisol insufficiency is a hormone-availability problem; glucocorticoid resistance is a receptor-signaling problem. In chronic stress, particularly chronic social threat and subordination, the mechanism of resistance appears to involve both reduced GR expression (lower receptor numbers on immune cells) and impaired GR signal transduction downstream of binding — reduced phosphorylation of GR and its nuclear translocation, reduced recruitment of coactivators to GR-tethering sites, or altered histone acetylation at GR-response-element-bearing promoters. Cohen's rhinovirus-challenge study (2012) directly measured both cortisol and ex vivo glucocorticoid suppression of cytokine production in leukocytes from chronically-stressed subjects: he found that stressed individuals with prior severe interpersonal stressors showed both elevated infection risk and *reduced ability of dexamethasone (a synthetic glucocorticoid) to suppress IL-6 production* — a functional measure of the brake — despite normal circulating cortisol levels, proving that the lesion is downstream of the hormone itself. Miller's monocyte-expression work (2008) in chronic caregivers showed the same dissociation: caregivers had normal ambulatory cortisol but diminished monocyte expression of genes bearing glucocorticoid-response elements (GRE), consistent with impaired GR signaling at the target level.

The clinical and research consequence of this distinction is profound and often missed. A normal serum cortisol level does not establish that the glucocorticoid brake is intact; it establishes only that the adrenal is producing hormone. Assessing whether the brake is functional requires a functional readout: ex vivo dexamethasone-suppression assay (do the patient's stimulated leukocytes suppress cytokine production when exposed to dexamethasone?), or a related test such as the low-dose dexamethasone-suppression test (does an oral dose of dexamethasone suppress cortisol and ACTH as expected?). A subject with normal cortisol but glucocorticoid resistance will show unbraked inflammation despite normal hormone, and the unbraked state is *not* addressable by giving more cortisol — it requires restoration of GR function itself, a far more difficult therapeutic target than hormone supplementation. This distinction matters enormously in ME/CFS: if some ME/CFS patients have glucocorticoid resistance (a hypothesis supported by blunted cortisol responses to challenge and by some studies of ex vivo dexamethasone suppression, though the literature is sparse and inconsistent), then administering more cortisol (as some older treatment trials did) would be mechanistically mismatched to the actual problem — the hormone would simply accumulate without the receptor machinery to use it.
