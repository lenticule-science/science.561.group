The canonical account of the stress response treats cortisol as the mobilizing hormone: it shifts metabolism toward gluconeogenesis and lipolysis, increases heart rate and blood pressure, and primes alertness. But cortisol carries a second, equally important job that is often backgrounded in that narrative — it is the primary brake on inflammation. When that brake is absent or fails, a specific temporal signature emerges: symptom onset delayed by one to three days after an exertional trigger, persisting for multiple days afterward, and disproportionate to the magnitude of the initial insult. This note argues that this temporal signature is mechanistically explained by the kinetics of an unchecked inflammatory cascade, and that understanding which brake is missing (absent hormone versus ineffective receptor) is critical for choosing where to intervene.

## The glucocorticoid brake: mechanism and loss

Under normal stress physiology, the sequence is choreographed: a perceived threat triggers the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, cortisol rises over minutes to hours, and at physiological concentrations, it acts on glucocorticoid receptors (GR) to accomplish multiple immunosuppressive moves. It transrepresses NF-κB and AP-1, the transcription factors that drive pro-inflammatory cytokine genes (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8); it induces IκBα, which sequesters NF-κB in the cytoplasm; and it upregulates anti-inflammatory mediators (IL-10, TGF-β). The net effect is that the inflammatory phase — useful acutely for clearing debris and pathogenic material — is terminated before it cascades into chronicity.

Two distinct physiological failure modes can lose this brake. **Absent cortisol** — adrenal insufficiency — leaves the system without the hormone entirely. The adrenal crisis in total ACTH or cortisol deficiency produces acute cardiovascular collapse as catecholamines continue to fire without cortisol's permissive upregulation of α1-adrenergic receptor expression; vasculature desensitizes, blood pressure plummets. This is a hormone-level problem. **Glucocorticoid resistance** — the state where cortisol is circulating at normal or elevated concentration yet fails to exert immunosuppressive effects — is a receptor or signal-transduction problem. As documented in the companion 2026-08-26 note on the cytokine-transcriptomic machinery (§1.2), chronic stress and social threat produce GR desensitization: reduced GR expression on immune cells, impaired phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of GR, diminished recruitment of coactivators to GR-binding sites. Both Cohen's rhinovirus data and Miller's monocyte-profiling work establish this directly: in chronically stressed individuals, dexamethasone (a synthetic glucocorticoid) fails to suppress ex vivo cytokine production despite normal circulating cortisol levels. The brake is intact but cannot engage.

## The delayed cytokine cascade: why symptoms appear on day two or three

Here is where the temporal signature becomes mechanistically intelligible. Consider the physiological response to an exertional challenge — a day of increased physical or cognitive activity — in someone with a broken inflammatory brake.

**Hour 0–6: Exertion-triggered inflammation begins.** Muscle exertion increases metabolic demand, potentially exceeding mitochondrial capacity and producing local tissue stress. The autonomic response is proportional and immediate: norepinephrine and epinephrine surge, sympathetic preganglionic neurons fire at the adrenal medulla. Tissue-resident mast cells degranulate via β2-adrenergic and neuropeptide (MRGPRX2) pathways, releasing preformed histamine, tryptase, and other mediators. Microglia in the brainstem and sensory cortex, already primed by prior chronic stress (via glucocorticoid-mediated priming, documented in the Maier/Watkins literature), become activated by the adrenergic surge and degranulate, releasing IL-1β and TNF-α into local neural parenchyma. This immediate phase feels sharp and localized — palpitations, nausea, transient cognitive fog — but subsides as exertion stops and acute sympathetic tone declines.

**Hour 6–24: The unchecked cascade.** In a healthy system, cortisol has risen and engaged its transrepression program on NF-κB and AP-1, suppressing further pro-inflammatory gene transcription. In someone with glucocorticoid resistance, cortisol is rising but the GR cannot translate that hormone into transcriptional restraint. The initial cytokine release (IL-1β, TNF-α) is not countered. These cytokines are not transient signaling molecules — they are autocrine and paracrine amplifiers. TNF-α and IL-1β both upregulate each other and amplify the transcription of additional pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-8). On the timeline of 6–24 hours, these secondary and tertiary cytokine waves reach tissue and blood levels that begin to produce systemic symptoms. But the person is now at rest; the exertion itself ended 6–18 hours ago. Yet symptoms are escalating.

**Hour 24–72: Peak symptom, self-reinforcing loop.** By day two to day three, circulating IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and reactive oxygen species have reached levels sufficient to activate vascular endothelium, impair nitric oxide production, and disrupt neurovascular coupling. Cytokine-induced endothelial activation and increased vascular permeability allow cytokines to cross the blood-brain barrier, activating microglia and astrocytes, which release additional IL-1β and TNF-α, sustaining central inflammatory tone. This is the sickness-behavior phase (as laid out in the 2026-08-26 inflammation-withdrawal note): the body interprets the persistent inflammatory signal as evidence of ongoing systemic threat and reallocates energy from voluntary motor control, cognition, and exertion capacity toward immune, metabolic, and restorative processes. Fatigue, myalgia, cognitive dysfunction, orthostatic intolerance, and headache all follow from this coordinated reallocation. The symptoms are not proportional to the trigger (a day of exertion), but proportional to the *persistent cytokine load* now acting on multiple target tissues. Without an effective glucocorticoid brake, there is no termination signal. Cytokines persist not because production remains elevated (the exertion is long over) but because degradation and clearance are slow — circulating IL-6 half-life is on the order of hours, but at elevated production rates and impaired dampening, steady-state levels remain high for days.

**Day 4+: Slow resolution.** Eventually, the inflammatory cascade self-resolves. The body is not in complete immune failure — other mechanisms (IL-10 and TGF-β production, oxidative stress tolerance, direct cytokine catabolism) eventually suppress the amplification loop. But resolution occurs on the timescale of many days, not hours. A symptom flare that began imperceptibly 24–36 hours after exertion persists 5–10 days as a macroscopic clinical event.

## Distinguishing absent cortisol from ineffective cortisol

This distinction has immediate mechanistic and therapeutic implications. If a patient has **absent cortisol** (adrenal insufficiency), the intervention is supplementation — glucocorticoid replacement at physiological doses restores the brake. If a patient has **glucocorticoid resistance** — normal or high cortisol but nonfunctional GR signaling — giving more cortisol is mechanistically mismatched to the problem. The hormone is already present but cannot be used. Correcting the resistance would require restoration of GR function itself (e.g., reducing the mechanisms of receptor desensitization, if they were reversible), not adding more ligand.

Assessing which is present requires functional readouts, not hormone concentrations alone. A serum cortisol level, no matter how carefully measured, does not establish brake function. The appropriate test is ex vivo dexamethasone suppression: are the patient's stimulated leukocytes capable of suppressing cytokine production when exposed to dexamethasone? Can ex vivo monocytes show normal GR-driven suppression of NF-κB-dependent transcription? If cortisol is present but leukocytes cannot suppress inflammatory transcripts in response to synthetic glucocorticoid, the brake is mechanistically broken at the receptor level. This is the distinction that Cohen's and Miller's work has made empirically clear, and it is the distinction that matters for understanding ME/CFS patients in whom cortisol responses to CRH and ACTH challenge are blunted (suggesting central HPA insufficiency) *and* in whom some studies document impaired ex vivo dexamethasone suppression — a dual lesion suggesting both reduced central drive *and* peripheral GR resistance.

## What is established versus inferred

The established facts here are: (1) cortisol suppresses NF-κB and AP-1-driven inflammatory transcription through GR transrepression, a mechanism with decades of cell-biology and clinical endocrinology backing; (2) chronic stress produces glucocorticoid receptor desensitization, documented in multiple cohorts by Cohen, Miller, and the social-genomics literature; (3) IL-6, TNF-α, and related cytokines cause sickness behavior and coordinate energy reallocation, a phenomenon established across rodent behavior models and human neuroimaging; (4) the temporal kinetics of cytokine accumulation and clearance in serum are consistent with a 24–72 hour onset for systemic inflammatory symptoms; (5) some ME/CFS patients show evidence of blunted HPA responses and, in smaller studies, impaired ex vivo dexamethasone suppression.

The inference, not yet directly demonstrated in humans with ME/CFS, is that this specific loop — loss of the glucocorticoid brake → unchecked NF-κB-driven cytokine amplification → persistent systemic and central inflammatory tone for days → coordinated sickness-behavior reallocation → symptom flare — is the causal mechanism underlying post-exertional malaise's characteristic temporal signature. This is a reasoned hypothesis built from established pieces, not a settled fact. Directly testing it would require (a) documenting blunted cortisol suppression of inflammatory transcription in ME/CFS patients prospectively, (b) measuring circulating cytokine kinetics (not single time-point sampling) across the 24–72 hour post-exertional window, and (c) showing correlation between the degree of GR resistance and the magnitude of delayed symptom flare. None of these experiments have been published in adequate detail to settle the question.

## Implications for understanding sickness behavior in ME/CFS

The framework laid out in the 2026-08-26 inflammation-withdrawal note treats sickness behavior as an organized, energy-conserving response to detected threat. Under that reading, PEM's enforced rest is not a bug — it is a feature: the body, detecting persistent inflammatory signaling that it interprets as evidence of ongoing systemic infection or damage, reallocates resources away from voluntary exertion and toward immune and restorative processes. The symptom flare is a *coordinated response to a real physiological signal* (the unbraked cytokine cascade), not a mere side effect of inflammation. That reframing does not make the symptom less disabling, but it does shift the mechanistic target: the problem is not that sickness behavior is being triggered *inappropriately*, but that the signal being detected (persistent inflammation due to a broken brake) is being amplified because the normal termination mechanism has failed. Restoring the brake, rather than suppressing sickness behavior directly, would be the indicated approach.
