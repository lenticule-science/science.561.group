Effort-based decision-making is the behavioral-economic computation an organism performs when choosing whether to expend physical or cognitive effort for a reward, weighing the cost of the action against the reward's expected magnitude and probability of delivery. In rodents, this computation depends causally on mesolimbic (nucleus accumbens) dopamine: depleting or antagonizing accumbens dopamine shifts choice toward lower-effort, lower-reward options without reducing consumption of, or preference for, the higher-value reward when effort is removed from the choice (Salamone, Cousins, & Bucher, 1994), and a later review frames accumbens dopamine as involved in "behavioral activation, exertion of effort, approach behavior, sustained task engagement" rather than primary food motivation or appetite (Salamone & Correa, 2012).

This is the same computation the effort literature distinguishes from anhedonia: what effort-based decision-making measures is willingness to mobilize effort, not the hedonic value of the reward once obtained.

In humans the computation is operationalized behaviorally by the Effort Expenditure for Rewards Task (EEfRT) and similar paradigms, in which participants repeatedly choose between an easy, low-reward option and a harder, higher-reward option at varying magnitudes and probabilities. Whether currently depressed patients show a robust deficit on this task relative to controls is a live, contested question, reported here with both sides' sample sizes rather than resolved toward either. Treadway, Bossaller, Shelton, and Zald (2012) found 20 individuals with major depressive disorder chose the harder/higher-reward option less often than 15 healthy controls, with longer episode duration predicting lower hard-task choice. Culbreth et al. (2024), directly comparing four diagnostic groups (33 schizophrenia, 28 schizoaffective, 39 bipolar, 40 major depressive disorder, 70 controls), found a significant overall group effect driven by reduced high-effort choice in schizophrenia specifically, while the MDD and bipolar groups did not differ from controls in that sample — a named non-replication of an MDD-specific deficit. Pooling across the field, Pillny et al. (2026), a preregistered transdiagnostic meta-analysis of 50 studies and 71 effect sizes across 886 MDD, 1,776 schizophrenia-spectrum, and 1,848 healthy-control participants, found combined clinical groups showed significantly less willingness to exert effort than controls, with more pronounced deficits in schizophrenia-spectrum disorder than in MDD — a smaller, but nonzero, pooled depression effect sitting alongside Culbreth's single-study null. Both results are reported rather than resolved: a pooled meta-analytic average across many studies can coexist with an individual, adequately powered study finding no MDD-control difference, and the two should not be flattened into one clean story.

The consequence for research design is that a single EEfRT-type study, in either direction, should not be treated as settling whether depression carries an effort-allocation deficit: the field itself is split on whether current MDD shows a robust deficit, whether remission normalizes it, and what its computational locus is, so a study should report its own sample and effect size rather than citing the literature's aggregate direction as if it applied uniformly to every clinical sample. A computational follow-up (Valton et al., preprint), using a physical-effort grip-strength task rather than the EEfRT in healthy, at-risk, remitted, and currently depressed groups, argued that lower willingness to exert effort in depression reflects a trait-like "effort acceptance bias" rather than altered effort or reward sensitivity — a different mechanistic story than Treadway et al.'s (2012) "impaired use of reward/probability information," obtained with a different task and a different modeling approach, and a genuine open disagreement about what kind of computation is disrupted, not merely whether a group difference exists.
