# Intentional stance

The **intentional stance** (Dennett 1971, 1987) is a predictive strategy: treating a system as a *rational agent with beliefs and desires* and predicting its behavior accordingly.
Dennett contrasts this with the *physical stance* (predicting from physical laws) and the *design stance* (predicting from how it was designed to work).

## The three stances

Dennett identifies three predictive stances available for any system:

- **Physical stance.** Predict by treating as a physical system governed by physical laws.
  Fully general but often intractable.
- **Design stance.** Predict by treating as a designed artifact and reasoning from its specification.
  Useful when design is known and the system functions as designed.
- **Intentional stance.** Predict by treating as a rational agent with beliefs (about its environment) and desires (about outcomes), and inferring what such an agent would do.

Each stance is a predictive *strategy*; which is most useful depends on the system and the prediction context.

## What "intentional" means

In Dennett's usage, *intentional* refers to *aboutness* — mental states are *about* things in the world.
Beliefs are about how the world is; desires are about how it should be.
The intentional stance treats systems as having such *intentional* (= about-ness-bearing) states.

This is broader than the colloquial sense of "intentional" as "deliberate."

## What it asserts

Dennett's claim is *predictive*, not *metaphysical*:

- The intentional stance is *useful* for predicting certain systems' behavior.
- It does *not* claim such systems "really have" beliefs and desires in some deep metaphysical sense.
- A system is an *intentional system* (per Dennett) if the intentional stance is predictively useful for it.
- Intentional-system status is therefore a matter of prediction-utility, not metaphysics.

This pragmatic framing dissolves debates about whether thermostats, plants, animals, or AI "really have" mental states: the question is reframed as whether the intentional stance predicts their behavior usefully.

## Implications for AI

The intentional stance has direct implications for AI:

- **AI agents may be intentional systems** in Dennett's sense — the intentional stance may predict their behavior usefully — without having "real" beliefs or desires in any deeper sense.
- **The "do AI systems have ToM" question** is partly defused: AI systems may *act as if* they have ToM in ways that the intentional stance captures, regardless of whether they "really" have ToM.
- **Intentional-stance prediction of AI** may be more reliable than mechanistic prediction for complex systems whose internals are opaque.

## Limits

- **Predictive failure.** The intentional stance can fail when systems behave non-rationally; need to retreat to design or physical stance.
- **Anthropomorphism risk.** Intentional-stance prediction can over-attribute, leading to systematic errors.
- **Doesn't dissolve all questions.** Whether a system is *conscious* or has *moral status* is not the same question as whether the intentional stance is useful for it; Dennett's framework defuses some questions but leaves others.

## Status

Influential.
Dennett's intentional-stance framework underpins much of philosophy of mind and AI alignment discourse around mental-state attribution.
The pragmatic framing has aged well as AI systems exhibit behavior amenable to intentional-stance prediction without clear metaphysical answers about "real" mentality.
