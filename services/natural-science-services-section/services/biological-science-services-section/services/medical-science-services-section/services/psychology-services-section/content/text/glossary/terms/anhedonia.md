Anhedonia is reduced capacity to experience pleasure from a rewarding stimulus once obtained — a deficit in consummatory, hedonic response, not in the drive to pursue reward in the first place. The distinction the effort-based decision-making literature turns on is between anhedonia and motivational anergia: reduced willingness or capacity to mobilize effort toward reward, independent of how pleasurable the reward is once obtained.

Salamone, Cousins, and Bucher (1994) posed the distinction directly, in a paper titled "Anhedonia or Anergia?": in a T-maze cost/benefit procedure, disrupting nucleus accumbens dopamine transmission in rats (via haloperidol or 6-hydroxydopamine lesion) shifted choice away from a high-reward, high-effort arm toward a low-reward, low-effort arm, while the same animals continued to consume the high-value food normally once effort was removed from the choice — dopamine loss reallocated effort, it did not blunt the reward's value. A companion design (Salamone et al., 1991) found the same dissociation from the other direction: accumbens dopamine depletion and haloperidol suppressed lever-pressing for a preferred food while increasing free consumption of a less-preferred one, with total food intake and preference-when-free both intact. Treadway and Zald (2011) generalized the argument to human depression research: the field's "underspecified definition of anhedonia has failed to dissociate between consummatory and motivational aspects of reward behavior," with substantial preclinical evidence implicating dopamine "primarily in motivational aspects of reward" rather than consummatory pleasure.

The consequence for clinical measurement is that a self-report anhedonia scale, which typically asks about anticipated or experienced pleasure, does not distinguish these two deficits, and a behavioral finding of reduced effort for reward should not automatically be read as evidence of reduced hedonic capacity. The Effort Expenditure for Rewards Task (EEfRT; Treadway, Buckholtz, Schwartzman, Lambert, & Zald, 2009) was built specifically to separate the two: in a nonclinical sample of 61 undergraduates, trait anhedonia scores predicted reduced willingness to choose a harder task for a larger reward, giving the effort measure construct validity against self-reported anhedonia while still targeting a motivational, not consummatory, construct. Husain and Roiser (2018) find shared mechanisms across apathy/anergia and anhedonia within a transdiagnostic effort-based framework without collapsing the two into one construct — they are correlated and co-occurring across many patients, not mechanistically identical, and the later finding that anhedonia severity independently moderates effort-based choice (see effort-based decision-making) is itself evidence the two dimensions are separable enough to interact rather than being one thing measured twice.
