Frustrative nonreward is the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) construct, under the Negative Valence Systems domain, defined by NIMH as "reactions elicited in response to withdrawal/prevention of reward, i.e., by the inability to obtain positive rewards following repeated or sustained efforts." It names one of five Negative Valence Systems constructs alongside Acute Threat, Potential Threat, Sustained Threat, and Loss.

The RDoC label draws its name from an older, operationally narrower construct in animal-learning theory: Amsel's (1958) frustrative non-reward, in which unexpected omission of an expected reward does not simply extinguish behavior but transiently energizes the next response in a learned chain. This "frustration effect" was first demonstrated with rats in a double-runway apparatus, where reward omitted at the first goal box produced faster running through the second alley than on rewarded trials (Amsel & Roussel, 1952), and was elaborated by Amsel into frustration as a conditionable internal state that can itself invigorate or suppress subsequent behavior, explaining phenomena such as greater resistance to extinction after partial than continuous reinforcement. The RDoC construct broadens this: it is a research-organizing framework spanning candidate molecules (dopamine, GABA, glutamate, serotonin, steroids, vasopressin), circuits (amygdala, hypothalamus, orbitofrontal cortex, striatum, among others), and behavioral or self-report units of analysis, explicitly not a diagnostic system and not a claim that any one of the listed circuits is a proven causal substrate in humans.

Human laboratory paradigms operationalize the construct behaviorally and with neuroimaging. The Affective Posner (frustration) task induces state frustration via rigged, non-contingent negative feedback rather than an actually unsolvable task; using it, Deveney and colleagues (2013) found chronically irritable children showed a group-by-feedback interaction with reduced amygdala and bilateral striatal activation to negative feedback relative to healthy comparison children — a direction the authors themselves flag as surprising, since it runs opposite to the adult reactive-aggression literature's amygdala-hyperactivation pattern (see reactive-aggression). For research design, the construct's own molecule and circuit lists are a catalog of research candidates, not a set of established findings, and a study result attributed to the construct — such as the counterintuitive reduced, not exaggerated, amygdala and striatal activation to rigged negative feedback found in chronically irritable children by Deveney et al. (2013, N = 42: 19 with severe mood dysregulation, 23 healthy comparison children) — must be read as evidence about that specific paradigm and population, not generalized to the construct's whole candidate circuitry.
