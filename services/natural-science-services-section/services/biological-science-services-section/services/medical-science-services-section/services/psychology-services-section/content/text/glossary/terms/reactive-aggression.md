Reactive aggression is aggression triggered by perceived threat, frustration, or provocation — an angry, retaliatory response to a proximate trigger — distinguished in the theoretical literature from proactive (instrumental) aggression, which is planned and reward-directed rather than triggered by an immediate provocation. It is modeled as arising from an amygdala–hypothalamus–periaqueductal-gray threat circuit, modulated (or insufficiently modulated) by attentional and prefrontal regulation circuits — an integrative theoretical account, referred to in this literature as the Blair model, synthesizing multiple imaging and lesion findings rather than a single confirmed causal pathway demonstrated end-to-end in humans; this dossier did not verify a specific Blair primary source directly, and the attribution should be checked before further use.

The clearest causal evidence for a specific node in this circuitry is rodent-only. Lin, Boyle, Dollar, Lee, Lein, Perona, and Anderson (2011) found that optogenetic stimulation of a subpopulation of neurons in the ventrolateral ventromedial hypothalamus (VMHvl) of male mice is sufficient to trigger attack on females, males, and inanimate objects, while pharmacogenetic silencing of the same region reversibly inhibits inter-male aggression — a bidirectional, activate-to-cause and silence-to-block, design that licenses a genuinely causal claim about this hypothalamic node in mice. No equivalent causal manipulation exists, or could ethically exist, in humans; any extension of the VMHvl findings to a human "aggression circuit" claim is extrapolation, not evidence.

Human evidence is correlational, cross-sectional imaging. Coccaro, McCloskey, Fitzgerald, and Phan (2007) found exaggerated amygdala reactivity to angry faces and diminished amygdala–orbitofrontal functional connectivity in 10 unmedicated adults with intermittent explosive disorder relative to 10 healthy matched comparison subjects, consistent with a model of impulsive aggression as amygdala hyperreactivity under insufficient prefrontal regulation. This runs in the opposite direction from a separate, developmental literature on frustrative nonreward (see frustrative-nonreward): Deveney and colleagues (2013), studying 42 children (19 with severe mood dysregulation, 23 healthy comparison) under a rigged-feedback frustration task, found chronically irritable children showed reduced, not exaggerated, amygdala and striatal activation to frustrating negative feedback. The two paradigms probe different things — social-threat faces in adults with a reactive-aggression diagnosis, versus non-social frustrating feedback in irritable children — and this directionality conflict is real and unresolved in the literature; it should not be papered over with one unified "hyperactive amygdala" story for blocked-goal-to-rage.

The consequence for research design is to keep the two evidentiary channels separate rather than substituting one for the other: a causal claim about a specific circuit node, as in the rodent VMHvl work, cannot be read onto a human correlational group-difference finding, as in the IED amygdala work, and a finding in one population or paradigm should not be assumed to generalize, in direction or magnitude, to a different population or paradigm without directly testing it.
