# Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith 1985 — Sally-Anne

Baron-Cohen, S., Leslie, A.M., & Frith, U.
(1985).
Does the autistic child have a 'theory of mind'? *Cognition*, 21(1), 37-46.

The paper introducing the Sally-Anne task — a doll-based variant of the Wimmer-Perner false-belief task — and reporting that children with autism systematically fail the task even when matched on general cognitive ability.
The paper interpreted this as evidence of an *autism-specific theory-of-mind deficit* — the *mindblindness* hypothesis.

The finding was deeply influential.
It established:

- ToM as a distinct cognitive capacity, separable from general intelligence.
- A specific cognitive profile in autism related to mental-state attribution.
- The methodological power of false-belief tasks for differential diagnosis.

Subsequent work has substantially complicated the mindblindness interpretation: ToM differences in autism appear quantitative and contextual rather than categorical, and many individuals with autism pass standard false-belief tasks (especially older children and adults).
But the foundational finding — that the Sally-Anne task differentiated autistic from typically- developing matched samples — remains influential.

The Sally-Anne paradigm has become the canonical visual variant of the false-belief task and is widely used in developmental research and (in adapted form) in machine ToM evaluation.
