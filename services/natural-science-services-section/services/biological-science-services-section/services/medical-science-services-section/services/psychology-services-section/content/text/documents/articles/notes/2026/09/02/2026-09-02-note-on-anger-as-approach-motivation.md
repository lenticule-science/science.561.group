## What this argues

Anger is a negatively valenced state that behaves, across several independent
lines of evidence, like an approach-motivated state rather than a
withdrawal-motivated one: it associates with relative left frontal cortical
activity (the classic marker of approach, not avoidance), with appraisals of
high certainty and personal control rather than fear's uncertainty and low
control, and — in its blocked-goal form — with a transient energizing of
behavior rather than its suppression. Each of these lines of evidence is
real and published, and each also carries a live, named problem: the
frontal-asymmetry marker has since failed a large multi-lab reanalysis of
its general validity; the appraisal-control evidence is a study of risk
judgment, not of mood or activity level; and the clinical anger-attack and
anger-suppression literatures, which most directly co-occur with depression,
run in the causal direction opposite to "anger relieves depression" — anger
attacks are described by their own investigators as pathology that remits
alongside depression under treatment, not as a mechanism that produces
relief. The specific question this note was commissioned to answer plainly
— whether an angry state transiently reduces depressive symptoms or
restores activity in a depressed population — has, as far as this search
found, no direct test in the literature. The closest available evidence
(anger induction reducing self-reported sadness and rumination) comes from a
nonclinical, non-depressed sample of high ruminators, not from anyone
diagnosed with depression, and should not be extended past that population
without saying so.

## Approach motivation as a third axis, and its disputed marker

This section's ruling on [affect](../../../../../../glossary/terms/affect.md)
fixes valence and arousal as the two dimensions locating any felt state.
[Approach motivation](../../../../../../glossary/terms/approach-motivation.md)
is ruled here as a third, orthogonal axis — motivational direction,
independent of valence — and anger is the paradigm case establishing that
the two are not the same construct: a negatively valenced state that
behaves like an approach-motivated one. That term already carries the
primary evidence in full: Harmon-Jones and Allen's (1998) trait-anger
correlation with relative left-frontal EEG alpha asymmetry, Harmon-Jones and
Sigelman's (2001) state replication tying the same asymmetry to insult-induced
anger and aggression, Harmon-Jones's own (2003) narrowing of the claim to
cases where the anger-producing situation is perceived as actionable, and —
carrying the criticism with the same weight as the founding claims, per this
corpus's own discipline — the two independent lines that now put the general
construct in serious doubt: van der Vinne and colleagues' (2017) meta-analysis
of 16 studies (pooled major depressive disorder n = 1,883, controls n = 2,161)
finding a non-significant, negligible effect size for frontal asymmetry as a
depression marker, and Paul and colleagues' (2025) large cooperative
multi-lab reanalysis (N = 740, many independent analysis teams each choosing
a defensible pipeline) concluding the data "could not support the validity of
frontal asymmetry as a marker of approach motivation, neither as a reliable
state nor as a trait marker." Neither critique names the anger studies by
title; both attack the general measurement construct the anger findings sit
inside. That term should be read before this one for the physiological
evidence in full; it is not restated here.

What that term does not yet carry is a complication internal to
Harmon-Jones's own research program, from the same 2003 *Psychophysiology*
review (p. 845): the author reports developing, in unpublished work, a
"withdrawal-oriented trait anger" measure that behaves oppositely to typical
anger measures — it "relates positively to trait BIS (r = .26, p < .01) and
negatively to trait BAS (r = -.21, p < .01)... These findings stand in
contrast to typical measures of trait anger, which relate positively to BAS
and not to BIS." Read plainly, this means the author's own model does not
claim anger is approach-motivated as a matter of definition; it claims that
the anger measures in common use happen to index an approach-oriented
disposition, while a differently constructed anger measure indexes a
withdrawal-oriented one. "Anger is approach-motivated" is, on the evidence
the same research program produced, a claim about how anger is usually
operationalized, not a fixed property of the emotion itself.

## Anger, perceived control, and the appraisal-tendency framework

A second, independent line of evidence for anger's approach character comes
from appraisal research rather than physiology. Lerner and Keltner (2001)
worked from the appraisal-tendency framework — the claim that each discrete
emotion is bound to a specific cognitive appraisal theme, not just a valence,
and that this appraisal persists and colors subsequent, unrelated judgments.
For fear and anger specifically, they report that "fearful people expressed
pessimistic risk estimates and risk-averse choices, whereas angry people
expressed optimistic risk estimates and risk-seeking choices," across both
naturally occurring and experimentally induced instances of both emotions,
and that appraisals of certainty and control moderated, and in the case of
control mediated, these effects. The theoretical grounding is that fear's
core appraisal theme is uncertainty and low control, while anger's is high
certainty and high perceived control — anger, on this framework, carries an
appraisal of individual control over the situation that fear's "unknown
risk" appraisal does not.

What this licenses is narrow and should be stated as narrowly as the study
itself supports: induced or trait anger is associated with appraisals of
higher perceived control and certainty than fear is, and this appraisal
carries over into unrelated risk judgments and choices. It is a study of
risk judgment and choice — a downstream cognitive and behavioral outcome —
not a study of mood state, depression, or activity level, and it should not
be read as licensing any claim about anger altering depressive symptoms or
arousal directly. This note could not independently pull exact per-study
sample sizes and statistics from the paper's own results sections on this
pass — the qualitative appraisal-framework claims above come from the
paper's abstract-level material, not from independently re-derived numbers,
and should be weighted accordingly pending a fuller read of the primary text.

## Frustrative nonreward: blocked reward becomes mobilized, not suppressed, behavior

[Frustrative nonreward](../../../../../../glossary/terms/frustrative-nonreward.md)
already carries the founding mechanism in full: Amsel's demonstration that
unexpected omission of an expected reward does not simply extinguish
behavior but transiently energizes the next response in a learned chain,
elaborated into a conditionable internal state, and later broadened into the
RDoC Negative Valence Systems construct — "reactions elicited in response to
withdrawal/prevention of reward" — that names, without itself validating,
candidate molecules and circuits for a mechanism connecting blocked goals to
an aroused, motivated state in humans. That construct is this note's
clearest evidence that blocked reward does not, by default, produce a
withdrawal or shutdown response; the direction of the founding animal
finding is energization, and the human research-organizing construct built
on it is explicitly a negative-valence, not a withdrawal-valence, category.

The bridge from that founding animal construct to human anger specifically
is the RDoC framework's own candidate status, not an established finding: the
construct page lists candidate circuits (amygdala, hypothalamus,
orbitofrontal cortex, striatum) and molecules without citing the primary
studies that would validate any one of them, and the one human paradigm this
corpus has verified in detail under this construct — Deveney and colleagues'
(2013) rigged-feedback frustration task in chronically irritable
children — found *reduced*, not exaggerated, amygdala and striatal
activation to frustrating feedback, a direction the authors themselves flag
as surprising because it runs opposite to the adult
[reactive-aggression](../../../../../../glossary/terms/reactive-aggression.md)
literature's amygdala-hyperactivation pattern. That directionality conflict —
pediatric frustration producing reduced amygdala response, adult reactive
aggression (intermittent explosive disorder) producing exaggerated amygdala
response to social threat — is real and unresolved, and this note does not
paper over it with one unified "blocked goal activates the amygdala"
story. What the construct licenses cleanly is the higher-level claim this
note needs — blocked reward energizes rather than shuts down subsequent
behavior — not a specific, validated neural pathway from blocked reward to
angry mobilization in adult humans.

The mobilization framing connects forward to two already-ruled distinctions
in this glossary that should not be conflated with it.
[Anhedonia](../../../../../../glossary/terms/anhedonia.md) is a reduced
capacity to experience pleasure from a reward once obtained; motivational
anergia, covered by
[effort-based decision-making](../../../../../../glossary/terms/effort-based-decision-making.md),
is a reduced willingness to mobilize effort toward reward in the first
place — a separable computation, causally tied in rodents to mesolimbic
dopamine. Frustrative nonreward names a third thing again: not a reduced
capacity for pleasure, and not a reduced willingness to expend effort, but
an actively energizing reaction to reward's withdrawal. A state that moves
from low mobilization toward reward (anergia) into an energized, angry
response to that same reward's withdrawal would be moving between two
different points on the effort-allocation axis, not between anhedonia and
its opposite — but no source reviewed for this note traces that specific
transition empirically; it is offered here as the conceptual seam between
three already-separated constructs, not as a demonstrated mechanism.

## The clinical anger literature in depression, and its causal direction

Two bodies of clinical literature associate anger, in different senses, with
depression, and both run in a direction opposite to "anger relieves
depression."

The first is
[anger attacks](../../../../../../../../../psychiatry-services-section/services/irritability-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/anger-attack.md),
already ruled in this corpus: sudden, autonomically marked spells of anger
occurring in a substantial minority of depressed outpatients (44% of 127
patients in Fava and colleagues' 1993 report), which resolved in 71% of
affected patients after eight weeks of open-label fluoxetine in the same
report. The ruled term is explicit that this is an uncontrolled, open-label
case series from a single research program with no placebo arm, so the
reported resolution cannot be separated from a general antidepressant
response, a placebo response, or regression to the mean. What matters for
this note is the causal framing the investigators themselves give it: anger
attacks are presented as a co-occurring symptom of the depressive episode
that remits together with the episode under treatment, not as a mechanism
that produces relief from depression. The direction implied is the reverse
of the phenomenon this note was commissioned to evaluate.

The second is anger-expression style as a stable trait or coping pattern,
studied with Spielberger's anger-in/anger-out/anger-control framework. This
literature, as located by search for this note, reports internalized anger
(anger-in) and low anger-control — not anger-out — as the components most
consistently associated with depressive symptom severity across nonclinical
and clinical samples, with anger-out showing a weaker, more sex-moderated
association; a body of work on anger suppression specifically (including a
study in women newly diagnosed with breast cancer reporting anger
suppression significantly predicting depressive symptom scores) reports the
same pattern from a different angle. This is reported here as a
characterization of a body of findings located by search, not as a set of
independently verified primary citations with author names and statistics —
no single paper name, year, or number could be pinned down for the general
anger-in/anger-control claim on this pass, and it is stated with that
limitation attached rather than dressed up with a citation it does not have.
What the pattern says, at the level it can be said: chronic *suppression* of
anger, not its outward expression, is what tracks with more depression over
time — a claim about anger expression style as a risk factor accumulating
across time, not about an acute state-switch from a low-arousal depressive
state into an angry, mobilized one. The anger-in/anger-out literature and
the question this note was commissioned to answer are, in short, about two
different timescales and two different phenomena; neither should be read as
evidence for the other.

## Does an angry state transiently reduce depressive symptoms or restore activity?

This is the specific question the commission asked to be answered honestly,
including if the direct evidence is thin or absent. It searched for
studies inducing or measuring a transition from a low-arousal, depressed, or
anhedonic state into anger, with depressive symptom severity or activity
level re-measured afterward. None was found.

The closest available evidence is Zhan and colleagues' (2017) test of a
"mutual promotion and mutual counteraction" theory of emotion regulation by
anger, in two studies of college students selected for high trait
rumination — not diagnosed depression. In Study 1 (90 students, top 20% of
scorers on the Ruminative Response Scale, randomly assigned to anger, joy,
or neutral mood induction after a rumination induction), the anger group
showed significantly lower self-reported sadness than both the joy group
(p < .05) and the neutral group (p < .001) after mood induction, with the
same pattern for tension (anger lower than neutral, p < .001, and joy,
p < .05); ruminative thought frequency itself did not differ between groups
(F(2,87) < 1, p > .05, η² = .044), a limitation the authors state directly.
In Study 2 (40 female students, top 10% on the same rumination scale, given
anger or neutral interventions across four consecutive days), the anger
group showed significantly lower trait rumination than the neutral group
after the fourth intervention (p < .05). The authors' own conclusion, quoted
directly: "Anger induction efficiently relieved sad and tense feelings
associated with state rumination," and was "more efficient than positive
reward" at doing so.

The authors' own stated limitations are the reason this cannot be read as
evidence for the commissioned question. Participants "were not depressed.
They were only individuals with relatively high ruminative tendencies in the
general population" — a nonclinical sample; Study 2 was all-female, so its
conclusions "cannot be merely extended to males"; the design compared anger
only to a neutral mood, not to other regulation strategies; and the
measurement throughout was self-reported sadness, tension, and rumination,
not clinically defined depressive symptom severity and not behavioral
activity or motor output — the authors themselves call for "the regulatory
effects through a physiological perspective" as future work still to be
done. Read plainly: this shows anger induction reducing self-reported
sadness, tension, and trait rumination, relative to neutral or joyful mood
induction, in a nonclinical, non-depressed population of high ruminators. It
does not show anger reduces clinically defined depressive symptoms, does not
use a depressed sample, and does not measure activity or energy level at
all.

Set beside the anger-attack literature above, the honest state of the
evidence is this: the one body of research that does study anger
co-occurring with actual clinical depression (anger attacks) frames anger as
pathology that resolves alongside depression under treatment — the reverse
causal direction from "anger relieves depression" — while the one body of
research that does test whether inducing anger relieves a low, ruminative
mood state does so in a population that was never depressed to begin with,
and measures mood and thought content, not depressive illness or restored
activity. Nothing located for this note tests the specific transition the
commission asked about, in the population it asked about, on the outcomes it
asked about. That is reported here as a null search result, not as proof the
mechanism does not exist — a targeted search combining anger induction,
depressive symptoms, mood repair, and depression returned only the two
bodies of evidence above, in opposite directions from what the question
asked and from a different population than the one asked about.
