# False-belief developmental bound

**Statement (empirical).** Children reliably pass classical first-order false-belief tasks around age 4 (typically between 3.5 and 5 years).
Younger children typically fail; older children typically pass.
Higher- order false-belief tasks emerge correspondingly later.

## The empirical pattern

Across many studies and cultures:

- **Children under 3** typically fail the standard false-belief task — predicting the actor will look where the object actually is, not where the actor believes it is.
- **Children around 4** show a transition; success rate climbs from low to high.
- **Children 5+** typically pass.
- **Higher-order ("she thinks he thinks") tasks** emerge around age 6-7.

The transition around age 4 is robust and cross-cultural.

## Variations

- **Implicit measures.** Looking-time and anticipatory- looking measures suggest younger infants (~15 months) may have implicit false-belief understanding (Onishi & Baillargeon 2005).
  Interpretation contested: genuine implicit ToM or low-level association?
- **Task simplifications.** Simplified false-belief tasks (with reduced narrative complexity) are passed by some 3-year-olds.
  The age threshold is task- sensitive.
- **Autism spectrum.** Children with autism systematically struggle with classical false-belief tasks even at older ages (Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith 1985).
  This was originally taken as evidence of ToM-specific deficit; subsequent work shows the picture is more complex.
- **Cross-cultural variation.** Age threshold varies modestly across cultures but the developmental pattern is robust.

## What the bound suggests

Several interpretations:

- **Maturational.** ToM relies on maturation of specific cognitive capacities (e.g., executive function, language) that develop around age 4.
- **Conceptual.** Children acquire concepts (belief as representational state) around age 4 enabling false- belief reasoning.
- **Theory-theory.** Children develop a *theory* of mind around age 4, becoming "little scientists."
- **Simulation theory.** Children develop *simulation* capacities around age 4 enabling perspective-taking.
- **Communicative.** Verbal false-belief tasks confound ToM with language understanding; the age bound partly reflects language development.

The interpretations are partially compatible.

## Implications for machine ToM

The developmental pattern bears on machine ToM in several ways:

- **What "ToM" requires.** If ToM requires specific developmental milestones, what's the analog for AI?
- **What "real" ToM looks like.** Children's pre-passing failures reveal what behavioral patterns ToM-lacking systems produce; comparison to LLM failure patterns is informative.
- **Robust ToM evaluation.** AI ToM evaluation should test the *transition* — what kinds of perturbations cause failure — not just final-task accuracy.
- **Higher-order ToM as a target.** If contemporary AI is at "first-order ToM," higher-order ToM is the next benchmark.

## Limits

- **Empirical, not theoretical.** The bound is empirically observed, not derived.
  There's no *principle* setting age 4; it could be different in other cognitive systems.
- **Task-sensitive.** Different tasks reveal slightly different age thresholds.
- **Aggregate statistic.** Individual variation is substantial.
- **AI is not children.** The developmental analogy may mislead; AI systems develop along different trajectories than children.

## Status

Well-established in developmental psychology.
Application to machine ToM provides useful framing ("what would 'developing' AI ToM look like?") but mechanical analogies should be treated with care.
