Approach motivation is the directional aspect of a motivated state — whether behavior is organized toward engaging and obtaining a goal (approach) or toward disengaging and avoiding it (withdrawal) — treated in this literature as a dimension that can vary independently of an affective state's valence. This section's ruling on affect fixes valence (pleasant to unpleasant) and arousal (calm to activated) as the two dimensions locating a felt state; approach motivation names a third, orthogonal axis: motivational direction. The paradigm case establishing the two are not the same construct is anger, a negatively valenced state that behaves, empirically, like an approach-motivated one rather than a withdrawal-motivated one.

The primary physiological evidence for this claim is relative left-frontal cortical activity. Harmon-Jones and Allen (1998) correlated resting EEG frontal alpha-power asymmetry with trait anger in an unselected undergraduate sample and found dispositional anger associated with greater left- than right-anterior activity, concluding that "anterior asymmetry varies as a function of motivational direction rather than affective valence." Harmon-Jones and Sigelman (2001) extended this to a state manipulation: participants insulted by a confederate showed greater relative left-frontal activity than neutrally treated participants, and this activation was itself associated, by regression, with more self-reported anger and more aggressive behavior toward the confederate. Harmon-Jones's own later review (2003) narrowed the claim considerably: left-frontal activation appeared specifically when participants believed they could act on their anger, and did not appear when the anger-producing situation was framed as one they could do nothing about — anger did not raise relative left-frontal activity unconditionally, by the author's own account.

Independent of the research group that produced these findings, the general validity of frontal alpha asymmetry as an approach-motivation marker is now seriously disputed. A meta-analysis of 16 studies (van der Vinne et al., 2017; pooled major depressive disorder n = 1,883, controls n = 2,161) found a non-significant, negligible effect size for frontal asymmetry as a depression marker. More directly on point, a large cooperative multi-lab reanalysis (Paul et al., 2025; N = 740, spanning resting, picture-viewing, and guessing-task paradigms, with many independent teams each choosing a defensible analysis pipeline) reported that "frontal asymmetry was not reliably affected by task manipulations and did not relate to self-reported traits," concluding the data "could not support the validity of frontal asymmetry as a marker of approach motivation, neither as a reliable state nor as a trait marker." This is a direct, large-sample, multi-team non-replication of the general construct that the anger-EEG findings sit inside, though it does not name the anger studies specifically.

Approach motivation is also operationalized as a self-report trait construct, independent of any EEG measure: the Behavioral Activation/Approach System (BAS) scales (Carver & White, 1994) ask directly about reward sensitivity and drive. Anger correlates positively with self-reported BAS in the same research tradition that produced the EEG claims, but this is a different measurement channel with its own, separately documented validity history — the EEG-asymmetry claim and the self-report BAS claim should not be merged into one effect size or read as replicating one another.

The consequence for research design and clinical observation is direct: resting or task-related frontal EEG asymmetry should not, on its own, be read as evidence of an approach-motivated state, and should especially not be used as a diagnostic or trait marker in depression, where the pooled meta-analytic effect is negligible. A finding of relative left-frontal activity accompanying a negative-valence state is consistent with the approach-motivation hypothesis but is not, given the 2025 multi-lab non-replication, sufficient evidence for it on its own; convergent behavioral or self-report evidence should be sought before the construct is asserted from an asymmetry measure alone.
