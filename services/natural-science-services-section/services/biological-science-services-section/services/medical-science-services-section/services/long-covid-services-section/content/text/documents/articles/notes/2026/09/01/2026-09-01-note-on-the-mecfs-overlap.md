## What this argues

A substantial minority to majority of long COVID patients meet clinical diagnostic criteria for myalgic encephalomyelitis (ME/CFS), with published estimates clustering around 43–58% in criteria-based studies. This overlap is studied as a boundary question: the phenotypic convergence licenses treating the two literatures as mechanistically relevant to each other, but it does not license claiming they share an identical pathophysiology or extending ME/CFS-specific findings to the broader long COVID population that does not meet ME/CFS criteria.

## The overlap: what fraction meets ME/CFS criteria

There is no single agreed number. Published criteria-based estimates range from 43% to 58%, and that range is not experimental error — it reflects genuine variation in cohort selection, case-definition instruments, and pandemic phase at infection. The best current synthesis is a 2024 meta-analysis pooling 13 clinical studies: **51% of long COVID patients (95% CI 42–60%) met ME/CFS diagnostic criteria** across the included cohorts. That figure is the current headline estimate and should be read as "about half."

The variation around it is methodologically instructive. Five single-study estimates anchor this:

1. **Kedor et al. (2022)**, prospective cohort, n=42 long-COVID patients with moderate-to-severe fatigue/exertion intolerance at 6 months: 45% (19/42) fulfilled Canadian Consensus Criteria for ME/CFS, matched against a non-COVID ME/CFS cohort on severity and symptom burden — single cohort not yet independently replicated (Kedor C, et al. Prospective observational study of post-COVID-19 chronic fatigue syndrome. *Nature Communications*. 2022;13:5104).

2. **Bonilla et al. (2023)**, retrospective clinic cohort, Stanford PASC clinic, n=134 of 140 referred, with 105 having persistent symptoms >6 months: 43% (45/105) of that subgroup met IOM 2015 criteria for ME/CFS, with obesity and worse functional status significantly associated with meeting criteria — clinic referral bias likely over-represents more severe PASC (Bonilla H, et al. Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome is common in post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection. *Frontiers in Neurology*. 2023;14:1090747).

3. **Jason & Dorri (2023)**, survey study, n=465 long COVID patients, DePaul Symptom Questionnaire-based ME/CFS case definition: 58% met criteria — notably, only 71% of those who self-reported ME/CFS actually met criteria, while 40% who did not self-report ME/CFS nevertheless did, indicating self-label is an unreliable proxy for formal diagnosis in this population (Jason LA, Dorri JA. ME/CFS and post-exertional malaise among patients with long COVID. *Neurology International*. 2023;15(1):1-11).

4. **Dehlia & Guthridge (2024)**, meta-analysis, 13 studies (2020–May 2023), n=1,973 pooled: 51% (95% CI 42–60%) met ME/CFS criteria — most common symptoms in the ME/CFS-meeting subgroup were fatigue, cognitive impairment, dyspnea, and post-exertional malaise, consistent across included studies; this is the methodologically strongest single estimate and anchors the "about half" framing (Dehlia A, Guthridge MA. The persistence of myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome after SARS-CoV-2 infection. *Journal of Infection*. 2024;89(6):106297).

5. **Morita et al. (2024)**, retrospective cohort, Okayama University Hospital, n=739, Japan, Feb 2021–Jul 2023: 8.4% (62/739) met ME/CFS criteria — but with a critical caveat: this cohort required *simultaneous fulfillment of three case definitions* (Fukuda 1994, Canadian Consensus Criteria, and IOM 2015) rather than any one of them — a triple bar that moved the estimate downward by roughly 6-fold compared to single-definition studies, and showed a strong phase-dependent gradient by pandemic variant at infection (23.9% pre-Delta, 13.7% Delta era, 3.3% Omicron era) (Morita S, et al. Phase-dependent trends in the prevalence of myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome. *PLoS ONE*. 2024;19(12):e0315385).

The Morita study is the keystone observation on case-definition sensitivity: the same cohort, the same patients, the same date range, but case-definition stringency alone moved the reported ME/CFS-meeting fraction from single-digit to mid-range percentages. This directly mirrors — and now quantifies in the long COVID literature — the case-definition heterogeneity problem [already documented in the ME/CFS literature](../../../../../../../../../myalgic-encephalomyelitis-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/01/2026-09-01-note-on-decoupling-as-the-shape-of-post-exertional-malaise.md).

## Post-exertional malaise as the shared cardinal feature

Post-exertional malaise — the disproportionate and delayed worsening of symptoms following exertion — is the cardinal distinguishing feature of ME/CFS (See [the PEM glossary term](../../../../../../../../../myalgic-encephalomyelitis-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/post-exertional-malaise.md)). A 2024 meta-analysis of PEM in long COVID, pooling 12 studies across 2,665 participants, found **55% of people with PASC reported post-exertional malaise** at the three-month mark (An Y, et al. Prevalence and measurement of post-exertional malaise in post-acute COVID-19 syndrome. *General Hospital Psychiatry*. 2024;91:130-142).

This ~55% PEM-prevalence figure in PASC, converging with the ~51% ME/CFS-criteria figure from separate criteria-based studies, is suggestive but not confirmatory: the two measurements are independent and operate on different criteria sets, so their overlap reflects genuine symptom convergence rather than mathematical redundancy. However, it is worth noting that PEM is only one of several criteria items in formal ME/CFS definitions; the fact that these two independently-derived ~50-55% figures bracket each other warrants treating them as pointing to a consistent subgroup.

## What the overlap licenses

**1. That a syndromic phenotype converges:** A large, clinically and statistically significant share of long COVID cases express the same cardinal symptom (PEM) and overlapping secondary symptom profile (fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, exercise intolerance, sleep disturbance, dysautonomia) that define ME/CFS as a syndrome. This licenses:

- Testing management and pacing frameworks developed in ME/CFS on the long-COVID-with-PEM subgroup.
- Using instruments designed to measure ME/CFS pathophysiology (two-day cardiopulmonary exercise testing, the DePaul Symptom Questionnaire) in long COVID cohorts to stratify the subset meeting ME/CFS criteria.
- Reading findings from ME/CFS mechanistic studies as evidententially relevant hypotheses to test in the long-COVID-with-PEM subgroup, and vice versa.

**2. That SARS-CoV-2 is a documented trigger for new-onset ME/CFS:** A 2025 retrospective cohort study from Montefiore Health System, following 36,076 COVID-positive vs. 111,301 COVID-negative controls without prior fatigue/CFS diagnosis, found an elevated risk of new-onset CFS/ME in the COVID-positive cohort (adjusted hazard ratio non-hospitalized COVID-19 vs. controls = 1.56 [95% CI 1.25–1.93]), with the increased risk holding regardless of acute-phase severity and showing no further elevation with reinfection (Hadidchi R, et al. Elevated risk of new-onset chronic fatigue syndrome/myalgic encephalomyelitis up to four years after SARS-CoV-2 infection. *Journal of Translational Medicine*. 2025;23:815). This is the strongest available causal-inference-adjacent evidence (large linked-EHR cohort with matched controls) that SARS-CoV-2 infection elevates ME/CFS incidence at the population level, not merely that long COVID patients happen to resemble ME/CFS patients cross-sectionally.

## What the overlap does NOT license

**1. That the mechanism is identical:** Meeting the same symptom-based case definition is compatible with multiple distinct underlying pathophysiologies converging on one clinical phenotype. The [decoupling note in the ME/CFS section](../../../../../../../../../myalgic-encephalomyelitis-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/01/2026-09-01-note-on-decoupling-as-the-shape-of-post-exertional-malaise.md) proposes that ME/CFS's cardinal features arise from a decoupling between the fast sympathoadrenal arm and the slow HPA arm of the stress response. That is an ME/CFS-specific mechanistic hypothesis, built from ME/CFS cohorts and evidence. Long COVID studies report candidate shared mechanisms — T-cell exhaustion and NK-cell dysfunction, mitochondrial dysfunction, endothelial dysfunction, neuroinflammation — but every item on that list is a *hypothesis* about shared pathways, not a demonstrated causal chain showing that long COVID's post-exertional malaise arises through the same neuroendocrine decoupling that the ME/CFS section argues for (Davis HE, et al. Long COVID: major findings, mechanisms and recommendations. *Nature Reviews Microbiology*. 2023;21:133-146). Mechanistic convergence is a live research question, not an established fact.

**2. That the overlap is SARS-CoV-2-specific:** This is the critical boundary statement. The post-viral ME/CFS-like illness literature predates SARS-CoV-2 by decades. A 2009 cohort study of severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS-CoV-1) survivors, followed for four years post-infection, found 40.3% had unexplained chronic fatigue and 27.1% met formal CFS diagnostic criteria at four years (Lam MHB, et al. Mental morbidities and chronic fatigue in severe acute respiratory syndrome survivors. *Archives of Internal Medicine*. 2009;169(22):2142-2147). The SARS-CoV-1 figure is measurably similar to the long-COVID-meets-ME/CFS fractions documented above. This indicates that SARS-CoV-2 is one more entry in a long list of viral triggers for the ME/CFS phenotype, not a mechanism-unique feature of the coronavirus itself. The overlap licenses the inference "viral infection can trigger the ME/CFS syndrome," not "SARS-CoV-2 specifically causes ME/CFS through a unique mechanism."

**3. That all long COVID is ME/CFS, or that ME/CFS findings apply to the broader long COVID population:** By definition, 42–57% of long COVID does *not* meet ME/CFS criteria (the inverse of the 43–58% that does). Long COVID is the broader, mechanistically heterogeneous condition; ME/CFS-meeting is one identifiable subtype within it, defined by the presence of post-exertional malaise and meeting formal case criteria. The [long COVID README](../../../../../../../../README.md) documents this heterogeneity through the NASEM definition, which frames long COVID as an umbrella disease state potentially resolving into multiple diagnosable conditions—including ME/CFS, POTS, interstitial lung disease, and autoimmune disorders—far broader than the neurological and metabolic cluster that defines ME/CFS alone. Finding a mechanism or treatment effective in the ME/CFS-meeting subgroup does not license claiming it applies to long COVID as a whole. Conversely, long COVID epidemiology at the population level — prevalence estimates, environmental risk factors, demographic distributions — cannot be assumed to hold for the ME/CFS-meeting minority without explicit stratification.

**4. That ME/CFS-specific mechanistic frameworks (the decoupling argument) apply to the long-COVID-not-meeting-criteria majority:** The [decoupling note](../../../../../../../../../myalgic-encephalomyelitis-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/01/2026-09-01-note-on-decoupling-as-the-shape-of-post-exertional-malaise.md) builds its HPA-axis/sympathoadrenal argument from ME/CFS cohorts and evidence. It is a valid frame for the subset of long COVID that clinically resembles ME/CFS (the ~40-58% above) and meets formal criteria. It is not automatically valid for the majority of long COVID — whether that majority shares some of the mechanistic elements (metabolic dysfunction, neuroinflammation) or follows a distinct pathway altogether is an empirical question, not something the phenotypic overlap settles. The decoupling note explicitly marks its own case-definition sensitivity; that same discipline applies here.

## Closing: the limit of the boundary

The overlap between long COVID and ME/CFS is real, substantial, and clinically important. It is the proper point to ask whether mechanistic findings from one condition illuminate the other. But the overlap is also bounded: it licenses hypothesis transfer between the two literatures only for the subset that actually converges; it establishes post-viral-illness-triggering as a class phenomenon, not SARS-CoV-2-specific mechanism; and it draws a hard line between syndromic phenotypic resemblance and proven shared pathophysiology. That line is exactly what this note exists to hold.
