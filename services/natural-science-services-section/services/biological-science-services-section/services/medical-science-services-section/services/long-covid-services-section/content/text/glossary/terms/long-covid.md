Long COVID is a chronic, often multi-system condition that follows SARS-CoV-2 infection, but it is not a single validated diagnostic entity — it is measured by at least three distinct instruments built for three different purposes, and a finding produced under one does not automatically transfer to the others. The WHO clinical case definition (Soriano et al., 2022, *Lancet Infectious Diseases* 22(4):e102-e107, doi:10.1016/S1473-3099(21)00703-9, PMID 34951953), reached by a two-round Delphi consensus of 265 patients, clinicians, researchers, and WHO staff, requires probable or confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, symptom onset usually within three months of that infection, symptoms lasting at least two months, and no alternative diagnosis that better explains them. The RECOVER research definition (Thaweethai et al., 2023, *JAMA* 329(22):1934-1946, doi:10.1001/jama.2023.8823, PMID 37278994) is a different instrument entirely: an empirically weighted composite score built from 12 of 44 screened symptoms — post-exertional malaise, fatigue, brain fog, dizziness, gastrointestinal symptoms, palpitations, changes in sexual desire or capacity, loss of or change in smell or taste, thirst, chronic cough, chest pain, and abnormal movements — assessed at six months or more post-infection in a cohort of 9,764 (8,646 infected, 1,118 uninfected controls), producing a research outcome variable rather than a clinical diagnosis. The 2024 NASEM definition (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, *A Long COVID Definition: A Chronic, Systemic Disease State with Profound Consequences*, 2024) widens the frame again: an infection-associated chronic condition present at least three months as a continuous, relapsing-remitting, or progressive disease state affecting one or more organ systems, explicitly encompassing single-symptom presentations through to diagnosable conditions such as POTS, ME/CFS, interstitial lung disease, arrhythmias, diabetes, and autoimmune disorders.

Case-definition heterogeneity here is a research-validity issue, not a semantic quibble. The three definitions differ on the duration threshold (WHO: symptoms ≥2 months, onset ~3 months post-infection, so roughly a 5-month window; RECOVER: assessed at ≥6 months post-infection with no separate onset-timing requirement; NASEM: present at ≥3 months, with no upper bound and explicit allowance for relapsing-remitting or progressive courses rather than continuous persistence), on what counts as evidence (WHO and NASEM are consensus/expert-panel definitions; RECOVER is a data-derived symptom score fit to distinguish infected from uninfected participants), and on scope (WHO and RECOVER describe a symptom-based condition; NASEM explicitly subsumes other named diagnosable conditions, including ME/CFS, under its umbrella). A prevalence estimate, a mechanism study, or a treatment trial that does not state which of these it used cannot be safely pooled with one that used a different definition — the populations selected are measurably different, the same failure mode documented for ME/CFS case definitions (Fukuda vs. Canadian Consensus Criteria vs. IOM/SEID). This is precisely why prevalence estimates published on the subject range so widely: CDC Household Pulse Survey self-report puts current US adult prevalence around 6-7% (MMWR 72(32):866-870, 2023, PMID 37561665); the Global Burden of Disease Long COVID Collaborators' modeled estimate for three symptom clusters worldwide in 2020-2021 is 144.7 million with a 95% uncertainty interval of 54.8-312.9 million (Wulf Hanson et al., 2022, *JAMA* 328(16):1604-1615, doi:10.1001/jama.2022.18931, PMID 36215063); and a pooled meta-analysis of heterogeneous, mostly uncontrolled studies reported 45% of survivors symptomatic at roughly four months (O'Mahoney et al., 2023, *eClinicalMedicine* 55:101762, PMID 36474804), a figure that drew published methodological criticism in the same journal for pooling across inconsistent symptom-duration thresholds and lacking uninfected comparators in most included studies. None of these three numbers is wrong on its own terms; none is directly comparable to either of the others.

## Core symptom clusters

Across the three definitional frameworks, the symptom domains that recur most consistently in both consensus definitions and empirically derived symptom scores are: **fatigue** and **post-exertional malaise** (worsening of symptoms after physical, cognitive, or emotional exertion); **cognitive dysfunction** ("brain fog," memory and concentration difficulty); **respiratory symptoms** (dyspnea, chronic cough); **cardiovascular/autonomic symptoms** (palpitations, orthostatic intolerance, and diagnosable POTS in a subset of patients); **gastrointestinal symptoms**; and **sensory changes** (altered or lost smell or taste). The Global Burden of Disease modeling work organizes the condition around three of these clusters specifically (fatigue, cognitive, respiratory) as the clusters with the strongest cross-cohort evidence base; the RECOVER composite score adds palpitations, thirst, chest pain, sexual-function changes, and abnormal movements as further symptoms found to statistically distinguish infected from uninfected participants at six months. Post-exertional malaise recurs as a symptom shared with myalgic encephalomyelitis, and a subset of long COVID patients meet ME/CFS case criteria outright — the NASEM definition names this overlap explicitly rather than treating the two as unrelated. No consensus symptom list is exhaustive or universal across patients; long COVID is understood as mechanistically and phenotypically heterogeneous, and subtype identification (which symptom clusters co-occur, and by which mechanism) remains active research rather than a settled taxonomy.

## Related terms

- Myalgic encephalomyelitis — the condition a subset of long COVID patients meet case criteria for, sharing post-exertional malaise as a cardinal feature and a structurally similar case-definition-heterogeneity problem
- Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome — the most frequently reported specific autonomic diagnosis among long COVID patients with dysautonomia
- Post-exertional malaise — the symptom retained in the RECOVER composite score and shared with myalgic encephalomyelitis
- Central sensitization — a contrasting mechanistic frame (peripheral/central pain amplification) relevant to distinguishing long COVID's pain and cognitive symptoms from other chronic-pain mechanisms
