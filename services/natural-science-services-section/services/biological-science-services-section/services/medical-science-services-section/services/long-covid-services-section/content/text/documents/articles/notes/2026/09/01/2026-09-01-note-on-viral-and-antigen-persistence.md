## What this addresses

A line of investigation into Long COVID holds that SARS-CoV-2 viral or viral-antigen persistence — the continued presence of intact virus or viral proteins months or years after acute infection — may be a proximate mechanism driving persistent symptoms. This note organizes the current literature on whether and where such persistence occurs, what is actually demonstrated in living and autopsy samples, and what remains untested mechanistic hypothesis.

The evidence comes from three tissue compartments: circulating plasma (spike and nucleocapsid antigen detected via ultrasensitive immunoassay), tissue samples at autopsy (replication-competent virus from multiple organs), and gastrointestinal biopsy and fecal samples (viral RNA and protein). The literature carries real findings; it also carries methodological questions, contested specificity, small early cohorts awaiting replication, and — most critically — no completed trial evidence that clearing a detected persistent reservoir resolves Long COVID symptoms.

## Plasma antigenemia: circulating spike and nucleocapsid antigen

### Swank et al., 2023 — the initial report

Swank and colleagues (Clin Infect Dis. 2023;76(3):e487-e490) measured ultrasensitive plasma-antigen levels in n=63 previously-infected individuals, 37 of whom had post-acute COVID-19 (PASC), up to 12 months post-diagnosis. They used Simoa (single-molecule array) immunoassay to detect circulating spike antigen.

Their finding was striking at the time: spike antigen detected in 60% of PASC-affected individuals vs 0% of non-PASC recovered COVID patients.

**Epistemic status**: This was the first report of its kind and remains conceptually important — it established that circulating spike antigen can be detected by ultrasensitive methods. It is limited by small N, single-cohort design, no pre-pandemic specificity control group reported in the letter, and strong selection for PASC association. Critically, this specific PASC-vs-recovered distinction that Swank reported was not reproduced by later, larger, blinded work (see Mateu below).

### Peluso et al., 2024 (Lancet Infectious Diseases) — established prevalence, unresolved specificity

Peluso and colleagues studied n=250 pre-pandemic negative controls (established assay specificity 98%, 2% false-positive rate) against n=171 pandemic-era participants who contributed 660 plasma specimens across three post-acute windows: 3–6 months, 6–10 months, and 10–14 months post-infection. They measured spike, S1, and nucleocapsid antigen via Simoa.

Their key findings: excess antigen positivity in pandemic-era specimens at every window studied (+11%, 95% CI 5–16% at 3–6 months; +8.7%, CI 3.1–14% at 6–10 months; +5.4%, CI 0.42–10% at 10–14 months). One-quarter of participants (42/171) tested positive for any antigen at least once. Hospitalization and worse acute illness correlated with later antigen detection.

**Epistemic status**: This is an established finding — a larger cohort with proper pre-pandemic negative controls, extending detection to 14 months. The paper establishes that excess antigenemia occurs post-infection in a subset of people generally. It is not itself a Long-COVID-stratified analysis, so it does not directly address whether antigen persistence is specific to Long COVID as opposed to post-infection status more broadly (that question is answered, complicatingly, by Mateu below).

### Mateu et al., 2026 (Clin Microbiol Infect) — the rigorous, complicating study

Mateu and colleagues conducted the methodologically most rigorous plasma-antigenemia study to date: n=425 total, comprising 167 with Long COVID, 148 fully recovered after COVID-19, and 110 uninfected controls. Samples were collected at 6–12 months and 18–24 months post-infection. Assay was run blinded to clinical status; spike, S1, and nucleocapsid antigen measured via Simoa.

**Key findings — and why they complicate the narrative**: At 6–12 months, any-antigen positivity was 31% (Long COVID), 20% (fully recovered), and 5.4% (uninfected). By 18–24 months, positivity had fallen to 3.5%, 0%, and 1.5% respectively. Critically, antigenemia was **not associated** with number or type of persistent symptoms, with antibody titers (including neutralizing antibody), or with vaccination status.

The authors' own conclusion: current evidence does not support using plasma antigenemia to guide clinical monitoring or treatment decisions in Long COVID.

**Epistemic status**: This is the current methodological gold standard — largest N, blinded, three-arm with uninfected controls, longest follow-up (to 24 months). It confirms that antigen can circulate for up to ~1 year in a subset of infected people (consistent with Swank and Peluso). It is a directly complicating result for the simple "persistent antigen explains Long COVID" hypothesis: it finds that circulating antigen is **not clinically specific to Long COVID** and **is not associated with symptom burden**, and is rarely detectable by 2 years. Of note: uninfected controls remained at 1.5% positivity by 18–24 months, higher than the fully recovered group at 0% — an anomaly contrary to the simple convergence-to-zero narrative. Any claim that plasma antigenemia is a validated Long COVID biomarker must cite this study as a counterweight to the earlier Swank finding.

## Tissue reservoirs: autopsy findings and living-tissue evidence

### Stein, Chertow, and colleagues, 2022 — replication-competent virus in multiple organs

Stein and colleagues (Nature. 2022;612(7941):758–763) conducted complete autopsies on n=44 individuals who died with COVID-19 (mostly unvaccinated, acute-fatal cases) and extended central-nervous-system sampling on n=11. They cultured viral samples and performed RNA and immunohistochemical staining.

**Findings**: SARS-CoV-2 RNA detected across 84 distinct anatomic sites (respiratory tract, heart, lymph nodes, GI tract, adrenal gland, eye, thyroid, spleen, appendix, gonads, endometrium, and multiple brain regions) up to 230 days post-symptom-onset in one case. Replication-competent (culturable) virus recovered from heart, lymph node, GI tract, adrenal gland, eye, and notably the brain (thalamus at day 13 post-symptom-onset).

**Epistemic status**: This is a rigorous, multi-organ autopsy finding establishing that SARS-CoV-2 can persist in tissue and remain replication-competent well past acute illness in people who died with severe/fatal COVID. Its relevance to Long COVID is explicitly mechanistic hypothesis, not demonstrated: the authors state directly that "although it is tempting to attribute clinical findings observed in post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 to viral persistence, our study was not designed to address this question." The cohort is autopsy cases of fatal/severe acute COVID, not living Long COVID patients. Whether living, non-fatal Long COVID is associated with tissue persistence remains undemonstrated in direct form.

### Peluso et al., 2024 (Science Translational Medicine) — T-cell activation and gut-RNA in living tissue

Peluso and colleagues performed whole-body PET imaging with 18F-AraG (a T-cell activation tracer) in n=24 post-acute-COVID participants at 27–910 days post-infection, compared to pre-pandemic controls. A subset (n=5) underwent rectosigmoid colonic biopsy with RNA in situ hybridization and immunohistochemistry.

**PET findings** (n=24 vs controls): T-cell activation tracer uptake significantly elevated across multiple anatomic regions including gut wall, lymphoid tissue, and other sites, detectable out to ~2.5 years post-infection.

**Gut-biopsy findings** (n=5 Long COVID participants): Intracellular spike-encoding single-stranded RNA detected in rectosigmoid lamina propria in all 5 individuals (up to day 676 post-infection). Double-stranded RNA (replicative-intermediate, suggesting active viral synthesis) found in 3 of 5.

**Epistemic status**: The PET finding is a more robust observation — n=24 with uninfected control comparator, showing measurable T-cell activation out to years. The gut-biopsy RNA finding is real but preliminary and single-study: n=5, all Long COVID patients (no non-Long-COVID or uninfected comparator biopsied), so specificity to Long COVID cannot be established from this small arm. The double-stranded RNA finding is notable mechanistically (suggesting low-level replication rather than passive antigen persistence) but is a single-study preliminary observation in n=3 of those 5.

## Gastrointestinal persistence: biopsy antigen, RNA, and fecal shedding

### Gaebler et al., 2021 — early, small gut-reservoir report

Gaebler and colleagues (Nature. 2021;591(7851):639–644) studied antibody evolution in n=87 participants post-COVID, with a subset undergoing intestinal biopsy at ~4 months post-onset. Most had mild or asymptomatic acute disease.

**Finding**: SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid and immunoreactivity (nucleocapsid protein) persisting in small-bowel biopsies of 7 of 14 individuals at ~4 months post-infection.

**Epistemic status**: Foundational early evidence establishing the gut as a candidate viral reservoir. Limited by small biopsy sub-study (n=14), no Long COVID phenotyping reported, and the paper's primary aim was memory B-cell evolution — antigen persistence is noted as a plausible driver of continued antibody affinity maturation, not characterized as a Long COVID mechanism per se.

### Zollner et al., 2022 — the IBD-restricted gut-antigen study

Zollner and colleagues (Gastroenterology. 2022;163(2):495–506) endoscoped n=46 patients with pre-existing inflammatory bowel disease at median 219 days (range 94–257) post-COVID, taking duodenal, ileal, and colonic biopsies. They used qPCR for viral transcripts (RdRP, spike, nucleocapsid, envelope), immunofluorescence for nucleocapsid protein, and attempted viral culture.

**Findings**: SARS-CoV-2 RNA in 31% of biopsy specimens. Nucleocapsid protein immunoreactivity in 52% of patients (24/46). A striking symptom correlation: 21 of 32 patients with detectable mucosal RNA reported postacute COVID-19 symptoms vs 0 of 14 patients without detectable RNA.

**Epistemic status**: This establishes an association within this IBD-specific cohort. The symptom correlation is notable (21/32 vs 0/14). However, this cohort is not generalizable — it is enriched for pre-existing gut inflammation, which may alter viral persistence, host clearance, or mucosal sampling adequacy. No non-IBD comparator arm. The association is correlational in a moderate-N, non-representative sample, not causal.

### Hany et al., 2024 — the largest gut-antigen prevalence study

Hany and colleagues (Endosc Int Open. 2024;12(1):E11–E22) endoscoped n=249 patients with prior PCR-confirmed COVID-19 (166 upper GI, 83 lower GI) at median 17 months post-infection (range 7–30 months). They immunostained biopsies from stomach, colon, and terminal ileum with anti-nucleocapsid monoclonal antibody.

**Finding**: Nucleocapsid protein detected in 30.5% of biopsies (76/249). Higher in upper-GI (37.3%) than lower-GI (16.9%) sites. Positivity higher in smokers (36.8% vs 7.5%, p<0.001) and diabetics (22.3% vs 8.1%).

**Epistemic status**: The largest gut-persistence-prevalence study to date, establishing that nucleocapsid antigen can be detected in gut mucosa out to 30 months in a substantial minority. Important caveat: the study has no uninfected/prior-COVID-negative comparator arm. Specificity of the immunohistochemical signal (cross-reactivity risk with anti-nucleocapsid antibodies, a known limitation of the platform) is not excluded. Long COVID symptom correlation is not reported. Treat as established prevalence finding with an unresolved specificity caveat, not proof of causal role.

### Natarajan et al., 2022 — prolonged fecal RNA shedding and symptom correlation

Natarajan and colleagues (Med (Cell Press). 2022;3(6):371–387) followed n=113 participants with mild-to-moderate acute COVID-19 up to 10 months with serial fecal and respiratory RT-PCR.

**Findings**: 49.2% (95% CI 38.2–60.3%) fecal-RNA-positive in week 1. Prolonged fecal shedding after respiratory clearance: 12.7% (CI 8.5–18.4%) positive at 4 months, 3.8% (CI 2.0–7.3%) at 7 months. Fecal shedding correlated with ongoing gastrointestinal symptoms.

**Epistemic status**: Established finding from a prospective moderate-N cohort of prolonged fecal RNA shedding that correlates with GI symptoms. RNA shedding is not proof of replicating virus and is not proof of a driving role in extraintestinal Long COVID symptoms. The paper frames this as evidence the GI tract can be a site of prolonged, low-level infection — a narrower claim than "the gut is the driver of Long COVID."

## Synthesis: what is demonstrated vs what remains hypothesis

### Demonstrated

- **SARS-CoV-2 antigen and RNA persistence across multiple compartments**: Circulating spike and nucleocapsid antigen can be detected in blood for months to over 1 year post-infection (Peluso/Lancet ID 2024, Mateu 2026). Viral RNA and/or protein can be detected in gut mucosa out to 30 months (Hany 2024). At autopsy, replication-competent virus has been cultured from multiple tissues including brain (Stein/Chertow 2022).

- **A subset of infected people carry detectable viral antigen long-term**: About 25% of post-COVID participants carry circulating antigen at some point in the first 14 months (Peluso/Lancet ID 2024). Nucleocapsid antigen is detectable in gut tissue in ~30% of endoscoped prior-COVID patients (Hany 2024).

### Demonstrated but not specific to Long COVID

- **Plasma antigenemia is not clinically specific to Long COVID**: The largest, most rigorous blinded study (Mateu 2026, n=425) found circulating antigen in 31% of Long COVID patients **and** in 20% of fully recovered COVID patients at 6–12 months post-infection. Antigenemia was not associated with symptom burden, symptom number, or type. By 18–24 months, antigenemia declined to 3.5% (Long COVID), 0% (recovered), and 1.5% (uninfected controls) — rare across all groups, with the anomalous finding that uninfected controls retained higher detectability than the recovered group. This directly contradicts the Swank 2023 finding that antigenemia distinguished PASC from recovery, and represents the most current, highest-quality data on this question.

### Still mechanistic hypothesis, not demonstrated

- **That persistent antigen/virus causes Long COVID symptoms**: A phase-2 controlled trial (PAX LC, Sawano et al., Lancet Infect Dis. 2025;PIIS1473-3099(25)00073-8) tested nirmatrelvir-ritonavir vs placebo in n=100 Long COVID patients (randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled) and reported results: nirmatrelvir-ritonavir did not significantly improve the primary endpoint (PROMIS-29 Physical Health Summary Score at day 28) vs placebo. This is a completed, reported antiviral trial in Long COVID with a null result—evidence that antiviral clearance of a hypothesized persistent reservoir does not necessarily resolve Long COVID symptoms, at least for this agent and endpoint. Other mechanistic hypotheses (immunological dysregulation, microvascular dysfunction, cellular dysfunction) remain plausible and untested. Detection of a persistent agent is not the same as demonstration of causation; equally, failure of one antiviral to improve one primary outcome does not exclude persistent infection as a contributing mechanism. The question remains open for other antivirals, other endpoints, and other pathophysiologic hypotheses.

### Not yet independently replicated, or too small for robust inference

- **The rectosigmoid-biopsy RNA findings** in Peluso et al. 2024 (STM): n=5 biopsied, all Long COVID, no non-Long-COVID comparator arm. Single study awaiting replication with proper control arms.

- **The Swank 2023 PASC-specific plasma antigenemia contrast** (60% PASC vs 0% recovered): This striking early finding was not reproduced by Peluso 2024 (which found antigen in both groups) or Mateu 2026 (which found no symptom association). Reported without pre-pandemic control in the original letter; larger, blinded, pre-pandemic-controlled work found a different answer.

### Contested methodological elements

- **Specificity of anti-nucleocapsid assays**: Simoa and immunohistochemical platforms for nucleocapsid antigen carry a known risk of cross-reactivity. Mateu 2026 explicitly flags this in their limitations and concludes that antigenemia should not currently guide clinical Long COVID decisions partly on these specificity grounds. The Hany 2024 study lacks an uninfected comparator, leaving open the possibility that detected signal partly represents assay artifact or cross-reactive antibody.

- **Long COVID phenotype definition**: Different case definitions (Fukuda criteria, Canadian Consensus Criteria, 2015 IOM criteria) select different populations. Gut-antigen prevalence may differ by definition; this is often not reported. Replication across definitions is not yet routine.

## Open questions

1. **Does clearing detected persistent virus/antigen resolve Long COVID symptoms?** This is the central clinical question. One phase-2 antiviral trial (PAX LC / Sawano et al., Lancet Infect Dis. 2025) reported in April 2025 that nirmatrelvir-ritonavir did not significantly improve the primary endpoint vs placebo in n=100 Long COVID patients. This is a data point, not a conclusive answer: negative results for one agent and endpoint do not exclude benefit from other antivirals, higher doses, or other primary outcomes. Mechanistic hypotheses remain open and further trials are ongoing.

2. **Is antigen persistence a marker of a different pathophysiology, or a consequence of more severe acute infection?** Mateu 2026 found antigen in both recovered and Long COVID patients. Does antigen mark a subset at higher risk of persistent symptoms, or is it a separate phenomenon? The current data cannot distinguish.

3. **Are the detected antigen/RNA molecules functionally pathogenic?** Viral RNA in tissue or feces does not prove replication. Double-stranded RNA (found in 3 of 5 Peluso-biopsied patients) suggests active synthesis, but this is preliminary. Single-stranded RNA is consistent with either replication or persistent capsid turnover without productive infection.

4. **Is gut persistence mechanistically distinct from systemic persistence?** Peluso and colleagues found T-cell activation throughout the body, yet the gut biopsies are n=5. Whether gut is a primary driver of systemic inflammation or a secondary site of delayed clearance is unresolved.
