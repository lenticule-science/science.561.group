## Abstract

The three best-regarded published reviews of long COVID mechanisms — [@davis2023] in *Nature Reviews Microbiology*, [@pelusodeeks2024] in *Cell*, and [@faghy2025] in *Communications Medicine* — already enumerate the field's candidate-mechanism list correctly: viral and antigen persistence, immune dysregulation and autoimmunity, latent-virus reactivation, endothelial and microclot pathology, dysautonomia, mitochondrial and metabolic dysfunction, and gut dysbiosis. The house research carriers this review synthesizes cover the first six; gut dysbiosis (compositional imbalance of the gut microbiome, distinct from the viral RNA and antigen persistence in gut tissue covered in Section 3) is out of scope here and named in Limitations. What none of the three published reviews consistently does is separate, at the sentence level, a demonstrated finding from a mechanistic hypothesis — "viral persistence drives long COVID" and "autoimmunity underlies dysautonomia" read as flat assertions whose hedge, when present at all, sits in a discussion paragraph the reader has to go excavate. This review's structural move is to make a four-state epistemic-status partition — **demonstrated**; **demonstrated but not specific to long COVID**; **mechanistic hypothesis** (tagged with its evidence's specificity: prospective, cross-sectional, animal-model-causal, or correlative); or **actively contested** (naming both sides) — the organizing device of the synthesis itself rather than an afterword, so that every substantive claim in the body carries its status inline. This is a falsifiable claim about the writing, not a rhetorical flourish: an independent reader handed the legend below should be able to locate, for any sampled claim, the same status label assigned here; disagreement on a sampled claim is a defect in this synthesis to fix, not a difference of interpretation to argue past.

**The four-state legend, used throughout:**

- **Demonstrated** — replicated, or established by a single sufficiently rigorous study, as a fact about the world (a measurement, a detected difference, a trial result).
- **Demonstrated but not specific to long COVID** — the finding is real, but it does not distinguish long COVID from another comparator (recovered patients, uninfected controls, another post-viral state).
- **Mechanistic hypothesis** — proposed but not established as causal; every use of this label in this review carries a specificity tag naming the kind of evidence behind it (prospective cohort, cross-sectional, animal-model-causal, or correlative).
- **Actively contested** — named parties in the literature disagree, and this review states both positions without adjudicating a winner.

## 1. Why "long COVID" is three instruments, not one condition

Every mechanism claim that follows is meaningful only relative to which case definition selected the cohort it was measured in. Three distinct instruments currently share the name "long COVID," built for three different purposes, and a finding produced under one does not automatically transfer to the others.

The **WHO clinical case definition** [@soriano2022] is a two-round Delphi consensus of 265 patients, clinicians, researchers, and WHO staff: probable or confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, symptom onset usually within three months, symptoms lasting at least two months, no better alternative explanation. This is **demonstrated** as a consensus artifact — it records what the field agreed to call the condition, not a mechanistic claim about its cause.

The **RECOVER research definition** [@thaweethai2023] is a different kind of instrument: an empirically weighted composite score built from 12 of 44 screened symptoms (post-exertional malaise, fatigue, brain fog, dizziness, gastrointestinal symptoms, palpitations, changes in sexual desire or capacity, altered smell or taste, thirst, chronic cough, chest pain, abnormal movements), assessed at six months or more post-infection in a cohort of 9,764 (8,646 infected, 1,118 uninfected controls). It is a research outcome variable engineered to distinguish infected from uninfected participants statistically, not a clinical diagnosis.

The **NASEM definition** [@nasem2024longcovid] widens the frame again: an infection-associated chronic condition present at least three months as a continuous, relapsing-remitting, or progressive disease state affecting one or more organ systems, explicitly subsuming diagnosable conditions — POTS, ME/CFS, interstitial lung disease, arrhythmias, diabetes, autoimmune disorders — under the umbrella.

These three differ on duration threshold, on what counts as evidence (consensus panel versus data-derived score), and on scope (symptom cluster versus umbrella disease state), and the difference is not cosmetic: it is the same case-definition-validity problem documented for myalgic encephalomyelitis, where Fukuda, Canadian Consensus, and IOM/SEID criteria select measurably different populations from the same clinic. Section 6 below quantifies exactly how large that effect can be for long COVID specifically — a single retrospective cohort's ME/CFS-meeting fraction moved roughly six-fold depending only on how many case definitions a patient had to satisfy simultaneously [@morita2024]. Two mechanism papers that appear to contradict each other may simply be describing different populations under the same name; before any mechanism claim in this review is read as a claim about "long COVID" writ large, its source cohort's case definition should be checked against this section.

## 2. Prevalence and burden: calibrating scope, not the paper's subject

This review's subject is mechanism, not epidemiology or economic burden, and this section is deliberately short. Three prevalence figures circulate, and none of them is a ground-truth number for a single validated condition, because no single validated case definition of it yet exists across all three source instruments.

The **CDC Household Pulse Survey**, a self-report tracking survey with no clinical or research case-definition confirmation behind its "yes/no," put current US adult prevalence at roughly 6-7% in mid-2023, down from 7.5% in June 2022 among all adults, and from 18.9% to 11.0% among adults who had ever had COVID-19 over the same interval [@cdc2023pulse]. This is **demonstrated** as a self-report trend, not a validated case count.

The **Global Burden of Disease Long COVID Collaborators**, modeling three symptom clusters (fatigue, cognitive problems, respiratory symptoms) across ten cohort studies in ten countries, estimated 144.7 million people worldwide experienced at least one cluster in 2020-2021, with a 95% uncertainty interval of 54.8-312.9 million [@wulfhanson2022]. This is **demonstrated** as a modeled estimate, not a validated case count; the near-six-fold spread between that interval's bounds is itself the finding: modeled global prevalence is not a settled number, and citing the point estimate alone misrepresents the underlying uncertainty.

The **O'Mahoney meta-analysis** [@omahoney2023] pooled studies with at least 100 participants at 28 or more days post-infection and reported 45% of COVID-19 survivors symptomatic at roughly four months. This figure is **actively contested**: published correspondence in the same journal argued the pooled estimate was inflated by uncontrolled studies lacking uninfected comparators and by heterogeneous, low symptom-duration thresholds across included studies — the case-definition-instrument problem from Section 1, propagated into a meta-analysis.

Faghy et al. lead their review with a claim that long COVID affects more than 400 million people and costs the global economy in excess of $1 trillion annually [@faghy2025]. This review does not attempt to verify, replicate, or build on that economic-burden framing; it is named here, and again in Section 12, as a question this synthesis deliberately does not take up.

## 3. Viral and antigen persistence

The hypothesis that persisting SARS-CoV-2 material — intact virus or viral antigen — proximately drives long COVID symptoms has been tested across three tissue compartments: circulating plasma, autopsy and living tissue, and the gastrointestinal tract. The field's own internal correction arc here is instructive, and this section tracks it explicitly rather than presenting only the current consensus.

### Plasma antigenemia

Swank and colleagues' initial report [@swank2023] measured ultrasensitive plasma spike antigen (Simoa immunoassay) in 63 previously infected individuals, 37 with post-acute sequelae (PASC): spike antigen was detected in 60% of PASC-affected individuals versus 0% of non-PASC recovered patients. This finding is real and was, at the time, the first demonstration that circulating spike antigen is detectable by ultrasensitive methods — **demonstrated**, as a detection capability. But it was a small, single-cohort, unblinded letter with no pre-pandemic specificity control reported, and its specific PASC-versus-recovered *contrast* did not survive later, larger, blinded work.

Peluso and colleagues [@peluso2024lancetid] extended detection with proper pre-pandemic negative controls (n=250, 98% assay specificity) against 171 pandemic-era participants contributing 660 specimens across three post-acute windows (3-6, 6-10, 10-14 months): excess antigen positivity was found at every window (+11%, +8.7%, +5.4% respectively), and roughly one-quarter of participants tested positive for any antigen at least once. This is **demonstrated**: excess antigenemia occurs post-infection in a subset of people generally. It was not itself a Long-COVID-stratified analysis.

Mateu and colleagues' blinded, three-arm, two-timepoint study [@mateu2026] — the field's current methodological standard on this question, with 425 participants (167 Long COVID, 148 fully recovered, 110 uninfected) — is the complicating result. At 6-12 months, any-antigen positivity was 31% (Long COVID), 20% (fully recovered), and 5.4% (uninfected); by 18-24 months it had fallen to 3.5%, 0%, and 1.5% respectively, with the anomaly that uninfected controls retained higher positivity than the recovered group. Antigenemia was not associated with symptom burden, symptom number or type, antibody titers, or vaccination status. This is **demonstrated but not specific to long COVID**: circulating antigen persists in a subset of infected people for up to roughly a year, but it does not discriminate Long COVID from full recovery, and it directly complicates the naive persistence-causes-symptoms hypothesis — a finding that must be cited as a counterweight whenever the Swank result is invoked as biomarker-grade evidence.

### Tissue reservoirs

Stein, Chertow, and colleagues' complete-autopsy study [@steinchertow2022] of 44 people who died with COVID-19 detected SARS-CoV-2 RNA across 84 anatomic sites up to 230 days post-onset in one case, and cultured replication-competent virus from heart, lymph node, gastrointestinal tract, adrenal gland, eye, and brain (thalamus, day 13). This is **demonstrated**: SARS-CoV-2 can persist and remain replication-competent well past acute illness in fatal, mostly-unvaccinated acute cases. Its relevance to living Long COVID patients is explicitly **mechanistic hypothesis (cross-sectional)** — the authors themselves state their autopsy cohort "was not designed to address" whether tissue persistence drives post-acute sequelae, and the population studied (fatal acute COVID) is not the population the hypothesis is about.

Peluso and colleagues' PET-imaging study [@peluso2024stm] used a T-cell-activation tracer (18F-AraG) in 24 post-acute participants versus pre-pandemic controls, finding significantly elevated tracer uptake across gut wall, lymphoid tissue, and other regions out to roughly 2.5 years post-infection — **demonstrated**, with an uninfected comparator and a meaningful n. A gut-biopsy sub-study (n=5) found intracellular spike-encoding RNA in all five, with double-stranded (replicative-intermediate) RNA in three of five — this is **demonstrated but not specific to long COVID**: no non-Long-COVID comparator was biopsied, so specificity cannot be established from this arm, and it is not yet independently replicated by another group.

### Gastrointestinal persistence

Gaebler and colleagues [@gaebler2021], studying antibody evolution, found SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid and nucleocapsid immunoreactivity in small-bowel biopsies of 7 of 14 individuals at roughly four months — foundational but a small sub-study with no Long COVID phenotyping. Zöllner and colleagues [@zollner2022], endoscoping 46 patients with pre-existing inflammatory bowel disease at a median of 219 days, found SARS-CoV-2 RNA in 31% of biopsies and nucleocapsid protein in 52% of patients, with a striking symptom correlation (21 of 32 patients with detectable mucosal RNA reported postacute symptoms versus 0 of 14 without) — **demonstrated** within this IBD-enriched, non-representative cohort, correlational rather than causal, and not generalizable without a non-IBD comparator. Hany and colleagues' larger prevalence study [@hany2024] (n=249, median 17 months post-infection) found nucleocapsid protein in 30.5% of biopsies, higher in smokers and diabetics, but reported no uninfected comparator arm and no Long COVID symptom correlation — **demonstrated** as a prevalence finding with an unresolved specificity caveat, not evidence of a causal role. Natarajan and colleagues [@natarajan2022] followed 113 participants with serial fecal RT-PCR and found prolonged fecal RNA shedding after respiratory clearance (12.7% at 4 months, 3.8% at 7 months) correlating with ongoing GI symptoms — **demonstrated** as a narrower claim (the GI tract can harbor prolonged low-level infection), not evidence that gut persistence drives extraintestinal symptoms.

### The falsification case: PAX LC

The clearest test of the naive "clear the reservoir, resolve the symptoms" hypothesis to date is a completed phase 2 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir in 100 Long COVID patients [@sawano2025]: the drug did not significantly improve the primary endpoint (PROMIS-29 Physical Health Summary Score at day 28) versus placebo. This is **demonstrated**: for this one agent and this one endpoint, antiviral clearance of a hypothesized persistent reservoir did not resolve Long COVID symptoms. It partially falsifies the naive version of the persistence hypothesis without excluding persistence as a contributing mechanism in some patients, for other agents, or against other endpoints — the question stays open, but the naive form of the claim no longer survives unqualified.

## 4. Immune dysregulation and autoimmunity

Five distinct sub-mechanisms are under active investigation here, each at a different evidence stage; none is proposed as sufficient on its own.

### T-cell exhaustion

Aid and colleagues [@aid2026] profiled 142 participants across infection status and outcome, finding persistent upregulation of CD8+ T-cell exhaustion signatures and PD-1-associated genes in Long COVID, alongside elevated soluble IL-6 receptor and JAK-STAT/IL-6 pathways as the top pathways distinguishing Long COVID from recovered individuals — replicated by the same group across a second, temporally separate 2023-2024 cohort. This is **demonstrated** — real methodological strength from within-group replication across two cohorts, but cross-lab replication is not yet complete.

### Autoantibodies

This is the only one of the five mechanisms in this section with direct causal evidence, and that evidence is animal-model evidence. Chen and colleagues [@santosguedesdesa2026] stratified Long COVID patients by Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP) and type-I interferon expression into three plasma-proteome subgroups; in a passive-transfer experiment, purified IgG from the two subgroups with higher neuronal-protein and leukocyte-activation-marker levels induced pronounced and persistent sensory hypersensitivity with distinct kinetics in mice, while IgG from the third subgroup (enriched for skeletal and cardiac muscle proteins) reduced locomotor activity in mice without affecting their motor coordination. This is **mechanistic hypothesis (animal-model-causal)**: genuine causal evidence within the mouse model; human disease causality remains an extrapolation from it, not itself demonstrated. A pooled systematic review of five studies (647 survivors, 266 controls) [@notarte2024] found antinuclear and GPCR-targeting autoantibodies predicting fatigue and dyspnea with high specificity, while its own authors state clinical relevance "seems modest at this stage" — **demonstrated** as an association between autoantibody positivity and Long COVID status, with the causal-significance claim explicitly hedged by the source study itself.

### Complement dysregulation

Baillie and colleagues' case-control study [@baillie2024], matched on age, ethnicity, sex, infection history, and vaccination status, found significantly elevated classical, alternative, and terminal complement pathway markers in Long COVID (combined ROC AUC 0.794) — **demonstrated** as a discriminatory marker in this well-controlled design. A 2026 systematic review of 11 studies (1,435 Long COVID patients, 1,124 controls) [@notarte2026complement] found the pattern was real overall but pathway-specific: classical pathway markers elevated but inconsistent for C1q, alternative pathway markers mixed, lectin pathway showing no significant overall difference though correlating with brain fog in a subset. "Complement system overall is dysregulated in Long COVID" is **demonstrated**; pathway-specific claims (e.g., "the lectin pathway is dysregulated") are **actively contested**, not consistently replicated across the pooled studies.

### The microclot and amyloid-fibrin dispute

This is an explicitly unresolved dispute between named research groups, and this review states both positions without adjudicating a winner. Kell, Pretorius, and colleagues [@kellpretorius2022] propose that fibrinogen can polymerize into an anomalous amyloid-like fibrin form, producing microclots resistant to normal fibrinolysis at higher prevalence in Long COVID plasma, framed by the originating group as central to Long COVID pathophysiology. Hunt and colleagues [@hunt2024] published a direct critique arguing that microclots are not unique or specific to Long COVID (seen in other inflammatory and aging states), that the correlative-to-causal inference is not evidence-based, and that quantification methods across the field lack standardization and proper controls. Kell, Khan, and Pretorius's rebuttal [@kellkhanpretorius2024] disputes both the characterization of their program's scale and the methodological critique. **Actively contested**: the specificity, standardization, and correlation-versus-causation objections have not been answered by a controlled or causal human trial on either side, and this line remains mechanistic hypothesis rather than established mechanism precisely because of the live dispute.

### EBV reactivation

Gold and colleagues' early sub-study [@gold2021] found EBV reactivation serology (EA-D IgG or VCA IgM) in 66.7% of 30 Long COVID subjects versus 10% of 20 controls — this is **demonstrated** as an association within a small, single-site, not-yet-replicated-at-scale sample. Peluso and colleagues' better-powered LIINC cohort study [@peluso2023liinc] (280 participants, 258 in final models) found recent EBV reactivation serology (EA-D IgG) associated with fatigue (OR 2.12) and high EBV nuclear-antigen IgG associated with neurocognitive dysfunction, while also finding EBV DNAemia rarely detected systemically (tissue-localized reactivation) and Long COVID occurring in some patients with no serologic evidence of EBV infection at all. This is **demonstrated** as a statistical association between EBV-reactivation serology and specific symptom clusters — EBV reactivation is associated with, but neither necessary nor sufficient for, Long COVID. Whether reactivation drives symptoms or Long-COVID-associated immune dysregulation permits reactivation remains **mechanistic hypothesis (correlative)**.

### Cross-cutting thread

Aid and colleagues [@aid2026] trace JAK-STAT/IL-6 elevation correlating with both CD8+ exhaustion signatures and complement/coagulation pathway upregulation within the same cohort — the strongest single-paper case for a unified proinflammatory-exhaustion axis across three of the five mechanisms above. The authors themselves label this **mechanistic hypothesis (correlative)**, not causal; the temporal or mechanistic relationship between the three axes is not established.

## 5. Dysautonomia and orthostatic intolerance

### The denominator problem

Population-level POTS incidence following infection and prevalence within symptomatic clinic cohorts are frequently conflated, and they are not the same number. A pooled meta-analysis of five studies [@yong2023] found population-level POTS incidence at 1.08% following SARS-CoV-2 infection (versus 0.039% following vaccination alone, a 2.12-fold difference), with age explaining 86.2% of the variance — **demonstrated** as population-level incidence. A prospective cohort of 467 highly symptomatic long-COVID clinic patients undergoing tilt-table and active-stand testing [@bjornson2024] found 31% met formal POTS criteria, with a further 27% having orthostatic symptoms without meeting diagnostic thresholds — **demonstrated**, but a figure produced by a referral-selected, already-symptomatic population, not comparable to the population-level number above. Smaller cohorts [@cantrell2024] report POTS prevalence spanning roughly 2-30%, tracking case definition and referral pattern rather than a single population parameter.

### GPCR autoimmunity: an assay-methodology dispute

Wallukat and colleagues' foundational single-center report [@wallukat2021] found functional (agonist-activity) autoantibodies against multiple GPCRs (β2-adrenergic, muscarinic M2, angiotensin II AT1, and others) in nearly all of 31 recovered COVID-19 patients — **demonstrated** as a detection finding, not evidence of causality, in a small unreplicated cohort. A larger single-group follow-up [@hofmann2025] (194 patients) found similar high seropositivity rates with symptom correlations (odds ratios 6.77-7.45 for dizziness, concentration difficulty, and POTS in seropositive patients) — again **demonstrated** within a single research group's series, no independent replication.

Directly in tension with both: a standardized-ELISA case-control study [@hall2022] measuring antibodies against 11 GPCRs in 116 POTS patients (general POTS, not specifically post-COVID) versus 81 healthy controls found no significant discrimination for any receptor (best single-receptor C-statistic 0.581; combined AUC 0.621, well below clinical utility). This is **actively contested**, and the dispute is most likely an assay-methodology artifact rather than a biological disagreement: functional bioassays measure agonist activity on living cells, while standardized ELISA measures binding to a fixed antigen — different properties. Whether the discrepancy reflects true biological heterogeneity, cross-reactivity in one method, or a technical ceiling in the other is unresolved, and whether these autoantibodies are causally pathogenic, an epiphenomenon of chronic immune activation, or partly an assay artifact is not established.

### Small-fiber neuropathy

A retrospective single-center study [@mcalpine2024] found small-fiber neuropathy diagnostic criteria met in 89% of 9 PASC patients on skin biopsy — **demonstrated** as an elevated-prevalence finding in a small sample. A larger prospective case-control study [@donadio2024] (33 post-COVID, 33 vaccinated, 33 controls) confirmed elevated SFN prevalence (94% post-COVID, 79% vaccinated) but, critically, found autoimmune serology was positive in both patient groups yet explicitly *not* correlated with SFN presence — **demonstrated but not specific to** a simple GPCR-autoimmunity-causes-neuropathy story; this directly complicates any account that routes small-fiber damage through the autoantibodies discussed above. A small IVIG treatment-response case series (16 patients, 9 treated) found 100% clinical response in the IVIG group versus 43% untreated — a real signal from an unblinded, non-randomized, very-small-n retrospective design, **not RCT-level evidence**, hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. The elevated SFN prevalence itself is **demonstrated**; that this neuropathy causally produces the orthostatic phenotype in Long COVID specifically remains **mechanistic hypothesis (correlative)**, inferred from the general pre-COVID POTS literature rather than established in this population.

### RECOVER-AUTONOMIC: a clean negative RCT, still unpublished

A systematic review of 32 published studies on general POTS pharmacotherapy [@pierson2025] found only 5 RCTs among case series, cohort studies, and reviews — the evidence base is thin, and no positive trial was identified for fludrocortisone specifically. The first purpose-built long-COVID-POTS RCT, RECOVER-AUTONOMIC's ivabradine arm (181 participants, randomized, placebo-controlled, 12-week treatment), produced a statistically significant heart-rate reduction without a corresponding improvement in symptom or quality-of-life measures on the primary outcome scales, while participants who additionally received coordinated nonpharmacologic care reported greater improvement than ivabradine-plus-usual-care alone. This is **demonstrated** as an RCT-level heart-rate effect without symptom benefit in this long-COVID-POTS population; **this result is presented at an American College of Cardiology conference session (March 2026) and has not yet been located in a peer-reviewed journal publication as of this review's drafting** — the figures should be treated as provisional pending journal publication, and this caveat is kept live in the text rather than resolved by assumption.

## 6. The ME/CFS overlap: what convergence licenses and what it does not

A meta-analysis pooling 13 clinical studies (n=1,973) [@dehlia2024] found 51% (95% CI 42-60%) of long COVID patients meet ME/CFS diagnostic criteria — the current headline "about half" estimate. Individual studies span a wide range around it: 45% by Canadian Consensus Criteria in a matched prospective cohort [@kedor2022]; 43% by IOM 2015 criteria in a Stanford clinic cohort with likely referral-severity bias [@bonilla2023]; 58% by DePaul Symptom Questionnaire in a survey study, where only 71% of self-reported ME/CFS patients actually met formal criteria while 40% who did not self-report nevertheless did [@jasondorri2023]. The keystone observation on why this range exists is Morita and colleagues' retrospective cohort [@morita2024]: requiring simultaneous fulfillment of three case definitions (Fukuda, Canadian Consensus, IOM 2015) rather than any single one moved the ME/CFS-meeting fraction from mid-range percentages down to 8.4% — roughly a six-fold swing from case-definition stringency alone, in the same patients over the same date range. Independently, a meta-analysis of post-exertional malaise prevalence in PASC (12 studies, 2,665 participants) [@an2024] found 55% of PASC patients reported PEM at three months — a figure that brackets the ~51% ME/CFS-criteria estimate closely enough to be suggestive of a consistent subgroup, though the two measurements are methodologically independent and their convergence is not confirmatory of identity.

### What the overlap licenses

Phenotypic convergence — shared post-exertional malaise as the cardinal symptom, plus overlapping fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, and dysautonomia — licenses testing ME/CFS pacing and management frameworks on the long-COVID-with-PEM subgroup, and licenses reading ME/CFS mechanistic hypotheses as relevant candidates to test in that subgroup specifically. A retrospective cohort of 36,076 COVID-positive versus 111,301 COVID-negative controls without prior fatigue diagnosis [@hadidchi2025] found an elevated new-onset ME/CFS hazard ratio (1.56, 95% CI 1.25-1.93) holding regardless of acute-phase severity and not further elevated by reinfection — **demonstrated**, and the strongest available large-linked-cohort evidence that SARS-CoV-2 infection elevates ME/CFS incidence at the population level, not merely that the two conditions resemble each other cross-sectionally.

### What the overlap does not license

It does not license claiming identical pathophysiology: meeting the same symptom-based case definition is compatible with multiple distinct underlying mechanisms converging on one clinical phenotype, and long COVID's candidate mechanisms (Sections 3-5) remain hypotheses about shared pathways, not a demonstrated causal chain identical to any specific ME/CFS mechanistic frame. It does not make the overlap SARS-CoV-2-specific: a 2009 cohort of 233 SARS-CoV-1 survivors followed four years post-infection [@lam2009] found 40.3% with unexplained chronic fatigue and 27.1% meeting formal CFS criteria at four years — measurably comparable to the long-COVID-meets-ME/CFS fractions above, indicating SARS-CoV-2 is one more entry in a list of viral ME/CFS triggers, not a uniquely coronaviral mechanism. And it does not extend to the long-COVID-not-meeting-criteria majority: by definition, 42-57% of long COVID does not meet ME/CFS criteria, and a mechanism or treatment effective in the ME/CFS-meeting subgroup cannot be assumed to generalize to long COVID as a whole.

### The wider research-coordination frame

This overlap sits inside a broader "infection-associated chronic illness" (IACC) umbrella [@choutka2022; @nasem2024workshop] grouping long COVID, ME/CFS, post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome, and post-Ebola, post-dengue, and post-SARS-1 chronic syndromes for shared research infrastructure and outcome-measure harmonization. This is explicitly named by its own proponents as a research-coordination strategy, not a claim of shared disease mechanism — no study to date has identified a single biomarker, pathway, or diagnostic test present across all named conditions. The cross-pathogen evidence supports treating post-viral-illness-triggering as a class phenomenon worth pooled study, precisely the same conclusion the SARS-CoV-1 comparison above reaches independently.

## 7. Cross-mechanism synthesis: heterogeneity, not a unified theory

The table below assembles the epistemic status of every mechanism this review covers. Rows for organ-specific structural pathology and the treatment landscape are gap markers, named as such rather than filled with borrowed confidence, per Section 8 and the Limitations below.

| Mechanism | Best-established claim | Status | Specificity of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plasma antigen persistence | Excess antigenemia occurs post-infection generally [@peluso2024lancetid]; does not discriminate long COVID from full recovery [@mateu2026] | Demonstrated but not specific to long COVID | Controlled, pre-pandemic negatives [@peluso2024lancetid]; blinded, 3-arm, controlled [@mateu2026] |
| Tissue/autopsy viral persistence | Replication-competent virus in fatal-acute-COVID tissue | Demonstrated (population is fatal acute COVID, not living Long COVID) | Autopsy [@steinchertow2022] |
| Gut mucosal viral persistence | Nucleocapsid antigen detectable to 30 months in a minority | Demonstrated prevalence; specificity uncaveated in most studies | Cross-sectional, no comparator [@hany2024] |
| Naive persistence-causes-symptoms | Nirmatrelvir-ritonavir did not improve primary endpoint | Demonstrated null for one agent/endpoint | RCT [@sawano2025] |
| T-cell exhaustion | CD8+ exhaustion signatures replicate across two cohorts | Demonstrated (single-group replication; cross-lab replication pending) | Cross-sectional, longitudinal within-group [@aid2026] |
| Autoantibodies (causal) | Subgroup-specific IgG transfer induces sensory hypersensitivity or reduced locomotion in mice | Mechanistic hypothesis | Animal-model-causal [@santosguedesdesa2026] |
| Complement dysregulation | Overall dysregulation discriminates cases from controls | Demonstrated overall; pathway-specific claims contested | Case-control, pooled review [@baillie2024; @notarte2026complement] |
| Microclots / amyloid-fibrin | Central mechanism claim vs. specificity critique | Actively contested | Named-group dispute [@kellpretorius2022; @hunt2024] |

Two structural points follow from reading the table as a whole rather than mechanism by mechanism. First, long COVID is very likely mechanistically heterogeneous across patient subsets: no row above is a unified theory, and several rows are actively contested. This review has not verified, sentence by sentence, that the field's best reviews [@davis2023; @pelusodeeks2024; @faghy2025] state the heterogeneity point as sharply as this review's legend requires — that comparison is out of scope here, and the heterogeneity claim above rests on this review's own table, not on a demonstrated reading of those three papers. Second, the case-definition problem from Section 1 propagates through every row: a "demonstrated" finding drawn from a RECOVER-defined cohort is not automatically a "demonstrated" finding under WHO or NASEM criteria, because the three instruments select measurably different populations — the table's status column should be read as conditional on its source cohort's case definition, not as a claim about "long COVID" as a single population.

## 8. Organ-specific structural pathology (partial synthesis)

Every comparison review devotes substantial space to organ-specific structural findings independent of the systemic mechanisms above — cardiac, pulmonary, and especially neurological. House carriers going into this review did not previously cover this ground; the following is a first-pass synthesis built from targeted literature search rather than an existing house research note, and it is thinner than Sections 3-6 as a result.

**Neuroinflammation.** Fernández-Castañeda and colleagues [@fernandezcastaneda2022] modeled mild respiratory SARS-CoV-2 infection in mice and found multi-lineage neural cell dysregulation: persistent white-matter microglial reactivity, reduced hippocampal neurogenesis, and elevated circulating cytokines including CCL11, resembling patterns seen after cancer chemotherapy ("chemo-brain") — **mechanistic hypothesis (animal-model-causal)**: a genuine causal demonstration in mice that respiratory-only infection can produce neuroinflammatory and neurogenesis changes, with human relevance an extrapolation rather than a direct finding, since the study did not test human Long COVID tissue or patients.

**Structural neuroimaging.** The UK Biobank sub-study [@douaud2022] compared brain MRI scans taken before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection in the same 401 individuals against matched uninfected controls, finding a greater reduction in grey-matter thickness in orbitofrontal and parahippocampal regions, greater changes in markers of tissue damage in regions functionally connected to the primary olfactory cortex, and a greater cognitive decline between the two timepoints in infected participants — **demonstrated**: a controlled, longitudinal, within-subject imaging finding, the strongest single piece of evidence in this section. Whether these structural changes are the substrate of long-COVID cognitive symptoms specifically, versus a general correlate of infection severity, is **mechanistic hypothesis (correlative)**, not established by this study alone.

**Cardiac MRI.** An early cardiovascular MRI cohort [@puntmann2020] of 100 patients recently recovered from COVID-19 found cardiac involvement in 78% and ongoing myocardial inflammation in 60%, independent of pre-existing conditions and severity of the acute illness — **demonstrated** as a prevalence finding in this cohort, but an early, single-center study without long-term outcome follow-up; whether these imaging findings predict or correlate with long-COVID cardiac symptoms specifically is not established by this study, and this review has not undertaken the fuller synthesis (replication status, symptom correlation, natural history) that a dedicated section would need.

This section is honestly scoped as thinner than Sections 3-6: it synthesizes three studies chosen to represent the neuroinflammatory, structural-imaging, and cardiac-imaging strands respectively, not the field's full organ-pathology literature. A pulmonary-fibrosis and diffusion-capacity strand exists in the published comparison reviews and is not covered here at all; see Limitations.

## 9. Mitochondrial and skeletal-muscle metabolic dysfunction

Appelman and colleagues [@appelman2024] put long COVID patients with post-exertional malaise through a standardized two-day exercise challenge and found measurably worse muscle abnormalities the day after exertion: lower oxidative phosphorylation capacity, reduced succinate dehydrogenase activity, amyloid-containing deposits in muscle tissue, and a shift toward glycolytic metabolism. This is **mechanistic hypothesis (correlative)**: a real, well-instrumented finding — but a single cohort, not yet independently replicated at scale, and it does not resolve whether the metabolic derangement is a cause of post-exertional malaise or a downstream consequence of whatever upstream process drives it. This is a direct structural parallel to the post-exertional-malaise mechanism under investigation in the ME/CFS literature (Section 6), and this review treats it as suggestive of, not proof of, a shared mechanism across the two conditions. Unlike Sections 3-6, this is a single citation rather than a synthesized sub-literature with its own replication and contested-status map; a dedicated research note would be needed to bring this section to the same depth, and until that exists this paragraph is the honest ceiling of what can be said here.

## Limitations

This review does not cover several things a competitive published review would. Naming them here, rather than leaving them silently absent, is the house discipline this synthesis follows.

**Organ-specific structural pathology, only partially covered.** Section 8 synthesizes three representative studies (neuroinflammation, structural neuroimaging, cardiac MRI) rather than the full literature each comparison review devotes a major section to; pulmonary fibrosis and diffusion-capacity follow-up data are not covered at all.

**Gut dysbiosis.** Entirely absent. Section 3's "Gastrointestinal persistence" subsection covers viral RNA and antigen persistence in gut tissue, a different mechanism from compositional dysbiosis of the gut microbiome, which the field's comparison reviews treat as a separate candidate mechanism.

**Reproductive and menstrual effects.** Entirely absent. Davis et al. [@davis2023] devote a dedicated section to this literature; this review does not take it up.

**Viral-variant era.** Entirely absent. Davis et al. [@davis2023] treat ancestral/Delta/Omicron era as a mechanistically relevant factor for long COVID risk and phenotype; this review does not test or discuss variant-specific effects anywhere, distinct from the vaccination and reinfection scope boundaries named below.

**The treatment and clinical-trial landscape, not synthesized as a whole.** [@pelusodeeks2024] is explicitly structured around which mechanistic hypotheses have and have not been tested interventionally; this review reports individual trial results embedded inside their relevant mechanism sections (PAX LC in Section 3, RECOVER-AUTONOMIC's ivabradine arm in Section 5) but does not attempt the cross-cutting synthesis of the RECOVER platform trial program as a whole, nor cover rehabilitation and pacing evidence, low-dose naltrexone case series, or other interventions currently under study.

**Pediatric long COVID and MIS-C.** Entirely absent from this review. It is a genuinely distinct literature with its own case-definition problems, not a smaller version of the adult literature synthesized here.

**Vaccination as a preventive intervention.** Touched only incidentally, via the population-level POTS comparator data in Section 5 (2.12-fold higher POTS incidence following infection than following vaccination [@yong2023]), never taken up as its own question.

**Risk-factor synthesis.** Age, sex, comorbidity burden, vaccination status, reinfection, and acute-phase severity appear scattered as incidental findings inside individual mechanism sections (age explaining most POTS-prevalence variance in Section 5; smoking and diabetes correlating with gut nucleocapsid positivity in Section 3) rather than assembled into their own section.

**Biomarker or diagnostic-panel translational framing.** Comparison reviews typically ask what a clinical biomarker panel could look like; this review is deliberately mechanism-focused rather than translational, consistent with the house carriers it synthesizes, and does not attempt this framing.

**The economic and societal burden framing.** [@faghy2025] leads with a claim of more than 400 million people affected and over $1 trillion in annual cost; this review neither reproduces nor verifies that framing (Section 2).

None of these omissions is a claim that the excluded material does not matter. Each is a scope boundary, stated so a reader can tell the difference between "this review found nothing here" and "this review did not look here."
