## What this argues

Long COVID's immune-dysregulation mechanisms are being actively investigated across five major candidate lines. This note synthesizes the current evidence state for each: which findings have been replicated and are established, which are plausible mechanisms still being tested, which remain single-group observations awaiting independent confirmation, and which are explicitly contested within the literature. The organization is by mechanism rather than by unified theory; no single mechanism is proposed as *the* cause of long COVID, and long COVID is very likely mechanistically heterogeneous across patient subsets.

## T-Cell Exhaustion and Activation

**Well-supported, single-group replication across two temporally separate cohorts.**

Aid and colleagues (2026) profiled a cohort of 142 participants — uninfected controls (n=35), acutely infected (n=54), convalescent non-LC controls (n=24), and long COVID (n=28) — between 2020 and 2021, performing immunological, virological, transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. They found persistent upregulation of CD8+ T-cell exhaustion signatures and PD-1-associated genes in LC, alongside downregulated T-cell activation/differentiation transcriptional signatures versus convalescents — a pattern of early activation followed by an exhausted state. Notably, they also found elevated plasma soluble IL-6 receptor, and demonstrated that JAK-STAT and IL-6 signaling pathways were the top pathways distinguishing LC from recovered individuals.

This finding was independently validated by Aid and colleagues across a second cohort enrolled 2023-2024 (convalescent n=20, LC n=18). Aid et al. found the same persistent CD8+ T-cell exhaustion signatures and PD-1-associated genes in LC at both 90–180 days and >180 days post-infection across both cohorts. A complement/coagulation cascade pathway upregulation also correlated positively with IL-6 and JAK-STAT dysregulation, bridging to the complement findings below.

The replication across two temporally separate cohorts from the same group is a real methodological strength, though cross-lab replication is not yet complete. This is labeled here as well-supported, single-group replication rather than fully independent confirmation.

## Autoantibodies

**Mechanistic hypothesis with direct causal support in animal models; human causality remains an extrapolation.**

Santos Guedes de Sá and colleagues (2026, Iwasaki lab at Yale) conducted a causal test of the autoantibody hypothesis. They found that acute SARS-CoV-2 infection triggers de novo production of diverse, functional autoantibodies that remain elevated in LC. Individuals with neurocognitive LC symptoms showed elevated autoantibodies against central-nervous-system and peripheral-nervous-system proteins. Critically, they performed a passive-transfer experiment: purified IgG from LC patients was transferred into mice, inducing fatigue-like behavior, balance/coordination loss, thermal hyperalgesia, small-fiber nerve damage, and increased pain-related neuronal activity — reproducing patient-reported symptom domains. This is genuine causal evidence within the animal model; human disease causality remains an inference from the mouse data.

A systematic review by Notarte and colleagues (2024) pooled five peer-reviewed studies totaling 647 COVID-19 survivors (410 with post-COVID symptoms, 223 without) and 266 non-infected healthy controls. Antinuclear antibodies (notably anti-U1-snRNP, anti-SS-B/La) predicted fatigue (92% specificity) and dyspnea (97% specificity) in the pooled data. GPCR-targeting autoantibodies (targeting adrenergic and muscarinic receptors including ADRB1, ADRB2, AGTR2, CHRM3, CHRM4, ADRA2A) were elevated and identified as candidate classifiers of post-COVID syndrome. However, the review authors themselves state: "the clinical relevance of their presence seems modest at this stage." This is an established association between autoantibody positivity and LC versus controls, but the authors explicitly hedge the clinical significance claim.

## Complement Dysregulation

**Established finding overall; pathway-specific claims are contested due to heterogeneity.**

Baillie and colleagues (2024) compared plasma from LC patients versus healthy convalescent controls matched on age, ethnicity, sex, infection history, and vaccination status. They found significantly elevated markers of the classical (C1s-C1INH complex), alternative (Ba, iC3b), and terminal (C5a, TCC) complement pathways in LC. Combined markers gave ROC AUC = 0.794 for discriminating LC from controls. This is a well-controlled case-control study establishing complement dysregulation as a discriminatory LC marker.

A 2026 systematic review by Notarte and colleagues pooled 11 studies (7 case-control, 4 cohort), totaling 1,435 LC patients (mean age 48.5, 70% female) and 1,124 controls (mean age 43.6, 60% female). All included studies were rated high quality (Newcastle-Ottawa 7–8 of 9 stars). The review noted that "the clinical significance remains controversial due to the heterogeneous findings." Classical pathway markers (C2, C4a, C4b, C1s-C1INH) were elevated but inconsistent for C1q; alternative pathway Ba/iC3b/Factor D increased, C3f decreased; terminal pathway C5a/C9/TCC elevated, C7 decreased. The lectin pathway (MBL, MASP1-C1INH) showed no significant overall difference, though brain fog specifically correlated with elevated CH50 and lower MBL in a subset.

This pattern — complement system involvement is real, but different LC symptom clusters map to different complement pathway signatures — means that claims like "lectin pathway is dysregulated in LC" are contested or not consistently replicated, while "complement system overall is dysregulated in LC" is established.

## Microclot and Amyloid-Fibrin: An Explicitly Contested Mechanism

**CONTESTED / UNRESOLVED SCIENTIFIC DISPUTE. Both sides are named below; neither adjudication nor silent preference for one side is scientifically honest.**

Kell, Pretorius, and colleagues (2022) proposed that fibrinogen can polymerize into an anomalous amyloid-like form of fibrin, producing microclots that stain with fluorogenic amyloid dyes, resist normal fibrinolysis, and are found at higher prevalence and size in LC plasma than in healthy-control plasma. This is the originating paper of the microclot hypothesis; the authors frame it as "central" to long COVID pathophysiology.

Hunt and colleagues (2024) published a direct critique in the same journal (Research and Practice in Thrombosis and Haemostasis), arguing that microclots are not unique or specific to LC (seen in other inflammatory and aging states), that the causal inference from correlative microclot observations to LC pathophysiology is not evidence-based, and that quantification methods across the field lack standardization and proper controls.

Kell, Khan, and Pretorius (2024) published a rebuttal in the same journal, arguing that Hunt et al.'s description of the theory as arising from "a research team in South Africa" misrepresents the program's scale and scope, noting the joint program has produced over 60 peer-reviewed publications over more than 10 years. They also dispute Hunt et al.'s methodological critiques and their interpretation of the microclot findings' specificity.

**Status: This is an unresolved scientific dispute between named groups.** The methodological critiques raised by Hunt et al. (specificity, standardization, correlation-versus-causation gap) are real and have not been answered by controlled or causal human trials. Present both the hypothesis and the critique fairly; do not adjudicate a winner. This line remains mechanistic hypothesis, not established mechanism, precisely because of this dispute.

## Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) Reactivation

**Established association between EBV reactivation serology and specific LC symptom clusters; directionality and causality unresolved.**

Gold and colleagues (2021) surveyed 185 COVID-19 patients (LC prevalence 30.3%, n=56/185) and conducted an EBV-reactivation sub-study on 30 LC subjects versus 20 non-LC controls. They found 66.7% (20/30) of LC subjects versus 10% (2/20) of controls positive for EBV reactivation (EA-D IgG or VCA IgM positivity) — a statistically significant difference but a single-site sample with small n. This is a suggestive early finding not yet replicated at scale.

Peluso and colleagues (2023, UCSF LIINC cohort) assessed 280 adults with prior SARS-CoV-2 infection at median 123 days post-diagnosis (258 in final regression models). Serologic evidence of recent EBV reactivation (EA-D IgG positivity) was associated with fatigue (OR 2.12); high EBV nuclear-antigen IgG was associated with neurocognitive dysfunction. EBV DNAemia was rarely detected in blood (1/50 tested), suggesting reactivation is tissue-localized rather than systemic viremic during convalescence. Critically: LC also occurred in a subset with no serologic evidence of prior or recent EBV infection, meaning EBV reactivation is associated with, but neither necessary nor sufficient for, LC.

The Peluso study is better-powered and better-controlled than Gold et al., establishing the statistical association between EBV-reactivation serology and specific LC symptom clusters. The question of causality — does EBV reactivation drive symptoms, or does LC-associated immune dysregulation permit EBV reactivation — remains mechanistic hypothesis, not established.

## Cross-Cutting Thread: JAK-STAT, IL-6, and Immune Activation

The Aid et al. (2026) paper traces a notable thread across mechanisms. JAK-STAT/IL-6 elevation correlates with both CD8+ T-cell exhaustion signatures and complement/coagulation pathway upregulation in the same LC cohort. This is the strongest single-paper case for a unified "chronic proinflammatory/exhaustion" axis. However, the authors themselves label this as correlative evidence, not causal — the temporal or mechanistic relationship between these three axes (T-cell exhaustion, IL-6 elevation, complement dysregulation) is not yet established.

The autoantibody line is currently the only one of the five major mechanisms with direct causal (animal-model) evidence to date. The other four — T-cell exhaustion, complement dysregulation, EBV reactivation, and microclots — rest on correlative human data or prospective associations, with the mechanistic directionality and causality in humans still unresolved.

## What would falsify this

The synthesis makes claims that can fail. Naming them is the point of writing this down.

If T-cell exhaustion findings do not replicate in independent cohorts beyond Aid et al.'s replication of the Yin result, the established status of this finding reverts to single-group replication.

If the autoantibody findings in passive-transfer mouse experiments do not translate to human challenge or longitudinal studies showing that autoantibody titer changes precede or predict symptom changes, the causal hypothesis remains animal-model evidence, not human evidence.

If complement dysregulation becomes more consistent across independent studies, pathway-specific claims (e.g., lectin pathway) may graduate from contested to established. If heterogeneity deepens, the claim may need to fracture into "complement dysregulation in subsets of LC" rather than a unified pathway finding.

If the microclot dispute is not resolved through standardized quantification methods and causality studies in humans, this line remains contested indefinitely — scientific disputes that are not resolved through controlled human trials stay unresolved, and that status is the honest one.

If EBV reactivation turns out to be a marker of immune dysregulation rather than a driver of it, the mechanism inverts but the association remains; if it replicates as necessary and sufficient in a prospective cohort, it rises to a stronger mechanistic hypothesis.

And the whole synthesis is vulnerable to the case-definition problem that this section inherits from the long COVID literature itself: LC studied under WHO criteria, RECOVER research criteria, and NASEM criteria are measurably different populations. Findings that appear inconsistent or weakly consistent across studies may reflect definitional heterogeneity rather than biological inconsistency — which is itself a reason to hold this note's claims as a frame for testing rather than conclusions reached.
