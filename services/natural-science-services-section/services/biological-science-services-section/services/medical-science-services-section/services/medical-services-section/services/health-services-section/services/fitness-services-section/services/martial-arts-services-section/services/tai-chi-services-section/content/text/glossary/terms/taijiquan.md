Taijiquan (太極拳), "supreme ultimate boxing", is a Chinese internal martial art. It is named for *taiji*, the cosmological principle from which yin and yang differentiate. It is practised as forms: long sequences of slow, continuous movements with constant weight shifting, a vertical, relaxed posture, and coordinated breath and attention. Partner drills (tuishou, "push hands") train sensitivity and balance against another person.

The family styles date from the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries. Chen style is traditionally the oldest, and Yang, Wu (Hao), Wu, and Sun styles descend from or beside it. A simplified 24-movement Yang form was standardized in 1956 and is the one most taught worldwide and most used in research.

As health practice it overlaps [qigong](../../../../../../../../../../../../../traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/qigong.md): both regulate body, breath, and mind together, as the [three regulations](../../../../../../../../../../../../../traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/san-tiao.md) describe. What sets taijiquan apart is its martial structure, its long choreographed forms, and its continuous single-leg weight transfer. That last feature is what makes it a balance training in biomechanical terms.

For research it is a complex multicomponent intervention. A class combines movement, breathing, attention, social contact, ritual, and a teacher's expectations, and the choice of control condition decides which of these a trial actually tests [@wayne2008]. The resulting evidence is weighed in the TCM section's [2026-09-24 note on the clinical evidence for qigong and taiji](../../../../../../../../../../../../../traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/09/24/2026-09-24-note-on-the-clinical-evidence-for-qigong-and-taiji.md).
