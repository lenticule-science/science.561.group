Cranial Electrotherapy Stimulation (CES) devices apply weak electrical current through electrodes placed on the earlobes or mastoid bone behind the ears. Current flows across the brain. Devices operate continuously at preset frequencies and intensities, typically for 20–60 minutes per session. CES is marketed for sleep, anxiety, and mood without targeting specific anatomical sites; intent is diffuse electrical influence on central nervous system function.

CES rests on a poorly validated premise. The claim that weak transcranial current meaningfully modulates brain activity via electrical field effects is mechanistically implausible at the currents and voltages used [@shekelle2018]. The human skull has high electrical resistance, and current of this magnitude cannot create organized electric fields in brain tissue. Modern neuroscience recognizes that weak transcranial stimulation (transcranial direct current stimulation, tDCS) requires much higher current densities to produce measurable brain effects, and even then the mechanisms remain debated.

The historical basis for CES was speculative: earlier researchers hypothesized that stimulating the ear near the brain could induce "alpha waves" and relaxation. This hypothesis was not mechanistically grounded in understood neurobiology. Biofeedback and placebo response account for many reported CES effects.

A comprehensive review [@shekelle2018] commissioned by the US Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality examined benefits and harms of CES for chronic pain, depression, anxiety, and insomnia. Key findings: insufficient evidence for any indication; small randomized trials showed minimal differences from sham; trials were underpowered, heterogeneous, and high-bias risk. Adverse effects: minimal; mild discomfort at electrode sites in some trials. The review concluded the mechanism remains unclear and high-quality randomized trials are lacking.

More recent CES research has similarly failed to demonstrate consistent efficacy above placebo in well-controlled trials. The technology remains marketed directly to consumers despite lack of robust evidence.
