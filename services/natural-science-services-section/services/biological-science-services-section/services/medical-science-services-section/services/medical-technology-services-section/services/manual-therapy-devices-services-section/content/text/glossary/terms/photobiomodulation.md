Photobiomodulation is the application of red or near-infrared light (typically 600–1000 nm wavelength) to tissue. Red and near-infrared light penetrate to approximately 5–10 mm below the skin surface [@hamblin2017], where they interact with mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase in the electron transport chain.

This interaction increases ATP (cellular energy) production in cells [@hamblin2017]. Downstream effects include increased cellular energy availability for protein synthesis (including collagen), upregulation of anti-inflammatory signaling (reduced TNF-α, IL-6), and angiogenesis (new blood vessel formation).

In controlled laboratory and clinical trials, photobiomodulation produces reproducible effects on tissue repair and inflammation in certain wound models and joint inflammation studies [@hamblin2017]. Evidence is moderate for muscle injury recovery and joint pain; evidence is weaker for cosmetic skin applications and cellulite reduction.

Photobiomodulation does not permanently alter tissue structure or resolve structural problems such as cellulite (subcutaneous fat herniation through connective tissue). While the technique may transiently improve skin appearance through edema reduction and modestly increased collagen turnover, long-term clinical benefit for cosmetic indications is not established.

Safety is generally good. Mild eye strain may occur with direct light exposure; protective eyewear is recommended. Skin irritation is rare. The technique is non-invasive and produces no known serious adverse effects.
