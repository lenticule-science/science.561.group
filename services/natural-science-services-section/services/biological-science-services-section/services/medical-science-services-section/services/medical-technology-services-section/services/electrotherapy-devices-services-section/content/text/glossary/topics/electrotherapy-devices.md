Electrotherapy devices are consumer-grade instruments that deliver electrical current to skin, peripheral nerves, or transcranial pathways to modulate pain, mood, autonomic function, or sleep. The devices operate by activating neural pathways that modulate these functions: low-frequency electrical current (2–10 Hz) stimulates release of endogenous opioid neuropeptides, reducing pain perception; stimulation of the vagus nerve through its auricular sensory branch activates parasympathetic nervous system tone and anti-inflammatory signaling; and weak transcranial current sets up small electric fields in the brain that can shift neuronal excitability and rhythms, at intensities near the lower limit of what is known to affect neurons.

Electrotherapy devices are distinct from transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), which applies electrical current for acute pain relief typically during physical therapy. Consumer electrotherapy devices are marketed for self-directed, long-term use without medical oversight, and their regulatory classification varies: cranial electrotherapy is regulated under 21 CFR 882.5800 and was reclassified by FDA order (December 2019, effective 2020) to Class II for anxiety/insomnia indications and Class III for depression; transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation devices lack specific FDA classification. The evidence base for efficacy is heterogeneous: taVNS has mechanistic plausibility and emerging clinical data, while cranial electrotherapy has low-strength evidence of modest benefit for anxiety, insufficient evidence for other uses, and an unsettled mechanism (transcranial, through the ear's nerves, or through expectation).
