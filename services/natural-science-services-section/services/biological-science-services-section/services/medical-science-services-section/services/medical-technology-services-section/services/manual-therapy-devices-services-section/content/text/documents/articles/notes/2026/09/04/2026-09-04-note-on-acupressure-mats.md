## What the devices are and how they are built

The acupressure mat is a textile or foam base (typically 30 × 60 cm, approximately 12 × 24 inches) embedded with hundreds of plastic or metal spikes. The user lies or places body parts on the mat, where the spikes apply distributed mechanical pressure to skin and superficial muscle tissue.

**Critical context:** These devices are known as "Kuznetsov applicators" after Vladimir Kuznetsov, a Soviet inventor who patented a spiked mechanical applicator in the 1970s. They are Soviet-era wellness devices, not Traditional Chinese Medicine instruments. While modern marketing often associates them with "[acupressure](../../../../../../../../../../../traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/acupressure.md)," they are purely mechanical—no philosophical framework of qi or meridians attaches to them. They are pressure-and-pain devices, not needling.

The design is intentionally simple and low-cost: no power required, no electronics, purely passive mechanical stimulation. Spike density and height vary; some mats have distributed spikes, applied across a large surface.

## Claimed mechanism versus supported mechanism

**Claimed mechanism:**
- Mechanical pressure stimulates acupressure points and promotes relaxation
- Spiked stimulation increases blood circulation
- Device reduces muscle tension and pain in the back and neck
- The spikes activate the same pathways as acupuncture needles

**Supported mechanism:**

The device is mechanically straightforward: pressure + pain = gate control. The [Gate Control Theory](../../../../../../../../../../../pain-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/gate-control-theory.md) of pain (postulated by Melzack and Wall, 1965) posits that non-painful tactile input (pressure, vibration) can suppress pain signal transmission in the spinal cord. When the mat's spikes apply pressure to skin, local nociceptors (pain receptors) are activated by the pressure itself, but concurrently, mechanoreceptors (pressure sensors) send signals that compete in the spinal cord, suppressing the upward transmission of pain. The result is transient analgesia or pain relief.

This is a documented neurophysiological mechanism, reproducible with any mechanical pressure stimulus, not unique to acupressure or spikes. A rubber ball, massage hand, or simple pressure pad could produce the same gate-control effect.

The claim that spikes specifically activate "acupressure points" is not mechanistically supported. The mat applies distributed pressure; it does not target anatomically identified acupoints. The mechanism is local gate-control, not acupoint-specific energy flow.

Blood circulation claims are modest: gentle prolonged pressure does increase local microcirculation acutely. This effect is not sustained and does not accelerate muscle recovery.

## Evidence

Hohmann et al. (2012) [@hohmann2012] ran two randomized controlled pilot studies of a needle stimulation pad in patients with chronic neck pain (n = 40) and chronic low back pain (n = 42), each split between a 14-day self-administered treatment group and a waiting-list control group. Key findings:

- Pain ratings were significantly reduced in the treatment groups relative to waiting-list controls, for both the neck pain study (P = .021) and the back pain study (P < .001)
- Pressure pain thresholds at the site of maximal pain increased significantly in both treatment groups
- There was no significant effect on mechanical or vibration detection thresholds
- The Northwick Park Neck Pain Questionnaire improved significantly in the neck pain study; the Oswestry Disability Index did not improve significantly in the back pain study
- Neither study compared the mat to other mechanical pressure devices (massage, heat application); both used only a waiting-list control, not a sham or active comparator

These are two unblinded pilot trials against a waiting list, not a systematic review, so the finding of benefit over doing nothing is real but preliminary, and it says nothing about how the mat compares to simpler pressure techniques.

The evidence base is limited to small, unblinded, waiting-list-controlled trials. Mechanistically, the analgesic effect is plausible via gate control, but the mat is not shown to be superior to simpler pressure techniques.

**Certainty assessment:**
- Short-term pain relief over no treatment (low-to-moderate certainty): Observed in two pilot RCTs; unblinded, waiting-list control only
- Superiority over other pressure methods (very low certainty): No comparative trials
- Long-term benefit or tissue healing (very low certainty): No evidence

## Regulatory status

**FDA classification:** Acupressure mats are not medical devices. They are marketed as wellness or personal care items and face no FDA regulation.

**IEC 60601-1:** Not applicable (no electrical components).

**Import status:** Widely imported and sold without regulatory restriction.

**Safety certification:** No third-party testing or certification typical.

## Safety and contraindications

**Adverse effects:**
- Discomfort or pain during use (the spikes are intentionally pressure-inducing)
- Local skin irritation or mild bruising with prolonged use
- Rare reports of bleeding if used on thin skin

**Absolute contraindications:**
- Active skin infection, open wounds, or severe dermatological condition at treatment site
- Anticoagulant therapy or bleeding disorder (increased bruising risk)
- Thin, fragile, or compromised skin (elderly, extreme age, certain medications)

**Relative cautions:**
- Acute inflammation or very recent injury (may exacerbate inflammation)
- Pregnancy (anecdotal concern; low absolute risk, but caution advised)
- Severe osteoporosis (risk of bruising or micro-fracture with intense pressure)
- Acute bone pain or suspected fracture at treatment site

**Safe use:**
- Start with light body weight (lay flat rather than kneeling on mat to distribute weight)
- Limit use to 10–20 minutes initially, building tolerance
- Use over clothing for comfort if needed
- Avoid use on bone, over the spine, or on injured areas
- Discontinue if pain becomes severe (distinguish therapeutic soreness from injury pain)

## What the 561 Group's medical program may say

**Regulatory gate:** Acupressure mats are not medical devices and meet the non-regulated device requirement.

**Evidence-based statement for the kit:**

The Group may include an acupressure mat with the following statement:

> The Kuznetsov acupressure mat is a mechanical pressure device that can produce transient pain relief and muscle relaxation through local nerve stimulation (gate control mechanism). It is a Soviet-era wellness device, not derived from Traditional Chinese Medicine. Evidence supports modest, temporary pain relief during and immediately after use, but no long-term clinical benefit has been established. The mat is safe for most users at moderate intensity. It is included as a supplemental wellness tool, not a therapeutic device.

**Boundaries:**
- May not be marketed with medical claims such as "treats chronic back pain," "heals injuries," or "improves circulation permanently"
- Should not be recommended for acute injury or inflammation; wait 48–72 hours after acute injury before use
- Not a substitute for evaluation of persistent pain

**Safety notes:**
- Warn users with bleeding disorders or on anticoagulants
- Caution pregnant individuals; recommend consulting a provider
- Advise against use on thin or compromised skin
