A vacuum cupping device made of silicone or rubber creates localized negative pressure by manual suction (squeezing the cup sides) when applied to skin. The negative pressure draws skin and superficial muscle tissue into the cup, dilates local capillaries, and mobilizes tissue temporarily. The cup is removed by releasing the vacuum.

The mechanisms are: (1) local capillary dilation and increased blood flow, which produces transient hyperemia; (2) activation of mechanoreceptors via negative pressure, triggering gate-control analgesia (transient pain relief); and (3) tissue mobilization via suction. All effects are temporary and resolve when the cup is removed.

Marketing claims that cupping removes stagnant blood or breaks up fascia lack mechanistic support. Fascia is not a discrete target amenable to mechanical breakup. Negative pressure mobilizes tissue temporarily but does not produce lasting structural change.

Evidence from small randomized trials [@cao2012] [@lauche2012] shows cupping produces modest, transient pain reduction comparable to massage or heat application. No evidence supports superiority over simpler methods or long-term benefit. Cupping combined with acupuncture showed slightly larger effects than cupping alone in some trials, but the independent contribution of cupping versus acupuncture is unclear.

Safety is generally good. Bruising is expected and dose-dependent; extreme negative pressure or prolonged application can produce blistering. Users on anticoagulants have increased bruising risk.
