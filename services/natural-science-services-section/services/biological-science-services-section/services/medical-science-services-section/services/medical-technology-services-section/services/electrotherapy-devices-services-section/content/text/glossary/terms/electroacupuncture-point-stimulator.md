Consumer electroacupuncture point stimulators are handheld battery-powered or rechargeable devices that apply electrical current to skin via a metallic probe tip. Two designs are common: (1) handheld digital pens with a single probe and frequency/intensity display; (2) multi-attachment systems with interchangeable heads and digital controls.

Devices often incorporate skin-impedance detection (ohm meters) to identify treatment points, on the premise that skin resistance dips at acupoint locations. Devices deliver low-frequency, low-amplitude asymmetric biphasic electrical current through the probe. Claimed mechanism is that electrical current applied at traditional acupuncture point coordinates triggers the same physiological responses as needling [@han2003] [@zhao2008].

Electrical nerve stimulation does activate local nerves and can produce pain relief through gate-control mechanisms [@johnson2022]. However, the notion that non-invasive electrical stimulation at traditional acupoint coordinates replicates needle acupuncture is not established. Skin impedance does not reliably identify anatomical acupoints. No credible evidence supports that transcutaneous electrical stimulation at traditionally named points produces the same physiological effects as needle insertion.

Evidence from trials [@johnson2022] shows transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (of which these devices are a variant) produces modest, transient pain reduction through peripheral nerve activation and spinal gating, effects independent of point location or traditional acupoint anatomy.

Safety is generally good. Mild skin irritation can occur; users with implanted devices (pacemakers) should avoid use. No serious adverse effects are documented.
