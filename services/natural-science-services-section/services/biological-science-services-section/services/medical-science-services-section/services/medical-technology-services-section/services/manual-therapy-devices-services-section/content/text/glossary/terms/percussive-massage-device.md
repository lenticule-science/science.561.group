A percussive massage device (commonly called a massage gun) is a handheld, battery-powered or rechargeable device that delivers rapid repetitive mechanical impacts to muscle tissue. A DC brushless motor drives a rotating cam or eccentric weight, which produces rapid compression and decompression of muscle.

Devices include adjustable frequency (impact rate) and amplitude (depth) controls, rechargeable power, and interchangeable applicator heads (ball for large muscle groups, fork for muscle bellies to avoid bone). Claimed mechanisms include increased blood flow, reduced muscle tension, accelerated muscle recovery, and breakdown of fascial adhesions through myofascial relaxation.

The actual mechanism is mechanical vibration applied to muscle tissue. Vibration activates muscle spindle reflexes and mechanoreceptors, producing transient reflex-mediated muscle relaxation via spinal gating mechanisms [@johnson2022]. Transient local blood flow increase follows mechanical stimulation. These effects are temporary and resolve when stimulation ceases.

Evidence from small trials [@behm2016] [@johnson2022] shows percussive devices produce modest, transient muscle tension relief and increased range of motion during and immediately after use. No evidence supports permanent tissue change, accelerated recovery, or superiority over simpler mechanical massage or heat application. Effects are comparable to manual massage at equivalent depth and duration.

Safety is generally good. Discomfort or transient bruising can occur with intense vibration. The device should be avoided over bone, on thin skin, or in users taking anticoagulants.
