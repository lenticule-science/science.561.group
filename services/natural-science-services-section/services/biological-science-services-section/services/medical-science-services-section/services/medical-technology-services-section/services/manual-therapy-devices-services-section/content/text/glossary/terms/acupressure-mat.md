An acupressure mat (also known as a Kuznetsov applicator, after its Soviet inventor) is a textile or foam base typically 30 × 60 cm embedded with hundreds of plastic or metal spikes. Users lie or press body parts on the mat, where the spikes apply distributed mechanical pressure to skin and superficial muscle tissue.

The mechanism is mechanical stimulation via spikes, which activate skin nociceptors (pain receptors) and mechanoreceptors (pressure sensors) simultaneously. Per gate control theory [@hohmann2012], this stimulation produces transient analgesia (pain relief) by suppressing pain signal transmission in the spinal cord through concurrent activation of non-painful tactile input.

Claims that the device stimulates acupressure points or activates meridians lack mechanistic support. The mat applies distributed pressure; it does not target anatomically identified acupoints. Claims that the device breaks up fascial adhesions or permanently changes tissue structure are not supported by evidence.

Evidence from small randomized trials [@hohmann2012] shows the device produces modest, transient pain reduction and muscle tension relief immediately after use. Pain typically returns to baseline within hours. No evidence supports long-term benefit or superiority over simpler mechanical pressure techniques such as massage or heat application.

Safety is generally good. Discomfort during use is expected; mild skin irritation and bruising are possible with prolonged contact. The device should be avoided on thin, fragile, or compromised skin and in users on anticoagulants.
