Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) passes a constant [current](../../../../../../../../../../../physical-science-services-section/services/physics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/electric-current.md), typically 1–2 mA for 20–30 minutes, between two saline-soaked sponge electrodes on the scalp. The electrodes are usually 25–35 cm², which keeps the [current density](../../../../../../../../../../../physical-science-services-section/services/physics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/current-density.md) at the skin low. The montage (where the anode and cathode sit) decides which cortex receives the strongest field.

Its modern form comes from Nitsche and Paulus's demonstration that a few minutes of 1 mA over the motor cortex changed its [excitability](../../../../../../../../../neuroscience-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/neuronal-excitability.md) beyond the stimulation period itself: raised under the anode, lowered under the cathode [@nitsche2000]. It has since been studied for depression, chronic pain, stroke rehabilitation and cognition, with mixed and often modest results.

A large evidence review found no serious adverse effects across tens of thousands of sessions at conventional parameters. Tingling, itching and mild skin redness were common and transient [@bikson2016]. Its currents are of the same order as those of [cranial electrotherapy stimulation](cranial-electrotherapy-stimulation.md) devices. CES differs in its waveform (pulsed alternating current, not direct current) and in its small electrodes on or near the ears.
