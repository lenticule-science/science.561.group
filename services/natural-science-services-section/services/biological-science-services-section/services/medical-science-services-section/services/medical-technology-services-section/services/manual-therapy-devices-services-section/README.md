## Scope

This section covers mechanical and thermomechanical devices that apply pressure, vibration, heat, or tension to skin and muscle tissue without electrical stimulation or pharmacological agent. These devices are used for transient pain relief, muscle relaxation, flexibility improvement, and cosmetic appearance changes.

## Research spine

The section grounds itself in the neuromechanical bases of manual therapy:

- **Gate control theory:** How mechanical pressure and vibration suppress pain signal transmission through spinal pathways (Melzack & Wall 1965, as applied to contemporary devices)
- **Autogenic inhibition:** How vibration frequency-specific activation of muscle spindles triggers transient muscle relaxation via spinal reflex
- **Mechanical decompression and blood flow:** How sustained pressure increases local microcirculation and tissue fluid mobilization acutely, though mechanisms for long-term recovery acceleration remain uncertain
- **Stretch mechanisms:** How sustained passive tension produces both mechanical tissue elongation and neural adaptation (reduced reflex resistance), contributing to chronic flexibility improvement
- **Photobiomodulation:** How red and near-infrared light penetrate tissue and interact with mitochondrial function to enhance cellular energy and inflammatory signaling (for devices combining light with other modalities)
- **Clinical evidence:** Systematic reviews and randomized trials assessing these devices for pain, muscle soreness, range of motion, and cosmetic outcomes, with attention to effect sizes, study quality, and duration of benefit

## Device families

1. **Percussion massage devices (massage guns):** Handheld electromechanical vibration applicators
2. **Acupressure mats (Kuznetsov applicators):** Passive mechanical pressure mats with spiked surface
3. **Vacuum cupping devices (silicone cups):** Negative-pressure mechanical stimulation
4. **Multi-modal massagers:** Combining heat, vibration, and red/near-infrared light
5. **Resistance stretching straps:** Elastic tension devices for assisted passive stretching

## Distinction from trained manual therapy

These devices are designed for self-application without training. They differ materially from trained manual therapy (massage, lymphatic drainage, proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation, mobilization) in:

- Lack of anatomical specificity and adaptive technique adjustment
- Inability to target specific tissues or techniques (manual lymphatic drainage, myofascial release)
- Transient rather than long-term therapeutic effects
- No clinical decision-making or contraindication assessment by a trained provider

The devices produce mechanistic effects (transient blood flow, muscle relaxation, pain gating) reproducible in studies, but evidence for superiority over simpler methods (manual massage, stretching, heat) or substitution for trained therapy is lacking.
