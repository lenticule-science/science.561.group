Deconditioning is the measurable physiological decline that occurs with sustained inactivity or bed rest, and it is real, quantifiable, and produces genuine functional impairment. It is essential to state plainly: deconditioning exists, it does occur in people with chronic illness, and it produces some of the physiological findings seen in myalgic encephalomyelitis. Avoiding deconditioning is important for maintaining function. The claim that deconditioning is irrelevant to ME/CFS outcomes is unsupported. The error lies not in acknowledging deconditioning as real, but in treating it as the sole or primary mechanism in ME/CFS, and in using the presence of some deconditioning-like findings as evidence that ME/CFS is "really just deconditioning."

The physiological changes of deconditioning are well-characterized and occur predictably. Sustained bed rest or inactivity produces a reduction in plasma volume within days, reflected in lower central venous pressure and reduced circulating catecholamine levels needed for compensation — patients feel orthostatic and symptomatic when mobilized. Stroke volume (the amount of blood the heart ejects per beat) declines due to reduced ventricular preload and reduced cardiac contractility from lack of demand. Over weeks, cardiac atrophy (actual reduction in heart mass) begins. Skeletal muscle atrophies and loses oxidative capacity through reduction in mitochondrial density, capillarity, and oxidative-enzyme expression. Skeletal-muscle force production falls. Orthostatic intolerance develops. Maximal oxygen consumption (VO₂ max) declines. All of these are measurable and reversible with progressive activity. The cardinal feature of deconditioning is that the physiological losses are reversible through graded exertion: an athlete who has been bedridden recovers stroke volume, plasma volume, muscle mass, and VO₂ max with weeks to months of progressive training, returning to near-baseline functional capacity. This reversibility with progressive exertion is the defining test of deconditioning.

Myalgic encephalomyelitis presents with some findings that superficially resemble deconditioning: reduced cardiac stroke volume, altered orthostatic responses, reduced VO₂ max on single-day exercise testing. The critical difference lies in the *two-day cardiopulmonary exercise test*, which measures exertion capacity on two consecutive days. In healthy people and in people with other fatiguing conditions (fibromyalgia, depression, post-viral fatigue not meeting ME/CFS criteria), performance on day two reproduces day-one values: VO₂ max, anaerobic threshold, and maximum workload are stable. In myalgic encephalomyelitis, the characteristic finding is a *decline on day two*: reduced VO₂ max, reduced anaerobic threshold, reduced workload tolerance compared to day one. This pattern — normal or near-normal single-day capacity with day-two decline — is the pathological signature that distinguishes ME/CFS from deconditioning. A deconditioned person who trained and returned to baseline would show stable two-day values; a person with ME/CFS shows the characteristic two-day decline.

Deconditioning is a confound to be measured and excluded through rigorous assessment. The appropriate approach is not to assume deconditioning is present or to assume it is absent, but to assess it objectively: measure baseline cardiac output, stroke volume, and maximal exercise capacity; determine whether it reproduces or declines on a second exertional challenge; assess whether progressive exertion improves or worsens function over time; and separate the reversible deconditioning component (if present) from the post-exertional-malaise component (the exertion-induced decline that distinguishes ME/CFS). Some ME/CFS patients may have coexisting deconditioning from illness-imposed inactivity; detecting and accounting for that deconditioning is necessary for clarity about the underlying ME/CFS pathophysiology. Conversely, a patient with what appears to be deconditioning-like findings may have early or mild ME/CFS, and mistakenly prescribing graded exertion without testing for the post-exertional-malaise response risks harm.

The scientific and clinical consequence is that deconditioning cannot be assumed from symptoms or even from single-day exercise testing, and claiming that ME/CFS findings reflect "nothing but deconditioning" without two-day testing is unsupported. Conversely, finding some deconditioning features does not establish that the patient's illness is deconditioning-based rather than ME/CFS. The tool that separates them is the two-day protocol and the demonstration of post-exertional malaise — the exertion-induced pathological decline that distinguishes ME/CFS from deconditioning.

## Related terms

- Two-day cardiopulmonary exercise testing — the protocol that distinguishes post-exertional malaise from deconditioning
- Post-exertional malaise — the exertion-induced decline characteristic of myalgic encephalomyelitis, not deconditioning
- Myalgic encephalomyelitis — the condition distinguished from deconditioning by the two-day CPET response
- Stroke volume — the cardiac output parameter measured in both deconditioning and ME/CFS
- Orthostatic intolerance — a symptom produced by both deconditioning and ME/CFS dysautonomia
- Graded exercise therapy — a treatment approach appropriate for deconditioning but mechanistically inappropriate for ME/CFS
