## What this argues

A biometric-guided pacing tool — one that reads heart rate, heart-rate
variability (HRV), or activity data from a wearable and turns it into
pacing guidance — needs an honest account of what its inputs actually
predict. This note is that account. It covers four bodies of evidence:
the two-day cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET) as the field's
flagship objective marker of post-exertional malaise, which now has a
live, unresolved replication conflict; HRV-based next-day-fatigue
prediction and HRV biofeedback, both real but each resting on a single
small cohort; the one meta-analysis of pacing itself; and the
heart-rate-ceiling heuristics already in clinical circulation, which
are rules of thumb, not validated instruments. Every claim below is
re-verified against the publisher record, PubMed, or CrossRef as of
2026-09-01; sample sizes and effect sizes are quoted from the primary
source, not carried forward from an earlier survey.

What this licenses downstream, stated in one line: a tool built on this
evidence can describe a person's own biometric history back to them and
flag deviations from their own baseline; it cannot diagnose post-exertional
malaise, cannot claim to detect it from heart-rate data alone, and cannot
present any single threshold — CPET-derived, HRV-derived, or the
Workwell heart-rate-ceiling rule — as validated for that purpose.

## What pacing is grounded in

[Pacing](../../../../../../glossary/terms/pacing.md) is the strategy of
keeping exertion within current metabolic and autonomic capacity to
avoid triggering post-exertional malaise (PEM), the delayed,
disproportionate symptom flare that is myalgic encephalomyelitis's
cardinal feature. The house glossary term already states the pacing
evidence base plainly: few randomized controlled trials, mostly small
observational and qualitative evidence, but a mechanistic rationale —
metabolic and autonomic dysfunction, not deconditioning — that is
better supported than graded exercise therapy's opposite rationale.
Pacing's biometric variant substitutes a measured signal (heart rate,
HRV) for the person's own symptom judgment as the exertion ceiling. The
evidence for the biometric substitution is the specific subject of this
note; the evidence for pacing as a strategy is the pacing term's.

## The two-day CPET marker: a live replication conflict

The [two-day CPET](../../../../../../glossary/terms/two-day-cardiopulmonary-exercise-testing.md)
protocol — a maximal exercise test repeated 24 hours later — is the
field's closest thing to an objective, reproducible signature of PEM:
healthy people and people with other fatiguing illnesses reproduce
day-one values on day two, while people with ME/CFS characteristically
show a day-two decline. As of this note, that marker is contested by a
direct 2026 replication, and the conflict is unresolved, not settled in
either direction.

**Keller, Pryor & Giloteaux (2014), *Journal of Translational
Medicine*, DOI 10.1186/1479-5876-12-104** (verified against the
PubMed abstract, PMID 24755065, PMCID PMC4004422). Twenty-two subjects
diagnosed with ME/CFS completed two maximal CPETs 24 hours apart, with
peak respiratory exchange ratio ≥1.1 on both tests confirming maximal
effort. Day-two declines: VO₂peak −13.8%, HRpeak −9 bpm, peak minute
ventilation −14.7%, workload at peak −12.5%; at ventilatory threshold,
VO₂ −15.8%, minute ventilation −7.4%, workload −21.3%. A standard
functional-impairment classification based on a single CPET
mis-estimated functional ability for 50% of participants. This study
had no healthy-control arm — it establishes a within-subject day-two
decline in ME/CFS patients, not a group difference against controls.

**Mancini et al. (2026), *Frontiers in Physiology*, DOI
10.3389/fphys.2026.1816082** (verified against the publisher abstract).
Fifty-eight ME/CFS patients and 25 healthy controls each completed
maximal bicycle-ergometer CPETs on two consecutive days. Result: no
significant change in peak VO₂ from day one to day two in either group.
ME/CFS patients showed greater perceived exertion and lower maximum
heart rates than controls, but standard cardiopulmonary measurements
were otherwise stable across days. The authors' own conclusion, quoted
directly: their data "do not support using the 2-day CPET protocol to
define PEM or disability."

**A third, earlier data point complicates the picture further,** and
belongs in the record because it predates and outsizes both: **Snell,
Stevens, Davenport & Van Ness (2013), *Physical Therapy*, DOI
10.2522/ptj.20110368** (verified against the PubMed abstract, PMID
23813081). Fifty-one women with CFS and 10 sedentary female controls
completed two maximal CPETs 24 hours apart. Test one showed no
significant multivariate difference between groups — consistent with
Mancini's day-one finding. Test two showed CFS patients achieving
significantly lower VO₂ and workload at peak and at ventilatory
threshold than controls, with 95.1% classification accuracy separating
the groups on day-two measures alone. This is the largest
controlled two-day-CPET study in the record and it supports the
day-two-decline finding, at odds with Mancini's larger and more recent
non-replication.

**The honest state of the evidence:** three studies, no consensus.
Keller (n=22, no controls) and Snell (n=51+10 controls) both find a
day-two decline; Mancini (n=58+25 controls) — larger than either, and
the only one of the three published in 2026 — finds none. Sample size
does not resolve this in Mancini's favor by itself, and no study here
has been independently replicated by a different research group under
matched protocol. A biometric-guided pacing tool cannot treat two-day
CPET decline, or any biometric correlate built to detect it, as an
established marker of PEM. It can note that the question is open and
contested in the current literature.

## HRV-based prediction and biofeedback: real, single-cohort

Two distinct claims get grouped under "HRV pacing" and should not be
conflated: HRV predicting next-day fatigue, and HRV biofeedback
training reducing symptoms. Both have real, positive single-study
evidence; neither has been externally validated.

### Next-day-fatigue prediction

**Aboagye, Germann, Baker, Baker & Del Din (2025), *Frontiers in
Digital Health*, DOI 10.3389/fdgth.2025.1689846** (verified against
the publisher abstract). Sixty-eight participants with post-COVID
fatigue wore cardiac and activity monitors for up to 21 days, reporting
fatigue levels every other day. An XGBoost model predicting next-day
fatigue from HRV and activity-sleep features achieved R² = 0.789,
outperforming a random-forest model (R² = 0.69). The strongest
predictors were HRV features — sample entropy, low-frequency power,
approximate entropy — combined with demographic and activity data.

This is a feasibility result from a single cohort (n=68) with no
external validation cohort, and the population is post-COVID fatigue,
not diagnosed ME/CFS — a related but distinct population with
overlapping but not identical pathophysiology. A prediction model
tuned and tested on the same 68 people has not shown it generalizes to
a different population, a different wearable, or even a held-out
cohort from the same recruitment site.

### HRV biofeedback

**HEARTLOC — Corrado et al. (2024), *Advances in Rehabilitation
Science and Practice*, DOI 10.1177/27536351241227261** (verified
against the PubMed abstract, PMID 38298551, PMCID PMC10826406). A
feasibility study: long-COVID patients used a four-week HRV-biofeedback
program (slow diaphragmatic breathing, 10 minutes twice daily) via a
Polar H10 chest strap and phone app. Thirteen participants completed
the study. Statistically significant improvements were observed on the
modified Yorkshire Rehabilitation Scale (p=.001), COMPASS-31 autonomic
symptom score (p=.007), RMSSD (p=.047), WHODAS disability score (p=.02),
and EQ-5D global health (p=.009). The authors' own limitation, stated
directly: "the small sample size limits generalisability," and they
call for a larger randomized controlled trial.

Thirteen completers with pre/post comparison and no control arm is a
real, statistically significant signal in a feasibility study — not
evidence a biofeedback feature reduces symptoms in general use. Trial
registration (NCT05228665) confirms this was designed and reported as
a feasibility study, not an efficacy trial.

### The population caveat, stated once

Both of the above studies — Aboagye and HEARTLOC — are post-COVID or
long-COVID cohorts, not diagnosed ME/CFS cohorts. Long COVID and
ME/CFS overlap substantially (see the cross-linked note below) but are
not interchangeable; evidence generated in one population is suggestive
for, not established in, the other.

## HRV during exercise near threshold: a newly grounded finding

**Ruijgt, Slaghekke, Ellens, Janssen & Wüst (2026), *Sports Medicine*,
DOI 10.1007/s40279-026-02487-4** (verified against the PubMed abstract,
PMID 42501245; "online ahead of print" as of this note). Continuous
multi-day HRV was recorded in 121 long-COVID patients and 21 healthy
controls, with a cardiopulmonary exercise test used to locate each
participant's first ventilatory threshold (VT1). HRV was lower in
patients than controls during daily activity and sleep (p=.027), and
remained lower for 24 hours following exercise around VT1 (p=.010);
nighttime HRV dropped further with more intense and longer exercise
(p=.018). The authors' interpretation: delayed recovery of
sympathovagal balance after exercise near or above VT1 suggests PEM
risk rises above that intensity — and they frame this as supporting
wearables for overexertion management, explicitly not as a validated
diagnostic threshold.

This is one observational study (n=121+21 controls) in a long-COVID,
not ME/CFS, population, published in 2026 with no independent
replication yet. It establishes an association between above-VT1
exertion and prolonged HRV suppression — it does not establish a
validated HRV threshold that predicts or defines PEM onset, and the
paper does not claim one.

## The pacing meta-analysis

**Sanal-Hayes et al. (2024/2025), *Fatigue: Biomedicine, Health &
Behavior*, DOI 10.1080/21641846.2024.2433390** (verified against the
publisher abstract). A PRISMA-accordant meta-analysis searched six
databases plus grey literature (210 records identified) and selected
five studies with a pacing component and pre/post outcome data: two
RCTs, one uncontrolled trial, one intervention case series, and one
sub-analysis of the PACE trial. Three separate random-effects
meta-analyses were run, each pooling four of the five studies (k=4 per
outcome, since not every study reported every outcome):

- Physical function: SMD = 0.15, 95% CI [−0.39, 0.68], p = .595
- Pain: SMD = −0.11, 95% CI [−0.32, 0.10], p = .309
- Fatigue: SMD = −1.09, 95% CI [−2.38, 0.21], p = .0998

The fatigue point estimate is large, but its confidence interval
crosses zero and the result does not reach conventional statistical
significance. The authors' own framing, quoted precisely: pacing
"exerted a trivial beneficial effect on physical function and pain,"
while fatigue "was improved with a large effect, which did not reach
p < 0.05." Their conclusion is that they "cautiously conclude pacing
likely exerts some beneficial effects," not that the fatigue result is
established. A biometric-guided pacing product should not cite "SMD
−1.09 for fatigue" without the same qualifier the authors themselves
attached to it: four small heterogeneous studies, a wide interval, and
a p-value short of significance.

## Heuristics in clinical use: no validating primary study

The most common heart-rate pacing rule in clinical and patient-advocacy
circulation is the Workwell Foundation's resting-heart-rate-plus-15-bpm
ceiling: stay below RHR+15 bpm during activity. A targeted PubMed
search for a primary validation study of this specific threshold
(searched 2026-09-01, terms combining Workwell, heart-rate ceiling,
resting heart rate +15, and chronic fatigue) returned zero results.
This is consistent with the rule's origin as clinical practice guidance
from a specialty rehabilitation practice (Workwell Foundation, whose
founders include the Snell/Stevens/Davenport/Van Ness group behind the
2013 CPET study above) rather than a published, tested threshold. It
should be presented to users as a clinical heuristic in wide informal
use, not as a validated instrument — the same distinction the two-day
CPET and HRV literature above draws for its own thresholds.

## The incumbent

Visible, a commercial pacing app, markets "PacePoints," a proprietary
zone-based exertion score derived from wearable heart-rate data. Its
scoring formula is not published, so it cannot be assessed against the
evidence above — neither confirmed nor falsified. Any biometric-guided
pacing tool built from this evidence base should be able to state, in
contrast, exactly which published finding (or heuristic, labeled as
such) backs each number it shows a user.

## What this licenses a supportive tool to do

Given the evidence above, in one place:

- **Describe.** Show a person their own heart rate, HRV, and activity
  history, and let them see how today compares to their own past days.
  This is supported by nothing more than the instruments' basic
  measurement validity — no disputed claim is involved.
- **Flag, against a person's own baseline.** Surface when today's
  values (elevated heart rate at rest, suppressed HRV, exertion near a
  person's own previously observed VT1-adjacent range) deviate from
  that person's own history, framed as "this differs from your usual
  pattern" — consistent with the Ruijgt et al. finding that
  above-threshold exertion associates with prolonged HRV suppression
  in that person, not with a population-level PEM-detection threshold.
- **Cite heuristics as heuristics.** Offer the Workwell RHR+15 rule, if
  offered at all, labeled explicitly as an informal clinical rule of
  thumb without a validating primary study — never presented with the
  authority of a tested instrument.
- **Never diagnose.** The tool must not claim to detect, predict, or
  confirm post-exertional malaise from biometric data. The CPET marker
  PEM detection would need to rest on is itself contested by a larger,
  more recent, controlled replication that found no effect
  (Mancini 2026 vs. Keller 2014 and Snell 2013); the HRV-prediction and
  biofeedback evidence is each a single, small, unreplicated cohort in
  a population (long COVID) related to but distinct from ME/CFS; and
  the pacing meta-analysis's own fatigue finding falls short of
  statistical significance. None of that is a foundation for a
  diagnostic claim, and describing it as one would overstate every
  study cited here beyond what its own authors claim for it.
