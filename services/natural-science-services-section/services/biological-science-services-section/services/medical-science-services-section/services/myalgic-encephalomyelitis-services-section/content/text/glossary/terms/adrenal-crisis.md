An adrenal crisis is a state of acute hemodynamic collapse characterized by severe hypotension (systolic blood pressure below 80 mmHg) that does not respond to aggressive fluid resuscitation, profound hypoglycemia (blood glucose below 40 mg/dL), hyponatremia (sodium below 120 mEq/L), and altered mental status. The clinical presentation is sudden: the patient becomes profoundly weak or paralyzed, experiences severe abdominal pain and vomiting, and may lose consciousness. This is a medical emergency; without immediate treatment the patient enters refractory shock and dies within hours. The defining feature of adrenal crisis, distinguishing it from other causes of shock, is its specific treatment response: hypotension and hyponatremia that do not improve with maximum doses of vasopressors (norepinephrine, dopamine, epinephrine) but respond dramatically within 15–30 minutes to parenteral glucocorticoid (intravenous hydrocortisone or dexamethasone).

The underlying cause is sudden, severe deficit of glucocorticoid hormones, whether from primary failure of the adrenal gland, pituitary failure, or sudden withdrawal of glucocorticoid replacement therapy. The immediate trigger in a susceptible person is typically an acute stressor: infection (sepsis, pneumonia, meningitis), physical trauma, surgery, myocardial infarction, or unexpected medication discontinuation. But the essence of the crisis is not the trigger itself; it is the hemodynamic and metabolic failure that occurs when the body cannot sustain vascular tone and glucose production under acute demand. Without adequate glucocorticoid signaling, the cardiovascular system cannot maintain responsiveness to its own catecholamine surge, and the liver cannot mobilize glucose effectively. The result is paradoxical: sympathetic activation is present but produces erratic vasoconstriction and profound hypotension because the glucocorticoid permissiveness for catecholamine action is absent.

Treatment must be immediate: intravenous hydrocortisone 100 mg or dexamethasone 4 mg is given on clinical suspicion alone, not delayed for test confirmation. This is one of the few medical emergencies where treatment is correct to begin on suspicion, because delay itself is the danger — the longer the patient remains untreated, the more likely irreversible shock and death. After glucocorticoid is given, aggressive fluid resuscitation with normal saline is administered, blood glucose and electrolytes are corrected, and any identified precipitating cause (infection, hemorrhage) is treated. Intensive care monitoring is mandatory. Hypotension typically begins to respond within 15–30 minutes of glucocorticoid administration; failure to respond raises the question of alternative diagnoses or complications.

In patients whose underlying condition is known, crisis is preventable: they are instructed to increase glucocorticoid doses during any acute illness or physiological stress, to keep injectable glucocorticoid immediately available, and to wear medical alert identification. In previously undiagnosed cases, the first crisis is often the event that reveals the underlying pathology and becomes the diagnostic turning point that enables subsequent appropriate management.

For clinical practice, the consequence is clear: any patient with acute unexplained hypotension, hypoglycemia, and altered mental status, in any clinical context, should receive glucocorticoid on suspicion if glucocorticoid deficiency is at all in the differential. The harm from unnecessary glucocorticoid use in acute settings is negligible; the harm from missed crisis is death.

## Related terms

- Cortisol — the glucocorticoid hormone whose deficiency is the proximal cause of crisis
- Glucocorticoid — the hormone class whose signaling fails in crisis
- Hypotension — the cardinal hemodynamic finding

- Hyponatremia — the electrolyte derangement that can cause seizures
- Shock — the hemodynamic state characterizing crisis
- Catecholamine — the hormone whose actions are rendered ineffective without glucocorticoid permissiveness
