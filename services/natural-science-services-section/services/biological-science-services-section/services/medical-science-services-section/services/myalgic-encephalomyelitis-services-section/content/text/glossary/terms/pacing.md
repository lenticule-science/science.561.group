Pacing is an activity management strategy whose explicit aim is to keep exertion within an individual's current metabolic and autonomic capacity and thereby prevent triggering post-exertional malaise. The strategy operates on the hypothesis that post-exertional malaise is triggered when demand exceeds capacity — when a person attempts exertion their mitochondrial output cannot sustain aerobically, forcing a shift to anaerobic metabolism, or when autonomic demand exceeds the available glucocorticoid and catecholamine support. Pacing aims to identify that threshold and stay beneath it, rather than attempting to expand capacity through progressive exertion.

Implementation of pacing takes several forms, all aimed at the same end of matching exertion to current capacity. One approach is heart-rate-threshold pacing, in which the patient monitors heart rate during activity and stops or reduces intensity when it rises above a predetermined ceiling (typically around 50–60% of age-predicted maximum, well below the threshold for maximal effort). Another is pre-emptive rest: dividing the day into blocks of activity separated by mandated rest periods, acknowledging that even subclinical exertion — thinking, social interaction, standing — carries metabolic cost, and preventing the gradual accumulation of deficit that leads to triggering post-exertional malaise. Symptom-contingent pacing adjusts activity based on the day's symptom baseline: when cognitive fog or fatigue worsens, exertion is reduced regardless of what was planned. The common thread is a discipline of staying within capacity and accepting reduced activity rather than accepting the cost of triggering malaise.

Pacing is often confounded with graded exercise therapy, and the distinction is fundamental and should be stated plainly. Graded exercise therapy prescribes progressive increases in exercise intensity and duration on a predetermined schedule, regardless of symptom response — the rationale being that deconditioning and catastrophic thinking about exercise perpetuate fatigue, and that breaking those cycles requires patients to override their symptoms and steadily increase exertion. The physiological logic of graded exercise therapy is sound in many fatiguing illnesses: deconditioning can be reversed by progressive activity increase, and symptom-focused avoidance can perpetuate disability. In myalgic encephalomyelitis, however, the logic inverts. The core pathophysiology is not deconditioning — it is metabolic and autonomic dysfunction — and the cardinal feature is that exertion beyond capacity triggers a disproportionate and prolonged response. Prescribing progressive exertion that systematically exceeds capacity does not reverse the deconditioning; it repeatedly triggers post-exertional malaise and may consolidate rather than resolve the illness. The two strategies are mechanistically opposite, and applying the wrong one can be harmful.

The evidence base for pacing is limited — there are few randomized controlled trials, and the qualitative evidence and small observational studies show benefit in terms of symptom stability and avoidance of flares. The evidence base against graded exercise in myalgic encephalomyelitis is stronger: the PACE trial, a large randomized controlled trial testing cognitive behavior therapy plus graded exercise versus standard care, had significant methodological problems, including mid-trial changes to outcome measures and recovery thresholds that overlapped with trial entry criteria, which led to overestimation of benefit and led the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence to withdraw its 2006 recommendation for graded-exercise-based treatment and replace it with a caution that pacing may be more appropriate for myalgic encephalomyelitis. The methodological specifics of the PACE trial are matters of published record, not advocacy — the study had real flaws that affect interpretation of its results.

For patient management and clinical research, the consequence is that an individual with myalgic encephalomyelitis should not be prescribed graded exercise on the assumption that it will restore function. Pacing, with its mechanistic rationale grounded in the actual pathophysiology, is a more defensible starting strategy, though it also requires evidence strengthening and careful long-term outcome measurement.

## Related terms
