Graded exercise therapy (GET) is a structured intervention program in which patients increase physical activity according to a fixed, predetermined schedule regardless of symptoms or exercise response. Typical programs start with a baseline activity level (e.g., 5 minutes of walking daily) and increase duration or intensity on a fixed weekly schedule (e.g., by 1 minute or by 5% increments per week), independent of how the patient responds. The underlying rationale, applied successfully in many conditions, is two-fold: first, that deconditioning and activity avoidance perpetuate fatigue and disability, and breaking that cycle through progressive exertion restores physiological capacity; second, that catastrophic thinking about exercise — the fear-avoidance cycle in which beliefs about exercise danger produce symptom-amplifying anxiety — maintains fatigue, and that exerting despite discomfort and habituation to exercise can reverse this cognitive component. For fatiguing conditions where deconditioning is the primary driver (recovered from mononucleosis, recovered from sepsis, or in chronic conditions perpetuated by inactivity), GET has a genuine evidence base and is a reasonable intervention.

The PACE trial (Prospective Study of Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Chronic Fatigue Syndrome) was a large, multi-site, randomized controlled trial conducted in the UK from 2004–2009, comparing cognitive behavior therapy plus graded exercise therapy versus standard care in chronic fatigue syndrome. The trial reported substantial benefit from the intervention. However, the trial had significant methodological problems that have been documented in published critiques and in the reanalysis by David et al. and others. The trial changed its primary outcome measure mid-study; it modified recovery thresholds during the study rather than pre-specifying them; and the recovery threshold for some outcomes (particularly for self-reported well-being and return-to-work) overlapped with the trial entry criteria, meaning some participants were classified as "recovered" despite outcomes objectively similar to their baseline. These changes inflated the apparent effect size. Additionally, the study did not measure the cardinal post-exertional malaise symptom — the characteristic worsening 24–48 hours after exertion — but rather used end-of-study assessments that would miss post-exertional flares. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), which initially recommended GET based partly on PACE, withdrew that recommendation in its 2021 guideline update, stating that evidence for GET in ME/CFS is weak and that some patients report harm, and recommending instead that pacing (remaining within an energy envelope) may be more appropriate.

The mechanistic argument for GET in ME/CFS specifically inverts from its logic in other fatiguing conditions. In myalgic encephalomyelitis, the core pathophysiology is not primarily deconditioning — it is metabolic dysfunction (possible impaired pyruvate dehydrogenase limiting aerobic capacity), autonomic dysregulation (sympathetic output without adequate vascular-compensatory response and glucocorticoid support), and a disproportionate post-exertional malaise response. The cardinal feature is that exertion beyond current metabolic capacity triggers a disproportionate and prolonged symptom flare that lasts 24–48 hours, and repeated exertional challenges that exceed capacity appear to worsen, not improve, the condition over time. Under these circumstances, a fixed-schedule progression that is indifferent to the patient's response is mechanistically inappropriate: a schedule that mandates progressive exertion that systematically exceeds current capacity will repeatedly trigger post-exertional malaise without producing the adaptive training response that occurs in deconditioning. The result of such a program is often consolidation of illness, not reversal. The fact that some ME/CFS patients do improve with structured activity (as has been reported anecdotally) is not evidence for GET; it likely reflects a subgroup with less-severe disease, who may be below the threshold where exertion triggers post-exertional malaise, or patients who spontaneously improve despite the program rather than because of it — the trial's methodological flaws prevent determination of which.

The evidence base for GET in ME/CFS is therefore weak and conditional on which case definition is used to diagnose ME/CFS. In Fukuda-defined cohorts (which may include people with chronic fatigue of diverse etiologies), some studies suggest modest benefit. In IOM/Canadian-Consensus-defined cohorts (which require post-exertional malaise as a mandatory feature), the evidence for GET is absent or suggests harm. For clinical practice, the consequence is clear: GET should not be prescribed as a first-line intervention for ME/CFS, particularly in patients with documented post-exertional malaise. Pacing — remaining within an energy envelope and symptom-responsive adjustment — is a more defensible starting approach, though the evidence base for any structured activity intervention in ME/CFS remains weak and deserves strengthening through rigorous research.

For patients and researchers, the critical issue is that the calendar-based progression of GET is by construction unable to respect the post-exertional malaise response, and this is not a minor implementation detail — it is the fundamental mechanistic incompatibility. An activity program responsive to symptoms would hold exertion within tolerance and advance only when recovery is complete; a fixed-schedule program explicitly ignores recovery and advances regardless. They are opposite strategies, and applying the wrong one can harm.

## Related terms

- Pacing — an activity management strategy emphasizing staying within current capacity, in contrast to graded exercise
- Post-exertional malaise — the symptom exacerbated by graded exercise progression in myalgic encephalomyelitis
- Myalgic encephalomyelitis — the condition for which GET is mechanistically inappropriate
- Deconditioning — a condition for which GET has genuine evidence and efficacy
- Two-day cardiopulmonary exercise testing — an objective measure of post-exertional malaise and a research tool for distinguishing ME/CFS from other fatigue illnesses
