Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome is an autonomic disorder characterized by an excessive increase in heart rate upon assuming an upright posture: a sustained heart-rate increase of at least 30 beats per minute within the first 10 minutes of standing or head-up tilt testing (40 beats per minute in adolescents), occurring without the orthostatic hypotension that defines orthostatic intolerance's most common form, the blood-pressure drop upon standing. The tachycardia is accompanied by characteristic symptoms: dizziness, lightheadedness, palpitations, chest discomfort, tremor, anxiety, fatigue, and cognitive impairment (sometimes called "brain fog"), with symptoms typically worsening upon prolonged standing or in warm environments, and improvement with supine posture or lying down. POTS is far more common in women than men, and has a typical onset in adolescence or early adulthood, though it can emerge at any age.

The diagnostic measurement is standardized: a 5–10 minute supine baseline heart rate and blood pressure, followed by active standing or head-up tilt to 60–80 degrees, with continuous heart rate and blood pressure monitoring for 10–20 minutes. The diagnosis requires sustained tachycardia (≥30 bpm increase, or ≥120 bpm standing if baseline is normal) without a concurrent ≥20 mmHg drop in systolic blood pressure. An incidental clinic pulse rate cannot establish or exclude POTS; the diagnosis requires the formal tilt-table or standing test.

POTS presents with three proposed mechanistic subtypes, though these are not cleanly separable and a given individual may show features of more than one. The neuropathic form shows reduced sudomotor and vasomotor function, implying denervation of distal sympathetic fibers; the hyperadrenergic form shows elevated plasma catecholamines at rest or upon standing, suggesting excessive sympathetic outflow; the hypovolemic form shows reduced plasma volume and exaggerated fluid shifts upon tilting. Some patients cannot be categorized into these subtypes (dysautonomic POTS), and the clinical utility of the distinction remains limited. The distinction from inappropriate sinus tachycardia — persistent elevated resting heart rate (>100 bpm) that worsens with minimal exertion — is important: POTS is provoked by postural change and improving with supine rest, while inappropriate sinus tachycardia is resting and exertional, and the two have different management approaches.

POTS frequently co-occurs with myalgic encephalomyelitis, with reported prevalence estimates ranging from 20% to 80% depending on how myalgic encephalomyelitis is defined and how POTS is tested. The relationship between the two conditions is not established as causal in either direction. POTS might be part of the autonomic dysregulation spectrum of myalgic encephalomyelitis, in which case the same underlying pathophysiology (sympathoadrenal-HPA decoupling and reduced glucocorticoid-driven vascular support) would contribute to both conditions. Alternatively, POTS and myalgic encephalomyelitis might be separable conditions that frequently co-occur in some populations, or POTS might in some cases be secondary to the deconditioning imposed by myalgic encephalomyelitis-related exercise intolerance. Disentangling this relationship is an open research question.

For clinical assessment, the consequence is that orthostatic intolerance or tachycardia in a patient with myalgic encephalomyelitis should be formally evaluated with tilt-table testing or standardized standing testing rather than inferred from symptoms. The test itself carries some burden in patients with myalgic encephalomyelitis, as the exertional demand of the test can trigger post-exertional malaise, which is why informed consent and follow-up support are essential.

## Related terms

- Orthostatic intolerance — the symptom POTS produces, and the broader category of dysfunctional blood-pressure and heart-rate responses to postural change
- Orthostatic hypotension — the blood-pressure-drop form of orthostatic intolerance, from which POTS is distinguished by its absence
- Sympathoadrenal system — the autonomic arm whose excessive or dysregulated output appears to drive POTS
- Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis — the axis whose impaired function may reduce the vascular support needed to compensate for sympathetic tachycardia
- Myalgic encephalomyelitis — the illness with which POTS frequently co-occurs, though causality is unclear
- Dysautonomia — the broader autonomic dysfunction category of which POTS is a specific, measurable form
- Cerebral hypoperfusion — a proposed mechanism for the cognitive symptoms in POTS and their worsening with standing
