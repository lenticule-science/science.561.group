## Case definitions: three instruments, three populations

Myalgic encephalomyelitis research has been conducted under at least three
substantially different case definitions since 1994, and — as with long
COVID's definitional history — a finding under one definition is not
automatically a finding about the population selected by another.

**Fukuda 1994.** The CDC's Fukuda criteria required six or more months of
unexplained persistent or relapsing fatigue that is not the result of
ongoing exertion, is not substantially alleviated by rest, and results in
substantial reduction in activity, plus four or more of eight minor
symptoms (impaired memory or concentration, sore throat, tender lymph
nodes, muscle pain, multi-joint pain, new headaches, unrefreshing sleep,
post-exertional malaise lasting more than 24 hours) (Fukuda et al., 1994,
*Annals of Internal Medicine* 121(12):953-959,
doi:10.7326/0003-4819-121-12-199412150-00009, PMID 7978722). **Established
finding as a consensus artifact**: post-exertional malaise is only one of
eight optional minor criteria under Fukuda, not a required feature — a
Fukuda-defined cohort can be entirely free of the illness's later-defined
cardinal symptom. Fukuda-selected samples are consequently the most
heterogeneous of the three definitions and the most likely to include
people whose fatigue has a different underlying physiology.

**Canadian Consensus Criteria 2003.** An international clinical/research
working group made post-exertional malaise (there termed "post-exertional
malaise and/or fatigue") a mandatory criterion for the first time,
alongside required fatigue, sleep dysfunction, and pain, plus at least two
neurological/cognitive manifestations and at least one manifestation each
from the autonomic, neuroendocrine, and immune categories (Carruthers et
al., 2003, *Journal of Chronic Fatigue Syndrome* 11(1):7-115,
doi:10.1300/j092v11n01_02). This is the first of the three definitions to
require multi-system involvement rather than fatigue alone, and it is the
definition most house carriers on this section treat as the operational
floor for calling a cohort "ME/CFS" rather than "chronic fatigue."

**IOM/NAM 2015.** The US Institute of Medicine (since renamed the National
Academy of Medicine) proposed replacing the name with "systemic exertion
intolerance disease" and elevated post-exertional malaise to the single
cardinal, always-required criterion, alongside substantial reduction in
activity with profound fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, and at least one of
cognitive impairment or orthostatic intolerance (Institute of Medicine,
*Beyond Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome: Redefining an
Illness*, National Academies Press, 2015, doi:10.17226/19012, PMID
25695122). This is the narrowest and most exertion-intolerance-centered of
the three definitions.

**The operational consequence.** These are not three attempts to describe
the same fixed population with different words; they select measurably
different samples, and the long-COVID overlap literature has now quantified
this rather than leaving it as a caveat — see Morita et al. below, where
requiring simultaneous fulfillment of all three definitions in one cohort
moved the reported prevalence down roughly sixfold from any single
definition alone. A finding in a Fukuda-selected sample is not
automatically a finding about an IOM-selected sample, and reviews that
pool across definitions without stratifying by them are pooling across
different populations.

## Epidemiology and prevalence uncertainty

Prevalence estimates for ME/CFS vary by an order of magnitude across
methods, and — as with long COVID — no single number is "the" prevalence.
A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis pooling worldwide studies
reported prevalence estimates ranging from under 0.1% to over 1% of the
population depending on case definition, case-ascertainment method
(clinical diagnosis versus symptom-questionnaire self-report), and study
setting, with pooled estimates differing by roughly an order of magnitude
between the most restrictive and most permissive definitions used across
included studies (Lim et al., 2020, *Journal of Translational Medicine*
18:100, doi:10.1186/s12967-020-02269-0, PMID 32093722). **Established
finding as a range, not a point estimate**: the review's own conclusion is
that heterogeneous case definitions and case-finding methods across the
included literature make a single pooled prevalence figure of limited
value, and that future studies should report case-definition-stratified
estimates.

The commonly cited US clinical estimate — 836,000 to 2.5 million adults —
comes from the IOM 2015 committee's own review of prior prevalence studies,
not from a single new survey (Institute of Medicine, 2015, doi:10.17226/19012).
The nearly threefold spread within that single citation is itself a
restatement of the same definitional problem: broader case-finding
criteria and less-rigorous diagnostic confirmation produce higher counts.
The IOM committee also estimated that 84-91% of people meeting criteria in
population surveys have never received a formal diagnosis, so
clinic-based and claims-based prevalence estimates systematically
undercount community-survey estimates.

## Post-exertional malaise and the two-day CPET: a live replication conflict

Post-exertional malaise (PEM) — a disproportionate, delayed (typically
12-72 hours), and prolonged (days to weeks) worsening of symptoms following
physical, cognitive, orthostatic, or emotional exertion — is the cardinal
feature that both the Canadian Consensus Criteria and the IOM criteria
require, and the feature qualitative research consistently identifies as
the illness's most distinctive and distressing symptom. Stussman and
colleagues' 2020 qualitative study of PEM narratives across 43 people with
ME/CFS at the NIH Clinical Center found participants consistently
describing an abrupt, delayed "crash" following even modest exertion, with
recovery times ranging from days to months and no single trigger threshold
common across participants (Stussman et al., 2020, *Frontiers in Neurology*
11:1025, doi:10.3389/fneur.2020.01025, PMID 33071931). **Established
finding as a qualitative pattern**: the delayed-and-prolonged shape of the
symptom is consistently reported across patients; a single quantitative
trigger threshold that predicts it is not.

The two-day cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET) — a maximal exercise
protocol repeated 24 hours later, testing whether day-two performance
reproduces day-one values — has been treated as the field's closest
approach to an objective marker of PEM, on the logic that healthy people
and people with other fatiguing conditions reproduce day-one values while
ME/CFS patients characteristically do not. As the house's 2026-09-02
pacing-evidence note documents, this marker is now a live, unresolved
replication conflict rather than a settled finding, and this document
adopts that note's account without softening it:

- **Keller, Pryor & Giloteaux (2014)**, 22 subjects with ME/CFS, no
  healthy-control arm: day-two declines of VO₂peak -13.8%, peak heart rate
  -9 bpm, peak minute ventilation -14.7%, and workload at peak -12.5%, with
  larger declines at ventilatory threshold (*Journal of Translational
  Medicine* 12:104, doi:10.1186/1479-5876-12-104, PMID 24755065). A
  within-subject decline, not a group-difference finding — no controls
  were tested.
- **Snell, Stevens, Davenport & Van Ness (2013)**, 51 women with CFS and 10
  sedentary female controls, both tested twice 24 hours apart: no
  significant group difference on day one, but a significant day-two
  decline in VO₂ and workload at peak and at ventilatory threshold in the
  CFS group relative to controls, with 95.1% classification accuracy
  separating the groups on day-two measures alone (*Physical Therapy*
  93(11):1484-1492, doi:10.2522/ptj.20110368, PMID 23813081). The largest
  controlled two-day-CPET study in the record, and it supports the
  day-two-decline finding.
- **Mancini et al. (2026)**, 58 ME/CFS patients and 25 healthy controls,
  both groups tested on two consecutive days: no significant change in
  peak VO₂ from day one to day two in either group; standard
  cardiopulmonary measures were stable across days for both groups
  (*Frontiers in Physiology* 17:1816082, doi:10.3389/fphys.2026.1816082,
  PMID 42212259). The authors' own conclusion, stated directly: their
  data "do not support using the 2-day CPET protocol to define PEM or
  disability." This is larger than either Keller or Snell, the only one
  of the three published in 2026, and it finds no day-two effect.

**The honest state of the evidence, stated plainly**: three studies, no
consensus. Keller and Snell find a day-two decline; the largest and most
recent (Mancini) does not. No study here has been independently
replicated by a separate group under a matched protocol, and sample size
alone does not settle the conflict either way. The two-day CPET is the
field's most-studied candidate objective marker of PEM, contested by a
direct and substantial non-replication — not an established or validated
diagnostic instrument.

## Autonomic findings

**Orthostatic intolerance and postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome
(POTS)** are frequently comorbid with ME/CFS, with reported co-occurrence
estimates spanning a wide range (roughly 20-80%) depending on how each
condition is defined and how orthostatic testing is performed — the same
definitional-heterogeneity problem restated at the intersection of two
conditions rather than one. Grubb's clinical review of POTS describes three
proposed mechanistic subtypes — neuropathic (denervation of distal
sympathetic fibers), hyperadrenergic (excessive sympathetic outflow), and
hypovolemic (reduced plasma volume) — that are not cleanly separable in
individual patients (Grubb, 2008, *Circulation* 117(21):2814-2817,
doi:10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.107.761643, PMID 18506020). The same
denominator-dependence shows up cleanly in the parallel post-COVID POTS
literature: Yong and colleagues' pooled meta-analysis found POTS
prevalence following SARS-CoV-2 infection of roughly 1% at the
population level versus figures an order of magnitude higher in
symptomatic specialty-clinic cohorts (Yong et al., 2023, *Autonomic
Neuroscience* 250:103132, doi:10.1016/j.autneu.2023.103132, PMID
38000119) — no comparably sized pooled ME/CFS-specific POTS estimate exists yet. **Established
finding**: POTS and ME/CFS co-occur at rates well above general-population
prevalence. **Unresolved**: whether POTS in this population reflects the
same underlying sympathoadrenal-HPA dysregulation proposed for ME/CFS more
broadly, is a separable comorbid condition, or is in some patients
secondary to illness-imposed deconditioning — the long-COVID dysautonomia
literature this section's sibling section has synthesized documents the
same three-way uncertainty for POTS following SARS-CoV-2 infection, and
it is unresolved for the ME/CFS population too.

**Cerebral blood flow during orthostatic challenge.** A quantitative,
controlled Doppler-echography study measured cerebral blood flow during
head-up tilt testing in 429 ME/CFS patients — subdivided by orthostatic-
testing phenotype into a normal heart-rate/blood-pressure response group
(n=247), a delayed-orthostatic-hypotension group (n=62), and a POTS group
(n=120) — against 44 separate healthy controls, finding a significant
reduction in cerebral blood flow during tilt in all three ME/CFS subgroups
relative to controls, including in the subgroup whose heart rate and blood
pressure readings remained within normal range during the same test (van
Campen, Verheugt, Rowe & Visser, 2020, *Clinical Neurophysiology Practice*
5:50-58, doi:10.1016/j.cnp.2020.01.003, PMID 32140630). POTS here names an
orthostatic-testing subtype within this ME/CFS cohort, not a separate
non-ME/CFS comparison population. **Mechanistic finding, single research group, awaiting independent
replication with a larger sample**: the pattern — cerebral hypoperfusion
despite normal heart rate and blood pressure — is exactly what the
house's decoupling note predicts if catecholamines are present but
vascular tone support from cortisol permissiveness is insufficient; this
study alone does not establish the mechanism, only that the hemodynamic
finding is real and measurable in this cohort.

## Neuroendocrine findings

**HPA-axis hypocortisolism.** Cleare's 2003 review of the neuroendocrinology
of chronic fatigue syndrome synthesizes a body of smaller studies reporting
mildly reduced basal cortisol output, a blunted cortisol awakening
response, and attenuated adrenocortical response to corticotropin-releasing
hormone and ACTH challenge in ME/CFS patients relative to healthy controls
(Cleare, 2003, *Endocrine Reviews* 24(2):236-252, doi:10.1210/er.2002-0014,
PMID 12700181). Papadopoulos and Cleare's later review restates this pattern and is
explicit that the alterations are modest — closer to the low end of
normal than to profound Addisonian hypocortisolism — with no consistent
primary lesion identified at the hypothalamic, pituitary, or adrenal
level across studies (Papadopoulos & Cleare, 2012, *Nature Reviews
Endocrinology* 8(1):22-32, doi:10.1038/nrendo.2011.153, PMID 21946893). **Established
finding, replicated across multiple smaller studies, but modest in
magnitude**: relative hypocortisolism is a real, recurring pattern in
ME/CFS cohorts, not an artifact of any single lab; it does not meet
clinical criteria for adrenal insufficiency (see the house glossary term
on that distinction), and its cause is unresolved.

**The direction-of-causation problem.** This is the central open question
in the neuroendocrine literature and it deserves stating without a
preferred answer, because the field itself has not settled it. Tak and
colleagues' meta-analysis of 85 studies on HPA-axis activity across
functional somatic disorders — chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, and
irritable bowel syndrome pooled together — found the pooled association
between basal hypocortisolism and FSD did not reach statistical
significance (SMD -0.07, 95% CI -0.17 to 0.04, p=0.241); assessed
separately, statistically significant basal hypocortisolism held only in
the CFS subgroup (SMD -0.14, 95% CI -0.28 to 0.00, p=0.047), not in
fibromyalgia or irritable bowel syndrome. The authors' own conclusion
declines to consider all three FSDs hypocortisolemic disorders on this
evidence. The meta-regression could not distinguish, for the CFS-specific
signal, whether the hypocortisolism is a primary feature of the condition,
a consequence of chronic illness duration, disrupted sleep, and inactivity
(the same confounds the pacing evidence note names for the CPET
literature), or a product of publication and selection biases in a field
with strong prior expectations (Tak et al., 2011, *Biological Psychology*
87(2):183-194, doi:10.1016/j.biopsycho.2011.02.002, PMID 21315796). This is the same unresolved question the house's decoupling note names
as its own weakest premise: "if HPA blunting in ME/CFS turns out to be
secondary to deconditioning, sleep disruption, or illness duration rather
than primary... the endocrine arm of the argument is an effect rather
than a cause." No study here has distinguished cause from consequence
with a prospective, pre-illness baseline.

**Glucocorticoid resistance as a distinct hypothesis.** Separately from
whether cortisol *output* is reduced, some of the literature proposes
impaired target-cell responsiveness to circulating cortisol — a
receptor-level rather than a hormone-availability problem, requiring
functional (ex vivo dexamethasone-suppression) testing rather than a serum
cortisol level to detect (see the house glossary term on glucocorticoid
resistance, which describes the existing functional-assay evidence as
mixed). This remains a plausible but unestablished mechanism.

## Metabolic findings

**Systemic metabolomics.** Naviaux and colleagues' broad metabolomic
profiling studied 84 subjects total — 45 who met ME/CFS diagnostic
criteria by IOM, Canadian Consensus, and Fukuda definitions together, and
39 age- and sex-matched controls — targeting 612 metabolites across 63
biochemical pathways in plasma. Patients showed abnormalities in 20 of the
63 pathways studied, with 80% of the diagnostic metabolites (the smaller
panel used to build the study's diagnostic classifier, not all 612 tested)
decreased, consistent with a hypometabolic "cell danger response" state,
and sex-specific classifiers reaching 94-96% diagnostic accuracy in this
cohort (Naviaux et al., 2016, *PNAS* 113(37):E5472-E5480,
doi:10.1073/pnas.1607571113, PMID 27573827).
**Single-cohort mechanistic hypothesis, with a real independent
replication attempt.** Germain, Ruppert, Levine and Hanson's 2018 targeted
metabolomic study of a separate cohort tested specific Naviaux-implicated
pathways and found partial concordance — some redox-imbalance-consistent
metabolite alterations replicated, others did not — describing the overall
pattern as consistent with disease-associated oxidative stress and altered
lipid metabolism while explicitly not reproducing the full Naviaux
signature (Germain, Ruppert, Levine & Hanson, 2018, *Metabolites* 8(4):90,
doi:10.3390/metabo8040090, PMID 30563204). **The honest state**: broad
metabolomic dysregulation in ME/CFS is a real and independently observed
phenomenon across at least two cohorts; the specific proposed
"hypometabolic" signature from the original study is a partial, not full,
replication, and metabolomic findings across the wider ME/CFS literature
are not yet consistent enough to name a single agreed biochemical
signature.

**Pyruvate dehydrogenase and the anaerobic-threshold hypothesis.** The
leading specific mechanistic account for the two-day CPET's proposed
day-two decline implicates impaired pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) activity,
the enzyme complex gating entry of pyruvate into the citric acid cycle;
reduced PDH activity would force cells toward anaerobic metabolism at
lower workloads than normal, prematurely elevating lactate and lowering
the ventilatory threshold. Fluge and colleagues' metabolic profiling study
in a cohort of ME/CFS patients reported findings consistent with PDH
inhibition — an altered amino-acid and fatty-acid profile suggestive of
a shift away from carbohydrate oxidation toward fat and amino-acid
oxidation for energy, interpreted as compensating for a PDH-limited
pathway (Fluge et al., 2016, *JCI Insight* 1(21):e89376,
doi:10.1172/jci.insight.89376, PMID 28018972). **Mechanistic hypothesis,
single research group, consistent with but not a direct enzymatic
measurement of PDH activity**: the study infers PDH involvement from a
metabolite profile rather than measuring PDH enzyme activity directly in
these patients, and — as the house glossary term on two-day CPET already
states — this hypothesis and the competing autonomic-dysfunction
hypothesis for the CPET's day-two decline are not mutually exclusive, and
neither has strong independent replication.

## Immune findings

**NK-cell cytotoxicity.** Reduced natural-killer-cell cytotoxic function is
one of the most frequently reported immune findings in ME/CFS across
several decades of smaller studies; Brenu and colleagues' primary
case-control study of 95 CFS/ME patients and 50 healthy controls found
significantly reduced NK- and CD8+-T-cell cytotoxic activity and reduced
CD56bright NK-cell counts, alongside increased IL-10, IFN-γ, and TNF-α,
expanded CD4+CD25+/FoxP3+ regulatory T cells, and altered granzyme
(decreased A and K) and perforin (increased) expression relative to
controls (Brenu et al., 2011, *Journal of Translational Medicine* 9:81,
doi:10.1186/1479-5876-9-81, PMID 21619669). This is one cohort's finding, not a synthesis across the wider
literature; the broader pattern of reduced NK-cell cytotoxicity across
independent studies is separately documented below. **Frequently
reported across the literature, but not uniformly replicated across
independent cohorts using standardized assay protocols**: NK-cell
cytotoxicity assays are notoriously sensitive to sample handling,
timing, and assay method, and the review itself frames the finding as a
promising biomarker candidate rather than an established diagnostic
feature.

**Cytokine studies.** Montoya and colleagues profiled 51 cytokines in
serum from 192 ME/CFS patients and 392 healthy controls and reported that
17 cytokines correlated with symptom severity, with a specific
proinflammatory-skewed pattern (elevated TGF-β among others) associated
with more severe disease — described by the authors as the first evidence
directly linking cytokine levels to symptom severity gradations within
ME/CFS rather than merely to disease-versus-control status (Montoya et
al., 2017, *PNAS* 114(34):E7150-E7158, doi:10.1073/pnas.1710519114, PMID
28760971). **A single, well-powered study reporting a severity-gradient
association, against a wider literature that has not replicated a
consistent signature.** Corbitt and colleagues' systematic review
of the cytokine literature in CFS/ME/SEID found substantial
inconsistency across studies in which specific cytokines were elevated,
depressed, or unchanged, attributing the disagreement to small sample sizes, heterogeneous case
definitions, and non-standardized assay platforms, and concluding no
single cytokine or small panel is yet consistently replicated
(Corbitt et al., 2019, *BMC Neurology* 19:207,
doi:10.1186/s12883-019-1433-0, PMID 31445522). **The honest state**:
immune activation broadly is a recurring theme across the ME/CFS cytokine
literature; a specific, reproducible cytokine signature is not
established, and Montoya's severity-correlation finding — real and
statistically strong in its own cohort — has not yet been replicated as a
standalone diagnostic or severity marker in an independent sample.

**Autoantibodies.** Loebel and colleagues measured antibodies against
α- and β-adrenergic, muscarinic (M1-5) acetylcholine, dopamine,
serotonin, angiotensin, and endothelin receptors in a CFS cohort (n=268)
against healthy controls (n=108), and found antibodies against β2-,
M3-, and M4-muscarinic-cholinergic receptors significantly elevated
(other receptor classes did not differ from controls); elevated β2
antibody levels correlated with more frequently activated HLA-DR+ T
cells and thyroid-peroxidase/anti-nuclear autoantibodies, and elevated
β2 and M4 antibodies declined significantly in clinical responders (but
not non-responders) to B-cell-depleting rituximab treatment in a
separate Bergen sub-cohort — the study does not report a direct
symptom-severity correlation. The authors propose — as in
the parallel long-COVID GPCR-autoantibody literature — that these act as
functional receptor agonists disrupting sympathetic/parasympathetic
signaling (Loebel et al., 2016, *Brain, Behavior, and Immunity*
52:32-39, doi:10.1016/j.bbi.2015.09.013, PMID 26399744). **Mechanistic hypothesis, single-group finding, not yet independently
replicated with the standardized case-control design that produced a
null result in the long-COVID POTS literature.** Sotzny
and colleagues' broader review argues the accumulated evidence — including
these autoantibody findings, HLA associations, and treatment-response
signals from B-cell-depleting therapy in some trials — is jointly
consistent with an autoimmune contribution to at least a subset of ME/CFS,
while explicitly stopping short of calling ME/CFS an established
autoimmune disease (Sotzny et al., 2018, *Autoimmunity Reviews*
17(6):601-609, doi:10.1016/j.autrev.2018.01.009, PMID 29635081). **This is
a review's synthesis judgment, not a new primary finding**, and it should
be read as marking autoimmunity as a live, structured hypothesis for a
patient subset rather than a settled mechanism for the illness as a
whole — exactly the same status the immune-dysregulation note assigns the
parallel long-COVID autoantibody hypothesis.

## Neuroinflammation

**PET microglial activation.** Nakatomi and colleagues used
¹¹C-(R)-PK11195 positron emission tomography, a translocator-protein (TSPO)
ligand used as a marker of activated microglia, to compare 9 ME/CFS
patients against 10 healthy controls, and reported significantly higher
binding — interpreted as greater neuroinflammation — in multiple brain
regions in the ME/CFS group, with binding in several regions correlating
with specific symptom severity scores (Nakatomi et al., 2014, *Journal of
Nuclear Medicine* 55(6):945-950, doi:10.2967/jnumed.113.131045, PMID
24665088). **A single small study (n=9 patients) that has not been
independently replicated, and whose method carries an independent
interpretive controversy**: TSPO-ligand PET binding is used across
neurology and psychiatry as a proxy for microglial activation, but the
biological specificity of TSPO binding — whether it tracks microglial
activation state cleanly, or is confounded by TSPO genetic polymorphisms
and non-microglial expression — is contested in the wider neuroimaging
literature independent of anything specific to ME/CFS. The house's decoupling note names this study as the neuroinflammatory
arm's single load-bearing piece of direct evidence and flags it,
correctly, as the weakest-evidenced of the four systems: a small sample,
unreplicated, resting on a contested imaging method.

## Overlap with long COVID

The long-COVID section's own note on the ME/CFS overlap already documents
this in detail; this section restates the load-bearing findings rather
than re-deriving them, because they bear directly on how ME/CFS mechanistic
claims should and should not be extended.

**A substantial minority to majority of long COVID meets ME/CFS criteria.**
The strongest current synthesis is Dehlia and Guthridge's 2024 meta-analysis
of 13 clinical studies (n=1,973 pooled): 51% (95% CI 42-60%) of long COVID
patients met ME/CFS diagnostic criteria (Dehlia & Guthridge, 2024, *Journal
of Infection* 89(6):106297, doi:10.1016/j.jinf.2024.106297, PMID 39353473).
Individual studies anchoring that pooled estimate span a wide range for
reasons that are themselves informative: Kedor et al.'s prospective German
cohort found 45% (19/42) met Canadian Consensus Criteria (Kedor et al.,
2022, *Nature Communications* 13:5104, doi:10.1038/s41467-022-32507-6, PMID
36042189); Bonilla and colleagues' Stanford PASC-clinic cohort found 43%
(45/105) met IOM criteria (Bonilla et al., 2023, *Frontiers in Neurology*
14:1090747, doi:10.3389/fneur.2023.1090747, PMID 36908615); Jason and
Dorri's 465-person survey using the DePaul Symptom Questionnaire found 58%
met criteria, with self-reported ME/CFS status an unreliable proxy for
formal criteria-based diagnosis (Jason & Dorri, 2022, *Neurology
International* 15(1):1-11, doi:10.3390/neurolint15010001, PMID 36648965).
Morita and colleagues' Japanese cohort (n=739) is the field's clearest
demonstration of case-definition sensitivity: requiring simultaneous
fulfillment of Fukuda, Canadian Consensus, and IOM criteria together
(rather than any one alone) produced an 8.4% (62/739) estimate — roughly a
sixfold reduction from the single-definition estimates above, in the same
kind of population (Morita et al., 2024, *PLOS ONE* 19(12):e0315385,
doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0315385, PMID 39652555).

**Post-exertional malaise prevalence in long COVID converges with the
ME/CFS-criteria estimate.** An and colleagues' 2024 meta-analysis pooling
12 studies (2,665 participants) found 55% of people with PASC reported PEM
at approximately the three-month mark (An et al., 2024, *General Hospital
Psychiatry* 91:130-142, doi:10.1016/j.genhosppsych.2024.10.011, PMID
39490027). This figure and the ~51% ME/CFS-criteria figure above are
independently derived from different instruments and different study sets;
their convergence is suggestive of a real, consistent subgroup rather than
evidence that the two numbers are measuring the same thing twice.

**SARS-CoV-2 as a documented trigger for new-onset ME/CFS, and the
historical precedent that it is not unique in this respect.** Hadidchi and
colleagues' retrospective cohort of 36,076 COVID-positive versus 111,301
COVID-negative controls without a prior fatigue/CFS diagnosis found an
elevated adjusted hazard ratio for new-onset CFS/ME in the COVID-positive
group (aHR 1.56, 95% CI 1.25-1.93 for non-hospitalized COVID-19), with no
further elevation on reinfection (Hadidchi et al., 2025, *Journal of
Translational Medicine* 23:815, doi:10.1186/s12967-025-06625-w, PMID
40702518). This is a large linked-EHR cohort with matched controls, stronger
causal-inference-adjacent evidence than the cross-sectional overlap
figures above. It is not, however, a novel phenomenon: Lam and
colleagues' 2009 follow-up of SARS-CoV-1 survivors at a mean of 41.3 months
post-infection found 27.1% (233 total survivors) met modified 1994 Fukuda
CFS criteria (Lam et al., 2009, *Archives of Internal Medicine*
169(22):2142-2147, doi:10.1001/archinternmed.2009.384, PMID 20008700) — a
figure in the same range as the SARS-CoV-2 overlap estimates, documented
over a decade before COVID-19 existed. **What this licenses**: treating viral infection generally, not
SARS-CoV-2 specifically, as a documented trigger class for the ME/CFS
phenotype, and treating the two mechanistic literatures as evidentially
relevant to each other for the overlapping subgroup. **What it does not
license**: mechanistic identity between the two conditions, extending
ME/CFS-specific findings (including this document's closing hypothesis)
to the long-COVID majority that does not meet ME/CFS criteria, or the
reverse — long-COVID population-level findings holding for the
ME/CFS-meeting minority without explicit stratification.

## Treatment evidence: pacing, PACE, and NICE NG206

**Pacing.** The one meta-analysis of pacing as an intervention — Sanal-Hayes
and colleagues' pooling of five studies (two RCTs, one uncontrolled trial,
one case series, one PACE-trial sub-analysis) — found a large point
estimate for fatigue improvement (SMD -1.09) that did not reach
conventional statistical significance (95% CI -2.38 to 0.21, p=.0998), and
smaller, non-significant effects for physical function (SMD 0.15) and pain
(SMD -0.11) (Sanal-Hayes et al., 2024, *Fatigue: Biomedicine, Health &
Behavior* 13(1):36-53, doi:10.1080/21641846.2024.2433390). The authors'
own framing, quoted precisely: they "cautiously conclude pacing likely
exerts some beneficial effects," not that the fatigue finding is
established. **Evidence base: few controlled trials, mostly small and
heterogeneous, a large but statistically inconclusive effect estimate for
the outcome (fatigue) most relevant to the illness, and a mechanistic
rationale — grounded in the metabolic and autonomic findings synthesized
above rather than in a deconditioning model — that the field currently
judges better-supported than graded exercise therapy's opposing
rationale.**

**The PACE trial and its reanalysis.** The PACE trial was a large,
multi-site UK randomized controlled trial comparing cognitive behavioral
therapy plus graded exercise therapy, adaptive pacing therapy, and
specialist medical care alone, reporting substantial benefit for CBT and
graded exercise on its primary outcomes (White et al., 2011, *The Lancet*
377(9768):823-836, doi:10.1016/s0140-6736(11)60096-2, PMID 21334061).
Wilshire and colleagues' independent reanalysis, using the trial's
originally pre-specified outcome thresholds rather than the thresholds the
investigators substituted mid-trial, found that recovery rates dropped
substantially from those originally reported, and identified that some
participants' trial-entry criteria overlapped with the post-treatment
"recovery" thresholds — meaning a participant could in principle enter the
trial already meeting a recovery criterion (Wilshire et al., 2018, *BMC
Psychology* 6:6, doi:10.1186/s40359-018-0218-3, PMID 29562932). **The
reanalysis is itself a peer-reviewed, published finding, not an
unreviewed critique**, and it materially changes what can be claimed about
PACE's effect sizes; the trial also did not directly measure
post-exertional malaise as an outcome, using end-of-study assessments that
would not capture a PEM flare occurring in the days after an exertional
episode.

**NICE NG206 (2021).** The UK National Institute for Health and Care
Excellence's 2021 guideline update withdrew its earlier recommendation for
graded exercise therapy in ME/CFS and stated that the evidence supporting
GET was of low to very low quality, with reports of harm from some
patients, replacing that recommendation with guidance favoring
individualized, symptom-responsive activity management consistent with
pacing (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, *NG206*, 2021).
**This is a national guideline body's position, arrived at through its
own evidence-grading process rather than a single new trial** — it is
included here as the current regulatory-guidance state, not as
independent primary evidence beyond what the PACE reanalysis and the
pacing meta-analysis already establish. It should be read as: the field's
guideline-setting body found the existing GET evidence base too weak to
recommend the intervention as first-line treatment, given documented harm
reports and the mechanistic case (metabolic and autonomic dysfunction
rather than deconditioning) that this document's metabolic and autonomic
sections above lay out.

## Closing: decoupling as a hypothesis, held against the evidence above

This section's organizing claim — synthesized at length in the house's
2026-09-01 decoupling note — is that ME/CFS's four-system findings are
better read as one coordination failure between the fast, neural
sympathoadrenal arm of the stress response and the slow, hormonal HPA arm,
than as four independent deficits. That note already marks its own
falsification conditions in detail; this closing section states the claim
plainly one more time, against the evidence this document has now laid
out system by system, and names what would settle it either way.

**Consistent with the hypothesis.** The autonomic findings above — POTS
co-occurrence at elevated rates, and specifically the van Campen finding
of cerebral hypoperfusion during tilt testing *despite normal heart rate
and blood pressure readings* — are exactly the signature the decoupling
account predicts: catecholamine output present and functioning enough to
produce tachycardia in some patients, but vascular tone support (which
depends on cortisol's permissive action on α1-adrenergic responsiveness)
insufficient to prevent perfusion failure. The neuroendocrine findings —
consistent, if modest, hypocortisolism and blunted CRH/ACTH-challenge
responses across multiple studies — supply the "insufficient funding" half
of the mechanism. The metabolic findings — reduced VO₂ and ventilatory
threshold on day two of CPET in two of three studies, and a PDH-inhibition
metabolite signature in one cohort — are consistent with cells reaching
anaerobic metabolism earlier than expected when hormonal substrate
mobilization is impaired. And the temporal mismatch between an acute,
minutes-scale sympathoadrenal surge and a PEM flare that arrives a day or
two later and lasts a week is explained, on this account, by an
unterminated inflammatory cascade rather than by the acute event itself —
consistent with the cytokine and (more weakly) neuroinflammatory findings
above, though none of those findings directly demonstrates the specific
termination-failure mechanism proposed.

**Strains the hypothesis.** Three findings sit uncomfortably with a clean
two-arm decoupling story. First, the Mancini 2026 non-replication of the
day-two CPET decline — the largest and most recent of the three studies —
means the metabolic arm's most-cited objective signature is contested,
not settled, and a hypothesis built partly on it inherits that
uncertainty. Second, Tak and colleagues' direction-of-causation problem
could invert the whole account: if hypocortisolism is a downstream
consequence of illness duration, sleep disruption, and inactivity rather
than a primary lesion, the "HPA arm fails first" premise the decoupling
note depends on is wrong, and blunted cortisol becomes an effect sitting
beside PEM rather than a cause of it. Third, the immune findings are the
least consistent of the four systems surveyed here: no cytokine panel has
replicated across independent cohorts, and the single neuroinflammation
PET study is small, unreplicated, and rests on a contested imaging
method. A synthesis that leans on "the inflammatory arm is unbraked" to
explain PEM's delayed, multi-day timescale leans on the
weakest-evidenced of its four pillars.

**What would falsify this.** Three observations, stated in advance, would
count as evidence against the decoupling hypothesis specifically —
distinct from evidence merely showing one of its component findings fails
to replicate, which would weaken but not by itself falsify the
integrative claim:

1. A prospective study following people from before illness onset (or from
   very early post-infection, in a post-viral-onset subgroup) that finds
   HPA-axis blunting *follows* rather than *precedes* the onset of
   post-exertional malaise and functional decline — this would invert the
   causal arrow the decoupling account requires and support the
   effect-not-cause reading Tak and colleagues' meta-regression already
   leaves open.
2. A well-powered, cross-lab-replicated two-day CPET study (or a direct
   PDH-activity assay, rather than an inferred metabolite profile) that
   confirms Mancini's null result and finds no day-two decline in any
   independently ascertained ME/CFS cohort — this would remove the
   metabolic arm's central empirical anchor and require the whole
   synthesis to be rebuilt around a different objective correlate of PEM,
   if one exists.
3. A study directly testing cortisol's proposed *termination* function in
   ME/CFS — for example, tracking cytokine time-courses after a
   controlled exertional challenge and finding that cytokine levels return
   to baseline on the same timescale in ME/CFS patients as in healthy
   controls, rather than persisting for the multi-day span PEM itself
   occupies — would remove the specific mechanism this document proposes
   for why the flare is delayed and prolonged rather than acute and brief,
   even if the other three systems' findings stood.

None of these observations exists yet as a completed study; each is a
research design this synthesis implies is worth running, because it is a
hypothesis under test, not a conclusion reached. The evidence above is
consistent with the decoupling frame's broad shape — full
sympathoadrenal output paired with insufficient HPA-axis support, over a
timescale the inflammatory arm's persistence rather than magnitude
explains — without any single system's evidence strong enough, on its
own, to make that frame more than the field's best current organizing
hypothesis for findings that, read individually, remain "four modest and
individually unimpressive findings, each with mixed replication."
