Myalgic encephalomyelitis is a multi-system illness characterized by a constellation of neuroendocrine, autonomic, metabolic, and neuroinflammatory dysfunction. It commonly follows an infection — viral or bacterial, often acute but sometimes insidious — and presents with cardinal symptoms of post-exertional malaise, unrefreshing sleep, cognitive impairment, and orthostatic intolerance, though the prominence of any given symptom varies widely between patients. The name itself asserts inflammation of the brain and spinal cord that has not been consistently demonstrated, and for that reason remains contested among clinicians; the label "systemic exertion intolerance disease" proposed by the 2015 US Institute of Medicine report makes exertional intolerance rather than encephalomyelitis the defining feature.

Case definitions have evolved substantially since the illness was first formally characterized. The 1994 Fukuda definition centered fatigue and required exclusion of alternative explanations; the 2003 Canadian Consensus Criteria made post-exertional malaise a mandatory feature and added immunological, neurological, and endocrine parameters; the 2015 Institute of Medicine report elevated post-exertional malaise to the cardinal diagnostic criterion and required it to be present alongside three of four additional major symptom clusters. These definitions select measurably different populations from clinical samples, which is a leading hypothesis for why ME/CFS research produces inconsistent findings — a study of Fukuda-defined patients is studying a different population than a study of IOM-defined patients, and the biological findings cannot be directly compared.

The distinction between myalgic encephalomyelitis and chronic fatigue as a symptom, or from idiopathic chronic fatigue unrelated to infection or from depression, is clinically essential and scientifically contested. Fatigue is a symptom; the illness is recognized by post-exertional malaise, the specific character of cognitive impairment, and the pattern of autonomic dysfunction, taken together. Depression can coexist with myalgic encephalomyelitis and can superficially resemble it, but the key distinguishing feature is that in myalgic encephalomyelitis, rest and sleep do not restore function — the unrefreshing quality of sleep is itself a cardinal feature — whereas in depression, structured activity and sleep improvement often produce symptomatic relief.

For epidemiological and mechanistic research, the critical consequence is that any study's findings must be read against which case definition was used to recruit and diagnose participants. A study showing normal inflammatory markers in Fukuda-defined ME/CFS patients does not contradict a study showing activated microglia in IOM-defined ones: they are studying different populations. Claims about ME/CFS pathophysiology require explicit statement of the diagnostic criteria used, and systematic reviews must account for definitional heterogeneity rather than treating all fatigue-illness studies as equivalent.

## Related terms

- Post-exertional malaise — the cardinal feature that distinguishes myalgic encephalomyelitis from other chronic fatiguing illnesses
- Two-day cardiopulmonary exercise testing — the closest objective measure of post-exertional malaise and a research assessment tool
- Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis — the neuroendocrine arm whose dysfunction characterizes the illness
- Sympathoadrenal system — the autonomic arm whose decoupling from HPA axis support drives orthostatic intolerance
- Allostatic load — the cumulative physiological cost framework within which the multi-system dysfunction can be understood
