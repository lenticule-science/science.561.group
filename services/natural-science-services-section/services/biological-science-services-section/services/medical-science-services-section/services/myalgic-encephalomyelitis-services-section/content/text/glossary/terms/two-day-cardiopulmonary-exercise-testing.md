Two-day cardiopulmonary exercise testing is a maximal or near-maximal exercise protocol performed on two consecutive days, with the critical feature being test-retest comparison 24 hours apart. In healthy people and in people with other fatiguing illnesses — fibromyalgia, post-viral fatigue from conditions other than myalgic encephalomyelitis, depression-related fatigue — performance on day two reproduces values from day one: VO₂ max, anaerobic threshold, maximum heart rate, and workload are stable across the two sessions. In myalgic encephalomyelitis, the characteristic finding is a second-day decrement: a reduced oxygen consumption at ventilatory threshold, a reduced anaerobic threshold workload, and a lower maximum VO₂ achieved. This pattern — normal or near-normal day-one values with day-two decline — is the signature of post-exertional malaise made objective and measurable in a controlled setting. The first day's demand appears to impose a cost that impairs capacity on the second day.

Why the two-day protocol matters scientifically is that single-day cardiopulmonary exercise testing in myalgic encephalomyelitis typically appears unremarkable: the day-one values fall within normal ranges or show only modest reductions. It is only when the test is repeated that the pathology becomes visible. This makes the two-day CPET a more sensitive instrument for myalgic encephalomyelitis than standard exercise testing, but it also makes it a substantial and potentially harmful research intervention. The test itself is maximal or near-maximal effort, which means it places demand on already-compromised metabolic and autonomic systems, and the predictable consequence is a prolonged post-exertional malaise flare in the days following testing. For research purposes, the benefit of an objective measure that captures the core pathophysiology has justified this cost, but it means two-day CPET is a research tool rather than a routine clinical assessment.

The mechanistic interpretation of the two-day CPET remains debated. The leading hypothesis, advanced by Fluge and colleagues, posits that reduced pyruvate dehydrogenase activity limits aerobic capacity and forces cells into anaerobic metabolism at low workloads, accumulating lactate and producing the post-exertional flare. Repeated use of this compensatory mechanism exhausts the anaerobic reserve, explaining the day-two decline. An alternative or complementary hypothesis implicates autonomic dysfunction: the sympathetic surge required for maximal exercise on day one is sustained into day two, producing vascular dysregulation and cerebral hypoperfusion that impairs exercise tolerance. Both mechanisms may operate; the evidence supporting each is limited and replication has been mixed.

For clinical and research practice, the consequence is clear: two-day CPET offers currently the closest thing to an objective, reproducible measure of the core pathophysiology of myalgic encephalomyelitis. However, modest sample sizes in published studies, inconsistent replication of the day-two effect, and the real risk of inducing prolonged symptom worsening mean that it remains a specialized research instrument appropriate for carefully-conducted studies with informed consent and follow-up support, not a routine or screening assessment.
