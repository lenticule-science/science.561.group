Adrenal insufficiency is inadequate production of adrenocortical hormones — primarily cortisol, and in certain forms also aldosterone — with sufficient reduction to impair the body's ability to sustain vascular tone, glucose homeostasis, and the metabolic permissiveness required for a normal stress response. It is a defined endocrine diagnosis, with specific diagnostic criteria, not a spectrum or a subjective state, and this distinction is essential because confusion between the clinical diagnosis and the informal concept of "adrenal fatigue" has muddied both the science and patient discussions.

Adrenal insufficiency is classified as primary, secondary, or tertiary based on where in the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis the failure occurs. Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease) is failure of the adrenal cortex itself: the gland cannot produce cortisol or aldosterone in response to ACTH, so both are profoundly reduced, and the loss of the negative-feedback signal causes ACTH and corticotropin-releasing hormone to rise uninhibited. The elevated ACTH stimulates melanocyte-stimulating hormone, a peptide produced from the same precursor, causing the characteristic hyperpigmentation — bronzing of the skin, especially in sun-exposed areas and in skin creases. Secondary adrenal insufficiency is loss of pituitary ACTH production (pituitary disease, pituitary surgery, pituitary apoplexy), so cortisol fails but aldosterone production is usually preserved via the renin-angiotensin system, and hyperpigmentation is absent because ACTH is low. Tertiary adrenal insufficiency is hypothalamic failure of corticotropin-releasing hormone production, producing the same pattern as secondary insufficiency — low cortisol, preserved aldosterone, no hyperpigmentation — but with distinguishable ACTH response to exogenous corticotropin-releasing hormone in testing.

Diagnosis requires specific tests: a morning cortisol below approximately 3 μg/dL (below 8 micromoles per liter) is generally considered insufficiently low for normal function; a corticotropin-releasing hormone or adrenocorticotropic hormone stimulation test establishes whether the adrenal gland or the pituitary can respond; and dexamethasone suppression testing (if secondary insufficiency is suspected) distinguishes pituitary from hypothalamic failure. Diagnosis is not made on a single cortisol measurement, because cortisol varies with time of day, stress, and recent glucocorticoid exposure. It is made by demonstrating persistent subnormal cortisol with failure of the gland or pituitary to respond to stimulation.

It is essential to address directly a widespread misconception: the "adrenal fatigue" concept popular outside medicine is not adrenal insufficiency and has no recognized medical definition. The systematic review by Cadegiani and Kater comprehensively found no substantiation for "adrenal fatigue" as a distinct pathological state. However, this does NOT mean the HPA-axis alterations reported in myalgic encephalomyelitis are unreal or unworthy of study. What the evidence shows is that myalgic encephalomyelitis presents with blunted or flattened HPA-axis responses — lower baseline cortisol, blunted awakening response, attenuated response to corticotropin-releasing hormone or adrenocorticotropic hormone stimulation — that are more modest than the profound insufficiency of diagnosed adrenal insufficiency. These alterations are real findings in peer-reviewed research but do not meet the threshold for a clinical diagnosis of adrenal insufficiency, and conflating "adrenal fatigue" with these axis alterations has been used both to dismiss legitimate findings and to overclaim clinical illness in people whose cortisol measurements are actually within normal range. The truth is: true adrenal insufficiency is a defined diagnosis that should be tested for and treated; blunted HPA-axis responses are real but distinct from true insufficiency; and neither one is the same as "adrenal fatigue."

Treatment of true adrenal insufficiency is glucocorticoid and (in primary insufficiency) mineralocorticoid replacement, with doses titrated to physiologic replacement — enough to restore vascular tone and glucose homeostasis without inducing iatrogenic glucocorticoid excess. The difference from management of blunted axis responses is crucial: patients with myalgic encephalomyelitis and mildly low cortisol do not necessarily benefit from glucocorticoid replacement, and blanket replacement of all "low cortisol" is a common clinical error that produces iatrogenic complications.

For clinical assessment and research, the consequence is clear: adrenal insufficiency must be diagnosed by the defined criteria (morning cortisol <3 μg/dL plus failure of stimulated response), not by symptoms or by the informal application of "adrenal fatigue." Findings of blunted axis responses in myalgic encephalomyelitis are scientifically meaningful and warrant further study, but they should not be conflated with clinical adrenal insufficiency or treated as though the diagnosis and treatment of one establishes the other.

## Related terms

- Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis — the axis whose failure in primary, secondary, or tertiary forms defines adrenal insufficiency
- Cortisol — the hormone whose inadequate production defines the condition
- Adrenal crisis — the acute, life-threatening decompensation of untreated or undertreated adrenal insufficiency
- Glucocorticoid — the broader hormone class of which cortisol is the principal representative
- Myalgic encephalomyelitis — a condition associated with blunted HPA-axis responses, distinct from adrenal insufficiency
- Allostatic load — the cumulative cost of running physiological systems without adequate glucocorticoid support
