Glucocorticoid resistance, also called glucocorticoid receptor (GR) resistance or GR desensitization, is a condition in which circulating glucocorticoid — typically cortisol in humans — is present at normal or even elevated levels, yet the target cells fail to respond appropriately to that hormone. This is mechanistically distinct from adrenal insufficiency, in which the hormone is insufficiently produced; glucocorticoid resistance is a receptor-level problem, not a hormone-availability problem. A person with true adrenal insufficiency has both too little cortisol and impaired vascular/metabolic function. A person with glucocorticoid resistance may have normal or high cortisol, yet still has impaired response at the cellular level. These two problems produce superficially similar clinical pictures — unbraked inflammation, impaired stress response, orthostatic intolerance, and metabolic dysregulation — but they are separated only by functional testing, not by cortisol measurements alone.

The underlying mechanisms of glucocorticoid resistance are multiple and often coexist. Reduced glucocorticoid receptor expression — fewer receptor proteins on the surface of target cells — means the same cortisol concentration activates fewer receptors and produces a weaker response. Impaired receptor signal transduction — defects in the intracellular signaling cascades downstream of receptor binding — produces weak activation despite adequate receptor number and cortisol presence. Altered GR-α/GR-β isoform ratio affects transcriptional activity; the two isoforms have different effects on gene expression, and a shift toward the less-active GR-β isoform produces functional resistance. Impaired nuclear translocation of the receptor, even after ligand binding, prevents the receptor from reaching its target genes in the nucleus. Chronic glucocorticoid exposure itself can produce desensitization through negative-feedback mechanisms and receptor downregulation. In chronic psychological or social stress, particularly involving subordination or social threat, research has demonstrated measurable reductions in GR expression on immune cells alongside impaired GR-mediated transcription of anti-inflammatory genes — a state of acquired functional resistance.

The diagnostic distinction from adrenal insufficiency is essential and rests on functional testing. A cortisol level — morning cortisol, 24-hour urinary free cortisol, or midnight cortisol — tells you how much hormone is circulating. It does not tell you whether the target tissues can respond to that hormone. Assessing glucocorticoid responsiveness requires measuring the actual physiological consequence of cortisol signaling, not just the hormone level. The gold standard for this is an ex vivo dexamethasone-suppression assay: take immune cells (monocytes, T cells, dendritic cells) from the patient's blood, stimulate them in culture (e.g., with lipopolysaccharide or anti-CD3), and measure whether exogenous dexamethasone (a synthetic glucocorticoid) suppresses the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-8, IL-1β) as it should. If dexamethasone suppresses cytokine production normally, the brake is intact. If dexamethasone fails to suppress, the brake is impaired — the cells have glucocorticoid resistance. This functional test is independent of circulating cortisol level; a person can have normal or high cortisol yet show impaired dexamethasone suppression, proving that the defect is downstream of the hormone.

Alternatively, the low-dose dexamethasone-suppression test (LDDST) — a single 1-mg dose of dexamethasone in the evening with cortisol and ACTH measured the following morning — assesses the overall feedback regulation of the HPA axis. In healthy people, 1 mg of dexamethasone suppresses cortisol below 1.8 μg/dL and ACTH below 5 pg/mL. People with impaired GR signaling may fail to suppress adequately despite normal unstimulated cortisol, suggesting receptor dysfunction. However, the LDDST is a whole-system test and does not isolate the receptor problem from other HPA-axis dysfunction; the ex vivo dexamethasone assay, while more technically demanding and not routinely available clinically, more directly assesses target-cell receptor function.

The clinical and research consequence of the distinction is profound. If a patient has glucocorticoid resistance, administering more glucocorticoid (as some older treatment protocols for ME/CFS attempted) will not restore the brake — the hormone will simply accumulate while the receptor-level defect persists. The appropriate approach is to address the receptor problem itself, through mechanisms that restore GR expression or GR signal transduction — an area of active research but with limited current therapeutic options. Conversely, a patient with true adrenal insufficiency needs glucocorticoid replacement to provide the hormone the adrenal cannot produce. A normal serum cortisol does not establish that glucocorticoid signaling is intact; it only establishes that production is adequate. Assessing the brake requires functional measurement.

For myalgic encephalomyelitis specifically, some evidence suggests glucocorticoid resistance may play a role. Studies have found blunted cortisol responses to challenge (corticotropin-releasing hormone stimulation, ACTH stimulation) in ME/CFS, but cortisol is present, not absent — suggesting a receptor-level or axis-level dysregulation rather than true insufficiency. Limited data on ex vivo dexamethasone suppression in ME/CFS patients show mixed results, but suggest possible impairment in some patients. If glucocorticoid resistance is present, this would explain why some ME/CFS patients have blunted inflammation-suppressive capacity despite normal resting cortisol, and why simple cortisol supplementation is unlikely to be effective. Establishing this definitively would require larger studies with functional assays, but the mechanistic plausibility is high given the neuroendocrine dysfunction characteristic of the condition.

## Related terms

- Glucocorticoid — the hormone class (cortisol, dexamethasone) to which resistance occurs
- Glucocorticoid receptor — the protein target whose dysfunction defines resistance
- Adrenal insufficiency — inadequate hormone production; distinguished from resistance by normal cortisol levels and functional testing
- Glucocorticoid immunosuppression — the normal brake that glucocorticoid resistance impairs
- Cortisol — the principal glucocorticoid in humans
- Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis — the endocrine system producing cortisol
- Myalgic encephalomyelitis — a condition possibly characterized by glucocorticoid resistance
- Allostatic load — the cumulative cost of running physiological systems without adequate glucocorticoid brake function
