Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) delivers electrical current through skin electrodes to modulate pain perception. A TENS device is a small battery-operated unit with leads connected to surface electrodes; the user adjusts frequency, intensity, and pulse width. TENS is widely used for acute pain, chronic pain, postoperative pain, and musculoskeletal conditions. Two primary mechanisms are proposed: (1) Gate control theory application — Low-frequency high-intensity TENS activates large-diameter A-beta fibers that inhibit nociceptive transmission at the spinal cord; (2) Endogenous opioid release — High-frequency TENS may activate descending opioid-sensitive pathways, triggering release of endogenous opioid peptides. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive; different TENS parameters engage different pathways. TENS effectiveness depends critically on stimulus parameters — frequency, intensity, and pattern determine which mechanism is engaged. Biomedical evidence for TENS shows it reduces pain compared to sham TENS or no treatment, though effect sizes vary across studies. TENS is most effective when applied according to principles matching one of the proposed mechanisms. TENS differs from electroacupuncture in that it uses surface electrodes without point-specific targeting and is grounded in gate control and neurochemical theories, while electroacupuncture targets specific points through needle insertion at depth.
