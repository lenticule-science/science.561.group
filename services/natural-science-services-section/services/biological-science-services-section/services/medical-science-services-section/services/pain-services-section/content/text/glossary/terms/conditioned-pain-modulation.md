Conditioned pain modulation (CPM) is a psychophysical phenomenon in which activation of one painful stimulus reduces perceived intensity of a second painful stimulus. The mechanism involves descending inhibitory pathways: painful input from one body region reaches the brainstem (periaqueductal gray, rostral ventromedial medulla), which sends inhibitory signals down the spinal cord, suppressing nociceptive transmission from a second region. CPM is also known as diffuse noxious inhibitory control (DNIC). The classical demonstration is cold-water immersion: immersing one hand in cold water reduces pain from simultaneous pressure to the foot. In acute pain, CPM is typically efficient, with painful input triggering strong descending inhibition. In chronic pain states, CPM efficiency is often reduced, contributing to pain amplification and central sensitization. CPM is distinct from distraction, which suppresses pain through attentional mechanisms. Clinically, CPM can be quantified by measuring pain perception with and without a conditioning stimulus. Efficient CPM is associated with better pain tolerance. Reduced CPM is found in many chronic pain conditions including fibromyalgia and persistent post-injury pain.
