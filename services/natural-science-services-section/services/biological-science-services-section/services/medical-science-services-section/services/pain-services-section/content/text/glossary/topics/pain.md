Pain is a complex physiological and subjective phenomenon produced by the nervous system when its threat assessment determines that pain would be useful for the organism. Pain is not a signal passively detected from tissues but an active construction by the nervous system that integrates sensory input with emotional, cognitive, and contextual information.

The nervous system's pain-production system comprises peripheral nociceptors that detect potential tissue damage, spinal cord processing centers that modulate incoming signals, and a distributed brain network that integrates multiple information streams and produces the conscious experience of pain. Descending pathways from the brain can suppress or amplify nociceptive transmission, and learned associations, expectations, and attention can substantially alter pain intensity independent of tissue state. This makes pain both a window into tissue health and a phenomenon that exceeds tissue state — pain can be absent when tissue is damaged and intense when tissue is intact.

Pain serves the domain of medicine as a symptom that indicates the need for assessment and as a phenomenon with its own physiology and therapeutic considerations. The study of pain encompasses the neurophysiology of pain production, the assessment and measurement of pain experience, the conditions that produce acute and chronic pain, the mechanisms of pain amplification and sensitization, and the therapeutic approaches that modify pain perception and magnitude. Pain research draws from neuroscience, psychology, medicine, and somatic studies to understand how pain arises and how it can be effectively addressed.
