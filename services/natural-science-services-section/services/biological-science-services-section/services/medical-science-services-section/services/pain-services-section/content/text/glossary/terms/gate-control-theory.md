Gate control theory proposes that pain perception is not a direct signal pathway but a modulated system in which nociceptive (pain-carrying) signals are gated at the spinal cord level before reaching the brain. The theory states that non-nociceptive input (touch, pressure) and descending signals from the brain can inhibit nociceptive transmission through spinal interneurons, effectively closing the gate and suppressing pain. The anatomy is the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, where nociceptive fibers synapse. Excitatory interneurons amplify nociceptive transmission while inhibitory interneurons suppress it. A-beta fibers carrying touch and pressure activate inhibitory interneurons, which suppress nociceptive transmission. The net result determines what signal ascends to the brain. Gate control theory explains why rubbing a bumped knee reduces pain, why distraction suppresses pain, and why chronic pain can persist without ongoing tissue damage. This theory contrasts with the peripheral theory, which held that pain is a direct signal determined solely by stimulus intensity. Contemporary pain neuroscience refines gate control theory with detailed understanding of neurochemical mechanisms (opioid receptors, GABA, glycine), descending modulation from the brainstem, and how context influences gate function.
