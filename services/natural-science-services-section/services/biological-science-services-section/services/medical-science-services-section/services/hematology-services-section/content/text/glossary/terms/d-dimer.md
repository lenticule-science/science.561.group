D-dimer is a fragment of cross-linked fibrin released into the blood during
fibrinolysis. When thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin, factor XIII
(fibrin-stabilizing factor) catalyzes the formation of covalent cross-links
between fibrin molecules. When plasmin subsequently degrades this cross-linked
fibrin, one of the major products is a fragment consisting of two fibrin D
domains held together by these cross-links — the D-dimer. It is released into
the circulation as a soluble protein and can be measured in blood plasma.

## What an elevated D-dimer indicates — and does not

An elevated D-dimer level indicates that fibrin has been formed and degraded
somewhere in the body within the preceding hours to days. This is the highest-
sensitivity test for venous or arterial thrombosis. If a patient with suspected
deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism has a normal D-dimer, thrombosis is
highly unlikely, and further imaging (ultrasound or CT angiography) is usually
not needed.

However, D-dimer elevation is not specific to thrombosis. Fibrin formation and
degradation happen in many physiological and pathological states besides clotting:

- Inflammation and infection elevate D-dimer, because inflammatory cytokines
  upregulate coagulation
- Pregnancy elevates D-dimer as a normal physiological change
- Recent surgery or trauma elevates D-dimer for days to weeks
- Liver disease elevates D-dimer because the liver produces both fibrinogen and
  its clearance mechanisms
- Sepsis, malignancy, and disseminated intravascular coagulation produce massive
  D-dimer elevation
- Intensive exercise, acute stress, and epinephrine administration elevate D-
  dimer acutely
- Aging itself elevates D-dimer; a 70-year-old healthy person can have a higher
  D-dimer than a 30-year-old with a pulmonary embolism

The consequence is that while D-dimer is sensitive (if negative, thrombosis is
ruled out), it is not specific (if positive, thrombosis is not ruled in). A
positive D-dimer requires either further testing to confirm thrombosis or, in
the presence of a low clinical probability, may be a false positive. For this
reason, modern clinical use of D-dimer relies on a two-step process: first,
assess clinical probability (Wells score or similar), then use D-dimer to
refine that assessment.

## Age-adjusted cutoffs

The D-dimer cutoff used to distinguish "normal" from "elevated" has major
implications for clinical practice. Using a fixed cutoff (e.g., 0.5 μg/mL) for
all ages means that older patients will exceed the threshold more frequently,
leading to more imaging studies and more false-positive findings in the elderly.
Studies since the early 2000s have documented that age-adjusted D-dimer
thresholds improve specificity in older patients. A common recommendation is to
use an age-adjusted cutoff of (patient age × 0.01) μg/mL for patients over 50
years old. This acknowledges that D-dimer rises with age, and that the mere
fact of being older does not warrant imaging if the D-dimer is elevated only
modestly above what is expected for age.

## What a normal D-dimer does and does not exclude

A normal D-dimer value has high negative predictive value: it makes acute
thrombosis very unlikely. However, a normal D-dimer does not exclude:

- Chronic thrombosis (a clot that formed weeks or months ago and is now stable)
- Microclot burden that is not currently being lysed
- A clot that is partially organized (no longer actively degrading)

This is especially relevant to hypotheses about microclots in long COVID and
ME/CFS. If microclots are present but not currently being actively lysed by
plasmin, they would not produce elevated D-dimer. Some studies report normal or
low-normal D-dimer in cohorts where microclot counts are reported as elevated.
This finding is either evidence that (1) the microclots are not actively being
degraded, consistent with their proposed resistance to fibrinolysis, or (2) the
microclots do not exist, and the D-dimer result is the true finding. This
ambiguity — that normal D-dimer does not resolve whether microclots are present
or absent — is itself a source of uncertainty in the field.

## Related terms

- Fibrin — the protein being cleaved
- Fibrinolysis — the process generating D-dimer
- Plasmin — the enzyme cleaving fibrin to produce D-dimer fragments
- Thrombosis — the condition D-dimer is used to detect
- Fibrin degradation product — the broader category of fragments produced by
  plasmin cleavage
