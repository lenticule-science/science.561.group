Erythrocyte deformability is the capacity of a red blood cell to deform
elastically and reversibly as it passes through capillaries that are narrower
than its resting diameter. A resting erythrocyte in a mammal is a biconcave
disc approximately 7.5 micrometers in diameter, but capillaries in tissue are
2–3 micrometers in diameter. For blood to flow through tissue capillaries and
deliver oxygen, red cells must deform to pass through these narrower vessels.
This is not passive squeezing; it is an active elastic deformation in which the
cell's membrane and cytoskeleton undergo shear stress and then recover their
original shape downstream.

## Determinants of red cell deformability

Red cell deformability is determined by three factors:

1. **Membrane cytoskeleton properties** — The red cell membrane is anchored to
   a two-dimensional network of protein (spectrin, actin, ankyrin, protein 4.1)
   beneath the lipid bilayer. This cytoskeleton gives the cell its elasticity;
   damage to it (by oxidative stress, mechanical trauma, or genetic mutation)
   reduces deformability. The cytoskeleton is also the site where hemoglobin
   binds at the cytoplasmic face, anchoring the hemoglobin to the membrane.
2. **Surface-to-volume ratio** — A biconcave disc has a higher surface-to-volume
   ratio than a sphere; this geometry allows the cell to deform without
   increasing membrane tension. A spherical cell (which occurs in hereditary
   spherocytosis or after osmotic shock) has lower surface-to-volume ratio and
   deforms less readily.
3. **Cytoplasmic viscosity** — The viscosity of the hemoglobin solution inside
   the cell affects how easily it can be displaced within the membrane during
   deformation. High hemoglobin concentration increases viscosity, slightly
   reducing deformability. Hemoglobinopathies with polymerizing hemoglobins
   (such as sickle hemoglobin) dramatically reduce deformability.

## Measurement: ektacytometry and filtration

**Ektacytometry** is the gold standard for measuring red cell deformability. A
red cell is placed in a shear flow field that exerts mechanical stress on it,
causing it to deform into an elliptical shape. The cell is illuminated by laser
light, and the diffraction pattern produced by the deformed cell is analyzed.
The pattern changes as deformation increases; from this, a deformability index
is calculated. Ektacytometry is quantitative, allows measurement at controlled
osmotic conditions and varying shear stress, and produces a direct, continuous
measure of deformability. However, ektacytometry is technically demanding and
expensive, requiring specialized equipment.

**Filtration methods** are simpler and less expensive. A sample of red cells is
passed through a filter with pores of defined size (typically 5 micrometers).
The pressure required to force the cells through, or the time it takes for a
fixed volume to pass through, is measured. Higher pressure or longer time
indicates lower deformability. Filtration is rapid but gives an all-or-nothing
measure: cells either pass or they don't, without a continuous measure of
deformability.

## Impaired deformability: consequences for perfusion

Reduced red cell deformability impairs tissue oxygen delivery by a mechanism
independent of hemoglobin concentration or blood pressure. If red cells cannot
deform to enter capillaries, blood flow into those capillaries decreases, and
oxygen delivery to the tissue falls. This is a rheological (flow-based) failure,
not an oxygen-carrying failure. A patient with normal hemoglobin, normal blood
pressure, and impaired red cell deformability will have reduced oxygen delivery
to tissues, manifesting as exercise intolerance, fatigue, or symptoms of
hypoxia, without showing obvious abnormality on a routine full blood count (which
measures hemoglobin concentration and red cell count, not deformability).

The consequence for clinical interpretation is critical: a normal complete blood
count (normal hemoglobin, normal hematocrit, normal red cell indices) does NOT
exclude a microcirculatory oxygen-delivery failure due to impaired deformability.
Deformability must be measured separately by ektacytometry or filtration.

## Eryptosis and phosphatidylserine externalization

Red cells, like platelets, can externalize phosphatidylserine. This process,
called eryptosis (erythrocyte apoptosis), is triggered by oxidative stress,
calcium overload, or activation of phospholipid scramblase. When PS is
externalized, the red cell becomes a target for macrophage clearance (the "eat
me" signal) and is removed from circulation. Eryptosis is a programmed cell-
removal pathway; it is not hemolysis (rupture of the cell), but a controlled
pathway to removal. The consequence is that a population of red cells with
elevated eryptosis will have accelerated clearance from the circulation, reducing
the circulating red cell count and potentially contributing to anemia.

The relationship between eryptosis and deformability is indirect. If red cells
are undergoing PS externalization, they are simultaneously undergoing changes in
calcium handling and membrane organization that may also reduce deformability.
Studies in ME/CFS and long COVID have reported both PS externalization and
impaired deformability in the same cohorts, consistent with a shared upstream
process affecting membrane regulation.

## Distinction: deformability vs. aggregation

Red cell deformability and red cell aggregation are different phenomena affecting
blood flow through different mechanisms:

- **Deformability** is the ability of a single cell to deform under shear stress.
  Impaired deformability reduces flow through small capillaries.
- **Aggregation** is the clumping of red cells into large structures (rouleaux).
  Elevated aggregation increases blood viscosity, especially at low shear rates
  in small vessels.

A patient can have normal deformability and elevated aggregation, or vice versa.
Each produces a different rheological problem: impaired deformability reduces
flow through capillaries directly; elevated aggregation increases whole-blood
viscosity. Measurement of aggregation (erythrocyte sedimentation rate, ESR, or
direct aggregometry) is separate from measurement of deformability.

## Related terms

- Erythrocyte — the red blood cell
- Membrane cytoskeleton — the protein network determining deformability
- Hemoglobin — the oxygen-carrying protein whose concentration affects viscosity
- Phosphatidylserine — the lipid externalized during eryptosis
- Microcirculation — the capillary beds where deformability matters
- Oxygen delivery — the physiological function impaired by reduced deformability
