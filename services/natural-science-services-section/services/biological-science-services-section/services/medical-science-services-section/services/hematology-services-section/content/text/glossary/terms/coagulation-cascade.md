The coagulation cascade is the series of enzyme-catalyzed reactions by which
blood clotting is initiated and propagated. Classically it is described in
three stages: the intrinsic pathway (initiated by contact with tissue damage
or foreign surfaces), the extrinsic pathway (initiated by tissue factor
exposure), and the common pathway, all converging on the generation of
thrombin, which then cleaves fibrinogen to fibrin and activates fibrin-
stabilizing factor XIII. Each stage consists of serine proteases activating
downstream serine proteases in a cascade of amplification.

The classical cascade model describes these reactions as occurring in bulk
aqueous phase. However, modern cellular and biophysical studies have
established that the coagulation cascade does not function as a soluble
cascade. Instead, it is a membrane-localized process: the critical amplification
step, the activation of factor X to factor Xa, and the subsequent formation of
thrombin-generating complex (the tenase complex), occur exclusively on the
surface of activated platelets. More specifically, these complexes assemble on
an anionic phospholipid bilayer with calcium and vitamin K-dependent factors
anchored to the membrane via calcium bridges. The essential anionic phospholipid
is phosphatidylserine.

## The prothrombinase complex and the membrane requirement

The prothrombinase complex itself is the assembly of factor Xa and factor Va
on a phospholipid surface in the presence of calcium. This complex catalyzes
the activation of prothrombin (factor II) to thrombin at a rate roughly 200,000
times faster than factor Xa alone in solution. This enormous rate enhancement —
the factor of 200,000 — is not because the enzyme has become more active; it is
because the enzyme and substrate are now constrained in two dimensions on the
membrane surface. The membrane brings them into proximity and holds them there.
Without the membrane, the reaction rate is negligible.

This has a crucial consequence: phosphatidylserine availability is not a
modifier of coagulation; it is a location requirement. Coagulation cannot occur
without it. In healthy hemostasis, phosphatidylserine is normally restricted to
the inner (cytoplasmic) leaflet of cell membranes by ATP-dependent flippase
activity. Upon platelet activation — by thrombin, collagen, ADP, or thromboxane
— this lipid is externalized to the outer leaflet through calcium-activated
scramblase activity. This externalization occurs on a seconds-to-minutes
timescale and exposes the anionic charge that the coagulation complexes require.

## The cell-based model replaces the cascade model

The classical cascade model has been superseded in modern hematology by the
cell-based model of coagulation, which describes coagulation as occurring on
the surfaces of platelets, tissue factor-bearing cells, and red cells. In this
model:

1. **Initiation phase** — Tissue factor on the surface of an injured cell
   encounters factor VII in the blood. Factor VII binds tissue factor, and the
   complex activates factor X. This occurs on the tissue factor-bearing cell
   surface.
2. **Amplification phase** — The small amount of thrombin generated in
   initiation is amplified by the "tenase complex" (factor IXa + factor VIIIa)
   assembling on the platelet surface, which then activates factor X to factor
   Xa at much higher rates than soluble factor VII can.
3. **Propagation phase** — The prothrombinase complex (factor Xa + factor Va)
   assembles on the phosphatidylserine-bearing platelet surface and generates
   large amounts of thrombin from prothrombin.
4. **Fibrin formation** — Thrombin cleaves fibrinogen to fibrin and cross-links
   the clot via factor XIII.

The advantage of the cell-based model is that it explains why clotting is
spatially localized. Coagulation does not happen throughout the entire blood
volume; it happens where platelets are activated and where phospholipid
surfaces are exposed. This spatial confinement is the whole purpose — to arrest
bleeding at the site of injury without ocluding the entire vascular tree.

## Consequence: phospholipid availability as a coagulation determinant

Because the prothrombinase complex absolutely requires a phosphatidylserine-
bearing surface, anything that causes phosphatidylserine to externalize more
widely — either on more cells or for longer periods — has the potential to
shift coagulation in a procoagulant direction. Conversely, anything that
impairs phosphatidylserine externalization (such as flippase gain-of-function,
ATP depletion that impairs scramblase, or deficiency of calcium signaling
cascades that normally trigger externalization) shifts toward an
anticoagulant phenotype. This is why phosphatidylserine externalization is not
a curiosity of cell biology; it is a load-bearing fact of hemostasis.
