Platelet activation is the sequence of cellular changes that occurs when a
platelet is stimulated by an agonist — a molecule that binds to receptors on
the platelet surface and initiates the activation cascade. The major
physiological agonists are:

1. **Thrombin** — the central coagulation protease, acting through protease-
   activated receptors (PARs)
2. **Collagen** — exposed when blood vessel endothelium is damaged, acting
   through integrin α2β1 and GPVI receptors
3. **Adenosine diphosphate (ADP)** — released from damaged red cells and
   platelets themselves, acting through P2Y1 and P2Y12 receptors
4. **Thromboxane A2** — produced by activated platelets themselves, amplifying
   activation

Each agonist triggers intracellular calcium release, activation of protein
kinase C, and mobilization of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate. This
leads to a cascade of morphological and biochemical changes:

## Morphological and granule-release changes

Upon activation, a resting discoid platelet undergoes shape change, becoming
spiculated and rounded as its cytoskeleton reorganizes. The platelet contains
two types of granules:

- **Alpha-granules** — storage organelles containing fibrinogen, von Willebrand
  factor, thrombospondin, and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF)
- **Dense granules** — containing ATP, ADP, serotonin, and divalent cations
  (calcium, magnesium)

Upon activation, both types of granules fuse with the plasma membrane and
release their contents. This is not a side effect; it is a programmed response
amplifying activation. The ADP and ATP released from dense granules activate
nearby resting platelets, propagating the activation response.

## Surface expression of activation markers

Activation brings hidden epitopes to the cell surface:

- **P-selectin** — a cell-adhesion molecule normally sequestered in alpha-granule
  membranes, exposed on the platelet surface upon granule fusion. P-selectin is
  the marker most commonly used clinically to identify activated platelets.
- **Phosphatidylserine (PS)** — normally restricted to the inner (cytoplasmic)
  leaflet of the plasma membrane by ATP-dependent flippase activity. Upon
  activation, calcium-activated scramblase scrambles the membrane, causing PS to
  externalize to the outer (blood-facing) leaflet. PS is an anionic lipid; its
  externalization exposes negative charge on the platelet surface.

## Integrin activation and aggregation

Platelet integrins — particularly αIIbβ3 (GPIIb/IIIa) — undergo conformational
change upon activation, shifting from a low-affinity to a high-affinity state.
This allows them to bind fibrinogen, which has two αIIbβ3 binding sites, one on
each end. A single fibrinogen molecule can therefore bridge two platelets,
linking them together. Multiple such bridges build a platelet aggregate — a
three-dimensional network of activated platelets held together by fibrinogen and
other adhesive proteins.

## Distinction: activation vs. aggregation

Platelet activation and platelet aggregation are often used interchangeably in
casual speech, but they are distinct events:

- **Activation** is the response of a single platelet to an agonist — the shape
  change, granule release, PS externalization, and integrin conformational change.
  Activation can be measured without aggregation by flow cytometry (measuring PS
  exposure or P-selectin surface expression on single cells) or by function assays
  (measuring phosphatidylserine exposure or granule release markers).
- **Aggregation** is the linking of multiple activated platelets into a clump. It
  requires integrin activation and fibrinogen (or another adhesive protein).

A patient can have platelet hyperactivation (elevated P-selectin, elevated PS
exposure) without visible aggregation. Similarly, aggregation assays (such as
light transmission aggregometry) can show reduced aggregation in a patient whose
platelets are actually hyperactivated but have a defect in integrin or in the
adhesive proteins needed for aggregation.

The consequence is that a study reporting "platelet hyperactivation" is saying
something specific — that activation markers are elevated — which is different
from a study reporting "platelet aggregate burden." Both can be true; neither
implies the other.

## The critical consequence: phosphatidylserine externalization

The externalization of phosphatidylserine upon platelet activation is the event
that makes coagulation possible. PS is the anionic membrane surface on which the
prothrombinase complex (factor Xa + factor Va + calcium) assembles. In the healthy
resting state, PS is kept on the inner membrane by flippase activity, and coagulation
complexes cannot form. Upon activation, scramblase externalizes PS, providing the
surface. Clotting then proceeds at the site of the activated platelet. This is why
phosphatidylserine externalization is not a curiosity; it is the essential membrane
change enabling the entire coagulation response.

## Related terms

- Phosphatidylserine — the anionic lipid externalized during activation
- P-selectin — surface adhesion molecule exposed upon activation
- Integrin αIIbβ3 — integrin activated to enable fibrinogen binding
- Platelet aggregation — the linking of multiple activated platelets
- Coagulation cascade — the process enabled by PS externalization
- Thrombin — the principal physiological platelet agonist
