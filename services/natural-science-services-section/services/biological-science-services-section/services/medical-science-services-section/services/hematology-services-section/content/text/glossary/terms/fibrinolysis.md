Fibrinolysis is the physiological process by which formed fibrin clots are
dissolved and cleared from circulation and tissues. It is the clearance phase
of hemostasis, balancing coagulation. Where coagulation assembles fibrin and
arrests bleeding, fibrinolysis removes that fibrin once healing is underway and
restores blood flow.

## The central enzymatic reaction

The core of fibrinolysis is the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin, catalyzed
by plasminogen activators. Plasminogen is a circulating zymogen (inactive
precursor enzyme) produced by the liver. Two physiological plasminogen activators
deliver it:

1. **Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA)** — produced by endothelial cells, released
   into the blood in response to thrombin and other signals. tPA has high affinity
   for fibrin; it binds to fibrin and efficiently activates plasminogen specifically
   on the clot surface.
2. **Urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA)** — produced by vascular endothelial
   cells, leukocytes, and other tissues. uPA has lower affinity for fibrin than
   tPA and more readily activates plasminogen in bulk plasma as well as on clots.

Both are serine proteases. They cleave a single peptide bond in plasminogen,
releasing the N-terminal peptide and leaving the active protease plasmin. Plasmin
is itself a powerful serine protease; it cleaves fibrin at multiple sites,
producing a series of soluble fibrin degradation products of decreasing size.

## Regulation of fibrinolysis

Fibrinolysis is tightly regulated by inhibitors that prevent excess fibrin
dissolution. Three main inhibitors control the system:

1. **Plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1)** — an inhibitor of both tPA and
   uPA, it forms 1:1 stoichiometric complexes with the plasminogen activators,
   inactivating them. PAI-1 is produced by endothelial cells and platelets, and
   its levels are elevated by inflammatory signals, stress hormones, and metabolic
   dysregulation.
2. **Alpha-2-antiplasmin** — a fast inhibitor of plasmin itself, circulating at
   high concentration. It reacts immediately with any plasmin that is released
   from plasminogen, preventing widespread proteolysis. Because of alpha-2-
   antiplasmin, fibrinolysis is localized to the clot surface, where plasmin
   generation can proceed locally without being immediately suppressed.
3. **Thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI)** — also called
   carboxypeptidase B, it is activated by thrombin and removes lysine residues
   from the carboxy-terminus of fibrin degradation products. Because plasmin and
   tPA both recognize and bind these C-terminal lysines, TAFI's removal of them
   reduces the binding of plasminogen activators to partially degraded fibrin.
   TAFI therefore acts as a brake on ongoing fibrinolysis once fibrin degradation
   has begun.

The result is a system in which excessive fibrinolysis is prevented, but fibrin
formed during hemostasis is steadily cleared once coagulation has done its job.

## Distinction from anticoagulation

Fibrinolysis and anticoagulation are often conflated, but they are distinct
processes operating at different stages:

- **Anticoagulation** prevents fibrin formation in the first place, by inhibiting
  coagulation factors (e.g., warfarin inhibits vitamin K-dependent factors,
  heparin potentiates thrombin inhibitors, direct oral anticoagulants block
  factor Xa or thrombin). A patient on anticoagulation therapy cannot form new
  clots efficiently, but existing clots that were formed before anticoagulation
  was started are not dissolved by the drug.
- **Fibrinolysis** dissolves clots that have already formed, by activating the
  plasminogen-plasmin system. A patient given fibrinolytic therapy (e.g., tPA,
  streptokinase) while having an acute thrombotic event has the existing clot
  actively dissolved.

In clinical practice, anticoagulation is used for prevention (preventing future
clots), and fibrinolysis is used for treatment (dissolving existing clots). They
are complementary, not equivalent. A normal fibrin level in the presence of
active fibrinolysis means clots that form are being cleared steadily. A normal
fibrin level in the absence of fibrinolytic activity means either no new clots
are being formed or they are being formed slowly.

## Consequence: resistance to fibrinolysis as a specific claim

If a fibrin deposit is described as "resistant to fibrinolysis," the claim is
specifically that plasmin does not degrade it normally. This could happen for
several reasons: (1) the fibrin has a conformation that plasmin cannot access
(e.g., an amyloid form that protects cleavage sites); (2) the fibrin is
protected by being wrapped in cells or protein; (3) PAI-1 or alpha-2-antiplasmin
is elevated and is suppressing plasmin activity before it can act. Resistance to
fibrinolysis is therefore not synonymous with "excessive clotting" — it is a
specific statement about the degradation of fibrin once it is formed. An ordinary
clotting excess (hypercoagulability) would show elevated fibrin or elevated D-
dimer; resistance to fibrinolysis specifically shows deposits that do not shrink
in the presence of tPA or uPA.

## Related terms

- Plasminogen — the zymogen activated to plasmin
- Plasmin — the active enzyme that degrades fibrin
- Tissue plasminogen activator — the physiological fibrinolytic enzyme
- Urokinase plasminogen activator — an alternative physiological activator
- Fibrin degradation product — the soluble products of fibrin cleavage by plasmin
- Fibrin — the substrate being degraded
