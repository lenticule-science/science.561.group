A fibrinaloid microclot is an anomalous fibrin deposit, reported primarily by
Pretorius, Kell, and colleagues, detected in platelet-poor plasma samples from
patients with Long COVID and myalgic encephalomyelitis / chronic fatigue
syndrome (ME/CFS). The defining characteristics, according to the original
reports, are: (1) amyloid fibrin conformation, detectable by amyloid-specific
staining with thioflavin T; (2) resistance to tissue plasminogen activator (tPA)
and urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA) — the normal fibrinolytic enzymes —
with the deposits persisting for hours in fibrinolysis assays where normal fibrin
would be completely degraded; (3) association with platelet hyperactivation
markers (elevated P-selectin, phosphatidylserine exposure) in the same plasma
samples. The mechanistic hypothesis is that these deposits impair capillary
blood flow, reduce tissue oxygen delivery, and thereby contribute to the fatigue
and post-exertional malaise characteristic of both diseases.

## Current evidence state: the core problem

The interpretation of fibrinaloid-microclot findings faces a specific and critical
problem: the discovery comes substantially from one research group's methodology,
independent large-cohort replication at scale is outstanding, and the assay's
specificity and reproducibility across laboratories remain unresolved.

**Specificity of thioflavin T.** Thioflavin T is a fluorescent dye that intercalates
into amyloid structures — beta-sheet-rich protein aggregates. Normal fibrin is not
amyloid; fibrin is fibrinogen molecules linked by thrombin-catalyzed covalent bonds
and held in a three-dimensional clot architecture. Under normal fibrinolysis,
plasmin cleaves fibrin into soluble degradation products and the clot dissolves.
However, thioflavin T fluorescence is not specific to fibrin amyloid. The dye binds
to any beta-sheet-rich structure, and amyloid-like conformations of fibrin can arise
not only as a novel pathologic state but also as an artifact of sample handling,
drying, or storage. Moreover, plasma contains other proteins (immunoglobulin, tau,
Aβ, among others) that can form amyloid structures. A thioflavin T-positive signal
in a plasma sample, by itself, does not distinguish fibrin-specific amyloid from
incidental amyloid formed from other plasma proteins.

**Lack of independent replication at scale.** The microclot findings have not been
independently replicated in large, well-controlled cohorts outside of the Pretorius
and Kell laboratory. This is the most critical gap. A single laboratory's discovery,
no matter how carefully conducted, does not establish a finding unless other
laboratories, using their own equipment, reagents, and technicians, can reproduce it.
The absence of this replication is not a minor oversight; it is the central
evidential problem. When replication attempts have been made by other groups, results
have been mixed.

**Imaging acquisition and quantification not standardized.** The microclot studies
report thioflavin T microscopy images showing deposits, but the acquisition
parameters (laser power, exposure time, gain), the quantification method (visual
scoring, automated thresholding, morphometry), and the operators who performed the
measurements are not standardized across published studies. Different microscopy
setups, different image processing algorithms, and different operator training will
detect and measure different numbers of deposits in the same sample. Without
standardization, the variability in reported microclot counts across studies cannot
be distinguished from true biological variation.

**No demonstrated causal link to symptoms.** The mechanistic hypothesis proposes that
microclot burden directly causes symptoms — that a higher count of fibrinaloid
deposits corresponds to more severe fatigue, more pronounced post-exertional malaise,
or greater tissue hypoxia. This causal link has not been demonstrated. What exists
are associations: patients with Long COVID and ME/CFS have been reported to have
higher microclot counts than healthy controls in some cohorts studied by the
Pretorius group. Association is not causation. To establish causation, one would
need to show that reducing microclot burden (through fibrinolytic therapy, for
example) improves symptoms, or that blocking microclot formation prevents symptom
onset. Neither of these experiments has been done.

## Distinction from related entities

**Microclot vs. thrombus.** A thrombus is a pathologically formed blood clot,
typically a platelet-rich clot with fibrin that forms inside a blood vessel,
occludes it partially or completely, and causes ischemic injury downstream. Thrombi
are typically millimeters to centimeters in size, detectable by ultrasound or
angiography, and are the pathologic event in myocardial infarction, stroke, and
pulmonary embolism. A fibrinaloid microclot, as defined here, is proposed to be a
much smaller fibrin deposit (micrometers), not associated with grossly visible
vascular occlusion, and detectable only by fluorescence microscopy. These are
different entities on different scales, formed by different mechanisms, and with
different clinical consequences. A patient with microclots as defined here would not
have a positive compression-ultrasound study of the veins (the standard for
detecting thrombosis), and a venous duplex showing clots is not the same finding as
microscopy showing microclots.

**Microclot vs. microthrombus in acute COVID-19.** Acute severe COVID-19 is
associated with frank microthrombi, detected by autopsy and pathology, particularly
in the lungs. These are actual thrombi on a microscopic scale — platelet-fibrin
clumps that occlude capillaries and contribute to the acute vascular injury,
respiratory failure, and death seen in severe COVID. These are established findings.
Fibrinaloid microclots as reported in long COVID are proposed to be different: not
platelet-rich thrombi, but fibrin deposits with amyloid conformation, resistant to
fibrinolysis. The long COVID literature treats microclots and microthrombi as
distinct findings, not the same entities persisting into chronic infection.

## What the entity is understood to be

If the fibrinaloid-microclot entity is real — if independent replication confirms
thioflavin T-positive amyloid fibrin deposits in long COVID and ME/CFS plasma,
with standardized quantification and imaging protocols — then the critical
remaining question is causation: do these deposits cause symptoms, or are they
incidental to the disease processes that do? A finding that is reliably detectable
is not automatically the pathogenic event. The entity as currently reported is
defined by its detection method. This means any claims about it depend entirely on
the assay's specificity and reproducibility, and that is exactly what is unsettled.
