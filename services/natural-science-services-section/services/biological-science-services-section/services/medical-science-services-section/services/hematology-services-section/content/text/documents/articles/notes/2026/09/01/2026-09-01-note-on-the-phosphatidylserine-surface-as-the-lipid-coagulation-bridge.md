## What this argues

The split between "lipid biology" and "coagulation biology" is convenient for
organizing knowledge into separate sections. However, it obscures a mechanistic
fact: coagulation cannot occur without a lipid. Specifically, the prothrombinase
complex — the serine protease assembly that generates thrombin — can only form on
an anionic membrane surface. In living hemostasis, that surface is
phosphatidylserine (PS), externalized from the inner to the outer leaflet of the
platelet membrane during platelet activation.

This note argues that PS externalization is the load-bearing connection between
lipid biochemistry and coagulation. More specifically, because flippase — the
ATP-dependent enzyme that maintains PS internalization — requires ATP to run,
anything that depletes ATP (mitochondrial dysfunction, hypoxia, metabolic failure)
will secondarily cause PS externalization and shift the coagulation state toward
the procoagulant. This observation offers a mechanistic bridge between the
cellular energy failure documented in Long COVID and myalgic encephalomyelitis
(ME/CFS) and the microvascular clotting reported by some researchers in those
conditions.

The bridge is testable: if ATP depletion drives PS externalization, and if PS
externalization drives microclot formation, then the three should be measured in
the same subjects, quantitatively, and correlated. This measurement has not been
done. Additionally, the downstream half of the hypothesis — that fibrinaloid
microclots exist and cause symptom burden — rests on findings from one research
group with limited independent replication. The hypothesis is elegant and
mechanistically plausible, but elegance is not evidence, and the connection
should be held as a framework for testing rather than a conclusion reached.

## The coagulation requirement: a membrane surface

The cell-based model of coagulation, which has superseded the classical cascade
model in modern hematology, describes coagulation as a membrane-localized
process. The critical amplification step is the assembly of the "tenase complex"
(factor IXa + factor VIIIa) and then the "prothrombinase complex" (factor Xa +
factor Va) on a negatively charged membrane surface. These are not soluble
complexes that can assemble anywhere in the bloodstream; they require physical
anchoring to a phospholipid bilayer with specific chemical properties.

The anchoring is mediated by calcium: vitamin K-dependent coagulation factors
(factors II, VII, IX, X) are carboxylated at their N-terminal residues,
producing negative charges. These negative charges coordinate calcium ions, which
in turn bridge the factors to the negative charges on anionic phospholipids in
the membrane. Without this bridging, the factors diffuse in solution and the
reaction rate is negligible — a factor of 200,000 slower than the membrane-
catalyzed rate.

In healthy hemostasis, PS is normally restricted to the inner (cytoplasmic)
leaflet of cell membranes by ATP-dependent flippase, which continuously pumps PS
inward. PS is almost never present on the outer (blood-facing) surface of resting
platelets. Upon platelet activation (by thrombin, collagen, ADP, or thromboxane),
intracellular calcium rises, activating scramblase — a calcium-activated, ATP-
independent lipid transporter that permits PS to move bidirectionally across the
membrane. PS rapidly accumulates on the outer leaflet. The coagulation complexes
then assemble on this newly exposed anionic surface.

This is why coagulation is described as a membrane-localized process: the
essential enzyme assembly sites exist only where PS is exposed. Coagulation is
confined in space, occurring only at sites of platelet activation. This spatial
confinement is the whole point — it arrests bleeding at the site of injury
without occlusion elsewhere.

## The ATP-dependent brake on PS externalization

Flippase, the enzyme that maintains PS internalization, is an ATP-dependent
pump. Every PS molecule transported inward requires ATP energy. In a healthy
resting cell with intact mitochondrial function, flippase is continuously active,
driven by ATP. Spontaneous flip-flop of PS from inner to outer leaflet is
immediately corrected by flippase transport back inward. Asymmetry is maintained.

If ATP levels fall — due to mitochondrial dysfunction, hypoxia, anoxia, or
metabolic failure — flippase activity drops immediately, proportional to ATP
concentration. The inward transport of PS slows. If scramblase is simultaneously
active (due to a concurrent calcium signal or cellular activation), PS movement
outward exceeds the inward flippase transport, and PS accumulates externally. Even
if scramblase is not active, PS that spontaneously flip-flops to the outer leaflet
is not being returned inward, so gradual PS accumulation occurs.

The consequence is that ATP depletion causes PS externalization even without
activation. The cell exposes PS not because it has received an activation signal,
but because it cannot afford the ATP cost of keeping PS hidden. And if PS is
exposed, the coagulation machinery can assemble on it. The cell becomes
procoagulant — capable of supporting coagulation — as a consequence of energy
failure.

This is the mechanistic link: energy failure → ATP depletion → flippase failure →
PS externalization → procoagulant surface → clot formation. Each step follows
from the transport biology. The connection is not speculative; it is what the
biochemistry predicts.

## Where this connects to Long COVID and ME/CFS

Both Long COVID and ME/CFS show two categories of findings that have been treated
as separate problems:

1. **Energy failure.** Reduced oxidative phosphorylation capacity (or its
   functional equivalent, lactate elevation on exertion, reduced ventilatory
   threshold), interpreted as mitochondrial dysfunction, documented in muscle
   biopsies and exercise tests. This points to ATP depletion, at least under
   stress.
2. **Microvascular findings.** Reports of fibrinaloid microclots, platelet
   hyperactivation, elevated P-selectin and PS exposure on platelets and red
   cells, reduced red cell deformability, elevated D-dimer in some cohorts and
   normal in others. These point to a clotting or microvascular problem.

These two finding categories have been treated as independent. The interpretation
has been roughly: "the energy system is broken, and the coagulation system is
broken, and we don't know how they connect." The PS externalization mechanism
offers a connection: energy failure drives PS externalization, and PS
externalization enables coagulation assembly.

If this is true, then energy failure in ME/CFS and Long COVID is not merely a
fatigue generator; it is a procoagulant driver. The microvascular symptoms
(exercise intolerance, dyspnea, brain fog) might be explained, at least in part,
by the impairment of capillary oxygen delivery caused by microclot burden,
enabled by ATP-driven PS externalization.

This would unify the findings into one mechanistic story: insufficient ATP
production means flippase failure means PS externalization means procoagulant
platelets means microclot formation means capillary obstruction means tissue
hypoxia means symptoms. It is an appealing story because each step has a known
mechanism.

## What would falsify or prove the hypothesis

The hypothesis makes specific, testable predictions:

1. **In the same subjects, measure ATP status (ATP/ADP ratio, phosphocreatine
   levels, or flux through oxidative phosphorylation), measure PS externalization
   on platelets and red cells (by flow cytometry, using anti-PS antibodies or
   annexin V binding), and measure microclot burden (if the assay is validated).
   These three should be correlated: subjects with lower ATP status should have
   higher PS externalization, and subjects with higher PS externalization should
   have higher microclot burden.**

   This measurement has not been done. Studies measure ATP status in some cohorts
   and microclot burden in other cohorts, but they do not measure both plus PS
   externalization in the same subjects. Until they do, the bridging connection
   remains untested.

2. **In vitro, deplete ATP in isolated platelets or cells and observe PS
   externalization without concurrent calcium signaling. Then ask whether the
   rate of PS externalization is proportional to the degree of ATP depletion.**

   This is a mechanistic experiment that would directly test the flippase-ATP
   dependency. ATP-depleted cells should externalize PS; ATP-replete cells should
   not, even if exposed to some non-physiological stimuli. This experiment has
   not been published in the Long COVID or ME/CFS context.

3. **In patients, measure PS externalization and ATP status before and after an
   intervention targeting ATP production (such as dichloroacetate, which shifts
   metabolism away from lactate toward oxidative phosphorylation). If ATP status
   improves, PS externalization should decrease; if PS externalization
   decreases, microclot burden should decrease (if measurable); if microclot
   burden decreases, symptoms should improve.**

   This is a therapeutic prediction. No such trial has been done.

If any of these predictions fail — if ATP status and PS externalization are not
correlated, or if restoring ATP does not reduce PS externalization, or if
reducing PS externalization or microclot burden does not improve symptoms — then
the connection is broken, and the hypothesis requires revision.

## The replication problem in microclot literature

The downstream half of the hypothesis — that fibrinaloid microclots exist, are
distinct entities, are resistant to fibrinolysis, and cause symptom burden — is
built on findings primarily from the Pretorius and Kell laboratory. Independent,
large-scale replication across multiple labs is outstanding. The methodology has
not been standardized; different labs using different thioflavin T protocols,
different imaging setups, and different quantification procedures report
inconsistent findings.

Thioflavin T is a fluorescent dye that binds to amyloid structures generally, not
specifically to fibrin amyloid. The specificity problem remains. Independent
confirmation that the thioflavin T signal in long COVID and ME/CFS plasma is
specifically fibrin amyloid (rather than some other amyloid-like structure) has
not been published.

The consequence is that the connection between PS externalization and microclot
burden, while mechanistically plausible, rests on a microclot entity that may not
survive replication. If the microclots do not exist — if independent labs cannot
reproduce the findings — then the downstream end of the bridge is not there, and
the hypothesis reverts to: energy failure causes PS externalization, which is
still testable, but loses the mechanistic explanation for symptom burden.

The bridging argument is therefore in two halves. The first half — ATP depletion
causes PS externalization — is well-founded in cell transport biology and is
testable. The second half — PS externalization causes clinically significant
microclot burden — is plausible mechanistically but depends on proof of the
microclot entity, which is unresolved. The hypothesis is worth testing at the
ATP-PS connection level, independent of whether the downstream microclot claim
survives.

## Discipline: what is not claimed

This synthesis does NOT claim:

- That microclots are proven (they are not; they are contested).
- That ATP depletion is the cause of Long COVID or ME/CFS (mitochondrial findings
  may be secondary to deconditioning, sleep disruption, or illness duration,
  rather than primary).
- That PS externalization accounts for all the symptom burden in these
  conditions (if it exists, it may be one of several concurrent mechanisms).
- That PS externalization has never been measured in Long COVID or ME/CFS (it
  has been reported in some studies), only that it has not been correlated with
  ATP status and microclot burden in the same subjects.

The claim is narrower and more testable: ATP-driven PS externalization is a
mechanism that connects energy failure to clotting potential, and this connection
is worth measuring because it would unify two otherwise separate finding
categories. Whether the connection is real is an open question.
