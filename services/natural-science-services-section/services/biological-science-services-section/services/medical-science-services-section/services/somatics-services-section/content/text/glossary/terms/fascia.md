Fascia is the continuous three-dimensional web of connective tissue pervading the body from head through feet. It envelops and interlaces every muscle, bone, organ, nerve, and blood vessel. Unlike discrete anatomical structures, fascia is a connected network without boundary—tissue traditionally discarded during dissection is now understood to be load-bearing and functionally significant. The fascial network has two complementary functions understood through different frameworks. Structurally, fascia forms the continuous tension elements of the [tensegrity](tensegrity-in-movement.md) model: it distributes mechanical load throughout the body rather than concentrating force at joints or through a stacked skeleton. Sensorially, fascia is densely innervated with mechanoreceptors (Ruffini endings, Pacinian corpuscles, interstitial receptors) contributing substantially to the nervous system's awareness of body position and state—[proprioception](proprioception.md). Robert Schleip's research at the Fascia Research Group established that fascia is not metabolically inert but responsive to mechanical input: stretching, pressure, movement, and injury trigger inflammatory and adaptive responses in fascial tissue. This plasticity means that movement practices creating specific fascial stress patterns produce specific adaptations in fascial organization and responsiveness. The distinction between fascia's structural and sensory roles dissolves at this point: the same tissue organizing mechanical load also organizes the nervous system's perception of that load. Fascia differs from muscle—muscle is contractile tissue producing force through sarcomere filament sliding; fascia is connective tissue primarily composed of collagen and elastin fibers arranged in networks. Muscle cells may be within a fascial envelope (fascicles, bundles of muscle fibers, wrapped in fascia), but fascia is not muscle. Fascial layers around muscle are epimysium; within muscle, fascia forms perimysium and endomysium. Fascia is distinguished from other connective tissues (ligaments, tendons) by its continuity and distribution. Ligaments and tendons are discrete structures with clear borders and specific attachments. Fascia is the continuous background tissue throughout which discrete structures embed. This continuity is functionally significant: a restriction or tension change at one point alters the mechanical environment at distant points.
