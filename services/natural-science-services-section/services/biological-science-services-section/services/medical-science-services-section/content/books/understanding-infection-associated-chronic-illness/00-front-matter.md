# About this book {.unnumbered}

## Who this book is for

You, or someone you love, has been told "your tests are normal" while
still living with fatigue, brain fog, or exercise intolerance that never
resolved after an infection. This book is for that reader: it does not
assume a medical or research background, and it does not promise a cure.
It exists to give you the same honest map researchers themselves are
working from -- what is established, what is a well-supported hypothesis
still being tested, and what is genuinely contested -- so you can read a
headline, a new study, or your own chart with better judgment.

## How this book is organized

Chapter 1 defines infection-associated chronic illness (IACI) itself --
the umbrella term the NIH and the National Academies now use for the
family of chronic conditions that follow an infection, and why that
framing matters for how research and care get organized. Chapter 2 takes
long COVID / post-COVID-19 condition as the case study: its three
competing case definitions (WHO, RECOVER, NASEM), the real uncertainty in
its prevalence estimates, and why a number from one study is not always a
claim about the same population as a number from another. Chapter 3 maps
the leading mechanistic hypotheses -- viral persistence, immune
dysregulation (including Epstein-Barr virus reactivation and the
contested microclot hypothesis), and dysautonomia -- honestly labeled by their
actual evidence state, not by which sounds most convincing. Chapter 4
covers post-exertional malaise (PEM) and pacing, the symptom and the
self-management practice that connects long COVID to myalgic
encephalomyelitis (ME/CFS) and that dominates how patients actually have
to live day to day. Chapter 5 teaches you to read the primary research
yourself: what a case definition controls for, what a prevalence
estimate's confidence interval is telling you, and how to tell a
replicated finding from a single group's still-unconfirmed result.

## A note on honesty

Nothing in this book is settled the way, say, germ theory is settled.
Infection-associated chronic illness is an active, rapidly moving research
field, and this book says so plainly wherever a claim is a hypothesis
rather than an established finding, and cites the primary study behind
every specific number or mechanism it names. This book is not medical
advice and does not replace a clinician who knows your case; it exists so
that the conversation you have with that clinician, or with a study you
read on your own, starts from an accurate picture of what is and is not
yet known.

Every chapter cites its sources. A parenthetical author-year citation like [@dehlia2024]
after a sentence points to a specific published study; the full citation
-- authors, journal, year, and a link you can look up yourself -- is
collected in the References section at the end of the book. You don't
need to interrupt your reading to check these, but they are there
whenever you want to see exactly where a number or claim came from.
