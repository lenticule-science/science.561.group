# Chapter 1: What Infection-Associated Chronic Illness Is

## An umbrella term for a pattern that keeps repeating

Let's start with a fact that researchers have noticed over the past few years: something happens after infection, and it keeps happening the same way, regardless of which infection triggers it.

When people survive certain infections — SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19), the Ebola virus, dengue fever, and the bacterium that causes Lyme disease — a fraction of them experience chronic symptoms that persist for months or years. These symptoms look similar across different infections: profound fatigue that rest doesn't fix, a difficulty with thinking that people call "brain fog," and something called post-exertional malaise — a worsening of symptoms that comes a day or two after physical or cognitive exertion and can last for weeks [@choutka2022].

The pattern is consistent enough that in 2024, the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine — a research body that advises the U.S. government — convened a workshop that used one umbrella term for these conditions: **infection-associated chronic illness**, or IACI for short [@nasem2024workshop]. The idea behind the umbrella is practical: if the pattern is the same across infections, maybe testing research across different illnesses can teach us something faster than studying each one alone.

This chapter explains what IACI is, which specific conditions it includes, and what researchers actually know versus what they're still trying to figure out.

## Which conditions are in the umbrella

This book discusses six infection-associated chronic conditions:

- **Long COVID** (also called post-COVID-19 condition, PASC, or post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection): chronic symptoms that appear after SARS-CoV-2 infection and persist for at least three months [@nasem2024longcovid].
- **Myalgic encephalomyelitis / chronic fatigue syndrome** (ME/CFS): a pre-existing condition (decades older than COVID) characterized by fatigue that rest does not fix, post-exertional malaise, and cognitive impairment [@iom2015].
- **Post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome** (PTLDS): persistent pain, fatigue, or cognitive impairment that lasts after antibiotic treatment for *Borrelia burgdorferi* infection (the bacterium that causes Lyme disease) [@aucott2013].
- **Post-Ebola syndrome**: chronic symptoms including fatigue, headache, muscle pain, and memory loss that persist after Ebola infection [@sneller2019].
- **Post-dengue fatigue syndrome**: significant fatigue that persists after hospitalization for dengue fever [@seet2007].
- **Post-SARS chronic illness**: chronic fatigue and disability that persisted for years in survivors of the 2003 SARS-CoV-1 outbreak [@moldofsky2011; @lam2009].

These six are not the whole list of post-infection syndromes, but they are the ones this book discusses because they show similar patterns and have attracted enough research interest to be measurable [@nasem2024workshop].

## Why this grouping matters

You might ask: why lump them together instead of treating each one separately?

The practical answer is a research problem. Individually, post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome or post-Ebola syndrome are rare enough and geographically scattered enough that it is nearly impossible to gather the number of patients needed to run a rigorous clinical trial or conduct the genetic and immunological studies that would reveal what's happening in the body. A single researcher in a single hospital might see a handful of cases a year.

But long COVID arrived suddenly, infecting hundreds of millions of people worldwide. A meaningful fraction of those people still have symptoms years later — a self-report tracking survey in the United States alone has put current prevalence at roughly 6-7.5% of all adults [@cdc2023pulse], and a global modeling effort estimated well over 100 million people worldwide experienced at least one persistent symptom cluster in 2020-2021 alone [@wulfhanson2022]. That scale created funding and research infrastructure that smaller post-infection illnesses had never had access to, including large, federally coordinated cohort studies like the NIH's RECOVER initiative, which enrolled nearly 10,000 participants to build a shared research definition of long COVID [@thaweethai2023].

The idea behind IACI is: test the hypotheses on long COVID, where you have millions of potential participants and large-scale research funding. Then take what you learn and ask whether it applies to the smaller, under-resourced conditions. If the mechanism that drives post-exertional malaise in long COVID is the same one driving it in ME/CFS, that finding helps both populations. If mechanisms differ, that difference is also useful to know.

As the National Academies put it, the IACI framing is a **strategy for improving research design**, not a claim that these six conditions are one disease [@nasem2024workshop]. Whether they turn out to share one mechanism, several mechanisms, or are etiologically distinct despite their symptom overlap — that is exactly the question the research agenda exists to answer.

## The class symptom cluster

Despite their different infectious triggers, these conditions share overlapping core symptoms. The three that show up most consistently across all named IACI conditions are:

**Fatigue that rest does not fix.** This is not ordinary tiredness. It is not the fatigue after exertion that resolves with sleep. It is a baseline level of exhaustion that persists even with adequate rest, and that worsens with activity in a way that simple deconditioning does not explain [@choutka2022].

**Cognitive impairment.** People commonly describe this as "brain fog": difficulty with concentration, memory problems, or a general slowness in thinking that was not there before the infection. In some people it is mild; in others it is severe enough to affect work or daily functioning [@choutka2022].

**Post-exertional malaise (PEM).** This is the symptom that links long COVID most directly to ME/CFS. Post-exertional malaise is not ordinary muscle soreness from exercise. It is a disproportionate worsening of symptoms — fatigue, pain, cognitive fog, or autonomic symptoms like heart palpitations — that often comes on hours to days after physical, cognitive, or emotional exertion and can last for days to weeks [@iom2015]. A person might take a short walk and feel fine immediately afterward, then become profoundly fatigued 24 or 48 hours later, with the worsening persisting for a week or longer.

Other symptoms show up frequently but not universally: difficulty regulating body temperature and blood pressure (dysautonomia), gastrointestinal problems, sleep that does not feel restorative, and various forms of pain (headaches, muscle or joint pain, or nerve pain).

## What is established, what is being tested, and what is genuinely unknown

This is the most important section of this chapter, because it is where the book separates what is actually known from what *sounds* known.

### Established findings

These facts are supported by evidence that has been tested and replicated across multiple studies:

1. **People do experience persistent symptoms after these infections.** This is not debated. Millions of people with long COVID have been documented. Studies of ME/CFS cohorts exist across multiple continents. Post-SARS chronic illness was documented in survivors from the 2003 outbreak, with symptoms lasting years [@moldofsky2011; @lam2009], and post-Ebola sequelae have been documented in a large longitudinal cohort in Liberia [@sneller2019].

2. **A substantial share of people infected experience these symptoms.** Prevalence varies by how you measure it. The U.S. CDC's self-report household survey (asking people "do you have long COVID now?") finds roughly 6-7.5% of all adults report current long COVID [@cdc2023pulse]. Smaller, clinically validated cohorts report lower numbers, but exact figures depend on which definition of long COVID you use — a problem discussed in Chapter 2. The point: it is not a tiny fraction, but it is also not everyone who is infected.

3. **Symptom clusters overlap across the IACI conditions.** Fatigue, post-exertional malaise, and cognitive impairment show up across long COVID, ME/CFS, PTLDS, post-Ebola, and post-dengue cohorts. Different cohorts emphasize different combinations, but the pattern is consistent enough to justify grouping them [@choutka2022; @nasem2024workshop].

4. **These are not simply deconditioning or psychological illness.** This is an important boundary to state plainly. The fatigue and post-exertional worsening are not explained by simple deconditioning. The post-exertional malaise response — a delayed and prolonged symptom flare — does not fit patterns of ordinary fatigue or typical exercise response, a distinction the 2015 U.S. Institute of Medicine report made central to its diagnostic criteria [@iom2015]. (Psychiatric symptoms *can* occur as a secondary consequence of living with a chronic disabling illness, which is a separate point.)

### Mechanistic hypotheses actively being tested

These are candidate explanations for what causes IACI symptoms. They have supporting evidence, but that evidence is not yet conclusive, and different research groups are actively testing them:

- **Viral persistence**: SARS-CoV-2 viral RNA has been detected in the blood of some patients, and in one study that followed patients for two to three months, viral RNA in the blood around the time of diagnosis was one of the early factors that predicted who went on to have lasting symptoms [@su2022]. Whether viral material that lingers is actively driving symptoms, or is an inert remnant of the infection, is not yet settled. One randomized, placebo-controlled trial testing an antiviral drug (nirmatrelvir-ritonavir) on long COVID patients found that it did not significantly improve symptoms compared to placebo, which complicates the simplest version of the "persistence causes symptoms" story without ruling out persistence as a contributing factor in some patients [@sawano2025].

- **Immune dysregulation**: Studies of long COVID show signs of abnormal immune activation: altered T-cell and B-cell populations, an exaggerated antibody response, and lower cortisol levels [@klein2023]. Whether these represent a cause of symptoms or a consequence of chronic illness is still being determined.

- **Latent virus reactivation**: Some long COVID patients show signs of reactivation of Epstein-Barr virus (a common virus that usually remains dormant after childhood infection) [@gold2021; @su2022]. Whether EBV reactivation is driving symptoms or is a consequence of immune dysregulation is not yet established.

- **Autonomic nervous system dysfunction**: Many people with long COVID have symptoms of dysautonomia — difficulty regulating heart rate, blood pressure, and other autonomic functions. Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), a specific form of dysautonomia, has been documented in a significant minority of long COVID patients evaluated in specialized clinics [@bjornson2025]. Whether this is a primary mechanism or a secondary consequence is still being studied.

- **Metabolic and mitochondrial dysfunction**: Some research suggests that cells in people with long COVID are not producing energy efficiently. One study found that in long COVID patients with post-exertional malaise, a standardized exercise challenge triggered measurably worse abnormalities in muscle metabolism the following day [@appelman2024]. Whether this is a primary cause of symptoms or a consequence of another upstream problem is not yet known.

- **Microbiome dysbiosis**: Alterations in gut bacterial composition at the time of hospital admission have been shown to predict long COVID status six months later in a prospective cohort, with the dysbiosis correlating with symptoms including fatigue and poor memory [@liu2022]. Whether the dysbiosis is causing the illness or is a consequence of it is unknown.

### Genuinely contested questions

These are areas where different research groups have published conflicting results or where the evidence is genuinely ambiguous:

- **Microclots and blood clotting abnormalities**: One research group has reported finding anomalous amyloid-form fibrin clots in long COVID blood samples that resist normal breakdown [@kellpretorius2022]. Another group has published a direct critique arguing these microclots are not unique to long COVID, appear in other inflammatory and aging states, and that the measurement methods used across the field lack standardization [@hunt2024]. This remains an active scientific dispute without resolution.

- **Whether G-protein-coupled receptor autoantibodies drive dysautonomia**: Some studies using functional assays (tests that measure biological activity on living cells) report high levels of autoantibodies against certain cell-surface receptors in long COVID patients, with correlations to specific symptoms [@hofmann2025]. A separate case-control study using a standardized binding assay (a test that measures antibody binding to antigens fixed on a plate) found no meaningful difference between patients with POTS (from all causes) and healthy controls for any of eleven receptors tested [@hall2022]. The discrepancy may reflect differences in methodology — a functional assay and a binding assay measure different properties — rather than disagreement about biology, but it remains unresolved.

### What is genuinely not yet studied

And finally, some important questions simply have not been adequately addressed yet:

- **Whether the same mechanism drives symptoms in all people with IACI, or whether different mechanisms produce the same symptom clusters in different people.** Long COVID is almost certainly mechanistically heterogeneous — different people may become ill through different pathways. Testing this requires large studies with detailed biomarking and careful phenotyping, which are only now being built.

- **Why the same infection produces different outcomes in different people** — why some people recover fully while others develop chronic symptoms. Early infection characteristics predict risk only partly [@su2022]. Most of the variation remains unexplained.

- **Which findings from long COVID mechanistic studies actually transfer to ME/CFS, PTLDS, or post-Ebola conditions.** Long COVID and ME/CFS show overlapping symptoms, but mechanistic findings from long COVID studies cannot be automatically assumed to apply to ME/CFS. Testing this requires cross-condition research that is only beginning [@nasem2024workshop].

## What this means for reading the rest of this book

The landscape of IACI is one where:

- **Facts exist and are often surprising.** Infection does produce chronic illness in a measurable fraction of people. The symptoms are real and disabling.

- **Mechanisms remain largely unknown.** Multiple candidate explanations exist, all with supporting evidence, none conclusively proven.

- **Numbers require context.** A prevalence figure, a mechanism study, or a treatment trial is only interpretable if you know which definition of the disease it used, which population it sampled, and what it is measuring. The same condition studied by different researchers sometimes produces dramatically different numbers — not because the researchers are wrong, but because they asked subtly different populations different questions.

- **Honesty about uncertainty is the mark of good science, not a bug.** When researchers say "we don't know yet," they are being precise about what the evidence supports. That precision is what allows the field to move forward.

The remaining chapters walk through long COVID's case definitions and prevalence (Chapter 2), the leading mechanistic hypotheses and their evidence state (Chapter 3), post-exertional malaise and the practice of pacing (Chapter 4), and how to read primary research yourself (Chapter 5). Each teaches you to ask the questions that separate established findings from active hypotheses, so that when you read a headline or talk with a clinician, you are working from the same map the researchers themselves are using.
