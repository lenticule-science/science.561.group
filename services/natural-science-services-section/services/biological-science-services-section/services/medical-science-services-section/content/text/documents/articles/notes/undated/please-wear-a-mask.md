In the past few weeks, there’s been a sharp rise in calls for mass action across parts of the U.S. political left. People who had previously emphasized electoral strategy — urging others to vote, “stay realistic,” or trust institutional processes — are now posting with a new sense of urgency. Many are calling for general strikes, student occupations, and escalated confrontation with state power. There’s a tone of frustration, panic, and resolve.

This shift is happening alongside a broader collapse in the perceived legitimacy of liberal governance. Court decisions, executive overreach, and rising far-right activity have made it harder to believe that change will come from within the system. For many, the pivot to movement-building feels necessary.

But the people making these calls are often the same ones who, over the last four years, demonstrated little willingness to take small, sustained political action in their own lives — especially when that action required stepping outside of institutional cues or social norms.

COVID made this clear. When public health measures were first introduced, many complied. But when mandates were lifted and public attention shifted, most dropped precautions. Masking, isolation, ventilation — these were treated less as practical tools for collective safety and more as artifacts of a previous moment. The virus didn’t stop spreading. The risk didn’t end. But the perception that “the moment had passed” was enough for most to disengage.

Throughout this period, people most directly impacted — disabled people, immunocompromised people, survivors of previous public health abandonment — continued to insist that conditions had not materially changed. Many continued to mask, to avoid high-risk gatherings, and to organize for safer collective practices. But these voices were often sidelined or ignored, even within ostensibly radical spaces.

The pattern this reveals is not simply one of neglect, but of political orientation.

A significant number of people treat their politics as responsive — something activated by external conditions, mass consensus, or visible crisis. They act when the moment feels historic, when others are already moving, when it feels socially or culturally reinforced to do so. Outside of those moments, their politics become dormant.

Others operate differently. They don’t wait for cues from institutions or peers. Their orientation is not reactive, but ongoing. They assess material conditions and act accordingly, even — especially — when doing so is inconvenient or unpopular. Their politics show up in habits, practices, and choices, not only in statements.

This difference has become particularly visible in how people are responding now.

The individuals who abandoned public health precautions when the mandates ended are now calling for mass collective action. In many cases, their behavior has not changed. They are still attending in-person gatherings without ventilation or masking. Still treating COVID as something that happened, rather than something that continues. Still planning political events in ways that exclude high-risk people. But now they’re doing so while invoking urgency, crisis, and the need for large-scale solidarity.

At the same time, the people who have been organizing consistently — who maintained care networks, distros, and health practices when it was no longer widely expected — are still doing that. They are less likely to be making dramatic calls. Their messaging is quieter, often limited to the same request they’ve been making for years:  
**“Please wear a mask.”**

This should not be misunderstood as hesitation or political softness. It reflects a different approach — one grounded in continuity rather than rupture. These are people who never stopped acting because they never required a sense of collective excitement to begin. They are not reactive because they never disengaged.

What makes this contrast significant is not just the surface inconsistency in who is calling for action now. It’s what that inconsistency reveals: that many people calling for mass movement have not demonstrated an ability or willingness to take responsibility at smaller scales — in their own behaviors, in their social environments, in the basic conditions of their organizing spaces.

The point is not to shame individuals for inconsistency. It’s to ask a structural question:  
What kind of organizing can be built by people who only act when the external pressure is high enough?  
What kind of collective can grow out of practices that depend on permission, consensus, or urgency to exist at all?

If someone cannot sustain a simple action like masking — one that materially reduces harm, requires no institutional support, and asks only for minor personal adjustment — it raises reasonable doubts about their readiness to participate in, let alone coordinate, more complex forms of resistance.

This is not a question of moral purity. It is a question of political reliability.

Movements require people who can take initiative without being prompted.  
Who can act with consistency even when others aren’t watching.  
Who don’t collapse their responsibilities the moment it becomes socially awkward to maintain them.

COVID continues. The conditions that made masking necessary have not gone away. The people who continue to mask are not holding onto the past — they are acting in accordance with the present. And they are demonstrating, with clarity, that they do not need a rupture to act. They act because the analysis they hold obligates them to. That is what makes it political.

This is what many of the recent calls for mass organizing fail to address.  
They skip over the foundation.  
They assume shared commitment where there is none.  
They imagine participation without the practices that make participation possible.

The result is a contradiction: people calling for a collective they have not shown they know how to sustain.

The people still masking are not disengaged from struggle.  
They’re asking a question:  
If you couldn’t hold the line when it was small,  
why should we trust you to hold it now?
