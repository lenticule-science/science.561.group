Pulse diagnosis is one of TCM&rsquo;s two signature diagnostic methods (alongside [tongue diagnosis](../../../../../../services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/tongue-diagnosis-basics.md)). Where Western medicine takes the pulse to measure rate and rhythm, TCM reads the pulse as a complex signal carrying information about the state of [Qi](../../../../../../services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/qi.md), [Blood](../../../../../../services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/xue.md), the [organ-function systems](../../../../../../services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/zang-fu.md), and the nature of any pathological pattern present. A skilled TCM practitioner can identify the Eight Principles character (interior/exterior, hot/cold, excess/deficient) and narrow the organ-system involvement from the pulse alone.

This guide introduces the basic framework — enough to understand pulse references in TCM term definitions.

## Where to take the pulse

TCM pulse diagnosis uses the **radial artery** at the wrist — the same artery Western medicine uses, but assessed at three adjacent positions on each wrist:

The practitioner places three fingers on the three positions simultaneously and assesses each position independently. This allows organ-specific assessment: a weak pulse at the right Guan position suggests Spleen Qi deficiency; a wiry pulse at the left Guan position suggests Liver Qi stagnation.

## What the practitioner feels for

TCM pulse assessment evaluates multiple qualities simultaneously:

**Depth** — how much pressure is needed to feel the pulse:

- **Floating** (fu 浮): Felt with light pressure, disappears with heavy pressure → exterior pattern, or Yin deficiency (Yin cannot anchor the pulse)

- **Deep** (chen 沉): Felt only with heavy pressure → interior pattern, or [Yang deficiency](../../../../../../services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/yang-deficiency.md)

**Rate** — beats per respiration (the traditional unit) or per minute:

- **Rapid** (shuo 数): >5 beats per respiration (~90+ bpm) → heat

- **Slow** (chi 迟): <4 beats per respiration (~60 bpm or less) → cold

**Width and force**:

- **Thin/thready** (xi 细): Narrow and weak → Blood or Yin deficiency (insufficient fluid volume)

- **Full/large** (da 大): Wide and forceful → excess heat

- **Weak** (ruo 弱): Deep, thin, and soft → Qi and Blood deficiency

- **Forceful** (you li 有力): Strong at all depths → excess pattern

**Shape and quality** — the distinctive feel:

- **Wiry** (xian 弦): Taut like a guitar string → Liver involvement, [Qi stagnation](../../../../../../services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/qi-stagnation.md), pain

- **Slippery** (hua 滑): Round, smooth, rolling like beads → [Phlegm](../../../../../../services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/phlegm.md), dampness, food stagnation, or pregnancy

- **Choppy** (se 涩): Rough, catching, uneven → [Blood stasis](../../../../glossary/terms/blood-stasis.md)

- **Soggy** (ru 濡): Floating, thin, and soft → dampness with deficiency

- **Tight** (jin 紧): Taut and vibrating like a twisted rope → cold, pain

## The most common clinical pulses

**Wiry and thin** → Liver Qi stagnation with Blood deficiency. The classic &ldquo;stressed and depleted&rdquo; pulse.

**Slippery and rapid** → [Damp-heat](../../../../../../services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/damp-heat.md). The dampness makes the pulse slippery; the heat makes it rapid. This is the pulse that accompanies the thick yellow greasy tongue in damp-heat patterns.

**Deep, slow, and weak** → [Yang deficiency](../../../../../../services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/yang-deficiency.md). The pulse retreats, slows, and loses force — the body lacks warming, activating power.

**Thin and rapid** → [Yin deficiency](../../../../../../services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/yin-deficiency.md). The pulse is thin (insufficient fluid) and rapid (relative heat from depleted cooling).

**Choppy** → [Blood stasis](../../../../glossary/terms/blood-stasis.md). The pulse catches and stutters, reflecting impeded blood flow.

## Learning to read the pulse

Pulse diagnosis is traditionally considered the most difficult TCM skill to learn — requiring years of supervised practice to develop sensitivity and reliability. The descriptions above are conceptual frameworks; the actual tactile experience of a wiry versus a slippery pulse can only be learned through practice on real patients under guidance.

This is not a limitation of the method — it is a recognition that pulse diagnosis is a skilled perception, like wine tasting or musical ear training. The information is in the signal; the challenge is developing the sensitivity to read it.
