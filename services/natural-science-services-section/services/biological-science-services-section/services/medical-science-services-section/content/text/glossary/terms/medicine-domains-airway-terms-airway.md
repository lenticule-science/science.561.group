The anatomical passage carrying gas between the environment and the lungs, assessed clinically through four functions: patency, oxygenation, ventilation, and protection.

The **airway** is the anatomical passage that carries gas between the environment and the lungs. In clinical practice it is divided into upper and lower segments, a distinction that organizes both assessment and intervention.

The **upper airway** extends from the nose and mouth through the pharynx (nasopharynx, oropharynx, hypopharynx) to the larynx and vocal cords. It is responsible for filtering, warming, and humidifying inspired air and for protecting the lower airway from aspiration. Upper-airway problems tend to produce [stridor](../../../../services/airway-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/stridor.md) — a high-pitched inspiratory sound indicating narrowing above or at the level of the vocal cords.

The **lower airway** extends from the trachea through the bronchi and bronchioles to the alveoli, where gas exchange occurs. Lower-airway problems tend to produce [wheeze](../../../../services/airway-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/wheeze.md) — a musical expiratory sound indicating narrowing below the vocal cords, typically from bronchospasm or mucus plugging.

The clinical significance of the airway is defined by four bedside functions:

- **Patency** — Is the passage open? Obstruction at any level threatens all downstream functions.

- **[Oxygenation](../../../../services/airway-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/oxygenation.md)** — Is oxygen reaching arterial blood?

- **[Ventilation](../../../../services/airway-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/ventilation.md)** — Is carbon dioxide being eliminated?

- **Protection** — Is the airway defended against aspiration of gastric contents, blood, or secretions?

Each function can fail independently. A patient can have a patent airway but inadequate oxygenation (as in pneumonia). A patient can be well-oxygenated but poorly ventilated (as in early opioid toxicity, where supplemental oxygen may mask rising CO2). Assessing all four functions — not just one — is the foundation of airway management.
