## Definition and scope

**Infection-associated chronic illness** (IACI) is a research-coordination and funding term, not a diagnostic category or a claim of shared disease mechanism.
The term groups chronic symptom clusters that appear following infection with diverse pathogens — long COVID (PASC), myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome (PTLDS), post-Ebola syndrome, post-dengue syndrome, and post-SARS-1 chronic illness — for the purposes of pooling research infrastructure, harmonizing outcome measures, and enabling hypothesis testing across conditions too individually rare or geographically dispersed to study at adequate statistical power.

The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine formalized the term in 2024, defining long COVID explicitly as "an infection-associated chronic condition occurring after SARS-CoV-2 infection that is present for at least 3 months as a continuous, relapsing and remitting, or progressive disease state that can present as singular or multiple symptoms and/or diagnosable conditions."
The umbrella framing originated in the earlier scientific literature as "post-acute infection syndromes" (PAIS), proposed by Choutka and colleagues in 2022 to describe the observation that symptoms following diverse infections showed "relatively similar symptom profiles... irrespective of the infectious agent."

### Important distinction: PAIS versus IACC/IACI

The term carries two related but distinct lineages.
**Post-acute infection syndromes (PAIS)** is the scientific framing: a hypothesis that these conditions share overlapping symptom patterns and warrant unified research attention.
**Infection-associated chronic condition/illness (IACC/IACI)** is the 2024 policy and funding language adopted by NASEM and NIH to operationalize that hypothesis.
This document discusses both, but the distinction matters: PAIS is a proposed scientific class based on symptom convergence; IACC is a governance term used to coordinate research funding and infrastructure.

### A research lens, not a settled entity

This is explicitly a research lens, not evidence of one disease.
IACC groups conditions for methodological purposes — enabling trials to harmonize outcome measures across structurally different infections, allowing biobank and cohort infrastructure to serve multiple conditions, and creating the statistical power needed to test mechanistic hypotheses that cannot be powered in single rare conditions.
As the NASEM 2024 workshop proceedings state explicitly, treating these conditions as one research class is a STRATEGY for improving research design, not a claim that they are one pathophysiology.

Whether these conditions share a common mechanism, several convergent mechanisms, or are etiologically distinct conditions that happen to produce overlapping symptom clusters because fatigue, post-exertional malaise, and cognitive impairment are downstream expressions of many different types of insults — this remains the central open question the research agenda exists to address.
No study to date has identified a single biomarker, causal pathway, or diagnostic test present across all six named conditions.

## Shared phenomenology

Despite their distinct infectious triggers, the named conditions share overlapping core symptoms, documented independently in multiple cohorts:

- **Fatigue not relieved by rest** — present across long COVID, ME/CFS, PTLDS, post-Ebola, post-dengue, and post-SARS-1 cohorts
- **Post-exertional malaise (PEM)** — disproportionate and delayed worsening of symptoms following physical, cognitive, or emotional exertion, documented in long COVID and ME/CFS cohorts; present in post-SARS-1 chronic syndrome
- **Cognitive impairment** — variously described as "brain fog," memory impairment, or attentional dysfunction, documented in long COVID, ME/CFS, and post-SARS-1 survivors
- **Autonomic symptoms** — orthostatic intolerance, POTS-like symptoms, dysautonomia, reported in long COVID and ME/CFS cohorts

### Long COVID / Post-acute COVID-19 (PASC)

Global prevalence: Wulf Hanson and colleagues' 2022 Global Burden of Disease analysis estimated ~6.2% of symptomatic COVID-19 cases developed one of three studied symptom clusters (fatigue, cognitive, respiratory) at 3 months post-infection; the same group estimated ~144.7 million individuals had developed long COVID by end of 2021, with ~15.1% (~22 million) remaining symptomatic at 12 months.
The RECOVER-Adult cohort (n=9,764) developed a consensus case definition distinguishing long COVID from baseline chronic disease or other diagnoses, establishing the phenomenological foundation for the term.

### Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS)

Established case definitions exist (IOM 2015, Canadian Consensus Criteria 2003), mandating post-exertional malaise as a cardinal feature.
The core diagnostic triad is fatigue lasting >6 months not relieved by rest, post-exertional malaise, and unrefreshing sleep.
Clinical prevalence is estimated at 836,000 to 2.5 million in the US (IOM 2015); the wide range reflects definitional heterogeneity in epidemiological studies.

### Overlap between long COVID and ME/CFS

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis by Dehlia and Guthridge pooling data from 13 studies (n=1,973 long-COVID patients) found that 51% (95% CI 42–60%) of long-COVID patients met ME/CFS diagnostic criteria, establishing substantial phenomenological overlap between the two conditions in the same patients.

### Post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome (PTLDS)

Defined as persisting pain, fatigue, or cognitive impairment following antibiotic-cleared *Borrelia burgdorferi* infection.
Prevalence estimates range from 10–20% of optimally treated patients in multiple cohort studies, with fatigue being the most frequent persistent symptom (~39% at 6 months in standardized-measure cohorts).
The pathophysiology of PTLDS remains unknown.

### Post-Ebola syndrome

The PREVAIL III prospective cohort study (n=966 Ebola-antibody-positive survivors vs. 2,350 antibody-negative contacts, ~90% followed 12 months) tracked six symptoms significantly elevated in survivors versus contacts: urinary frequency, headache, fatigue, muscle pain, memory loss, and joint pain.
All six declined during follow-up.
Uveitis was the sole exception, rising from 26.4% in survivors at baseline to 33.3% by year 1.

### Post-dengue fatigue syndrome

Approximately 20–25% of hospitalized dengue-fever patients in cohort studies report significant fatigue at 2 months post-infection, establishing post-dengue fatigue syndrome as a common post-viral sequela.

### Post-SARS (SARS-CoV-1, 2003) chronic syndrome

The 2009 Archives of Internal Medicine study by Lam and colleagues found that 27.1% of 233 SARS survivors met modified 1994 CDC chronic fatigue syndrome criteria at a mean follow-up of 41.3 months (approximately 3.4 years) after infection, documenting long-term disability in a substantial fraction of survivors.
This finding predates long COVID by over a decade and is the historical precedent explicitly cited in recent long-COVID/ME-CFS overlap literature.

## Why the class framing matters for research design

NASEM 2024 and Choutka et al. 2022 state the methodological rationale explicitly:

1. **Statistical power through pooling** — each individual condition (PTLDS, post-dengue, post-Ebola, post-SARS-1) has historically been too rare or too temporally/geographically isolated to support well-powered biomarker research or clinical trials.
  Grouping them allows shared case definitions, outcome measures, and research infrastructure to serve multiple conditions simultaneously.

2. **Long COVID as a research engine** — the much larger patient population (tens of millions) and dramatically increased funding for long COVID (the NIH RECOVER initiative allocated ~$1 billion) creates unprecedented opportunity to test hypotheses about autonomic dysfunction, viral persistence, EBV reactivation, autoimmunity, and microbiome dysbiosis at a scale previously impossible for ME/CFS or PTLDS alone.
  Findings generated at this scale can then be tested for transfer to the smaller, under-resourced conditions.

3. **Harmonized measurement frameworks** — a shared symptom-based case definition and unified outcome-measure set (validated instruments for post-exertional malaise, cognitive impairment, autonomic dysfunction) allow trials to compare results across studies despite different infectious triggers, creating a common evidence base where none existed.

4. **Historical precedent argument** — the post-SARS-1 chronic-illness literature (Lam 2009, Moldofsky 2011) was a small, underfunded area of research for over a decade.
  The 2022–2024 framing of PAIS and IACC explicitly cites this history to argue against treating each new infectious agent (dengue, Ebola, COVID-19) as an unprecedented phenomenon requiring research to restart from zero each time.
  The recurrence of the pattern across pathogens is itself evidence for taking the class perspective seriously.

## What is not yet established: mechanism

The NASEM 2024 workshop chapter "Common Mechanistic Factors of Infection-Associated Chronic Illnesses" documents the following status:

### Hypothesized mechanisms under active investigation

- **Viral persistence or latent reactivation** — SARS-CoV-2 RNA has been detected in tissue reservoirs; EBV reactivation is documented in some long-COVID cohorts.
  Su and colleagues' 2022 study of 309 COVID-19 patients identified four early factors predicting progression to PASC, including SARS-CoV-2 RNAemia and EBV viremia.
  However, the NASEM workshop explicitly notes that "whether EBV infection is associated with developing ME/CFS, or whether the chronic illness is creating an environment where EBV can reactivate" remains unresolved — direction of causation is not established.

- **Autoimmunity and molecular mimicry** — elevated autoantibodies (against ACE2, β2-adrenoceptors, M2 muscarinic receptors, and other targets) are associated with long COVID in multiple cohorts.
  A causal or diagnostic role remains unproven.

- **Autonomic nervous system dysfunction** — POTS-type symptoms and orthostatic intolerance are common findings in long-COVID and ME/CFS cohorts.
  These are established as co-occurring phenomena but not established as the primary root mechanism.

- **Microclots and endotheliopathy** — fibrinaloid microclots in platelet-poor plasma have been reported in long-COVID and ME/CFS cohorts.
  This finding remains contested and is not yet replicated at the scale and rigor required for firm establishment.

- **Microbiome dysbiasis and neuroinflammation** — altered bacterial composition and metabolic dysfunction (particularly dopamine and glutamate metabolism pathways) have been documented.
  Status: mechanistic hypothesis, early-stage research.

### Explicitly unresolved

- Direction of causality between viral reactivation and chronic-illness state
- The mechanism by which different infectious agents produce similar phenotypes
- Whether the conditions share one underlying mechanism, several convergent mechanisms, or are etiologically distinct conditions whose symptom overlap reflects common downstream effects of different insults
- Causal versus correlative roles for any candidate biomarker (autoantibodies, microclots, metabolic markers)

## Clinical implications

For clinical research: the critical consequence is that studies of a single condition cannot be directly compared without explicit statement of case definitions used.
Findings in Fukuda-defined ME/CFS patients (focused on fatigue) are not directly comparable to findings in IOM-defined ME/CFS patients (mandating post-exertional malaise), and neither is directly comparable to findings in PASC cohorts that use yet another case definition.

For trial design: the IACC grouping enables outcome-measure harmonization, allowing future trials to build a comparable evidence base.
It does not settle what should be treated, how, or whether the same interventions work across all six conditions.
