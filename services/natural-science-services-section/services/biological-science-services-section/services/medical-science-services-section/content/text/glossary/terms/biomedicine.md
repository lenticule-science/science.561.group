Biomedicine is medicine grounded in the biological and physical sciences. It explains disease by mechanism: anatomical lesion, disturbed physiology, cellular and molecular pathology, infectious agent. It locates illness in the body of the individual patient. It judges treatments by controlled comparison, with the randomized controlled trial as its strongest evidence.

It took its modern form in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries through pathological anatomy, cell theory, germ theory, and experimental physiology. In the twentieth century it became the medicine of hospitals, universities, and state health systems across the world.

The name is used mainly by those who compare it with other medical systems: anthropologists, historians, and practitioners of other traditions. It names biomedicine as one system among several rather than as "medicine" without qualification. "Western medicine", the common gloss in China and elsewhere, is inexact, since the system is practised and developed worldwide. "Allopathic" is a nineteenth-century homeopathic coinage.

From within, its best-known critique is Engel's argument that a purely biological model cannot account for illness as people live it, and his proposal of a biopsychosocial model [@engel1977]. In this forest it is the frame through which the medical sections read other systems, such as [traditional Chinese medicine](../../../../services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/traditional-chinese-medicine.md), from the outside. Those sections keep the two readings side by side instead of letting either adjudicate the other.
