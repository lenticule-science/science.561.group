A placebo effect is a genuine change in how a person feels or functions, caused by the context of a treatment rather than by its specific ingredient or mechanism. That context includes expectation, the ritual of treatment, the relationship with a clinician and conditioning from past treatment [@kaptchuk2015]. Its mirror image, the nocebo effect, is harm from negative expectation, such as side effects reported on a placebo.

Placebo effects are largest for symptoms a person reports themselves: pain, anxiety, insomnia, fatigue and nausea. They are smallest or absent for objective measures such as tumour size or bacterial load. The conditions treated by [cranial electrotherapy](../../../../services/medical-technology-services-section/services/electrotherapy-devices-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/cranial-electrotherapy-stimulation.md) and by many practices of [Chinese medicine](../../../../services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/traditional-chinese-medicine.md) and [qigong](../../../../services/traditional-chinese-medicine-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/qigong.md) sit in the first group. That does not make their effects unreal; it makes them hard to attribute. It is why trials compare the real treatment against a convincing [sham](sham-stimulation.md) under [blinding](blinding.md).
