A defensive compound synthesized de novo by a plant in response to stress — pathogen attack, UV radiation, or tissue damage — explaining why stressed plants are often more pharmacologically potent than unstressed ones

A phytoalexin is a defensive compound that a plant synthesizes *de novo* in response to stress — pathogen infection, ultraviolet radiation, physical damage, or herbivore attack. Unlike constitutive defenses (compounds always present), phytoalexins are induced: the plant produces them only when threatened. The term was coined by Müller and Börger in 1940 from Greek *phyton* (plant) + *alexein* (to ward off).

Phytoalexins are typically low-molecular-weight antimicrobial compounds that accumulate at the site of infection, creating a localized chemical barrier against the invading pathogen. They belong to diverse chemical classes depending on the plant family: [stilbenoids](../../../../services/pharmacology-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/stilbenoid.md) in Vitaceae (grapes) and [Polygonaceae](../../../../../botany-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/polygonaceae.md) (knotweeds), isoflavonoids in Fabaceae (legumes), sesquiterpenes in Solanaceae (nightshades), indole derivatives in Brassicaceae (mustards).

## Resveratrol as phytoalexin

[Resveratrol](../../../../services/pharmacology-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/resveratrol.md) — the compound that made [Japanese knotweed](../../../../../botany-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/japanese-knotweed.md) pharmacologically famous — is a phytoalexin. The plant produces it in response to fungal infection, UV radiation, and tissue damage. This explains several observations:

- **Why knotweed rhizome is richer in resveratrol than aerial tissue**: The underground rhizome is the plant&rsquo;s survival organ, under constant microbial challenge from soil organisms

- **Why resveratrol concentration peaks in autumn**: The plant is translocating defensive compounds to the rhizome for winter storage

- **Why stressed plants are more pharmacologically potent**: Traditional herbalists have long observed that wild-harvested plants are often more medicinally active than cultivated ones — the wild plant faces more stress and produces more defensive phytoalexins

## Pharmacological implications

The phytoalexin concept has a practical implication for herbalism and pharmacognosy: the very compounds that make a plant medicinally valuable are the compounds the plant produces to defend itself. The plant&rsquo;s evolutionary defense strategy is the source of human therapeutic benefit. This is not coincidence — pathogens that attack plants share biochemical targets with pathogens and inflammatory processes that affect humans. An antimicrobial compound effective against a plant fungus may be effective against a human pathogen through the same mechanism.
