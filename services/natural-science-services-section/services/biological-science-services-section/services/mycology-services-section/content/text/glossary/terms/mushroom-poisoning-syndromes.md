# Mushroom Poisoning Syndromes

Clinical toxicology does not classify mushroom poisonings by the mushroom species involved but by **syndrome** — the pattern of onset timing, organ system affected, and toxic mechanism, because the same syndrome can be produced by multiple unrelated genera and because syndrome, not species, is what drives emergency treatment decisions before a mycological identification (if one is even possible) can be confirmed. The foundational modern classification is J.H. Diaz's 2005 review, "Syndromic diagnosis and management of confirmed mushroom poisonings" (*Critical Care Medicine*, 33(2):427-436, [PubMed 15699849](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15699849/)), which analyzed 28,018 documented US mushroom poisonings from 1951–2002 and organized them into eight syndromes, including four described as newly recognized at the time: accelerated nephrotoxicity, rhabdomyolysis, erythromelalgia, and delayed neurotoxicity, alongside the longer-established syndromes below. A companion paper by the same author covers the global epidemiology and general management principles ([PubMed 15699848](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15699848/)). The PubMed abstract page for the 2005 paper itself loads behind a cookie-consent wall that could not be cleared during this revision, so the 28,018-case figure and the eight-syndrome count are reported here as they are consistently cited across the secondary clinical and toxicology literature, not confirmed against Diaz's own abstract text directly. The six longer-established syndrome classes below (all but the four "newer" ones) independently match the North American Mycological Association's own clinical summary, which was read directly (see Sources).

## Major syndrome classes

**Cyclopeptide (amatoxin) syndrome.** The most lethal class, caused by [amatoxins](./amatoxin.md) — chiefly alpha-amanitin — from *Amanita* section Phalloideae (including [*Amanita phalloides*, the death cap](./destroying-angel-and-death-cap.md)), *Galerina marginata*, some *Lepiota* species, and *Conocybe filaris*. Distinguished from nearly every other syndrome by its long latency (6–36 hours) before any symptom appears, followed by gastrointestinal illness, a deceptive apparent-recovery phase, and then hepatorenal failure. See [Amatoxin](./amatoxin.md) for the full mechanism and clinical course.

**Gyromitrin syndrome.** Caused by gyromitrin, found in *Gyromitra esculenta*, *G. infula*, and *Cudonia circinans* (false morels and relatives), which hydrolyzes in the body to monomethylhydrazine (MMH), a compound also used as a rocket propellant component and classified as a carcinogen. Onset is delayed (2–24 hours), producing headaches and severe abdominal distress, diarrhea, and vomiting, with liver and kidney damage in severe cases; management is supportive, with physician consultation recommended (per the North American Mycological Association's clinical summary, below).

**Orellanine syndrome.** Caused by orellanine, found in *Cortinarius orellanus*, *C. rubellus*, and *C. orellanoides*. Notable for the longest latency of any mushroom syndrome — 36 hours to as long as 3 weeks, averaging around 8 days — before progressive, often irreversible kidney failure (with nausea, vomiting, lethargy, polyuria, and thirst) becomes apparent; there is no specific antidote, only supportive management of the kidney failure. The multi-week latency makes this the hardest syndrome of all to trace back to a specific meal.

**Muscarine syndrome.** Caused by the muscarine alkaloid, found in some *Inocybe*, *Clitocybe rivulosa*, and certain *Boletus* species. Onset is rapid (15–30 minutes) and produces excessive salivation, sweating, and tearing alongside severe gastrointestinal distress, visual disturbance, and irregular pulse and breathing; treated with atropine, a muscarinic antagonist, administered by a physician.

**Ibotenic acid/muscimol syndrome.** Caused by ibotenic acid and its decarboxylation product muscimol, found in *Amanita muscaria* (fly agaric), *A. pantherina*, and related isoxazole-containing *Amanita* species. Onset within 30 minutes to 2 hours, producing CNS effects — confusion, visual distortion, delusions, drowsiness, and in severe cases coma — that are distinct from the cholinergic muscarine syndrome despite arising in the same genus; treatment is supportive only, and atropine (the muscarine antidote) is specifically contraindicated here.

**Coprine syndrome.** Caused by coprine, found in *Coprinopsis atramentaria* (the "tippler's bane" or common inkcap) and *C. insignis*. Coprine itself is not directly toxic — the reaction appears only when the mushroom is consumed together with alcohol, anywhere from minutes to hours after drinking and for as long as 5 days after the mushroom meal if alcohol is then consumed, because a coprine metabolite inhibits aldehyde dehydrogenase and causes acetaldehyde to accumulate exactly as in disulfiram (Antabuse) therapy: racing heart, palpitations, tingling, flushing, and headache. Coprine itself has also been reported, in animal studies, to be mutagenic and gonadotoxic at chronic high-exposure levels — properties distinct from, and not to be confused with, the alcohol-interaction reaction that is the syndrome's defining clinical presentation. (An earlier version of this entry described coprine as carcinogenic; that characterization was not found in the toxicology literature and has been corrected here.)

**Gastrointestinal irritant syndrome.** The most common presentation by case count, caused by a range of unidentified or poorly characterized irritant compounds across many genera (including some *Chlorophyllum*, *Boletus*, and *Omphalotus* species). Onset is rapid (usually under 2 hours), symptoms are self-limited nausea/vomiting/diarrhea, and — critically for triage — the fast onset itself is a favorable prognostic sign, since it rules out the long-latency syndromes (amatoxin, gyromitrin, orellanine) that are the ones capable of killing.

**Newer syndromes (Diaz 2005).** Accelerated nephrotoxicity (renal failure with faster onset than classical orellanine poisoning), rhabdomyolysis (documented especially with some *Tricholoma* species consumed with alcohol), erythromelalgia (a burning-pain syndrome in the extremities associated with some *Clitocybe* species), and delayed neurotoxicity (progressive neurological symptoms appearing after some *Pleurocybella* and related species, most notably documented in patients with pre-existing renal impairment).

## Why syndrome classification matters for identification safety

The syndrome framework is the clinical answer to the same lookalike problem that drives fatal misidentification: because onset latency correlates strongly with lethality (the syndromes capable of killing — amatoxin, gyromitrin, orellanine — are precisely the ones with delayed onset, while fast-onset syndromes are overwhelmingly self-limited), a poison center's first and most useful triage question is not "which species was it" but "how many hours after eating did symptoms start." National US surveillance data illustrates the scale involved: CDC MMWR's "Health Care Utilization and Outcomes Associated with Accidental Poisonous Mushroom Ingestions — United States, 2016–2018" (*MMWR* 70(10), 2021, [PubMed 33705365](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33705365/)) found approximately 1,328 emergency department visits and 100 hospitalizations in 2016 alone for accidental poisonous-mushroom ingestion, roughly 7,500 poisonous-mushroom exposures reported annually to US poison control centers, and — among 556 patients who sought care between 2016 and 2018 — 8.6% (48 patients) suffered a serious adverse outcome (arrhythmia, kidney failure, liver failure, or seizures). The same report documented severe poisonings from foraged mushrooms rising over 1999–2016.
