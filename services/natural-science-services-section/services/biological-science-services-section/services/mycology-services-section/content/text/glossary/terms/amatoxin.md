Amatoxins are a family of bicyclic octapeptide toxins — the most medically significant being **alpha-amanitin** — produced by [*Amanita phalloides*](./destroying-angel-and-death-cap.md) (the death cap) and a small set of other genera, most importantly certain *Amanita* section Phalloideae species, *Galerina marginata*, some *Lepiota* species, and *Conocybe filaris*. They are the single most dangerous class of mushroom toxin: amatoxin-containing species account for the large majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide (Diaz, J.H. (2005), "Syndromic diagnosis and management of confirmed mushroom poisonings," *Critical Care Medicine*, 33(2):427-436, [PubMed 15699849](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15699849/), reviewing 28,018 documented US mushroom poisonings 1951–2002). Their danger is not merely biochemical potency — it is the combination of potency with a long, symptom-free latency that routinely defeats early clinical recognition.

## Mechanism

Alpha-amanitin's toxic action is a single, well-characterized molecular event: it binds non-covalently but with very slow dissociation to **RNA polymerase II**, the enzyme responsible for transcribing messenger RNA in eukaryotic cells. Binding at nanogram-per-milliliter concentrations reduces the enzyme's activity by 60–70%, and because the toxin releases so slowly, functional recovery of an affected cell's transcription machinery is effectively blocked rather than merely slowed. With mRNA synthesis halted, the cell cannot replace proteins as they turn over, and it progresses to apoptosis or necrosis. ("Amanitins: The Most Poisonous Molecules of the Fungal World," *Toxins*/PMC review citing Vetter (2023), [PMC10421264](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10421264/).)

The toxin's organ selectivity follows its transport biology rather than any special vulnerability of liver tissue at the enzyme level: absorbed amatoxins are carried into hepatocytes chiefly by the organic anion-transporting polypeptides **OATP1B3** and the bile-acid transporter **NTCP**, concentrating the toxin in the liver on first pass. A substantial fraction is excreted into bile and then reabsorbed from the gut — an **enterohepatic recirculation** loop that repeatedly re-exposes hepatocytes to toxin already absorbed once, and that several modern treatment protocols (below) are specifically designed to interrupt. Amatoxin also concentrates in the kidney, where measured tissue levels can run 6 to 90 times higher than in the liver, which is why acute kidney injury frequently accompanies acute liver failure in severe cases. ([PMC10421264](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10421264/).)

## The deceptive latency: four clinical phases

What makes amatoxin poisoning distinctively dangerous — more than raw lethality — is that its clinical course is structured to produce false reassurance at exactly the moments when a victim or clinician might otherwise act. The course runs in four phases:

1. **Latency (lag) phase** — typically 6–10 hours after ingestion, occasionally extending to 24–36 hours, during which the patient is entirely asymptomatic while RNA polymerase II inhibition is already under way in the liver. A poisoning that will later prove fatal produces no warning sign in this window.
2. **Gastrointestinal phase** — roughly 1–2 days of intense, repeated vomiting, cramping abdominal pain, and watery (often choleriform) diarrhea severe enough to cause dehydration and hypovolemic shock; amatoxins also impair pancreatic beta-cell function, which can drive hypoglycemia independent of liver damage. This phase is easily and dangerously mistaken for ordinary gastroenteritis or food poisoning — a misdiagnosis documented as a recurring failure mode in the clinical literature.
3. **Apparent convalescence (false recovery)** — GI symptoms subside, and the patient often feels and appears to be recovering, while liver transaminases are already climbing and hepatic injury is progressing beneath the symptomatic surface. This is the phase name that best captures the syndrome's deceptiveness: relief of symptoms coincides with, rather than indicates the end of, organ damage.
4. **Hepatorenal (multiorgan) failure** — typically 3–10 days after ingestion, presenting as jaundice, coagulopathy, hyperbilirubinemia, metabolic acidosis, and progressive hepatic and renal failure; death, when it occurs, is usually reported in the 6–8 day range post-ingestion without effective treatment. (Phase timeline per [PMC10421264](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10421264/); consistent staging is described across the amatoxin clinical literature.)

Because the first symptoms a patient notices (phase 2) arrive hours after the toxin is already acting, and because those symptoms then appear to resolve (phase 3) before the real damage becomes clinically obvious (phase 4), amatoxin poisoning is structurally suited to delay both self-recognition and medical intervention until the treatment window has narrowed.

## Treatment

There is no single specific antidote in routine clinical use, so management is a combination of decontamination, supportive care, and therapies aimed at interrupting hepatic uptake and recirculation of the toxin:

- **Activated charcoal**, given early, for gastrointestinal decontamination.
- **Silibinin** (a milk thistle flavonoid, given as the proprietary IV formulation silibinin-legalon/Legalon SIL), which competes with amatoxin for hepatocyte uptake via OATP1B3 and has membrane-stabilizing and antioxidant effects; it is described in the recent clinical literature as the most effective available clinical antidote. Dosing is cited across clinical sources as a loading dose (commonly 5 mg/kg over one hour) followed by continuous infusion, with published maintenance-dose ranges from about 20 mg/kg/day up to 20–50 mg/kg/day depending on the source, continued until coagulopathy resolves and liver function trends back toward normal — commonly, but not universally, within roughly 48–96 hours of starting treatment; the specific "48–96 hours" framing in this entry should be read as an approximate, commonly-cited window rather than a fixed protocol figure. In a pooled analysis of nearly 1,500 documented cases, overall mortality in silibinin-treated patients was under 10%, versus over 20% for penicillin or penicillin-plus-silibinin. High-dose **penicillin G** and **N-acetylcysteine (NAC)** have also been used, historically and in combination protocols.
- **Sustained hydration, fasting, and octreotide** to suppress meal-driven bile flow and interrupt enterohepatic recirculation of amatoxin trapped in the gallbladder — the mechanism behind a 2026 prospective toxicokinetic-management protocol that reported transplant-free recovery in 98.8% of its protocol-adherent efficacy population (85 of 86 patients recovered without transplant, 1 required transplant, 0 deaths), against 88.0% transplant-free recovery in its broader safety population of 99 patients (87 recoveries, 6 transplants, 6 deaths). Both figures are confirmed against the preprint's own reported results. ([medRxiv 2026 preprint, "Elimination drives recovery in amatoxin-induced acute liver failure"](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.03.05.26345777v1.full) — a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed at time of citation; flagged as such.)
- **Hemodialysis or MARS** (Molecular Adsorbent Recirculating System) extracorporeal liver support, effective mainly when started early.
- **Liver transplantation** for patients who progress to severe coagulopathy, acidosis, or hyperammonemia despite the above; historical mortality without transplant is cited at roughly 10–30%, which is the figure the 2026 elimination-focused protocol above reports substantially improving on in its study population.

Untreated or late-treated amatoxin poisoning remains lethal in a meaningful fraction of cases, and the US/European death-rate gap (roughly 1–2 deaths/year in the US versus ~50/year in Europe and Asia, with some sources citing 50–100/year in Western Europe specifically and up to 5/year in France alone) is attributed, per a clinical-literature summary in UpToDate, to differing foraging prevalence rather than any difference in toxin potency [attributed to UpToDate's "Amatoxin-containing mushroom poisoning" entry via secondary literature and search-indexed excerpts of that entry's own wording; UpToDate is subscription-gated and its full article text was not directly read during this revision, so this remains a secondary-source attribution rather than a primary-source confirmation] — a gap that is itself evidence of how much outcome depends on early, informed recognition rather than on the biochemistry, which is identical wherever the mushroom grows.
