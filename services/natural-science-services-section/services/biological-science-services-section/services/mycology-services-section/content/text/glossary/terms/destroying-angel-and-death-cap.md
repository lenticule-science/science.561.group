# Destroying Angel and Death Cap

**Death cap** (*Amanita phalloides*) and the **destroying angels** — chiefly *Amanita bisporigera* in eastern North America and *Amanita ocreata* in the west — are the [amatoxin](./amatoxin.md)-producing *Amanita* species responsible for the large majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide. They sit at the center of foraging's most consequential identification failure: unlike most poisonous-lookalike problems, where the toxic species causes illness, these three cause a syndrome that kills through a mechanism ([alpha-amanitin's irreversible inhibition of RNA polymerase II](./amatoxin.md)) that is completely indifferent to how confident the forager or the identification tool was.

## Identity and range

*Amanita phalloides* is notable for not being native to North America. It is believed to have been introduced via contaminated nursery soil and is now established on the US west coast, the mid-Atlantic, and the northeast — meaning the single most lethal mushroom species foraged in North America is, ecologically, an invasive species there. *Amanita bisporigera* — commonly called the eastern North American destroying angel, found in mixed coniferous/deciduous forest from eastern North America south to Mexico and rare in the west — and *A. ocreata* — the western North American destroying angel, found chiefly in western North America and identified by name in CDPH's own reporting on the 2025–2026 California outbreak (below) — are, by contrast, native destroying angels: white-capped, white-gilled, white-spored *Amanita* species that share the death cap's amatoxin chemistry and its overall silhouette (a bulbous, volva-bearing base; a ring on the stem; white or pale gills) even though the death cap itself is typically greenish to olive-brown-capped rather than pure white. The eastern/western range split for *A. bisporigera* and *A. ocreata* is confirmed via species-level references (see Sources); the morphological contrast with *A. phalloides* is standard, widely repeated mycological description rather than independently verified here against a single primary taxonomic source. All three belong to *Amanita* section Phalloideae, the taxonomic section within which amatoxin production is concentrated.

## Documented outbreaks and epidemiology

CDC MMWR case-cluster reports document repeated *Amanita*-driven outbreaks in California specifically: "*Amanita phalloides* Mushroom Poisoning — Northern California, January 1997"; a follow-on 2016 report ([PMC5657817](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5657817/)); and the most recent documented outbreak, "Amanita Species Mushroom Poisonings — Northern California, November 2025–March 2026" (*MMWR* vol. 75, [cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7520a2.htm](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7520a2.htm)), which recorded 39 cases, 3 liver transplants, and 4 deaths (82% of patients recovered without transplant). The California Poison Control System's later tally put the same outbreak at 47 cases, 4 deaths, and 4 liver transplants through May 2026, across counties from Humboldt to San Luis Obispo ([CDPH update](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/OPA/Pages/CAHAN/Outbreak-of-Severe-Illness-and-Deaths-Linked-to-Ingestion-of-Poisonous-Wild-Mushrooms-%E2%80%93-May-2026-Update.aspx)). At the national scale, CDC's 2021 MMWR analysis of 2016–2018 data found roughly 7,500 poisonous-mushroom exposures reported annually to US poison centers, with 8.6% of 556 care-seeking patients suffering a serious adverse outcome ([cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/70/wr/mm7010a1.htm](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/70/wr/mm7010a1.htm)). Amatoxin mushrooms broadly are attributed roughly 50 deaths/year in Europe and Asia against only 1–2/year in the US — a gap the clinical literature attributes to foraging prevalence rather than any difference in toxin potency [attributed to UpToDate's "Amatoxin-containing mushroom poisoning" entry via secondary sources and search-indexed excerpts of that entry's wording, cross-consistent with other clinical reviews (e.g. *Journal of Hepatology*); UpToDate's full article is subscription-gated and was not read directly, so this remains a secondary-source attribution — see the fuller treatment and citation at [Amatoxin](./amatoxin.md)].

## Why identification tools still fail on exactly this species

The death cap and destroying angels are not merely dangerous in the abstract — they are the specific, named failure case in the peer-reviewed literature evaluating whether consumer mushroom-identification technology is safe to rely on. A study published in *Clinical Toxicology* (2023; conducted using Victorian Poisons Information Centre and Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria specimens) compared three consumer apps — Picture Mushroom, Mushroom Identificator, and iNaturalist — against real specimens and found Picture Mushroom, the best overall performer, correct 49% of the time across all species and 44% of the time specifically on poisonous species; per-species, Picture Mushroom **misidentified *Amanita phalloides* itself twice**, and iNaturalist misidentified it once (with Mushroom Identificator correctly identifying *A. phalloides* more often than the other two apps, at 67% versus Picture Mushroom's 60% and iNaturalist's 27%, even though Mushroom Identificator's *overall* toxic-species accuracy was the lowest of the three, at 30%). These figures are confirmed via the paper's own abstract and multiple secondary summaries of it, not via a full read of the paywalled full text. A separate, distinct claim from an earlier draft of this entry — that lookalikes of the deadly genera *Amanita* and *Galerina* specifically were misidentified "more than 30% of the time" across the tested apps — could not be located in the study or its summaries and has been removed; the closest confirmed figure is Mushroom Identificator's 30% overall accuracy on toxic species, which is a different measurement (identification accuracy, not a genus-specific misidentification rate) and should not be conflated with it. A 2024 Public Citizen report on AI-based mushroom identification tools separately documented a generative-AI chatbot ("GPT Mycology") failing to correctly identify photographed *Galerina* specimens and offering non-committal or misleading descriptions instead, and documented AI tools repeating the unreliable folk heuristic that bright coloration indicates toxicity — a heuristic the report identifies as false, since numerous edible species (e.g., chicken of the woods, cinnabar chanterelles) are brightly colored and numerous deadly ones, including the death cap and destroying angels, are drab. *Galerina marginata* itself contains amatoxins and is a classic lookalike for edible "little brown mushrooms." ([citizen.org](https://www.citizen.org/article/mushroom-risk-ai-app-misinformation/))

This is the concrete evidence behind a broader structural critique that recurs across the identification-technology literature: mushroom ID apps present results with unwarranted confidence and no calibrated uncertainty, which is judged to be the core safety failure mode — more than raw model accuracy per se — precisely because the species where a wrong answer is fatal (the death cap and destroying angels) are the species these tools have been shown, directly, to get wrong.
