The cortisol awakening response is the characteristic sharp rise in free cortisol that occurs 30-45 minutes after waking from sleep, observable on any morning and distinct from both the basal cortisol level and the subsequent diurnal decline across the day. This peak reflects an endogenous phase of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, probably driven by the transition from sleep to wakefulness and the associated activation of the locus coeruleus, and is the closest thing to a standardized cortisol output of the axis in healthy individuals because it occurs predictably and is less contaminated by the moment-to-moment stressors that make baseline and reactive cortisol measurements so noisy. The cortisol awakening response appears to prepare the organism for the day's metabolic and cognitive demands by mobilizing substrate and raising core alertness.

Measuring the cortisol awakening response is methodologically straightforward but operationally demanding: it requires multiple saliva samples timed at exact intervals after verified waking time, usually at waking, 15-30 minutes post-waking, and 30-45 minutes post-waking, with sampling adherence monitored because the response is steep enough that a ten-minute timing error substantially changes the trajectory. A typical healthy cortisol awakening response shows free cortisol of 5-20 nmol/L at waking, rising to 10-30 nmol/L at the 30-45 minute peak — roughly a 50-100% increase. This makes it distinguishable from stress-reactive cortisol, which is the surge produced by an acute psychological or physical stressor and has a different timescale and trajectory, and from the diurnal slope, which is the gradual decline of cortisol through the day.

A flattened or absent cortisol awakening response is clinically notable because it is observed in a number of stress-related and psychiatric conditions, including burnout, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, and myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome. The finding is significant but non-specific: burnout, major depression, and certain chronic illnesses all show blunted awakening responses, but the same finding also appears in some healthy individuals and in some cases of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis dysregulation where basal and reactive cortisol are entirely normal. A flattened cortisol awakening response can therefore indicate neuroendocrine pathology, but normal values do not rule it out, and an absent or inverted response (cortisol remaining flat or declining after waking) is a red flag for substantial hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal impairment. The consequence for clinical measurement is that cortisol awakening response and baseline cortisol are independent outputs of the axis, and a patient may present with normal baseline cortisol while showing markedly blunted or absent awakening response, or vice versa — both trajectories predict different downstream dysfunction.

For myalgic encephalomyelitis patients in particular, the flattened cortisol awakening response is part of the broader picture of impaired hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis output discussed under the main axis term. It sits alongside delayed cortisol response to ACTH challenge, low baseline free cortisol, and a failed negative-feedback shutdown, leaving hypothalamic drive running unbuffered and contributing to the allostatic cost and autonomic dysregulation that characterize the condition.
