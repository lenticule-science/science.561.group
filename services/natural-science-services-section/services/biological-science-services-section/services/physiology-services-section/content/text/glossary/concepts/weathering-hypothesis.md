The weathering hypothesis, developed by Arline Geronimus beginning in the late 1980s and restated most recently in her 2023 book *Weathering*, holds that health disparities between socially advantaged and disadvantaged populations are not fixed at birth but accumulate: chronic exposure to structural adversity — racial discrimination, economic exploitation, marginalization, the accumulated stress of navigating an unjust society — produces cumulative physiological wear that accelerates biological aging, so that the same chronological age corresponds to a worse physiological age in the more exposed population, and the gap widens as both groups grow older. The hypothesis was developed initially to explain the counterintuitive finding that Black women's birth outcomes worsen, relative to white women's, specifically as maternal age increases, a pattern inconsistent with any account that treats the disparity as static rather than accumulating.

The hypothesis sits naturally alongside allostatic load: weathering describes the population-level, life-course pattern, while allostatic load supplies the proposed physiological currency in which the cumulative cost is paid. Chronic inflammation is one of the more frequently cited candidate indicators, tested directly as a marker of biological weathering in structurally disadvantaged groups.

The health-disparity pattern weathering was built to explain is well replicated and remains one of the most robust findings in the health-disparities literature. Its proposed molecular mechanisms are considerably less settled, and this distinction matters for how confidently the hypothesis should be cited. Discrimination and telomere length — often invoked as the clearest molecular signature of weathering — has produced inconsistent results across studies: a meta-analysis of the Everyday Discrimination Scale against biomarker outcomes found discrimination associated with elevated C-reactive protein but not with cortisol, interleukin-6, or telomere length once models were adjusted, and other systematic reviews describe the discrimination-telomere relationship as a weak main effect that becomes visible mainly as an interaction with depressive symptoms, socioeconomic status, or internalized bias, rather than as a robust, direct association. Epigenetic-clock evidence for discrimination-driven aging is similarly less mature than the confidence with which the mechanism is sometimes cited would suggest.

For the estate's relational and attention-economic framework, weathering is important evidence for the general claim that structurally produced, chronic threat has a real cumulative physiological cost distributed unequally across a population — a claim the pattern-level evidence supports strongly. But theories drawing on weathering should be careful to keep that claim separate from any specific molecular story (telomeres, epigenetic clocks) offered as its mechanism, since the mechanism-level evidence is currently much weaker than the pattern it is meant to explain.

## Related concepts

- Allostatic load — the proposed physiological currency in which weathering's cumulative cost is paid, itself contested at the biomarker level
- Social baseline theory — a proposed protective, buffering counterforce to the chronic threat exposure weathering describes
