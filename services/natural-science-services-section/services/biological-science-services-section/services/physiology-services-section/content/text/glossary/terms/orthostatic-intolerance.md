Orthostatic intolerance is a class of symptoms that arise when a person assumes or attempts to maintain upright posture and are relieved by lying down: dizziness, visual blurring, lightheadedness, palpitations, fatigue, weakness, and in severe cases syncope or near-syncope. These symptoms reflect inadequate cerebral perfusion pressure when gravity redirects blood toward the legs. In a healthy person, standing triggers an immediate sympathetically-mediated reflex: the baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch sense the transient drop in arterial pressure, sympathetic outflow to blood vessels increases, peripheral arteries and veins constrict, venous return to the heart is restored, cardiac output is maintained, and cerebral perfusion is preserved — all within seconds. Orthostatic intolerance arises when this reflex fails: gravity causes venous blood pooling in the dependent tissues, the baroreceptor reflex does not adequately constrict the vasculature, cerebral perfusion drops below the threshold needed for consciousness and attention, and symptoms result.

Critically, orthostatic intolerance is not defined by blood-pressure numbers. A person may have entirely normal blood pressure in the supine position and normal or only mildly abnormal blood pressure changes on standing (orthostatic hypotension proper, defined as a drop of more than 20/10 mmHg systolic/diastolic), yet still experience severe orthostatic intolerance symptoms. Conversely, someone with true orthostatic hypotension — a substantial documented drop in blood pressure on standing — may be asymptomatic if their individual tolerance for hypotension is high. The distinction matters clinically because a normal orthostatic blood-pressure response does not rule out orthostatic intolerance, and conversely, a symptomatic patient with normal stand-up blood pressures is not having their symptoms caused by orthostatic hypotension as a blood-pressure phenomenon. The mechanism in such cases is usually one of vascular dysregulation rather than frank failure: sympathetic output may be present and high, but peripheral vascular resistance fails to increase appropriately, or the sympathetic response is erratic, producing surges and crashes rather than steady compensation.

Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) is one named form of orthostatic intolerance, characterized specifically by excessive heart-rate increase on standing (increase of 30 beats per minute or more, typically in the absence of or out of proportion to blood-pressure change). POTS itself can arise from multiple mechanisms: hyperadrenergic (exaggerated sympathetic response), hypovolemic (insufficient circulating blood volume), or neurogenic (impaired sympathetic function), and each form has different pathophysiology and sometimes different treatments. The existence of POTS as a named subtype within the broader orthostatic-intolerance category illustrates an important point: orthostatic intolerance is not synonymous with POTS or with any single mechanism, but rather a symptom class with diverse underlying causes.

For myalgic encephalomyelitis patients, orthostatic intolerance is often severe and characteristically resistant to standard orthostatic-hypotension treatments (volume expansion, salt loading, compression garments) because the pathophysiology is sympathoadrenal-hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal decoupling rather than simple hypovolemia or sympathetic failure. High sympathetic output without glucocorticoid-driven vascular-receptor upregulation produces erratic and insufficient vasoconstriction; the vascular dysregulation is aggravated by exertion, which drives a sympathetic surge that occurs without adequate metabolic substrate mobilization or cortisol support. The orthostatic intolerance typically worsens during or immediately after exertion and persists or worsens 24-48 hours later as part of the post-exertional malaise picture.

## Related terms

- Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis — whose impairment compounds sympathoadrenal dysregulation and orthostatic intolerance
