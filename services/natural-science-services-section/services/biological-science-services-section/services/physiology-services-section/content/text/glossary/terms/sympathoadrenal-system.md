The sympathoadrenal system is the fast, neural arm of stress response, distinct from the slower, hormonal hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. Its substrate is the sympathetic nervous system: preganglionic parasympathetic neurons synapse on the chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla using acetylcholine, and this synaptic transmission causes the medulla to release epinephrine and norepinephrine directly into the bloodstream within seconds. This is immediate compared to the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, which operates on a timescale of minutes to hours. The sympathoadrenal surge imposes immediate metabolic and cardiovascular demand: increased heart rate, elevated blood pressure, redirection of blood from gut and skin to muscle, mobilization of glucose and fatty acids from storage, enhanced alertness, and readiness for action. The catecholamines also act on vascular alpha-1-adrenergic receptors to maintain arterial tone during the demand surge.

The critical physiological fact about the sympathoadrenal system, and the one most often overlooked, is that it does not operate independently. It depends on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis for maintenance of vascular responsiveness to its output. Glucocorticoids, particularly cortisol, upregulate alpha-1-adrenergic receptors throughout the vasculature, making blood vessels responsive to catecholamine signaling. If cortisol is absent or severely reduced — as in acute adrenal insufficiency or in the profoundly impaired hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis output seen in myalgic encephalomyelitis — the sympathetic surge occurs but the vascular system desensitizes to catecholamines because the receptor-upregulation signal is missing. The result is a paradoxical failure: high sympathetic output with erratic vasoconstriction and venous pooling, leading to orthostatic intolerance, hypoglycemia, and potential hemodynamic collapse, despite high circulating catecholamines. This is the reason the two arms must be measured separately and their coordination assessed independently: one arm can appear to fire normally while the other is uncoupled from it.

Measuring the sympathoadrenal system requires either direct sympathetic nerve recording (uncommon outside research settings), measurement of plasma catecholamines or their metabolites, heart-rate variability analysis reflecting autonomic tone, or challenge testing such as the tilt-table test which provokes sympathetic response and measures the vascular and heart-rate reaction. Each measure captures a different aspect: catecholamine levels report output; heart-rate variability reports autonomic balance; tilt-table response reports whether sympathetic activation produces the expected vascular and heart-rate compensation. In dysautonomia or in the autonomic dysregulation of myalgic encephalomyelitis, these measures can show discordant results — sympathetic output present or even elevated, but vascular tone response blunted or paradoxical — indicating decoupling of the two arms.

For understanding post-exertional malaise and the extreme orthostatic intolerance seen in myalgic encephalomyelitis, the decoupling of sympathoadrenal output from hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal support is the key mechanical story: exertion drives sympathetic activation, which initiates the cascade of metabolic demand and vascular adjustment, but without adequate cortisol-driven substrate mobilization and vascular permissiveness, the sympathetic surge produces hemodynamic strain rather than successful adaptation. Repeated sympathetic activation under these conditions, without successful resolution, appears to drive the collapse into a dorsal vagal shutdown state.

## Related terms

- Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis — the slow, hormonal arm that permits sympathoadrenal vascular effectiveness
- Catecholamine — the hormones released by the sympathoadrenal system
- Dysautonomia — the disordered autonomic regulation that results when the two arms decouple
- Orthostatic intolerance — a symptom of sympathoadrenal-hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal decoupling
- Allostatic load — the cumulative cost of repeated sympathoadrenal surges under metabolic and vascular strain
