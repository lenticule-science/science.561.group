The vagus nerve (cranial nerve X) is one of the longest and most widely distributed cranial nerves. It carries parasympathetic efferent fibers innervating heart, lungs, and abdominal organs, and afferent (sensory) fibers carrying information from these organs to the brainstem. The vagus comprises approximately 100,000 axons, of which roughly 80% are afferent and 20% efferent. This asymmetry is functionally significant: the vagus primarily conveys information from the body's organs to the brain, not motor commands. Afferent vagal fibers sense mechanical events (gut distention, vessel stretch), chemical conditions (inflammatory molecules), and temperature, reaching the nucleus tractus solitarius in the brainstem. Efferent vagal fibers carry parasympathetic output that slows heart rate, promotes digestion, and modulates immune responses. The vagal brake is parasympathetic suppression of sympathetic activity through efferent fibers. The auricular branch innervates the external ear, particularly the concha and tragus, forming the basis for transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS). Stimulation of this branch engages vagal afferent pathways without requiring needle insertion or implant. Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory proposed anatomically and functionally distinct ventral and dorsal vagal branches mediating different functions; however, the polyvagal theory's specific neuroanatomical claims remain contested in comparative neuroanatomy. The vagus nerve's genuine role as the primary afferent conduit for organ state and efferent pathway for parasympathetic regulation is distinct from polyvagal theory's mechanistic claims. Clinical use of vagal stimulation does not depend on polyvagal theory being correct.
