# Stress, Holding, and Provision: An Annotated Physiology Bibliography

This is the full citation record behind
[The Physiology of Threat, Care, and Holding](../notes/2026/08/26/2026-08-26-note-on-the-physiology-of-threat-care-and-holding.md) (2026-08-26).
Every entry was checked via web search in the research session that produced the note, and solidity is stated plainly for each — several entries are flagged as contested or actively non-replicating, and those flags are load-bearing for the note's verdicts, not incidental caveats.

## 1. Allostasis and predictive regulation

**Sterling, P. & Eyer, J. (1988). Allostasis: A new paradigm to explain arousal pathology.** The founding proposal: organisms achieve stability through change, predicting demand and adjusting physiological parameters in advance rather than defending a fixed setpoint. Solid — the field's foundational reframing, still the reference point for every successor account.

**McEwen, B.S. & Stellar, E. (1993). Stress and the individual: Mechanisms leading to disease. *Archives of Internal Medicine*.** Popularized "allostatic load" as the cumulative cost of repeated allostatic adjustment. Solid as a theoretical framework; see the 2021/2023 entries below for the trouble with its biomarker operationalization.

**McEwen, B.S. & Wingfield, J.C. (2003). The concept of allostasis in biology and biomedicine. *Hormones and Behavior*, 43, 2-15.** Extended allostasis across species and life-history stages. Solid, widely cited comparative extension.

**Sterling, P. (2012). Allostasis: A model of predictive regulation. *Physiology & Behavior*.** Sharpens allostasis into an explicitly predictive-control account. Solid.

**Schulkin, J. & Sterling, P. (2019). Allostasis: A brain-centered, predictive mode of physiological regulation.** Consolidates the predictive-regulation lineage. Solid, current standard reference.

**"Allostasis Revisited: A Perception, Variation, and Risk Framework." *Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution*, 2022.** Recent reframing connecting allostasis to perception and risk. Solid as a current update; less citation-tested than the foundational papers given its recency.

**Katsumi, Y., Theriault, J., Quigley, K., & Barrett, L.F. (2022, 2023). Allostasis, interoception, and hierarchical brain gradients.** Connects allostasis to Barrett's Theory of Constructed Emotion via predictive processing. Solid and active research program, but the Theory of Constructed Emotion itself remains debated within affective science — treat as a productive framework, not settled consensus.

## 2. Allostatic load — contested operationalization

**Guidi, J., Lucente, M., Sonino, N., & Fava, G.A. (2021). Allostatic load and its impact on health: A systematic review. *Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics*, 90, 11-27.** Establishes allostatic load's clinical relevance but explicitly flags major inconsistency in biomarker selection and scoring across studies. **Contested/flagged**: this is the paper that documents the construct-validity problem the note relies on — cite it for the problem, not as proof the construct is unified.

**Fava, G.A., Sonino, N., Lucente, M., & Guidi, J. (2023). Allostatic load in clinical practice. *Clinical Psychological Science*.** Follow-up arguing for clinimetric assessment despite the operationalization problems. Solid as a clinical-application proposal; does not resolve the construct-validity issue.

**McCrory, C. et al. (2023). Towards a consensus definition of allostatic load: a multi-cohort, multi-system, multi-biomarker individual participant data meta-analysis. *Psychoneuroendocrinology*.** Large IPD meta-analysis (67,126 people, 13 cohorts, 40 biomarkers) finding a minimal 5-marker index predicts mortality about as well as elaborate composites. **Contested/flagged**: this result cuts both ways — it rescues predictive validity while undermining the idea that "allostatic load" measures one coherent multi-system quantity rather than a handful of metabolic/inflammatory proxies. Treat as the most current and most important caveat on the whole construct.

## 3. Glucocorticoids and prefrontal cortex

**Arnsten, A.F.T. (2009). Stress signalling pathways that impair prefrontal cortex structure and function. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 10, 410-422.** Establishes that mild, uncontrollable acute stress rapidly impairs PFC-dependent working memory via catecholamine/glucocorticoid signaling, and that chronic stress produces PFC dendritic retraction while sparing or strengthening amygdala circuits. Solid — the field's central mechanistic anchor, still the most-cited reference for this pathway; successor work extends but does not overturn it.

## 4. Anticipatory stress and uncertainty

**NPU (No-shock/Predictable/Unpredictable) threat paradigm literature.** Shows startle potentiation and endocrine/autonomic activation scale with uncertainty about threat rather than with realized outcome. Solid, well-replicated experimental paradigm.

**"Computations of uncertainty mediate acute stress responses in humans." *Nature Communications*.** Direct demonstration that subjective uncertainty, not outcome, drives cortisol and physiological stress markers. Solid.

## 5. Weathering hypothesis

**Geronimus, A. (2023). *Weathering: The Extraordinary Stress of Ordinary Life in an Unjust Society*. Little, Brown.** Synthesizes decades of weathering research into a public-facing statement of the hypothesis: chronic structural adversity accelerates biological aging and widens health disparities with age. Solid at the level of the health-disparity pattern it explains (well replicated in mortality and birth-outcome data going back decades).

**Forde, A.T. et al. (2019). The weathering hypothesis as an explanation for racial disparities in health: a systematic review. *Annals of Epidemiology*.** Systematic review of supporting evidence. Solid as a survey of the pattern-level literature.

**Systematic review/meta-analysis of the Everyday Discrimination Scale and biomarker outcomes (2022).** Found discrimination associated with elevated CRP but **not** with cortisol, IL-6, or telomere length in adjusted models. **Contested/flagged**: this is the key evidence that the specific molecular mechanism (telomere shortening) commonly invoked for weathering is not reliably replicated, even though the disparity pattern itself is robust. Other reviews describe the discrimination-telomere link as a weak main effect, visible mainly as an interaction with depression, SES, or internalized bias — do not cite telomere shortening as a settled weathering mechanism.

## 6. Burnout

**World Health Organization (2019). Burn-out an "occupational phenomenon": International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11, QD85).** Establishes the tripartite definition (exhaustion, cynicism, reduced efficacy) and explicitly classifies burnout as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical diagnosis. Solid as a description of the field's working consensus definition — but see the discriminant-validity challenge below.

**Verkuilen, J., Bianchi, R., Schonfeld, I.S., & Laurent, E. (2021). Burnout-depression overlap: exploratory structural equation modeling bifactor analysis and network analysis. *Assessment*.** Finds burnout's tripartite structure lacks discriminant validity from depression. **Contested/flagged**: central to the note's downgrade of C2 — burnout may not be a syndromally unified, distinct clinical entity.

**Bianchi, R., Verkuilen, J., Schonfeld, I.S., Hakanen, J.J., Jansson-Fröjmark, M., Manzano-García, G., Laurent, E., & Meier, L.L. (2021). Is burnout a depressive condition? A 14-sample meta-analytic and bifactor analytic study. *Clinical Psychological Science*.** Large multi-sample study finding exhaustion (burnout's core) correlates more strongly with depression than with the other two burnout dimensions. **Contested/flagged**: same lineage as above, strongest single piece of evidence against burnout as a distinct entity separable from mood disorder.

**Bianchi, R., Schonfeld, I.S., Wac, K., & Sowden, W.J. (2022). Historical/conceptual update on burnout-depression overlap.** Extends the critique historically. Solid as a synthesis of the Bianchi lineage's position; note this is one research group's sustained (if well-evidenced) critical program, not universal consensus — Maslach and WHO continue to treat burnout as a useful occupational construct even while acknowledging the overlap.

**HPA axis and autonomic nervous system in burnout, 2021 review, *Psychoneuroendocrinology*.** Finds no compelling evidence of consistent HPA or ANS dysfunction specific to burnout, with at best partial support for a staged hyperactivity-then-hyporeactivity pattern in severe cases. **Contested/flagged**: reported honestly in the note as the absence of a reliable physiological signature, which is itself an important finding, not a gap in the search.

**Regensburg Burnout Project: hair cortisol and work-related burnout (2023). *Psychoneuroendocrinology*.** Cross-sectional and longitudinal hair-cortisol study finding inconsistent associations. **Contested/flagged**: consistent with the broader pattern of null/mixed HPA findings in burnout research, likely reflecting sample heterogeneity given the absence of a stable case definition.

## 7. Caregiving

**Schulz, R. & Beach, S.R. (1999). Caregiving as a risk factor for mortality: the Caregiver Health Effects Study. *JAMA*, 282, 2215-2219.** The landmark finding that *strained* spousal caregivers had elevated mortality risk. **Contested/flagged**: widely over-generalized in later citation to "caregiving causes mortality," when the finding was specific to the strained subgroup (44% of the original sample reported no strain at all) — see the Roth et al. reversal below.

**Roth, D.L., Fredman, L., & Haley, W.E. (2018 and lineage). Reduced mortality rates among caregivers: does family caregiving provide a stress-buffering effect? *The Gerontologist*.** Surveys at least five to seven larger, more diverse population-based studies finding caregivers have *lower* average mortality than non-caregiving controls. **Contested/flagged, directly reversing Schulz & Beach's popular reading**: this is the paper the note relies on to show that "care predictably terminates in exhaustion" is not a general law — the theory needs to explain why the direction reverses once sample size and diversity increase, and the honest answer is selection effects and unmeasured heterogeneity in caregiving conditions (support, reciprocity, meaning), not a refutation of caregiving cost under adverse conditions specifically.

**Family caregiving and inflammation/immune function, meta-analysis, 2019. *The Gerontologist*.** Finds elevated inflammatory markers in caregivers on average. Solid at the level of documenting real biomarker cost under caregiving, but coexists with the Roth et al. mortality reversal — the note treats this as evidence that cost is real but conditional on burden and support, not evidence against Roth et al.

**The transition to family caregiving and its effect on biomarkers of inflammation (2020). *PNAS*.** Longitudinal evidence that beginning caregiving produces measurable inflammatory changes. Solid, well-designed longitudinal study.

**Helgeson, V.S. & Fritz, H.L. (foundational and ongoing). Unmitigated communion.** Defines excessive other-focus to the exclusion of self, providing support without reciprocity. Solid, well-validated psychological construct with a substantial replication record.

**Helgeson, V.S., Escobar, O., Siminerio, L., & Becker, D. (2007). Unmitigated communion and health among adolescents with and without diabetes. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*.** Empirical demonstration linking unmitigated communion to worse metabolic control in a clinical population. Solid — the note recommends this construct as the better-validated replacement for "burnout" in the theory's care claim.

## 8. Social buffering

**Coan, J.A., Schaefer, H.S., & Davidson, R.J. (2006). Lending a hand: social regulation of the neural response to threat. *Psychological Science*.** The original hand-holding fMRI study: spousal and stranger hand-holding attenuate neural threat responses under shock threat, moderated by relationship quality. Solid — a landmark, frequently cited finding.

**Coan, J.A. et al. Relationship status and perceived support in the social regulation of neural responses to threat. *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience*.** Replication in a larger, more representative community sample, with hand-holding-related hypothalamic reduction predicting better self-reported general health. Solid — this is a genuine replication, not merely a related study, and is the basis for treating the original finding as reliable.

**Coan, J.A. & Sbarra, D.A. Social baseline theory.** Theoretical framework: social co-regulation lowers the metabolic cost of managing uncertainty. Solid as an integrative framework built on the replicated hand-holding findings above.

## 9. Oxytocin — replication crisis

**Registered report: absence of a meaningful effect of intranasal oxytocin on trusting behavior, pooled equivalence testing (~2024-2026).** A large, pre-registered study finding no meaningful effect where earlier underpowered studies had claimed one. **Contested/flagged — load-bearing, not a footnote**: this is direct evidence of the field's replication crisis, and the note uses it to recommend dropping oxytocin as the proposed biochemical mechanism for "holding."

**Meta-analyses of intranasal oxytocin and autism/social cognition (2024).** Conclude the evidence is insufficient to confidently support or reject oxytocin as an effective mechanism in this domain. **Contested/flagged**: consistent pattern across the oxytocin literature — early promising single studies, systematically weaker or null results at scale.

## 10. Attachment and play

**Winnicott, D.W. (1958). The capacity to be alone. *International Journal of Psycho-Analysis*.** Clinical-theoretical account: the capacity for chosen solitude develops inside reliable holding by a caregiver. Solid as an influential clinical theory; not an experimentally isolated causal test, but consistent with the secure-base literature below.

**Ainsworth, M.D.S. et al. Strange Situation and secure-base research lineage.** Foundational and extensively replicated finding that securely attached children explore novel environments more confidently. Solid — one of developmental psychology's best-replicated findings.

**Burghardt, G.M. (2005). *The Genesis of Animal Play: Testing the Limits*.** Establishes the five criteria of play, including initiation only in a "relaxed field" free of acute stress, and the surplus-resource theory of play's origin. Solid, foundational, still the reference definition in the field.

**Pellis, S.M. et al. (2020-2024). Social play deprivation and medial prefrontal cortex development. *Journal of Neuroscience* (2022); *Behavioural Brain Research* (2022); *European Journal of Neuroscience* (2024).** Causal rat studies showing juvenile play deprivation alters mPFC dendritic architecture and GABAergic inhibitory synapse development, producing adult deficits in impulse control and cognitive flexibility. Solid — current, active, mechanistic research program; the note treats this as the strongest single evidentiary pillar in the whole audit.

## 11. Natural experiments in structural security

**Haushofer, J. & Shapiro, J. (2016/2018). The short-term and long-term impact of unconditional cash transfers to the poor: experimental evidence from Kenya. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*.** RCT finding large wellbeing and consumption gains but **no overall cortisol effect**. **Contested/flagged**: the note's key null result — reported honestly rather than omitted, with the authors' own noted confound (measurement timing relative to spending down lump-sum transfers) as an open question for replication.

**McGuire, J., Kaiser, C., & Bach-Mortensen, A.M. (2022). A systematic review and meta-analysis of the impact of cash transfers on subjective well-being and mental health in low- and middle-income countries. *Nature Human Behaviour*, 6, 359-370.** 45 studies, ~117,000 people; small but significant positive effects on wellbeing (d=0.13) and mental health (d=0.07). Solid — large, well-conducted meta-analysis, effect sizes small but real and reported as such.

**Baicker, K., Finkelstein, A. et al. (2013). The Oregon Experiment — effects of Medicaid on clinical outcomes. *NEJM*.** RCT via lottery access to Medicaid; large reductions in undiagnosed and untreated depression. Solid — one of the cleanest randomized natural experiments in this literature.

**Effect of Medicaid on management of depression: evidence from the Oregon Health Insurance Experiment (2018). *Milbank Quarterly*.** Follow-up detailing the depression-specific findings. Solid.

**Ludwig, J. et al. / Opportunity Insights. Long-term Moving to Opportunity analyses.** 10-15 year follow-up finding improved adult mental/physical health and reduced psychological distress in the low-poverty voucher group, with more mixed effects on some youth economic outcomes. Solid, though heterogeneous by outcome and age-at-move — report the mixed pattern honestly rather than only the mental-health headline.

**Kela / Finnish Government (2020). The Basic Income Experiment 2017-2018 in Finland: preliminary results.** RCT-style unconditional payment trial; no significant employment effect, significant gains in life satisfaction and reductions in mental strain, depression, and loneliness. Solid — official government-published trial results.

**Stockton Economic Empowerment Demonstration (SEED) reports (2021). University of Pennsylvania School of Social Policy & Practice.** Small guaranteed-income pilot (125 recipients); reduced anxiety and depression, increased full-time employment. Solid as a pilot study, though small sample size relative to the RCTs above should temper confidence in precise effect sizes.
