Dysautonomia is a class of disorders of autonomic regulation, characterized by failure of the autonomic nervous system to appropriately control heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, sweating, or digestion. The term encompasses a broad spectrum of conditions and should be understood as a category of dysfunction rather than a single disease — dysautonomia includes everything from complete autonomic failure (in which the autonomic system produces little or no output) to purely regulatory failures (in which autonomic output is present or even elevated but mistimed, miscalibrated, or uncoupled from what it should be coordinated with). This distinction between output failure and coordination failure is critical because the measurement, mechanism, and prognosis differ substantially.

Autonomic failure proper — severe, generalized insufficiency of parasympathetic and sympathetic output — is rare and typically indicates serious structural damage to the autonomic ganglia or their axons (as in Pandysautonomia or familial dysautonomia). By contrast, autonomic dysregulation — the far more common presentation in modern clinical populations — often features entirely normal or even elevated output from one or both arms of the autonomic system, but with dyscoordination: sympathetic surges that fail to produce expected vascular compensation, parasympathetic withdrawal that lags when it should lead, heart-rate responses that exceed what the blood-pressure change would predict, or temperature-regulation set-point disturbances despite normal sweating capacity. The pathophysiology can be central (medullary or hypothalamic), peripheral (receptors or nerves), or functional (dyscoordination without structural lesion).

The measurement consequence is substantial. Heart-rate variability — which quantifies the beat-to-beat variation in interval and is often used as a proxy for autonomic balance — can be suppressed in either autonomic failure or dysregulation but for opposite reasons: autonomic failure shows low variability because there is little autonomic tone to vary; dysregulation can show low variability despite high sympathetic output because the variability is suppressed by rigidity or dyscoordination rather than by absence. Tilt-table testing, which provokes sympathetic activation by gravitational blood pooling, yields normal responses in some dysautonomic patients (sympathetic output fires correctly) but abnormal responses in others (output present but vascular tone response impaired). Catecholamine levels are often elevated in dysautonomia yet vascular responsiveness is low, a paradox resolved only by recognizing that receptor sensitivity, not absolute hormone level, is what governs the effect.

Most dysautonomia, including the form that presents prominently in myalgic encephalomyelitis, falls on the dysregulation side of the spectrum rather than the failure side. The sympathetic and parasympathetic arms are present and often firing strongly, but their coordination with each other and with the cardiovascular, metabolic, and respiratory systems is broken. This is why dysautonomia in myalgic encephalomyelitis presents with tachycardia and palpitations despite low blood pressure, why exertion provokes sympathetic activation that fails to stabilize the vascular system, and why the autonomic dysregulation worsens dramatically when the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis is also impaired — the two failures compound each other.

## Related terms

- Orthostatic intolerance — a symptom produced by dysregulation of the sympathoadrenal and parasympathetic arms
- Sympathoadrenal system — one arm whose dysregulation produces dysautonomia
- Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis — whose impairment can compound autonomic dysregulation
- Allostatic load — the cumulative cost of running dysregulated autonomic responses
