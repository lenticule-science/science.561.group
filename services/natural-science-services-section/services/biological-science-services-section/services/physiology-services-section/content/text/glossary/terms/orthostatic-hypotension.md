Orthostatic hypotension is a measurable drop in blood pressure that occurs upon standing or prolonged head-up tilt, conventionally defined as a reduction of at least 20 millimeters of mercury in systolic pressure or 10 millimeters of mercury in diastolic pressure within three minutes of moving from a supine position to upright. It is a blood-pressure phenomenon, not a symptom phenomenon, and this distinction is essential for research and clinical interpretation: a person can have orthostatic hypotension without experiencing orthostatic intolerance symptoms (high individual tolerance or adequate cerebral autoregulation despite the pressure drop), or conversely, can have orthostatic intolerance symptoms without meeting blood-pressure criteria for orthostatic hypotension.

Orthostatic hypotension arises through several distinct mechanisms, often coexisting. Hypovolemia — inadequate circulating blood volume from dehydration, bleeding, or chronic illness — reduces venous return and therefore cardiac output when gravity redistributes blood into dependent tissues, producing a pressure drop. Autonomic failure or dysfunction, whether central (medullary lesions), spinal cord injury affecting sympathetic outflow, or peripheral (postganglionic sympathetic axonal damage), impairs the sympathetic reflex that normally constricts blood vessels and restores pressure within seconds of standing. Medication-induced orthostatic hypotension occurs with any agent that impairs sympathetic tone: antihypertensives (beta-blockers, alpha-1 antagonists, ACE inhibitors), tricyclic antidepressants, SSRIs, dopamine agonists, and diuretics. Prolonged recumbency from bed rest or hospitalization causes deconditioning, reducing plasma volume and cardiovascular reflex responsiveness, producing a reversible form upon remobilization. These mechanisms are mechanistically distinct and have distinct time courses: medication effect is immediate upon dose, volume depletion develops over days or weeks, autonomic failure is chronic or acute depending on cause, and deconditioning develops over days to weeks with full recovery possible within weeks of activity restoration.

Two clinical presentations of orthostatic hypotension are clinically and mechanistically important to distinguish: *initial orthostatic hypotension* is a brief, transient pressure dip within the first few seconds of standing, reflecting the instantaneous gravity effect on the blood column before reflex compensation — this is a normal finding in healthy people and does not meet the three-minute definition. *Delayed orthostatic hypotension* develops over 30 seconds to three minutes or occurs later after standing, reflecting a progressive loss of compensation — this indicates true autonomic or volume-related failure and merits investigation.

Critically, orthostatic hypotension is frequently conflated with postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), a distinct condition. POTS is defined by a heart-rate increase of 30 beats per minute or more upon standing (or 40 bpm in those aged 12–19) in the *absence of or out of proportion to* orthostatic hypotension — that is, the blood-pressure drop required for the orthostatic-hypotension diagnosis is often *absent* in POTS. The two conditions can coexist but are mechanistically separable: POTS involves dysregulated heart-rate increase (sometimes excessive sympathetic activation, sometimes hypovolemia, sometimes impaired parasympathetic withdrawal), whereas orthostatic hypotension involves vascular dysregulation (inadequate sympathetic vasoconstriction, volume loss, or central failure). A person can have a normal blood-pressure response to standing with an excessive heart-rate increase (POTS without orthostatic hypotension), a blood-pressure drop with an adequate heart-rate increase (orthostatic hypotension from volume loss, with intact sympathetic tone), or both. Measurement protocol is the tool that separates them: supine and upright blood pressure at specific time points (1 minute, 3 minutes) with simultaneous heart-rate recording allows distinction. Studies claiming to assess either condition without specifying timing and method are reporting unknown physiology.

## Related terms

- Orthostatic intolerance — the symptom class (dizziness, visual blurring, syncope) that may result from orthostatic hypotension but has broader causes
- Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome — excessive heart-rate increase upon standing, often without blood-pressure drop
- Autonomic failure — loss of sympathetic or parasympathetic output, a major cause of orthostatic hypotension
- Hypovolemia — blood volume deficit, a mechanical cause of orthostatic hypotension
- Cerebral autoregulation — the system that may maintain adequate brain perfusion despite blood-pressure changes, determining whether hypotension produces symptoms
- Dysautonomia — disordered autonomic regulation, including dysregulated orthostatic responses
