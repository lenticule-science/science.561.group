Unmitigated communion, a construct developed by Vicki Helgeson and Heidi Fritz, names a specific failure mode of other-directedness: an excessive focus on and involvement with others carried to the point of self-neglect, in which support flows outward without being received in return. It is distinguished from ordinary communion — the healthy capacity for connection and mutual concern, which correlates with good relationships and good health — precisely by its imbalance. Where communion is reciprocal, unmitigated communion is a standing outflow: the person organizes their attention and effort around others' needs while their own needs go unattended, often as a consequence of gender-role socialization that prizes relational harmony over self-regard.

The health consequences are well documented and reasonably consistent across studies. Helgeson, Oscar Escobar, Linda Siminerio, and Dorothy Becker's 2007 study of adolescents with diabetes found that unmitigated communion predicted greater psychological distress and lower competence over time, and specifically predicted deterioration in metabolic control over the following year — a case in which an interpersonal disposition measurably tracked a hard physiological outcome, not merely self-reported mood. Daily-diary studies have extended the pattern to everyday interpersonal behavior, and the construct has also been linked to disturbed eating and to being overly intrusive or nurturant in relationships, at cost to the person carrying it.

Unmitigated communion is best understood as the individual-disposition counterpart of an argument made at the institutional level in the social-reproduction critique of care: that care work which is systematically unaccounted for — given without being metered, valued, or reciprocated — predictably exhausts the person supplying it, and that this exhaustion is a structural consequence of the arrangement rather than a personal failure of resilience on the carer's part. Unmitigated communion gives that argument an operationalized, individually measurable form with a real replication record, in a way that the looser and more contested clinical construct of occupational burnout currently does not.

For the estate's relational and attention-economic framework, unmitigated communion is the recommended replacement for "burnout" wherever a theory wants to claim that unpriced, one-directional care predictably damages the carer. Burnout, examined closely, correlates so strongly with depression that its status as a distinct clinical entity is disputed, and the caregiving-mortality literature more broadly finds that caregivers as a population tend to outlive non-caregivers, complicating any claim that care-as-such is terminal. Unmitigated communion sidesteps both problems: it is defined relationally, as an accounting failure — support given without being received — rather than as a generic exhaustion syndrome, and it predicts poor outcomes specifically under that imbalance, leaving room for the same underlying fact the theory needs: that reciprocated, supported, or chosen care does not show the same cost.

## Related concepts

- Social baseline theory — describes the mutually co-regulating social contact whose absence unmitigated communion's asymmetry forecloses
- Allostatic load — the general physiological wear that chronic, unreciprocated caregiving strain is hypothesized to accumulate
