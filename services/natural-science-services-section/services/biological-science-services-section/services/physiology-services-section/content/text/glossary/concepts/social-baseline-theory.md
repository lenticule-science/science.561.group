Social baseline theory, developed by James Coan, holds that proximity to familiar, trusted others is not an optional comfort layered on top of an otherwise self-sufficient nervous system but the default condition against which human physiological regulation is calibrated. On this account, being unaccompanied is the metabolically expensive state — the individual nervous system must model and respond to threat and uncertainty on its own resources — while the presence of an attuned social partner allows some of that regulatory work to be distributed, lowering the energetic and neural cost of managing risk. Social contact, in this framework, functions less like a mood enhancer and more like a baseline utility the organism is built to expect.

The theory's empirical anchor is Coan's hand-holding research. In the original 2006 study with Hannah Schaefer and Richard Davidson, married women underwent fMRI while under threat of mild electric shock, either alone, holding a stranger's hand, or holding their husband's hand. Spousal hand-holding produced pervasive attenuation of activity in neural threat-response systems, with stranger hand-holding producing a smaller effect, and the degree of attenuation during spousal contact tracked independently rated marital quality. The finding has since replicated in a larger, more representative community sample, with hand-holding-related reductions in hypothalamic activation predicting better self-reported general health — a genuine replication, not merely an adjacent study, which is what elevates social baseline theory above a single striking result.

The theory sits deliberately apart from oxytocin-centered accounts of social bonding. Where the popular account of social buffering often reaches for a single "bonding hormone" as the mechanism, Coan's evidence points instead to blunted activity in neural threat-processing circuits and reduced downstream HPA-axis and autonomic engagement — a systems-level regulatory effect rather than a single-molecule one. This distinction has become more important as the intranasal oxytocin literature has run into a serious replication crisis, with large pre-registered studies failing to reproduce earlier claimed prosocial effects; social baseline theory's hand-holding findings, by contrast, have held up under replication.

For the estate's relational and attention-economic framework, social baseline theory supplies the clearest physiological demonstration available that being held — the mere reliable presence of a trusted other — changes measurable neural and physiological processing of threat without any change in the threat itself. This is close to a literal confirmation, at the level of a single dyad, of the broader claim that standing and relationship can alter an organism's affective economy independent of any campaign to change how it feels: the hand in one's own hand is doing regulatory work the isolated nervous system would otherwise have to fund alone.

## Related concepts

- Allostasis — the predictive regulatory process whose metabolic cost social co-regulation is theorized to reduce
- Unmitigated communion — the failure mode of social support that is asymmetric rather than mutually co-regulating
- Weathering hypothesis — a population-level account of chronic threat's cumulative cost, for which social buffering is one proposed protective factor
