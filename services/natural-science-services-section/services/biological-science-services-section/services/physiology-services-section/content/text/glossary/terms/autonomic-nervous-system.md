The autonomic nervous system is a division of the vertebrate peripheral nervous system responsible for the involuntary control of visceral organs: the heart, blood vessels, lungs, digestive tract, sweat glands, and urinary system. The name 'autonomic' refers to involuntary operation — that is, operation not under conscious volitional control — rather than independence from the central nervous system; the autonomic system receives continuous brain control via the brainstem and hypothalamus. The autonomic nervous system is organized into three partially independent divisions: the sympathetic, parasympathetic, and enteric divisions.

The sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions follow a shared two-neuron organizational principle: a preganglionic neuron originates from the brain or spinal cord and synapses on a postganglionic neuron in a ganglion located either alongside the spinal cord (sympathetic paravertebral ganglia) or distant from it (sympathetic collateral ganglia) or located directly in or on the wall of the target organ (parasympathetic ganglia). The distinction from the somatic motor system is sharp: the somatic motor system is a single-neuron arrangement, with one motor neuron reaching from the spinal cord directly to skeletal muscle, and is entirely voluntary in operation and rapid (millisecond latencies). Autonomic output has inherent delays from synaptic transmission and diffusion of neurotransmitter. The enteric division — the nervous system of the digestive tract — operates semi-autonomously, receiving sympathetic and parasympathetic input but capable of independent reflex operation.

The sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions typically produce opposing effects on the same organs: sympathetic activation raises heart rate and blood pressure, constricts blood vessels to skin and digestive organs, dilates the pupils, and slows digestion, supporting a 'fight or flight' mobilization. Parasympathetic activation lowers heart rate, dilates blood vessels to the digestive system, promotes digestion, and supports 'rest and digest' functions. However, this opposition is not absolute or universal — both divisions innervate sweat glands exclusively, and at some sites, the divisions coordinate rather than oppose. The critical physiological fact is that most organs receive both divisions with tonic (ongoing) input, so autonomic state is fundamentally a *balance* between the two rather than a level or intensity of one. Measuring only sympathetic tone without assessing parasympathetic tone simultaneously provides incomplete information about autonomic function. Similarly, a single measure like heart rate or blood pressure reflects only the net outcome of sympathetic and parasympathetic drive and cannot reveal the underlying division-by-division state; high heart rate could result from excessive sympathetic activation, insufficient parasympathetic tone, or both. This is why dysautonomia presentations can be confusing: high sympathetic catecholamine output without adequate parasympathetic withdrawal can produce identical symptoms to low parasympathetic output with normal sympathetic tone, yet the two require different interventions.

## Related terms

- Sympathetic nervous system — the arousal-promoting division of the autonomic system
- Parasympathetic nervous system — the recovery-supporting division of the autonomic system
- Enteric nervous system — the semi-autonomous nervous system of the digestive tract
- Somatic motor system — the voluntary motor system controlling skeletal muscle; distinguished by single-neuron organization, voluntary operation, and speed
- Dysautonomia — disordered regulation of the autonomic nervous system
- Sympathoadrenal system — the sympathetic and adrenal medullary output under stress
