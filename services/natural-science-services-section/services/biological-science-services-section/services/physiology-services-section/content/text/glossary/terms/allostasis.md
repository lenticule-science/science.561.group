Allostasis is the process by which an organism achieves physiological stability through change rather than through the defense of a fixed internal setpoint. The term was introduced by Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer in 1988 as a deliberate departure from homeostasis: where the classical model holds that a controller detects deviation from a target and corrects it after the fact, allostasis holds that the brain predicts upcoming demand and adjusts blood pressure, heart rate, glucose availability, and hormonal output in advance of need. Bruce McEwen and Eliot Stellar extended the concept in 1993, and McEwen and John Wingfield generalized it across species and life-history stages in 2003, establishing allostasis as the dominant vocabulary in contemporary stress physiology for describing anticipatory rather than reactive regulation.

The predictive character of allostasis has since been formalized. Sterling's later work (2012), and Jay Schulkin and Sterling's joint statement (2019), place allostasis within the broader family of predictive-regulation theories: the organism does not wait to be pushed out of range before responding, it maintains a running model of what its tissues will require and overrides local feedback loops to meet that anticipated demand. This reframing has recently been drawn into predictive-processing accounts of affect itself — work connecting allostasis to interoception and to Lisa Feldman Barrett's Theory of Constructed Emotion treats affective experience as, in part, the felt signature of the brain's ongoing allostatic prediction and prediction-error correction, rather than a separate system layered on top of physiological regulation.

Allostasis is not free. Sustained or repeated predictive adjustment — the system running "hot" in anticipation of demand that a chronically uncertain or threatening environment keeps signaling — produces cumulative wear, a cost McEwen named allostatic load (see the companion term). This is the crucial move for anyone studying anticipatory stress: because allostasis is prediction, not reaction, its cost is incurred by the act of modeling a threat, independent of whether the threat materializes. An organism that must continuously simulate a bad outcome pays a real physiological price for the simulation itself.

For the estate's relational and attention-economic framework, allostasis is the physiological literalization of a claim the critical-theory notes make on independent grounds: that chronic, unresolved threat is a standing cost on cognition and body regardless of any realized harm. Allostasis gives that claim a mechanism rather than a metaphor — the organism is not damaged by the blow that never lands, it is taxed by the prediction it was compelled to keep running. See also allostatic load, for the load-bearing caveat that measuring this cost as a single unified biomarker composite is currently a contested exercise, not a settled one.

## Related terms

- Allostatic load — the cumulative cost of repeated allostatic adjustment, and the current dispute over how to measure it
- Homeostasis — the classical setpoint-defense model allostasis was proposed to supersede
- Glucocorticoid — the hormone family whose signaling carries much of allostasis's cost to prefrontal cortex function
