## 0. The distinction: incoherence, not hyperarousal

Myalgic encephalomyelitis and post-exertional malaise are commonly described in the literature as manifestations of dysregulated stress response — too much activation, too much sympathetic drive, a hyperaroused nervous system failing to return to baseline. This framing treats the condition as a matter of *degree*: the stress system is running too high, and the job of treatment is to bring it down. The mechanical story offered in this note is different. The problem is not excessive output from a single system, but *structural incoherence* between two systems that must coordinate to produce successful physiological adaptation. The pathology is not "too much stress response" but a response whose two arms are decoupled: full sympathoadrenal activation occurring without the metabolic substrate mobilization, vascular tone support, and inflammatory brake that only the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis can provide. This is a category error in the wrong direction — the two arms are misfiring together, not one arm firing too hard.

## 1. Healthy adaptation: coordinated two-arm response

In a healthy person, the two arms of the stress response act as one integrated system. A detected demand — physical exertion, psychological threat, or anticipatory modeling of a future demand — activates both the sympathoadrenal system (the fast, neural arm) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (the slow, hormonal arm) in parallel. The sympathetic surge is immediate: preganglionic sympathetic fibers reach the adrenal medulla within milliseconds and trigger a burst of epinephrine and norepinephrine into the bloodstream. This surge imposes three immediate metabolic and cardiovascular demands. First, it increases heart rate and cardiac output, demanding that the cardiovascular system handle increased flow. Second, it activates cellular metabolism — glucose and fatty acids must be mobilized from storage to fuel the muscles and elevated cognition required for fight or flight. Third, it imposes vascular strain: as peripheral blood vessels constrict to redirect blood to muscles and brain, arteries must maintain tone against that demand, a response that requires maintained vascular responsiveness to catecholamine signaling.

The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis provides the substrate and permission for all three demands. Over the course of 15-30 minutes, the cascade releases cortisol into the circulation, and cortisol does three load-bearing things in the context of a sympathetic surge. First, it mobilizes glucose from hepatic and muscle glycogen stores and suppresses peripheral glucose utilization, so substrate is available to fuel the sympathetic demand. Second, and crucially, cortisol upregulates alpha-1-adrenergic receptors on vascular smooth muscle, so blood vessels remain responsive to the sympathetic surge — without this upregulation, even high circulating catecholamines fail to maintain vascular tone. Third, cortisol acts as the anti-inflammatory brake: when the sympathetic surge and the inflammatory cascade it triggers begin to resolve, cortisol suppresses further cytokine release, microglia activation, and oxidative stress, allowing the inflammatory episode to terminate cleanly rather than persist for days.

In this coordinated state, a bout of exertion or a resolved threat episode terminates successfully. The sympathetic activation reaches its apex, achieves the goal (escape, action, resolution), and then winds down over minutes to an hour. The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis remains elevated during this window, funding the metabolic cost and stabilizing the vascular changes. As the demand resolves, negative feedback from elevated cortisol shuts off further hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal output, the sympathetic drive withdraws, and both arms return to baseline within a few hours. The person recovers, the cost is paid and cleared, and the system is ready for the next demand.

## 2. The decoupling: sympathoadrenal output without hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal support

In myalgic encephalomyelitis, this coordination is broken. The pathophysiology involves multiple substrates — blunted central hypothalamic drive to the pituitary, attenuated pituitary and adrenal responsiveness to stimulation, impaired negative-feedback shutdown, and metabolic dysfunction at the mitochondrial level — but the effect is coherent: the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis fails to deliver adequate cortisol output relative to the sympathetic demand the system is imposing.

The result is a structurally incoherent stress response. Exertion or threat triggers a sympathoadrenal surge: the locus coeruleus fires, sympathetic preganglionic fibers dump acetylcholine into the adrenal medulla, catecholamines flood the bloodstream, heart rate rises, palpitations occur, and vascular tone attempts to adjust. But the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal output is inadequate to fund this demand. Cortisol levels may be low in absolute terms, or delayed in reaching peak, or both. The consequence is that three load-bearing mechanisms fail simultaneously. First, substrate mobilization is blunted: glucose mobilization is inadequate relative to the metabolic demand the sympathetic surge is imposing, and cells that have already suffered mitochondrial impairment exhaust their limited aerobic capacity within minutes and flip to anaerobic glycolysis, accumulating lactate and protons. Second, vascular responsiveness collapses: without adequate cortisol-driven upregulation of alpha-1-adrenergic receptors, even elevated circulating catecholamines fail to maintain arterial tone, producing erratic vasoconstriction and venous pooling that choke cerebral perfusion. Third, the inflammatory brake is missing: without glucocorticoid signaling to suppress the inflammatory cascade, microglia, mast cells, and circulating immune cells remain primed and activated, and any sympathetic surge triggers their degranulation, releasing IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and reactive oxygen species that persist for days rather than being counter-regulated into termination.

## 3. The clinical picture this mechanism predicts

This specific failure shape — massive sympathetic output with no substrate, no vascular support, and no anti-inflammatory brake — predicts exactly the observed clinical picture of myalgic encephalomyelitis and post-exertional malaise.

**Orthostatic intolerance despite normal blood pressure.** When a patient stands, gravity pulls 500-800 milliliters of blood into the dependent tissues, and the baroreceptor reflex must respond within seconds by increasing sympathetic vasoconstriction to restore cerebral perfusion. In a decoupled system, sympathetic output is often present and sometimes elevated, but vascular responsiveness is impaired because the glucocorticoid permission is missing. The result is that sympathetic activation occurs without adequate vasoconstriction — the sympathetic surge fails to reach its goal, and cerebral perfusion drops despite apparently normal or even elevated sympathetic output. Blood pressure may remain entirely normal because cardiac output is maintained, yet the patient experiences severe dizziness, visual blurring, and weakness. A normal orthostatic blood-pressure response therefore does not rule out the underlying decoupling; the decoupling is in the vascular system's ability to respond, not in the blood-pressure number.

**Delayed rather than immediate symptom flare.** The inflammatory signature of post-exertional malaise — cytokine elevation and microglia activation persisting for 24-72 hours — follows directly from the missing glucocorticoid brake. When cortisol is present at adequate levels, the inflammatory cascade triggered by exertion reaches a peak and then is actively suppressed by glucocorticoid signaling within hours. In the decoupled system, this suppression does not occur: IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and reactive oxygen species persist in neural parenchyma and peripheral circulation unchecked, continuing to drive symptoms, sickness behavior, and further cellular damage. The timeline is therefore not immediate (symptoms from the exertion itself) but delayed (symptoms from the uncontrolled inflammatory response to the exertion). This is why patients often describe a symptom flare that begins 12-48 hours after exertion rather than during it.

**Eventual collapse into immobility.** The repeated cycles of sympathetic surge followed by failure to achieve homeostasis appear to drive a collapse into a dorsal vagal shutdown state — immobility, profound weakness, cognitive fog, and dissociation lasting hours to days. This is not a volitional rest response but a physiological collapse of the organism into a conservation mode when repeated mobilization has failed to achieve anything. The state itself is metabolically minimal, which may represent an adaptive attempt at energy conservation given the chronically depleted mitochondrial substrate; but the state is deeply disabling and produces its own cost. A patient trapped in this state cannot move without profound effort, cannot think clearly, and cannot engage with the world. When the collapse finally begins to lift, another exertion attempt can trigger the cycle again.

## 4. How this differs from allostatic load and why that distinction matters

The house concept of allostatic load, described in the existing physiology-section carriers, frames the cost of a stress response as *cumulative wear from running the response too often*. The system fires correctly, but fires too many times, and the accumulation of physiological wear across multiple systems (cardiovascular, metabolic, immune, neuroendocrine) produces increasingly degraded function. This is genuinely true in many stress-related and health-disparity contexts, and allostatic load is the right frame for conditions driven by chronic social threat, precarity, discrimination, and burnout.

Post-exertional malaise is not allostatic load in this sense. The pathology is not that the stress response is firing too many times; it is that it is firing *incoherently*. The metabolic cost per cycle is not merely high; it is *unmanageable* because the substrate mobilization is inadequate and the inflammatory response is not being braked. A single exertion episode in a decoupled system can produce more cumulative damage than many exertion episodes in a healthy system, because the inflammatory response is not being counter-regulated and the metabolic mismatch is more severe. The consequence is that allostatic load models that score cumulative physiological wear may actually *underpredict* the cost in myalgic encephalomyelitis, because they score the cost as a function of frequency when the real cost is a function of coherence failure. A patient who exercises mildly and carefully may accumulate massive allostatic cost from a small number of exertion episodes precisely because each episode is handled so incoherently.

## 5. Mechanistic synthesis and open questions

The story presented here — sympathoadrenal output decoupled from hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal support, producing metabolic substrate insufficiency, vascular dysregulation, and unchecked inflammation — brings together observations from multiple domains: the autonomic dysregulation and orthostatic intolerance literatures, the two-day exercise-challenge findings showing anaerobic threshold drops on day two, the delayed cytokine and microglial activation findings, and the clinical observation of symptom flare rather than immediate response. But this is a synthesis of partially-established findings, not a settled causal chain. The Fluge-Mella hypothesis of pyruvate dehydrogenase inhibition, the specific microglia activation patterns described in the small Nakatomi positron-emission-tomography sample, the exact role of mitochondrial substrate insufficiency versus oxidative stress — these remain open mechanistic questions. What is more established is that some form of metabolic-autonomic-endocrine decoupling occurs in myalgic encephalomyelitis, and that framing the pathology as decoupling rather than as hyperarousal correctly predicts the clinical observations that hyperarousal models do not.
