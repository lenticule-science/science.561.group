The dorsal vagal state is a parasympathetic-dominant physiological state characterized by immobility, profound muscular weakness, and severe cognitive shutdown — what is clinically observed as a collapse into lethargy, vacancy, and inability to move or engage. The term originates from Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory, which proposes that the parasympathetic nervous system has two anatomically and functionally distinct branches: a ventral vagal system (via the ventral nucleus of the vagus) mediating calm alertness and social engagement, and a dorsal vagal system (via the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus) mediating a primitive immobilization response. According to Porges, when an organism fails to escape a threat or resolve a mobilization effort, it collapses into a dorsal vagal shutdown: the organism conserves energy by suppressing movement and cognition, heart rate drops (bradycardia), respiration becomes shallow, and awareness narrows dramatically. Clinically, this is the state described in some trauma and dissociation literatures as freeze or collapse.

This is a point at which honesty about the underlying theory is required. The polyvagal theory's specific evolutionary and anatomical claims — particularly the claimed embryological distinction between ventral and dorsal vagal branches in mammals as Porges frames it — are substantially contested in the comparative-neuroanatomy literature. Paul Grossman and colleagues have published detailed critiques identifying anatomical and evolutionary problems with the ventral-dorsal vagal split as Porges proposes it, and the theory's neuroanatomical foundations have been disputed by other specialists in vagal anatomy. This is important context: the phenomenon the term names is real and clinically relevant, but the framework that produced the term is neither settled nor universally accepted in neuroscience. The recommendation for use of the term is therefore to honor what the clinical phenomenon actually is — a collapse into immobility and cognitive shutdown — while being explicit that the polyvagal theory's anatomical mechanism is disputed and not foundational to recognizing the phenomenon itself.

What is clinically unambiguous is the state itself: after a period of sustained sympathetic mobilization (exertion, threat-response) that fails to resolve or reach homeostasis, an organism may collapse into a state of immobility, extreme weakness, profound fatigue, and cognitive fog. This is the state most relevant to myalgic encephalomyelitis, where repeated failed attempts at mobilization — exertion driving a sympathoadrenal surge that fails to reach successful completion and cannot be halted — lead to a collapse into immobility and brain fog lasting hours to days. The state includes diminished heart rate relative to the preceding mobilization, shallow or dysrhythmic breathing, severe difficulty thinking or attending, loss of capacity for physical movement or vocalization, and in severe cases, a dissolution of the boundary between sleep and wakefulness. It is a state of minimal metabolic demand, minimal cognitive function, and minimal physical capacity — what appears to be an energy-conservation mode.

The polyvagal-theory framework holds that this state is a mammalian primitive defense response, and there is clinical credence to treating it as such: the state appears to serve no current adaptive function and is deeply disabling. However, the theory that this response is mediated specifically by a dorsal vagal branch distinguished from a ventral one is not the only possible explanation of the phenomenon, and the patient's recovery from the state should not be understood as depending on the polyvagal theory being correct. The state is best understood as a collapse of sustained mobilization in the context of failed neuroendocrine and autonomic coordination — what happens when the sympathoadrenal system has fired for hours or days without achieving homeostasis and without adequate hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal and metabolic support. The shut-down that follows is a real switching of the organism into a conservation mode, but this can be explained through the failure of the normal regulatory cascades without requiring the polyvagal theory's specific anatomical claims.

## Related terms

- Sympathoadrenal system — the mobilization system whose failure to resolve leads to dorsal vagal collapse
- Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis — whose impairment contributes to failed resolution of the mobilization state
- Dysautonomia — the autonomic dysregulation underlying the collapse into dorsal vagal shutdown
- Post-exertional malaise — the symptom flare in myalgic encephalomyelitis, often featuring profound collapse into dorsal vagal-like states
- Allostatic load — the cumulative cost of cycling into and out of dorsal vagal states
