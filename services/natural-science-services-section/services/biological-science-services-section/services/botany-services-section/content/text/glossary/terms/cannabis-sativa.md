*Cannabis sativa* L. is an annual, herbaceous, wind-pollinated flowering plant in the family Cannabaceae, whose closest taxonomically informative relative is the sister genus *Humulus* (hops) — a relationship that explains the two plants' shared floral architecture (small, petal-reduced, wind-dispersed flowers) and, more strikingly, their independent evolution of glandular trichome systems that manufacture and externally store lipophilic secondary metabolites. *Cannabis* is typically dioecious, with individual plants bearing either male or female flowers, though monoecious variants bearing both sexes exist and are exploited in some breeding programs. Sex is chromosomally determined by an X/Y system in which females are homogametic (XX) and males heterogametic (XY); the X chromosome is the largest in the genome and carries an unusually old, large, non-recombining region implicated in sex determination, though the specific causal genes remain incompletely resolved.

The species' taxonomy has been disputed since the eighteenth century, when Linnaeus described a single species (*Cannabis sativa*, 1753) and Lamarck later argued for a second (*Cannabis indica*, 1785) based on differences in growth habit and intoxicating effect. That formal dispute — one polytypic species versus two or three separate species — persists in the scientific literature, but it has become entangled with, and largely eclipsed by, an entirely separate vernacular classification used in the cannabis market ("indica" versus "sativa" strains, sold on the premise that indica-type cannabis produces sedative, body-focused effects and sativa-type produces energetic, cerebral effects). This vernacular distinction is one of the most widely believed claims about the plant, and it is worth being precise about why it does not hold up: taxonomic historians (notably McPartland) have shown that the market's use of "indica" and "sativa" does not even consistently correspond to the historical scientific names it borrows from, and modern population-genomic studies find that cultivated cannabis forms a continuously admixed genetic landscape shaped by trait-based breeding and repeated hybridization, not two or three clean, chemically or genetically distinct lineages matching the vernacular labels. Cannabinoid and terpene chemistry in particular do not sort along the indica/sativa axis in any way that has been reliably demonstrated — chemotype (see chemotype) is governed by a largely separate genomic region and does not track the indica/sativa distinction at all.

Chemically, *Cannabis* is defined by cannabinoid and terpene biosynthesis occurring inside specialized glandular trichome secretory cells and being exported into an extracellular, subcuticular storage cavity — the plant does not store these compounds in living cytoplasm, plausibly because several major cannabinoids are demonstrably cytotoxic to the plant's own cells at the concentrations produced. The compounds are synthesized and stored in acid form (tetrahydrocannabinolic acid, cannabidiolic acid, and related acids); the well-known neutral compounds (THC, CBD) are produced from these acids by decarboxylation, a heat- or time-driven chemical reaction that occurs after harvest, not inside the living plant.

Genomic evidence (Ren et al. 2021) points to a single domestication origin in East Asia roughly 12,000 years ago, with hemp-type (fiber and seed) and drug-type (cannabinoid-rich) lineages diverging under separate selection pressures several thousand years later from a shared ancestral gene pool. Archaeological chemical-residue evidence (Ren et al. 2019, from the Jirzankal Cemetery in the eastern Pamirs) documents deliberate ritual burning of high-THC cannabis by roughly 2,500 years before present. For the estate's biochemical research line, *Cannabis sativa* stands as an unusually clean natural case study in compartmentalization: a plant that solves the problem of a self-toxic biosynthetic product by relocating both the product and, in the case of THCA synthase, the terminal enzyme itself, into a storage space physically outside the cell that made them.
