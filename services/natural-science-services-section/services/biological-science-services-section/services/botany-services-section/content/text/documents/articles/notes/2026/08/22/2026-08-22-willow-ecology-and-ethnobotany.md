## The genus that resists counting

Salix has somewhere between ~300 and ~520 species depending on circumscription; Argus's own working figure was "about 400 species and numerous subspecific kinds of willows worldwide, with about 62 species native to Canada" (Brodo & Haber's tribute, *Canadian Field-Naturalist* 136(3), 2022, read in full). The instability is intrinsic, not sloppy bookkeeping. George Argus — the genus's modern monographer (M.S. on Wyoming willows 1957; Ph.D. Harvard 1961 under Hugh Raup; the 140-page *Flora of North America* Salix treatment, 2010; a donated bibliography of "more than 3600 publications related to the genus Salix"; also, at 25, a Denali summit and a nearly fatal fall on the descent) — states five compounding reasons in his Alberta identification guide, read directly:

1. Dioecy — "flowers of only one sex occur on a single individual," so half of all flowering specimens frustrate floral keys;
2. Floral reduction — "there are relatively few floral characteristics on which to draw for identification" (staminate flowers are stamens plus nectaries; pistillate, an ovary plus nectaries);
3. Developmental asynchrony — at any moment "some plants may bear only flowers, others flowers and juvenile leaves, and yet other may have only leaves";
4. Phenotypic plasticity — diagnostic-seeming characters "are often distressingly variable" with moisture, nutrients, shade, wind;
5. Hybridization and polyploidy — about 40% of Salix is polyploid, and "it is likely that most of these polyploids are alloploids which arose through hybridization," leaving taxonomists, in Argus's phrase, "walking an a priori tightrope."

The last point deserves its own sentence: hybridization in willows may not be noise obscuring species boundaries but a primary engine of the genus's diversification. And the disconnect persists into the genomic era — a 2024 *American Journal of Botany* study (with Argus as coauthor, in his nineties) reports most species monophyletic but "the existing sectional classification could not be supported by molecular data" (abstract only; full text blocked). Argus's infrageneric scheme itself moved from four New World subgenera (1997, *Syst. Bot. Monogr.* 52) to five in FNA 2010 (Protitea, Longifoliae, Salix, Chamaetia, Vetrix).

## Reproduction on a deadline

Willows are dioecious catkin-bearers with three flowering-timing classes (precocious, coetaneous, serotinous — pussy willows are the famous precocious case). Unusually for catkin plants, they are **ambophilous** — both insect- and wind-pollinated — and the balance is weather-contingent within a species: Tamura & Kudo (*Plant Ecology*, 2000) found the same clones leaning on insects in a wet cloudy spring and on wind in a dry sunny one (search-summarized; primary not fully read). A *Biological Reviews* 2023 synthesis suggests ambophily "may be the general pollination mode" for the genus, a transitional configuration between the pollination worlds.

The seeds are the extreme trait: non-dormant, and viable for almost no time. USDA Forest Service silvics for black willow: "Unless the willow seed is floating on water, it must reach the seedbed within 12 to 24 hours because viability is greatly reduced by only a few days of dry conditions" — among the shortest viability windows of any North American tree. A peer-reviewed *Salix variegata* study puts hard numbers on the pattern (seeds sojourn in capsules ~3–5 days; fresh-seed germination up to 93%; viability retained at most 8–16 days). Germination is effectively obligate on bare, wet mineral sediment — which is why willow demography is chained to flood disturbance (Karrenberg, Edwards & Kollmann's *Freshwater Biology* 2002 review is the standard citation; paywalled, characterized here secondhand).

## The tree that plants itself

Willows hedge the fragile-seed bet with vegetative prowess. Crack willow (*Salix × fragilis*) is *named* for cladoptosis: twigs "break off very easily and cleanly at the base with an audible crack," and "broken twigs and branches can readily take root, enabling the species to colonise new areas when they fall into waterways and are carried some distance downstream" (Wikipedia, with the Latin epithet itself as independent corroboration; a dedicated primary paper on willow abscission zones was not located). The mechanism behind the stake-becomes-tree trick is **preformed root primordia**: dormant adventitious root initials already present in the stem (Fjell 1985, *Nordic J. Botany*, abstract only), which is why willow stakes root where most woody cuttings fail — species without preformed primordia "are extremely poor rooters" (search synthesis; the circulating 48-hour-activation/6-day-emergence timeline was not verified against the primary text). Black willow also sprouts prolifically from the base after fire (USDA FEIS).

## Water-table work

Riparian willows are phreatophytes — groundwater plants — with typical rooting depths ≤2.5 m, occurring "in locations with stable and shallow groundwater," their canopy greenness a sensitive indicator of water-table depth (Rohde, Stella, Roberts & Singer, *PNAS* 118(25), 2021, read directly). The dependence is lethal when broken: "Whole stands may die out when water tables lower and soil dries up" (USDA FEIS). On the constructive side this is the oldest engineering plant there is: live staking (dormant cuttings driven into banks — Penn State Extension, current, read directly), fascines (buried ropes of dormant branches that sprout into "a living wall"), and the whole discipline of soil bioengineering, whose very name is a 1951 translation of the German *Ingenieurbiologie*, with willow bundle-work documented from medieval England and claimed (unverified against a primary source) for Chinese dike repair as early as 28 BC. The sandbar-succession partnership with cottonwood is covered in the willow-negative companion note; worth adding here that willow colonization is fast enough to be a *management problem* — succession toward willow-cottonwood forest destroys the bare-sandbar nesting habitat of least terns and piping plovers, a case of the genus's load-bearing ecology cutting both ways (lead flagged, primary not read).

## The load the genus carries

- **Lepidoptera:** Tallamy's ranking makes Salix the second most important host genus in North America after oaks; the circulating national figure is 455 species, but regional counts run 312 (Pacific Northwest), 375, 397 — the spread is real (different scopes) and the 455 could not be verified against the live database (404). Grow Native Massachusetts (read directly) carries the Tallamy ranking and the pollinator case: willows bloom "at the beginning of April, when few other plants are in flower."
- **Early bees:** mining bees (*Andrena*) include strict willow specialists — "Across the continent, other mining bees survive solely on the pollen of native willows" (Marinelli, *National Wildlife*, 2017, read directly); Massachusetts alone has seven willow-specialist *Andrena* (search synthesis). A *Salix nigra* floral-community study with named *Andrena* species exists (PMC8093708) and was not read — flagged lead.
- **Moose:** "The most important winter food for moose is willow" — higher digestible organic matter and crude protein, less lignin, than other winter habitats offer (agency and journal sources, search-synthesized).
- **Willow ptarmigan:** 44–95% willow in winter/spring diet (Alaska/BC). The common name encodes the ecological relationship; the binomial does not — *Lagopus* is "hare-foot," for the feathered feet.
- **Beavers:** willow is consistently preferred browse — with the honest caveat that the defensive-chemistry explanation (salicinoids, "found only in poplars and willows," plus condensed tannins) is much better worked out for aspen (Bryant's classic central-place foraging work) than for Salix itself. That gap is a finding: willow beaver-preference chemistry is genuinely less studied, not just less searched.
- **Smallest tree:** *Salix herbacea*, the dwarf willow, under 6 cm tall, with true secondary xylem and growth rings — Guinness's official shortest tree species, the "is it a tree" dispute being itself a live definitional argument. (The circulating 50–100-year longevity figure was not traced to its primary dendrochronology source; a Southampton eprint is the flagged lead.)

## The Yellowstone dispute: willow's most famous story is not settled

The wolf → elk → willow → beaver trophic cascade is probably the most widely-retold willow ecology narrative in existence. As of October 2025 it is under live, sharp methodological attack: Ripple et al. (2025) claimed a ~1500% increase in willow crown volume after wolf reintroduction; MacNulty and colleagues' published comment calls the conclusion "invalid because it relies on circular reasoning" — "Because height was used both to compute and to predict volume, the relationship is circular — mathematically guaranteed to look strong even if no biological change occurred" — and Cooper states "there is no evidence that predator recovery caused a large or system-wide increase in willow growth," with Hobbs et al. (2024), who collected the original two decades of field data, finding "only weak cascade effects." The data instead support "a more modest and spatially variable response influenced by hydrology, browsing, and local site conditions." (Quotes from the phys.org coverage of the comment, read directly; the Ripple originals were not successfully fetched.) The corpus should not repeat the wolves-saved-the-willows story as settled; it is an active argument about how to model willow crown volume, conducted over the *same* underlying dataset.

## Chemistry, and two folklore chains broken at the root

Salicin → salicylic acid → aspirin is covered in the willow-negative companion note; the deeper historiography (Stone's 1763 Royal Society letter as the real documented start; Martyr's debunking of the Hippocrates story) is in the human-relationships section below. Two additional widely-circulated claims failed their citation chase outright:

1. **"Willow water contains IBA and is a natural rooting hormone."** IBA (indole-3-butyric acid) was discovered in 1935 as a *synthetic* compound (Zimmerman & Wilcoxon) and only later found endogenous in plants (potato, 1954). No primary paper identifying IBA in *Salix* tissue could be located; the Wikipedia article on willow water cites no scientific source at all; IBA's water solubility (~250 ppm) sits below commercial application rates (500–1500 ppm); and the one skeptical practical test found willow water at 96% rooting vs. **92% for plain water** (Pavlis, gardenmyths.com, read directly). Worse: specific "citations" circulating on gardening sites — a "HortScience 2021" study, a "University of Vermont Horticultural Extension trial" reporting 73% improvement, an RHS replication — **could not be located and appear to be fabricated or AI-generated content-farm citations**. One real peer-reviewed trial exists (Rehman et al. 2018, IISTE — a low-rigor publisher, no water-only control); it is weak-tier. The corpus should treat willow water as unsupported folklore with a fabricated modern citation apparatus.
2. **"Willow bends but does not break — a teaching of humility"** as the rationale for the sweat-lodge frame: no named source whatsoever — see below.

## Human relationships, graded by attribution

**Grade A — named authors or consultants, directly read.** Gilmore's *Uses of Plants by the Indians of the Missouri River Region* (1919), fetched in full: sandbar willow "peeled and used in basketry by the Omaha and other tribes"; willow poles in earth-lodge roofs and "to form the frame of the sudatory, or bath lodge" (fact stated, no rationale given); and the Omaha funeral rite — young men gashing their forearms and threading willow twigs beneath the skin, "bathed in the blood of the young men, who thus attested to the living their sympathy and condolence, while they sang the tribal Song to the Spirit." Gilmore's general gloss: the willow "was always found along the watercourses, as though it had some duty or function in the world in connection with this element." **Gilmore contains no backrests, no cradleboards, no drying racks — anyone citing him for those is fabricating.** Black Elk & Flying By's *Culturally Important Plants of the Lakota* (Sitting Bull College, 1998; named Lakota consultant Wilbur D. Flying By, Sr.; PDF extracted directly): entry #270, *Salix exigua* (čȟoȟáŋ waŋžíča) — "The branches of this willow species are used in making sweatlodge frames. The peeled outer bark is used for tying sweatlodge frames together," plus a mourning-ceremony use and bark decoction for headache and fever; entry #269, diamond willow (*S. eriocephala*) — fungus-marked branches "prized for making sacred staffs and walking sticks" (the source itself hedges the fungus as "probably *Valsa sordida*" — its epistemic humility preserved here). This document is species-precise, consultant-named, tribally published, and underused relative to the popular sources that get cited instead. Also grade A: Edward Stone's 1763 Royal Society letter on willow bark for agues (Royal Society-archived primary); Philippa Martyr's named academic debunking of the Hippocrates story — Hippocrates' corpus contains *one* willow reference, fumigation after miscarriage, not analgesic bark; white willow bark is salicin-poor; "By early modern Europe, willow was considered largely useless as a medicine"; the cricket-bat willow (*S. alba* var. *caerulea*), osier/withy coppice culture, the willow-pattern china seam (pattern c. 1790, its "ancient Chinese legend" reaching final form 1849 — a marketing retrofit two generations later), and the weeping willow's misnaming (*S. babylonica* is Chinese; Psalm 137's trees were likely *Populus euphratica*); named master weavers Dat-So-La-Lee/Louisa Keyser (Washoe, degikup style), Julia Parker (Coast Miwok–Kashaya Pomo), Elsie Allen (Cloverdale Pomo).

**Grade B–C — real but not primary-verified.** Bucko's and Powers's sweat-lodge monographs were unreachable in full text; any specific figures attributed to them (sixteen withes, pole dimensions) are secondary paraphrase. Lakota willow-rod backrests: a Smithsonian catalog record exists (E391995-0, Teton attribution) but the live page was bot-blocked and no fieldwork description with a named consultant surfaced. Willow cradleboards (Cheyenne, Apache): general-audience sources only. Hupa/Yurok/Karuk willow-and-hazel basketry: named ethnographers (Lila O'Neale's 1920s ethnoaesthetics fieldwork; Goddard) via secondary description. Cherokee black-willow bark medicine: Hamel & Chiltoskey 1975 via a USDA citation — Chiltoskey's Eastern Band affiliation stated from general knowledge, unverified, flagged.

**Grade F — do not present as documented.** The ubiquitous "willow bends but does not break — symbolizing humility" rationale for the inípi frame traces to no named ethnographer, elder, or academic source; near-identical phrasing recurs across wellness/tourism sites, the signature of one unsourced original copied forward. Same structural finding as the cottonwood star's provenance, and it belongs beside it. Likewise: Grandmother Willow (*Pocahontas*, 1995) is a documented studio invention (originally an "Old Man River" figure, changed on Gregory Peck's suggestion) corresponding to no actual Indigenous spiritual figure — a useful explicit negative for the attribution ledger. The čhaŋšáša matter is settled in the provenance note: "red willow" smoking bark is *Cornus sericea*, not Salix — corroborated independently by the Black Elk & Flying By document's own separate entry #87.

Literature, briefly and adequately sourced: Shakespeare fixes willow as a grief-marker twice — Ophelia's willow "aslant a brook" (*Hamlet* 4.7, thence Millais) and Desdemona's Willow Song (*Othello*, an older ballad adapted, not invented); Western funerary art of the 18th–19th centuries wears the weeping willow; the resilience/flexibility emblem tracks the plant's real biology and is strongest in Chinese tradition. No named folklorist located for the cross-cultural survey claims; graded B.

## What this note does and does not establish

It establishes a sourced natural-history foundation (taxonomic instability as intrinsic, hours-scale seed viability, preformed root primordia, phreatophyte dependence, the ecological load) and an attribution-graded human ledger with two strong primary anchors (Gilmore 1919; Black Elk & Flying By 1998) and three explicit negatives (willow-water's fabricated citations; the unattributed bends-but-does-not-break teaching; Grandmother Willow). It does not establish: why beavers prefer willow chemically (the aspen literature does not transfer automatically); the Yellowstone cascade either way (live dispute — report it as such); Bucko's and Powers's actual text; the *Salix*-endogenous-IBA claim. Leads, consolidated: Bucko and Powers via interlibrary loan; Walker's informants George Sword and Thomas Tyon on willow testimony; DeMallie & Parks's Sun Dance volume (Amiotte) for willow switches in arbor construction; the *S. nigra*/*Andrena* study; Hobbs et al. 2024; the Southampton *S. herbacea* dendrochronology eprint; Argus's 1997 and 2010 monographs in primary text; the unnamed Ebers-papyrus critic; non-English (Russian, Chinese, German) Salix literature, entirely untouched.
