Compiled 2026-08-22 as the botany leg of a three-note study of the number five, alongside a [note on the study of five](../../../../../../../../../../../../../information-science-services-section/services/math-services-section/services/obsmetrological-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-the-study-of-five.md) (mathematics side) and a [note on the provenance of the cottonwood star](../../../../../../../../../../../../../history-and-archaeology-science-services-section/services/social-history-science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-the-provenance-of-the-cottonwood-star.md) (attribution side). This note reports what the botanical literature establishes about the five-pointed figure in cottonwood twig pith, states plainly where the causal story is this compilation's own inference rather than a published finding, and records the negative result on willow — which is a finding, not a failure.

## The star is real, and hedged

The five-pointed pith of *Populus deltoides* is attested in floristic sources. The Brooklyn Botanic Garden's New York Metropolitan Flora (Steven D. Glenn) describes the eastern cottonwood twig: "Pith brown, 5-pointed, continuous, nodal diaphragm absent." A. C. Gibson's *Vascular Plants of Williamson County* treatment of *P. deltoides* subsp. *deltoides* independently gives "pith 5-angled and spongy whitish," and notes the leaves are "helically alternate." Two cautions ride along:

- **The authoritative treatment hedges.** Flora of North America (Eckenwalder, vol. 7) describes *P. deltoides* branchlets as "round or 5-angled" — an external cross-section character, variable, not a fixed law — and the retrieved species and genus text uses neither "pith" nor "stellate" at all. The popular framing that cottonwood pith is reliably and always a clean five-pointed star outruns the authoritative flora.
- **The sources disagree on color.** NYMF says the pith is brown; Gibson says spongy whitish — for nominally the same taxon. This compilation does not adjudicate; it records the conflict.
- **Per-species claims need per-species citations.** The NYMF "5-pointed" sentence describes *P. deltoides* only. Similar descriptions exist for *P. tremuloides* (Missouri Botanical Garden Plant Finder) and *P. alba* (extension sources), but during verification an earlier draft of this claim was caught stretching the single NYMF page across four species — a citation-inflation error worth remembering as method. (Verification also caught a search-engine summary attributing a "5-pointed pith" sentence to the FNA page that the actual page source does not contain — snippet trust is not source verification.)

## The mechanism, and where our inference begins

The developmental anatomy lives in a separate literature that never mentions the twig-snapping demonstration at all: P. R. Larson's and J. G. Isebrands's peer-reviewed work on *Populus deltoides* vascular development. Larson (1980, *Annals of Botany* 46:757–769) reports: "The procambial system of *Populus deltoides* Bartr. ex Marsh. plants progressed from 1/2 phyllotaxy in the cotyledon stage through the phyllotactic orders 1/3 → 2/5 → 3/8 → 5/13." Larson (1977, *Planta* 134:241–249, by ¹⁴C labeling) showed that leaf traces join the stem's vascular bundles into discrete longitudinal chains — sympodia — and that **the number of sympodia at a given stem level equals the denominator of the phyllotactic fraction there**: five sympodia in the 2/5 region, eight in the 3/8 region (as reproduced with Larson's Fig. 16 in arXiv:1207.2838). Companion papers: Isebrands & Larson 1977 (*Am. J. Bot.* 64:1066–1077, nodal anatomy) and Larson & Isebrands 1974 (*Wood Sci. Technol.* 8:11–26, primary–secondary transition proceeding bundle-by-bundle by phyllotactic position). The 1974 and 1977 AJB papers were paywalled during compilation and are cited here without direct quotation.

**The bridge is ours.** No located source states "the five sympodia are why the snapped twig shows a five-pointed star." The floristic literature says "5-pointed" and stops; the developmental literature counts five bundle-chains and never mentions the star. That the visible star is the cross-section of the five-sympodium primary vascular architecture is this compilation's inference from convergent evidence — plausible, mechanistically natural, and unpublished as far as we found. It is exactly the kind of claim a targeted read of Larson 1977's figures could confirm or refute.

Two scope limits on any "five is cottonwood's number" reading:

- **Not unique to *Populus*.** Beck et al. (1982, cited via arXiv:1207.2838) report that open five-sympodia systems under 2/5 phyllotaxis characterize about 67% of dicot species with helical phyllotaxis. The cottonwood star is a common dicot architecture made unusually visible, not a *Populus* signature.
- **Not even constant within the tree.** The classic phyllotaxis tables put "poplar" at 3/8 (MathWorld, after Ball & Coxeter), while Larson's mature basal stem sits at 2/5 — reconciled (again, our reconciliation, flagged as such) by Larson's own finding that one tree carries different phyllotactic orders at different stem levels and ages. The tables themselves disagree between reproductions: Wikipedia's Coxeter table gives 2/5 to "oak and apricot," MathWorld to "oak and apple," Okabe (*Sci. Rep.*, PMC4607949) to "oak, cherry, apple, holly and plum." The species lists attached to these fractions are folklore-grade; the fraction sequence itself is the stable content.

## The willow negative

Willow was the unexplored sibling; explored, it supplies almost no fiveness — and the shape of the absence is instructive. (This section is only the five-specific finding; the corpus's standalone willow research is the same-day [willow ecology and ethnobotany note](2026-08-22-willow-ecology-and-ethnobotany.md).)

- **No five-part flower.** Salicaceae flowers elsewhere in the family keep a five-part calyx — "All genera have a calyx of (3–)5(–6) sepals, **except *Salix* and *Populus* which lack an obvious perianth entirely**" (PMC4671327, on catkin evolution; its Table 1: "Sepals 0, petals 0" for both genera, "the extent of the process of floral reduction in those genera"). The two great riparian genera are precisely the ones that abandoned the family's floral five for wind- and insect-pollinated catkins.
- **No star in the pith.** Go Botany (Native Plant Trust) for *Salix nigra*: "the outline of the pith in a twig is roughly round." Diamond willow's diamonds are a fungal canker (*Valsa sordida*), not pith geometry. One untraced non-English wood-anatomy source describes "almost stellate" to "stellate" pith in a few Old World species (*S. alba*, *S. babylonica*, *S. excelsa*, *S. fragilis*); it could not be verified against a readable primary text and is recorded only as an unconfirmed minority report. Note the direct conflict with a Montana Public Radio "Field Notes" piece claiming the star is common to the whole willow family including willows — the checkable sources side against MTPR for *Salix* itself.
- **The table entry moves on.** Where the classic tables list willow at all, it is at 5/13 ("willow and almond," Wikipedia and MathWorld after Coxeter) — not 2/5. Willow's five, such as it is, has migrated to the numerator of a later Fibonacci convergent. We flag and resist the temptation to read anything into that.

What willow does carry, richly, is non-five riparian significance: salicin, isolated by Büchner (1828), purified by Leroux (1829), converted toward salicylic acid by Piria (1838), acetylated by Gerhardt (1853) and developed at Bayer by Hoffmann under Eichengrün (whose exact role is genuinely contested in the historiography), trademark "Aspirin" registered 6 March 1899 (Science History Institute). And the successional partnership with cottonwood: Meriwether Lewis, 14 June 1804, on sandbar willow — "invariably the first which makes it's appearance on the newly made Lands on the borders of the Mississippi and Missouri, and seems to contribute much towards facilitating the operation of raisin[g] this ground still higher" — with USDA Forest Service FEIS material describing cottonwood establishment after sandbar willows stabilize the site, and ash, elm, boxelder, and hackberry arriving over the following decades. (Caution from verification: the Missouri Department of Conservation field guide cited in an earlier draft for "willow first" only says willow is "often associated with" cottonwood on river flats — the succession claim rests on the USDA material and Lewis, not on MDC.) Willow's ceremonial material culture (sweat-lodge frames; the čhaŋšáša identification) is covered in the provenance companion note.

See the [riparian glossary entry](../../../../../../../../../ecology-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/riparian.md) for the community context, [plant-morphogenesis](../../../../../../glossary/terms/plant-morphogenesis.md) for the auxin-transport machinery that positions primordia (the Fibonacci-angle connection is not yet stated in that entry — a future edit could add it), and [rosaceae](../../../../../../glossary/terms/rosaceae.md) for the family where the eudicot floral five persists undiminished ("Diagnostic 5 petals"). That the rose family wears its five in the flower while the cottonwood carries its five hidden in the stem's circulatory architecture is a pleasing contrast, and nothing more than that.

## What this note does and does not establish

Established, with sources: the "5-pointed" pith description for *P. deltoides* (floristic, hedged by FNA); the 1/2 → 1/3 → 2/5 → 3/8 → 5/13 developmental progression and the sympodium-count-equals-denominator result (Larson, peer-reviewed); the ~67% prevalence of five-sympodia architecture among helical-phyllotaxis dicots (Beck et al., secondhand); the willow negatives (apetalous catkins, round pith in *S. nigra*, 5/13 table entry). Not established: the causal bridge from five sympodia to the visible star (our inference, flagged); pith color; universality across *Populus* species and sections (Larson's work is exclusively *P. deltoides*; no source compares cottonwoods, aspens, and balsam poplars on this trait); the Old World stellate-*Salix* report. Leads: the four Larson/Isebrands primaries in full text; Beck et al. 1982 directly; Argus's *Salix* identification guides for a rigorous North American willow pith description; Dixon et al. 2012 (*Environmental Management*) for modern point-bar succession timing.
