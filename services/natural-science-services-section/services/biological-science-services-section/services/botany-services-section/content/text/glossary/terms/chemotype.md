A chemotype (chemical type, or chemovar) is a category applied within a single plant species to distinguish populations or individuals that differ mainly or entirely in their secondary-metabolite chemistry rather than in gross morphology. The concept exists because plant taxonomy is traditionally built on morphological characters — leaf shape, flower structure, growth habit — which do not reliably track chemical composition; two individuals that are morphologically indistinguishable, and would be classified as the same species or subspecies on structural grounds, can nonetheless differ dramatically in which secondary metabolites they accumulate, in what ratios. The chemotype concept gives that kind of variation a formal name without requiring (or implying) that it corresponds to any separate taxonomic rank.

*Cannabis sativa* is the most thoroughly worked-out chemotype system in the botanical literature, because the underlying genetics turned out to be unusually tractable. De Meijer and colleagues' controlled crosses (2003) between pure cannabidiolic-acid (CBDA) and pure tetrahydrocannabinolic-acid (THCA) lines produced first-generation offspring with mixed chemotype, consistent with a single genomic region carrying two co-dominant variants — one favoring CBDA-synthase activity, one favoring THCA-synthase activity — segregating in simple, close-to-Mendelian fashion. This gave rise to the conventional five-chemotype scheme still used as a reference point: Chemotype I (THCA-dominant), Chemotype II (mixed THCA/CBDA), Chemotype III (CBDA-dominant), Chemotype IV (CBGA-dominant, in which the shared precursor cannabigerolic acid accumulates because conversion to THCA or CBDA is minimal), and Chemotype V (near-cannabinoid-free). This scheme underlies, at one remove, the entire modern legal and commercial distinction between "hemp" and "drug-type" cannabis, which is fundamentally a chemotype distinction (dominant cannabinoid and its concentration) rather than a distinction of species, subspecies, or the popular "indica/sativa" categories.

Later genomic work has complicated the tidy two-allele picture without discarding the chemotype categories themselves. The genomic region responsible turns out to contain the THCA-synthase and CBDA-synthase genes embedded in large, repetitive, non-recombining stretches of DNA containing tandem, partially duplicated copies and apparent pseudogenes, rather than a single clean gene pair — the discrete inheritance pattern de Meijer's crosses revealed reflects strong linkage across this structurally divergent region rather than literal single-gene co-dominance. The practical chemotype categories remain useful and largely predictive at the level of a breeder or grower deciding what a given cross will produce, even as the underlying molecular picture has grown more intricate.

For the estate's compartmentalization research line, chemotype is a useful bridge concept between genetics and storage biology: it names the outcome (which acid cannabinoid dominates a given plant's trichome resin) of a genetically determined enzymatic choice made inside the same secretory disc cells that then export the product into the extracellular storage cavity described under glandular trichome. Chemotype variation is, in that sense, variation in what gets compartmentalized, not variation in whether compartmentalization occurs — every chemotype still stores its dominant cannabinoid the same way, outside the cell.
