Cannabaceae, the hemp family, is a modest angiosperm family in the order Rosales comprising roughly ten genera and about 170 species. It is a small family by flowering-plant standards, but an economically and chemically disproportionate one: its two best-known genera, *Cannabis* and *Humulus* (hops), are both cultivated worldwide for chemically active secretions produced in specialized epidermal glands, and a third genus, *Celtis* (hackberries), contributes substantial tree diversity to the family's less conspicuous, non-glandular side. The family sits within a broader Rosales clade that also contains Rosaceae, Moraceae, and Urticaceae — an assemblage that looks superficially disparate (roses, mulberries, nettles, hemp) but is united by molecular phylogenetic evidence rather than obvious shared morphology.

Cannabaceae flowers are small, greenish, and largely petal-reduced — a floral architecture that trades pollinator-attracting display for efficiency at wind dispersal. Most of the family's economically significant genera, including *Cannabis* and *Humulus*, are wind-pollinated (anemophilous), and several — again including both *Cannabis* and *Humulus* — are dioecious, with separate male and female individuals producing structurally distinct flowers. This combination of wind pollination and dioecy is a recurring pattern in plant lineages that have shifted away from insect pollination, since separating the sexes onto different plants removes any residual selective pressure to maintain showy, insect-attracting flowers on a wind-pollinated individual.

The family's most taxonomically informative internal relationship, for anyone approaching *Cannabis* biology, is the sister-genus pairing with *Humulus*. The two genera are each other's closest relatives within the family, share the wind-pollinated, petal-reduced, often-dioecious floral pattern just described, and — independently or from a shared ancestral predisposition — both evolved glandular trichome systems capable of synthesizing and externally storing complex lipophilic secondary metabolites: cannabinoids and terpenes in *Cannabis*, bitter acids (humulone and lupulone, the compounds responsible for beer's bitterness) and terpenes in *Humulus*. The compound classes diverged after the genera split, but the underlying secretory strategy — build the compound in specialized cells, then export it outside the plasma membrane into an extracellular storage structure rather than accumulating it in living cytoplasm — appears in both genera, which is suggestive (though not proof) that trichome-based extracellular secretion is an old capacity in this lineage rather than a *Cannabis*-specific novelty.

For the estate's compartmentalization research line, Cannabaceae is worth treating as more than backdrop to *Cannabis* itself: the family offers a natural comparative case for the trichome-storage strategy, since *Humulus* independently faces (and appears to solve) a similar problem of storing reactive, resinous secondary metabolites outside living cells. A family-level account of glandular trichome evolution across Cannabaceae — rather than a *Cannabis*-only account — would be the natural next step for testing whether the extracellular-storage solution is convergent within the family or a shared derived trait from a common secretory ancestor.
