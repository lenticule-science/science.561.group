# Cannabis: Trichomes, Chemotypes, and the Plant's Own Compartments

A prior body of estate work treats the endocannabinoid system as an animal
receptor problem: CB1/CB2 pharmacology, on-demand retrograde signaling,
allosteric modulation of a mammalian G-protein-coupled receptor. That is a
legitimate and separate subject. This note is about the organism that
happens to make ligands for that receptor by evolutionary accident,
considered on its own terms — as a wind-pollinated annual in the hemp
family with an unusual and genuinely interesting solution to a chemical
storage problem. The throughline is compartmentalization: not as metaphor,
but as the literal, physical fact that *Cannabis sativa* synthesizes a
class of molecules cytotoxic enough to its own cells that it exports them
outside the plasma membrane before they accumulate, and stores them there,
outside living cytoplasm, in an extracellular cavity built for the
purpose.

## 1. The plant

*Cannabis sativa* L. is the sole cultivated genus-representative in
general use, in the family Cannabaceae (order Rosales), a family of ten
genera and roughly 170 species whose most taxonomically informative member
outside *Cannabis* itself is *Humulus* — hops. The two genera are sister
lineages, share obvious floral architecture (small, petal-reduced, wind-
pollinated flowers arranged in ways that trade insect-attracting showiness
for pollen dispersal by air), and — this is the point worth underlining —
both independently evolved glandular trichome systems that manufacture and
externally store lipophilic secondary metabolites: cannabinoids and
terpenes in *Cannabis*, bitter acids (humulone, lupulone) and terpenes in
*Humulus*. The shared trichome-based secretory strategy across the two
genera is consistent with it being an old feature of the lineage rather
than a *Cannabis*-specific novelty, though the compound classes stored
diverged after the genera split.

*Cannabis* is an annual, herbaceous, wind-pollinated plant, typically
**dioecious** (individual plants are male or female) with well-documented
monoecious variants that carry both sexes on one individual, sometimes
induced by stress or manipulated for breeding (feminized seed production
relies on inducing a genetically female plant to produce male flowers).
Sex is chromosomally determined by an **X/Y system**: females are
homogametic (XX), males heterogametic (XY), with 18 autosomes plus the
sex-chromosome pair. The Y chromosome is heterochromatic on one arm; the X
is the largest chromosome in the genome and carries a large,
non-recombining region — on the order of 70 megabases — that is one of the
oldest documented sex-determining regions in flowering plants. Multiple
MADS-box floral-identity gene homologs have been proposed as candidate sex
determinants, but the size and repetitive structure of the region has kept
the exact causal genes unresolved as of recent literature.

### The indica/sativa problem

This is worth stating carefully because it is one of the most confidently
believed false things about the plant, repeated by dispensary staff,
breeders, and casual users as though it were settled botany. The formal
taxonomic question — is *Cannabis* one polytypic species (*C. sativa*,
with subspecies) or multiple species (*C. sativa*, *C. indica*, *C.
ruderalis*) — has been argued since Linnaeus (1753, one species) and
Lamarck (1785, a second species, *C. indica*, based on intoxicating
effect and growth habit). John McPartland's taxonomic reviews trace how
the *vernacular* indica/sativa distinction used in the cannabis market
today diverged from and scrambled the original scientific usage: he
argues that what the market calls "Sativa" corresponds botanically closer
to what Lamarck called *indica*, what the market calls "Indica" is closer
to a narrower *afghanica* type, and what the market calls "Ruderalis" is
often closer to true *sativa* — i.e., the popular labels and the
historical scientific names do not point at the same plants. Genomic
population-structure work (see §5) finds that cultivated plants form a
messy, admixed continuum shaped by trait-based selection and repeated
hybridization rather than two (or three) clean, monophyletic clusters
corresponding to the vernacular categories. The practical upshot: the
indica/sativa split as used in retail — "indica for body/sedative
effects, sativa for head/energetic effects" — has **little to no
demonstrated chemotaxonomic support**; cannabinoid and terpene profiles do
not sort cleanly along that axis, and genetic ancestry does not track
plant morphology or vernacular labeling reliably. Growth habit
differences (indica-type: shorter, broader-leaved, more branched; sativa-
type: taller, narrower-leaved) have somewhat more basis as morphotypes
selected under different climates, but even morphotype and
chemotype/genotype correlate only loosely, and the effect labels applied
in retail (indica = "couch-lock," sativa = "energetic") reflect market
narrative built on top of an already-blurred taxonomic foundation, not a
demonstrated pharmacological or chemical mapping.

## 2. Trichomes as compartments

Cannabis develops several trichome morphotypes, and getting the
terminology right matters because these are not simply "hairs" in the
generic epidermal sense but purpose-built, multicellular secretory
organs.

**Non-glandular (cystolithic) trichomes** occur on leaves and stems and
are mostly structural/defensive in the ordinary trichome sense — physical
deterrence, reduced water loss — and are not cannabinoid-producing.

**Glandular trichomes** come in three recognized types, distinguished
mainly by stalk length and gland-head size:

- **Bulbous trichomes** — the smallest, a single tier of secretory cells
  under a thin cuticle, found across the epidermis of vegetative and
  floral tissue, with modest cannabinoid/terpene content per trichome.
- **Capitate-sessile trichomes** — larger glandular heads sitting nearly
  flush on the epidermis (a very short stalk), abundant on leaf surfaces
  and bracts, more productive than bulbous types.
- **Capitate-stalked trichomes** — the largest and, on floral tissue
  (bracts and the calyx surrounding the ovule), the dominant contributor
  to total cannabinoid and terpene yield; these have an elevated
  multicellular stalk supporting a globose glandular head.

The glandular head, in both sessile and stalked capitate types, is built
from a **secretory disc** of eight to sixteen cells (radially arranged,
often described as a "cellular rosette") that sit beneath the cuticle.
The disc cells are metabolically the business end of the structure — this
is where cannabinoid and terpenoid biosynthetic enzymes are expressed and
where synthesis actually happens — but the cuticle over the disc lifts
away from the cell wall during trichome maturation, a process of
wall/cuticle delamination that opens a **subcuticular storage cavity**: an
extracellular, apoplastic space bounded on one side by the disc cells'
outer wall and on the other by the detached cuticle. Newer ultrastructural
work (Livingston et al. 2020, *Plant Journal*; a 2021 *Plant and Cell
Physiology* study on trichome cell-wall remodeling; and single-cell
multiomic work extending the picture in 2024–2026) describes the mature
disc as behaving like a coordinated multicellular unit — cells connected
through cytoplasmic bridges via wall perforations, described in recent
work as a polarized secretory "supercell" — that synthesizes lipophilic
cannabinoids and terpenes, ferries them across the plasma membrane and
outward through the cell wall (partly via secretory vesicles, partly by
direct diffusion through a remodeled, permeable wall), and deposits them
into the subcuticular cavity as an oily resin.

**This is the compartmentalization fact worth dwelling on.** Cannabinoids
are synthesized inside living cells but are not *stored* inside living
cells. Sirikantaramas et al. (2005, *Plant and Cell Physiology*)
established this directly for THCA synthase: the enzyme itself is
secreted out of the disc cells and functions in the storage cavity, and
cannabinoids demonstrably exert a cytotoxic effect on cannabis's own
cells — reported mitochondrial disruption among the mechanisms — meaning
sequestration in an extracellular oil droplet is plausibly read as an
**autotoxicity-avoidance mechanism**: the plant is not simply excreting a
waste product, it appears to be routing around a molecule that would
damage the very cells that make it if it accumulated inside them. That
the biosynthetic terminal enzyme (THCAS) and the final product both end
up outside the plasma membrane, in a cavity that is topologically outside
the cell despite being physically enclosed and hidden under the cuticle,
is a clean illustration of compartmentalization doing real protective
work rather than being an incidental packaging detail.

**Trichome development** proceeds through discrete stages
(often described, following light and electron microscopy staging
schemes, as pre-secretory initiation, active secretory-cavity expansion,
and post-secretory senescence), with cannabinoid content and ratio of
cannabinoids to terpenes shifting measurably across flower maturation —
i.e., a given trichome's chemical output is not fixed at initiation but
changes as the gland ages, a point demonstrated directly by Livingston et
al. (2020). Trichome **density** is strongly biased toward reproductive
tissue: capitate-stalked trichome density on bracts and calyxes vastly
exceeds density on leaves, concentrating the resin where flower/seed
tissue is most vulnerable and most valuable reproductively.

**Why does the plant do this — herbivore defense, UV protection,
antimicrobial action?** The honest state of the evidence differs sharply
by claim:

- **Herbivore defense** has the most direct experimental support.
  Studies correlating foliar cannabinoid concentration with reduced
  chewing-herbivore damage, and demonstrating that herbivore larvae eat
  less leaf tissue and grow more slowly on higher-cannabinoid foliage
  (a 2023 study explicitly framed as testing "cannabinoids function in
  defense against chewing herbivores"), give this the strongest
  causal footing of the three hypotheses, though the literature base is
  still thin compared to well-studied model-plant defense systems.
- **UV protection** is plausible and supported by correlative and some
  mechanistic evidence — UV-B exposure measurably modulates trichome
  density and cannabinoid/terpene output, and *in vitro* work shows CBD
  can protect cultured cells from UV-B damage — but the case that this is
  a primary evolved *function* of trichome resin, as opposed to a
  convenient secondary property of a compound evolved for something else,
  is not settled. Positioning of dense trichome cover directly around
  reproductive structures is often read as support for a UV/ovule-
  protection role, but this reading is inferential rather than
  demonstrated by manipulation experiments isolating UV as the selective
  pressure.
- **Antimicrobial function** is the weakest-supported of the three in
  terms of ecological/whole-plant evidence. Cannabinoids show antimicrobial
  activity in vitro against various bacteria and fungi (a long-standing and
  frequently cited finding), but *in vitro* antimicrobial activity against
  cultured pathogens is not the same evidence as demonstrating that
  trichome resin actually suppresses pathogen load on a living plant in
  a way that matters for fitness; the ecological/field-level case remains
  largely asserted rather than tested.

In short: the "textbook" three-part defensive-function story
(herbivore/UV/microbe) is repeated with roughly equal confidence in
popular and even some academic secondary literature, but the underlying
evidence is not equally strong across the three claims — a point worth
stating plainly rather than passing along the flattened version.

## 3. Biosynthesis: acids, not neutrals

Cannabinoid biosynthesis begins from two convergent precursor pools:

1. A polyketide branch producing **olivetolic acid** from **hexanoyl-CoA**
   (itself derived from fatty-acid metabolism) plus three malonyl-CoA
   units, condensed by a type III polyketide synthase historically called
   **olivetol synthase (OLS)**, and specifically cyclized by **olivetolic
   acid cyclase (OAC)** — Gagne et al. (2012, *PNAS*) identified OAC and
   showed it performs an otherwise-unusual C2–C7 intramolecular aldol
   condensation that correctly cyclizes the polyketide intermediate with
   carboxylate retention, a step OLS alone cannot perform cleanly.
2. An isoprenoid branch producing **geranyl pyrophosphate (GPP)** via the
   plastidial **MEP (methylerythritol phosphate) pathway** — the dominant
   route in most plant terpenoid biosynthesis for plastid-localized
   isoprenoids — with the cytosolic **mevalonate (MVA) pathway**
   contributing to the broader isoprenoid precursor pool as well. Both
   pathways are active in the trichome disc cells, and terpene synthases
   (a substantial gene family in *Cannabis*, functionally characterized by
   Booth et al. 2017 among others) draw on this same GPP/FPP pool that
   feeds cannabinoid prenylation — meaning cannabinoid and terpene
   biosynthesis are not just co-localized in the same cell type but
   partly share upstream metabolic substrate.

A prenyltransferase (CsPT4, identified in later work building on the
Keasling lab's yeast-reconstitution efforts) joins olivetolic acid and GPP
to form **cannabigerolic acid (CBGA)** — the so-called "mother
cannabinoid," because essentially every other cannabinoid the plant makes
in quantity branches from this one compound. Three oxidocyclase enzymes,
each converting CBGA into a different cyclized acid product, define the
major chemotype-determining branches:

- **THCA synthase (THCAS)** → tetrahydrocannabinolic acid (THCA)
- **CBDA synthase (CBDAS)** → cannabidiolic acid (CBDA)
- **CBCA synthase (CBCAS)** → cannabichromenic acid (CBCA), typically a
  minor product

All three are members of the berberine-bridge-enzyme-like (BBE-like) gene
family; THCAS and CBCAS share roughly 92% amino-acid identity, consistent
with relatively recent gene duplication rather than deeply separate
evolutionary origins.

**The plant makes THCA. It does not make THC.** This is a distinction
frequently collapsed in casual and even some semi-technical writing, and
it should not be, because the mechanism is entirely different in each
case: THCA is the enzymatically produced, biologically stable,
non-intoxicating carboxylic-acid form the plant actually manufactures and
stores in trichome resin. **Decarboxylation** — loss of the carboxyl
group as CO2, converting THCA to Δ9-THC — occurs post-harvest, driven by
heat and/or extended time, and is a **human intervention** (drying,
curing, smoking, vaporizing, baking) rather than a plant metabolic
product. Freshly harvested, undried cannabis flower is overwhelmingly
THCA, with only trace non-enzymatic THC from ambient heat/light exposure;
substantial THC content is an artifact of processing, not of the living
plant's biochemistry. The same logic applies to CBDA → CBD.

## 4. Chemotype genetics: one locus doing a lot of work

De Meijer et al. (2003, *Genetics*) established the model still in use as
the starting reference: crossing pure-CBDA and pure-THCA inbred lines
produced F1 plants with mixed CBDA/THCA chemotype, consistent with a
single locus, **B**, with two co-dominant alleles, **B_D** (associated
with CBDA-synthase activity) and **B_T** (associated with THCA-synthase
activity). Genotype B_D/B_D gives a CBDA-dominant plant, B_T/B_T gives a
THCA-dominant plant, and the heterozygote B_D/B_T gives a mixed
chemotype — a strikingly simple, largely Mendelian, single-locus
architecture for what the market treats as a fundamental category
distinction ("hemp" vs. "drug-type" cannabis by chemotype). De Meijer and
colleagues extended the model in follow-up papers describing a
CBGA-dominant chemotype (associated with non-functional or absent
THCAS/CBDAS activity, leaving the CBGA precursor unconverted) and a
cannabinoid-free chemotype. The conventional five-chemotype scheme is:

- **Chemotype I** — THCA-dominant ("drug-type")
- **Chemotype II** — mixed THCA/CBDA
- **Chemotype III** — CBDA-dominant ("hemp-type," in the modern regulatory
  sense of low-THC fiber/seed cultivars)
- **Chemotype IV** — CBGA-dominant (rare; the precursor accumulates
  because downstream conversion is blocked or minimal)
- **Chemotype V** — near-cannabinoid-free

**What modern genomic work has revised**: the tidy single-gene-pair
picture undersells the actual molecular architecture. Grassa et al.
(2018/2019, *Genome Research*) built a physical and genetic map showing
that THCAS and CBDAS sit inside large (>250 kb), retrotransposon-rich,
highly non-homologous regions between drug-type and hemp-type
haplotypes, embedded within tens of megabases of repetitive,
non-recombining DNA — meaning the "B locus" is not one clean gene pair but
a structurally divergent genomic region containing **tandem arrays** of
THCAS- and CBDAS-like sequences, including apparent pseudogenes and
copy-number variation, that happen to behave, at the level of inherited
phenotype, as though they were a single co-dominant locus because they
essentially never recombine. Subsequent work on the CBCAS gene family
(2021) and broader cannabinoid-synthase gene-family evolution studies
(2022–2025, including pangenome-scale work published in *Nature* in 2025)
has continued to find this pattern of duplicated, tandemly arrayed,
partially degenerated synthase paralogs rather than three cleanly
separate single-copy genes — the practical chemotype categories survive
as a useful shorthand, but the genomic reality generating them is
messier, and still being actively resolved, than the 2003 two-allele
model implies.

## 5. Domestication and genomics

Ren et al. (2021, *Science Advances*), using whole-genome resequencing of
110 accessions spanning feral plants, landraces, historical cultivars, and
modern hybrid drug- and hemp-type lines, argue for a **single
domestication origin in East Asia**, with all sampled hemp- and drug-type
lineages diverging from an ancestral gene pool best represented today by
feral plants and landraces in China. Their dating places the split of
early domesticated ancestors from a **"basal cannabis"** ancestral
population at roughly 12,000 years ago (early Neolithic), with the
functional divergence into distinct hemp-selected (fiber/seed) and
drug-selected (cannabinoid-rich) lineages occurring substantially later —
their estimate is on the order of 4,000 years ago, meaning early
domesticated cannabis appears to have been used for several thousand
years as a general-purpose, multi-use crop before selection pressures
split fiber/seed and drug lineages apart. This is broadly consistent with,
though not identical in every detail to, archaeobotanical evidence: seed
and phytolith finds place *Cannabis* cultivation or use in the Yellow
River region and broader northern China from roughly 10,000–8,000 years
before present, with additional phytolith evidence extending the crop's
presence in the prehistoric Shandong crop assemblage into the Late
Neolithic/Early Bronze Age (roughly 4500–3400 BP); some archaeobotanical
readings (e.g., Long et al. 2017) have argued for more than one
domestication center (Europe and Asia) based on pollen/seed/fiber
evidence, so the genomic single-origin conclusion is influential but not
universally uncontested against the archaeobotanical record.

Direct evidence of **ritual use** — as opposed to inferred cultivation — is
the more visceral finding: Ren et al. (2019, *Science Advances*, a
different lead-author paper sharing name only) analyzed ten wooden
braziers containing fire-exposed stones from burials at the **Jirzankal
Cemetery**, a site in the eastern Pamir plateau in far-western China,
radiocarbon-dated to roughly 2,500 years before present (circa 500 BCE).
Chemical residue analysis of the burnt stones and brazier interiors
detected cannabinol (CBN, the oxidative-degradation product of THC formed
on aging/combustion) at levels consistent with the plants burned having
notably higher THC content than typical wild-type hemp — direct chemical
evidence that this specific population was deliberately burning
cannabis with above-average psychoactive potential as part of mortuary
ritual, not merely growing hemp for fiber.

**Hemp as a fiber/seed crop** sits under a completely different selection
regime from drug-type cultivation: tall, minimally branched growth habit
(competing plants grown densely for straight bast-fiber stalks), early
flowering, low cannabinoid content (often bred deliberately toward
Chemotype III or regulatory THC thresholds), and seed-yield/oil traits —
a divergent-selection story that genomic work (Ren et al. 2021 again)
reads as a relatively late split from the shared ancestral gene pool
rather than a separate domestication event.

**Modern genetic narrowing**: prohibition-era cultivation is widely
credited (in genomic-survey literature reviewing the point, e.g. work
summarized around Vergara and Kane's studies and subsequent GWAS/pangenome
papers) with having compressed drug-type cannabis genetic diversity —
decades of clandestine breeding under selection almost exclusively for
THC content and covert-cultivation traits, with limited outcrossing and
heavy reliance on a comparatively small number of founder lineages
("Skunk," "Haze," "Kush" lines recur repeatedly as ancestral hubs in
population-structure analyses), produced a genetically narrower,
more homogeneous drug-type gene pool than the historical landrace
diversity the crop once had. On top of that narrowing sits a second,
more embarrassing problem: **cultivar-name unreliability**. Vergara and
Kane's genetic-tools work (2017 preprint; 2019, *Journal of Cannabis
Research*) genotyped commercially available "strains" and found cases
where plants sold under different strain names were genetically
indistinguishable or near-identical (their reported example: "Larry OG"
and "Tahoe OG" samples with matching genotypes), alongside cases of
probable simple mislabeling (e.g., "Chemdog 1" for the historically
described "Chemdawg 1"). Follow-up work (2021, on governmental versus
commercial cannabis genetics) extended the concern to regulatory/research
supply chains, finding that federally or governmentally produced research
cannabis in some cases poorly represents the genetic diversity actually
present in commercial markets. The practical implication across this
body of work is blunt: a cannabis strain name is a marketing label with
weak and inconsistent correspondence to a plant's actual genotype, and
research or purchasing decisions that treat strain names as meaningful
taxonomic or chemotype identifiers are relying on a naming system that
has been repeatedly shown not to track what it claims to track.

## 6. The "entourage" question at the plant level

Whole *Cannabis* flower is chemically complex — dozens of cannabinoids at
varying concentrations (THCA, CBDA, CBGA, CBCA, and minor cannabinoids)
plus a large terpene/terpenoid profile (commonly dominated by
myrcene, limonene, pinene, caryophyllene, humulene, linalool, and others,
varying substantially by cultivar and even by trichome-developmental
stage) plus flavonoids and other minor secondary metabolites. **That
whole-plant chemical complexity is real, measurable, and varies
substantially across cultivars, growing conditions, and harvest timing is
well supported** — this is straightforward analytical chemistry, not a
contested claim.

What is far less well supported is the specific, popularized
"entourage effect" claim that this chemical complexity produces
predictable, synergistic *human* pharmacological or therapeutic effects
beyond what isolated cannabinoids alone would produce — i.e., that
specific terpene-cannabinoid combinations reliably modulate the human
experience of cannabis in the ways marketing materials and some review
articles assert (terpene X "adds" a calming effect, terpene Y "boosts"
euphoria, etc.). Preclinical work gives mixed and partial support: a 2021
*Scientific Reports* study reported that several cannabis terpenes
(α-humulene, geraniol, linalool, β-pinene) produced cannabimimetic
behavioral effects in mice and were selectively additive with a
cannabinoid receptor agonist — real evidence that at least some terpenes
can interact with the cannabinoid signaling system in an animal model, at
doses used in that study. But a 2024 systematic review characterized the
overall evidence as offering only exploratory support for terpenes
modulating cannabinoid activity, explicitly concluding that terpenes'
capacity to meaningfully boost cannabinoid efficacy in humans "hasn't yet
been demonstrated" and calling for further clinical trials; other
reviews describe much of the supporting literature for human synergy as
preclinical, in vitro, or anecdotal, with rigorous human clinical data
still limited as of the most recent (2024–2025) reviews available. The
honest summary: **whole-plant chemical complexity is not in dispute; a
mechanistic, dose-general human "entourage" effect from specific
terpene-cannabinoid pairings is asserted with far more confidence in
popular and industry literature than the clinical evidence currently
supports.**

## Hypotheses

1. **Trichome-cavity rupture is acutely cytotoxic to disc cells.**
   (i) *Prediction*: mechanically or chemically inducing premature release
   of subcuticular cavity contents onto the underlying disc cells (rather
   than into the extracellular cavity) will produce measurable disc-cell
   damage (membrane permeability increase, mitochondrial dysfunction
   markers) within a short exposure window, consistent with the
   autotoxicity model implied by Sirikantaramas et al. (2005).
   (ii) *Test*: in a controlled trichome-explant or isolated-disc-cell
   assay, compare cell viability (e.g., propidium iodide exclusion,
   mitochondrial membrane potential dye) between trichomes with an intact
   subcuticular cavity versus trichomes where the cuticle has been
   punctured/collapsed to re-expose disc cells to native-concentration
   THCA/CBDA resin, controlling for mechanical damage alone using an inert
   puncture control.
   (iii) *Kill condition*: if disc-cell viability is statistically
   indistinguishable between cavity-intact and cavity-ruptured
   (resin-re-exposed) trichomes relative to the puncture-only mechanical
   control, the autotoxicity-avoidance interpretation of the storage
   cavity is not supported by this assay and should be revised toward a
   purely export/transport-convenience explanation instead.

2. **Chemotype-locus "cleanliness" predicts recombination suppression, not gene number.**
   (i) *Prediction*: across wild/feral, landrace, and modern cultivar
   populations, the apparent single-locus (B_D/B_T) co-dominant
   inheritance pattern will hold more reliably in populations with lower
   effective recombination at the THCAS/CBDAS genomic region (as measured
   by linkage disequilibrium decay) than in populations where
   recombination within that region is less suppressed, even though the
   underlying tandem-array gene content is similar across populations.
   (ii) *Test*: genotype THCAS/CBDAS haplotype structure and measure local
   LD decay across the >250 kb region in several genetically distinct
   population groups (e.g., feral Chinese landraces vs. modern
   commercial hybrids), and cross-reference against observed F2/backcross
   segregation ratios for chemotype in controlled crosses from each
   population.
   (iii) *Kill condition*: if simple co-dominant single-locus segregation
   ratios are observed equally well in populations with markedly different
   local recombination rates at the region, recombination suppression is
   not the operative explanation for the discrete-appearing inheritance
   pattern, and some other mechanism (e.g., strong linkage via physical
   proximity alone, without unusual suppression) should be investigated
   instead.

3. **The 12,000-year single-origin domestication signature should be visible as a bottleneck, not just a coalescence date.**
   (i) *Prediction*: if Ren et al.'s (2021) single East Asian domestication
   origin around 12,000 years ago is correct, genome-wide diversity
   statistics (e.g., nucleotide diversity π, Tajima's D) in domesticated
   hemp- and drug-type lineages should show a detectable population
   bottleneck signature dated consistent with that divergence, distinct
   from the basal/feral Chinese population, in addition to the
   phylogenetic coalescence pattern already reported.
   (ii) *Test*: apply bottleneck-detection methods (e.g., PSMC or similar
   coalescent-based effective population size reconstruction) to the
   existing or an expanded whole-genome resequencing dataset, comparing
   basal/feral Chinese accessions against hemp-type and drug-type
   lineages, and check whether the inferred bottleneck timing clusters
   near 12,000 BP (domestication) and again near ~4,000 BP (hemp/drug
   split) as the two-stage model predicts.
   (iii) *Kill condition*: if no detectable bottleneck signature
   distinguishes domesticated lineages from the basal/feral population at
   either proposed timepoint, or if a bottleneck is found but dates
   substantially earlier or later than both proposed divergence times, the
   single-origin/two-stage domestication timeline is not supported by
   population-genetic bottleneck evidence and should be revised or treated
   as reflecting bulk phylogenetic relatedness alone rather than an actual
   demographic domestication event at either date.

4. **Strain-name genetic unreliability scales with market distance from breeder.**
   (i) *Prediction*: genetic identity between same-named cannabis
   accessions will be higher (more consistent) among samples obtained
   directly from a single named breeder/seed source than among
   same-named samples collected across independent retail dispensaries or
   secondary markets, because label drift accumulates with each
   uncontrolled propagation/reselling step.
   (ii) *Test*: collect multiple samples of several identically-named
   cultivars, stratified by supply-chain distance (direct-from-breeder vs.
   single-dispensary-sourced vs. multi-dispensary/secondary-market-sourced),
   genotype with a SNP panel or whole-genome sequencing as in Vergara and
   Kane's protocol, and compare within-name genetic distance across the
   three supply-chain strata.
   (iii) *Kill condition*: if within-name genetic distance is statistically
   indistinguishable across supply-chain strata (i.e., direct-from-breeder
   samples are just as internally inconsistent as multi-market samples),
   the "label drift accumulates with distance from origin" explanation is
   not supported, and strain-name unreliability should instead be
   attributed to a cause present already at the breeder/origin level
   (e.g., seed-line instability, deliberate renaming) rather than to
   downstream propagation and resale.

5. **Terpene co-elevation with cannabinoid content is a shared-precursor artifact, not independent selection.**
   (i) *Prediction*: because cannabinoid prenylation (CBGA formation) and
   monoterpene synthesis draw on an overlapping GPP pool from the MEP
   pathway in the same trichome disc cells, cultivars bred for higher
   total cannabinoid content will show correlated (not independent)
   increases in total monoterpene content, even absent direct selection
   on terpene profile, because both draw down the same upstream precursor
   supply and cells with higher overall secretory/biosynthetic capacity
   produce more of both classes.
   (ii) *Test*: across a panel of cultivars/accessions with documented
   breeding history (some selected explicitly for cannabinoid content,
   others not), measure total cannabinoid and total monoterpene
   concentration per gram of trichome resin, and test whether the
   correlation between the two holds even after statistically controlling
   for trichome density (to rule out "more trichomes make more of
   everything" as a trivial confound).
   (iii) *Kill condition*: if total cannabinoid and total monoterpene
   content show no significant correlation once trichome density is
   controlled for, or if specific terpenes vary independently of
   cannabinoid content across cultivars with similar trichome density, the
   shared-precursor-pool explanation for co-elevated chemical complexity is
   not supported, and terpene profile differences should be attributed
   primarily to independent terpene-synthase-gene selection rather than to
   upstream metabolic-flux coupling.
