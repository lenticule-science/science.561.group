How do you measure the distance to something you cannot visit? You use a ladder.

## The problem

Distance is the most fundamental measurement in cosmology, and the hardest to make. You cannot stretch a tape measure to a galaxy. You cannot bounce a radar signal off something billions of light-years away. Every technique for measuring cosmic distances relies on indirect methods, each with its own assumptions and limitations.

## Rung 1: Parallax

The first rung is geometry. As Earth orbits the Sun, nearby [stars](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/star.md) appear to shift slightly against the background of distant stars. This apparent shift is [parallax](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/parallax.md). The parallax angle gives the distance directly, by trigonometry, with no assumptions about the star's properties.

Parallax is the gold standard -- pure geometry -- but it only works for nearby stars. Even with the Gaia satellite's milliarcsecond precision, parallax reaches only a few kiloparsecs. To go further, you need the next rung.

## Rung 2: Cepheid variables

Cepheid variable stars pulsate, and their pulsation period is correlated with their intrinsic luminosity (the Leavitt law, discovered by Henrietta Leavitt in 1912). Measure the period, infer the luminosity, compare to observed brightness, calculate the distance. Cepheids are bright enough to see in nearby galaxies, extending the ladder to about 30 megaparsecs.

## Rung 3: Type Ia supernovae

[Type Ia supernovae](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/type-ia-supernova.md) are thermonuclear explosions of white dwarf [stars](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/star.md) with nearly uniform peak luminosity. After correction for the light curve shape, they are precise [standard candles](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/standard-candle.md) visible across billions of light-years. This is the rung that revealed the [accelerating expansion](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/accelerating-expansion.md).

## Rung 4: The Hubble flow

At the greatest distances, [redshift](../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/redshift.md) itself becomes a distance proxy. If you know the [Hubble parameter](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/hubble-parameter.md) $H_0$, then a galaxy's redshift gives its distance via Hubble's law (with corrections for the expansion history).

## The Hubble tension

Each rung calibrates the next, so errors propagate upward. The local distance ladder (parallax → Cepheids → supernovae) gives $H_0 \approx 73$ km/s/Mpc. The [cosmic microwave background](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/cosmic-microwave-background.md), analyzed independently using the [Lambda-CDM model](../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/lambda-cdm-model.md), gives $H_0 \approx 67$ km/s/Mpc. The disagreement is statistically significant (~5 sigma) and persistent. It may reflect a systematic error in one or both methods, or it may be a sign of physics beyond the standard model. This is the Hubble tension, and resolving it is one of the most potential problems in cosmology.

## Why this matters

Every number in cosmology -- the age of the universe, its size, the distances to galaxies, the expansion rate -- ultimately rests on the distance ladder. If the ladder is wrong, the numbers are wrong. Understanding how distances are measured, and where the measurements might fail, is essential to understanding how much we actually know.
