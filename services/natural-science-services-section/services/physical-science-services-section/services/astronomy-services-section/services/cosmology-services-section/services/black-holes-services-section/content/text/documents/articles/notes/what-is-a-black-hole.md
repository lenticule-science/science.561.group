Take a star and compress it. Make it smaller and smaller, denser and denser. At some point, the gravitational pull at the surface becomes so strong that [light](../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/light.md) itself cannot escape. You have made a [black hole](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/black-hole.md).

## The geometry

A black hole is not an object in the usual sense. It is a region of [spacetime](../../../../../../../../../../physics-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/spacetime.md) where [gravity](../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/gravity.md) -- or more precisely, the curvature of spacetime -- is so extreme that all paths lead inward. The boundary of this region is the [event horizon](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/event-horizon.md): the surface at which the escape velocity equals the speed of light.

For a non-rotating black hole, the event horizon is a sphere with the [Schwarzschild radius](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/schwarzschild-radius.md):

$$r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}$$

This is proportional to mass. A black hole with the mass of the Sun would have a Schwarzschild radius of about 3 kilometers. The supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way (Sagittarius A*, about 4 million solar masses) has a Schwarzschild radius of about 12 million kilometers -- roughly 17 times the radius of the Sun.

## What happens at the horizon

The event horizon is not a physical surface. There is no wall, no membrane, no material barrier. An observer falling through the horizon would notice nothing locally unusual -- spacetime looks flat in a sufficiently small region, even at the horizon. This is a consequence of the equivalence principle.

The horizon's significance is global: once inside, every future-directed path through spacetime leads to the [singularity](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/singularity.md) at the center. There is no trajectory, no amount of thrust, no signal fast enough to escape. This is not because something is pulling you back. It is because spacetime inside the horizon is structured so that "outward" is no longer a direction that leads to the future.

## The singularity

At the center of a black hole, [general relativity](../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/general-relativity.md) predicts a singularity: a point of infinite curvature and infinite density. This is almost universally understood as a breakdown of the theory, not a physical reality. At the extreme conditions near the singularity, quantum gravitational effects should dominate, and we have no complete theory of quantum gravity. What actually happens at the center of a black hole is an open question.

## How black holes form

Stellar-mass black holes form when massive [stars](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/star.md) (roughly 20+ solar masses) exhaust their nuclear fuel. Without fusion to support them, their cores collapse. If the remnant core exceeds about 3 solar masses (the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff limit), no known force can halt the collapse, and a black hole forms.

Supermassive black holes ($10^6$ to $10^{10}$ solar masses) reside at the centers of most [galaxies](../../../../../../../../../content/text/glossary/terms/galaxy.md). Their formation mechanism is not fully understood -- they may have grown from stellar-mass seeds through accretion and mergers, or they may have formed from the direct collapse of massive gas clouds in the early universe.

## Observational evidence

For decades, black holes were theoretical constructs. The observational evidence is now overwhelming:

- **X-ray binaries.** Matter from a companion star spiraling into a black hole heats up and emits X-rays.
- **Gravitational waves.** LIGO detected gravitational waves from merging black holes in 2015 -- the first direct detection of gravitational waves and of binary black hole systems.
- **The Event Horizon Telescope.** In 2019, the EHT produced the first image of a black hole's shadow: the supermassive black hole in the galaxy M87. In 2022, it imaged Sagittarius A*.
- **Stellar orbits.** Stars orbiting Sagittarius A* trace paths that reveal the central mass with precision (Genzel and Ghez, 2020 Nobel Prize).

## Why this matters

Black holes are where general relativity meets its limits. They are the simplest macroscopic objects in the universe (described entirely by mass, charge, and spin), yet they raise the deepest questions in physics: what happens to information that falls in? How does gravity behave at quantum scales? Black holes are not exotic curiosities at the edge of physics. They are the testing ground for our most fundamental theories.
