Redshift is the stretching of [light](./light.md) to longer wavelengths. In cosmology, the dominant cause is the expansion of space itself: as [light](./light.md) travels through an expanding universe, the space it traverses stretches, and the light's wavelength stretches with it. Longer wavelength means redder, hence "redshift."

The redshift $z$ of a source is defined by:

$$1 + z = \frac{\lambda_{\text{observed}}}{\lambda_{\text{emitted}}} = \frac{a_{\text{now}}}{a_{\text{emit}}} = \frac{1}{a_{\text{emit}}}$$

where $\lambda$ is wavelength and $a$ is the [scale factor](../domains/evolution/terms/scale-factor.md). A galaxy at $z = 1$ emitted its light when the universe was half its current size. The [cosmic microwave background](../domains/structure/terms/cosmic-microwave-background.md) has $z \approx 1100$.

Three types of redshift should be distinguished:

**Cosmological redshift.** Caused by the expansion of space. This is the dominant effect for distant objects and the one most relevant to cosmology.

**Doppler redshift (or blueshift).** Caused by relative motion between source and observer. A source moving away is redshifted; a source approaching is blueshifted. Familiar from the change in pitch of a passing siren.

**Gravitational redshift.** Caused by light climbing out of a gravitational potential well. A photon emitted near a massive object loses energy (increases wavelength) as it escapes. Predicted by [general relativity](./general-relativity.md) and confirmed experimentally.

In practice, an observed redshift is a combination of all three effects, but at cosmological distances the expansion term dominates. Redshift is the primary observable in cosmological surveys: it tells you how much the universe has expanded since the light was emitted, which (given a cosmological model) tells you the distance and the look-back time.
