The event horizon is the boundary of a [black hole](./black-hole.md) -- the surface beyond which events cannot affect an outside observer.
It is defined by the requirement that outgoing [light](../../../terms/light.md) rays at the horizon remain trapped: they can neither escape nor fall inward, but hover at the boundary forever.

The event horizon is not a physical surface.
There is no wall, no barrier, no material boundary.
An observer falling through it would notice nothing locally remarkable -- no sign, no sensation, no discontinuity.
This is a consequence of the equivalence principle: locally, [spacetime](../../../terms/spacetime.md) looks flat everywhere, including at the horizon.
The horizon's significance is global, not local.
Once you cross it, no path through spacetime leads back out, because all future-directed paths inside the horizon lead to the [singularity](./singularity.md).

For a non-rotating black hole, the event horizon is a sphere at the [Schwarzschild radius](./schwarzschild-radius.md).
For a rotating black hole (the Kerr solution), the horizon has a more complex structure, with an ergosphere outside the horizon where spacetime is dragged by the rotation.

The event horizon is central to black hole thermodynamics.
The [Bekenstein-Hawking entropy](./bekenstein-hawking-entropy.md) is proportional to the horizon's area, not the volume it encloses.
The [holographic principle](./holographic-principle.md) generalizes this observation: the maximum information content of any region of space is determined by its boundary area, not its volume.
