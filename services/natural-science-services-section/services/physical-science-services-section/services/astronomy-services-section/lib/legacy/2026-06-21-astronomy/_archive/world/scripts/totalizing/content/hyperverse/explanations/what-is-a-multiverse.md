# What is a multiverse?

A short orientation for readers approaching multiverse cosmology for the first time, or approaching it through this library's structural / predicated work and wanting the empirical context first.

## The core move

Standard cosmology speaks of *the universe* — a singular noun, all there is, by construction. Multiverse cosmology refuses the singular: it treats *universe* as a count noun and asks how many there are, what they have in common, and how they relate. The shift in vocabulary is the whole move; once made, the questions multiply.

The shift is not new — Giordano Bruno argued for an infinity of worlds in the 16th century — but the modern resurgence is driven by three independent developments:

1. **[Eternal inflation](../terms/eternal-inflation.md)**, proposed in the 1980s, is a relatively conservative inference from inflationary cosmology that produces a population of post-inflationary bubble [worlds](../terms/world.md) continually nucleating from an inflating background.
2. **The string-theory landscape** estimates that the equations of string theory admit on the order of $10^{500}$ stable solutions ("vacua"), each corresponding to a world with different effective physical constants. If string theory describes nature, these vacua need to be populated somehow; the natural candidate is the bubbles produced by eternal inflation.
3. **Everettian quantum mechanics** ("many-worlds") interprets the formalism of quantum mechanics literally: every measurement is a branching of the universal wavefunction, and each branch is its own world. This is not a new physical postulate but a re-reading of standard quantum mechanics.

The [Tegmark classification](../terms/tegmark-classification.md) groups these and other proposals into four levels.

## Why a cosmologist might care

Three reasons that do not require committing to any specific multiverse:

1. **It clarifies what is contingent in our cosmology.** Once one allows that the constants of nature could have been different elsewhere, one is forced to ask which of our cosmological observations would change if they were, and why they have the values they do. This sharpens the [fine-tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine-tuned_universe) discussion regardless of whether multiverses are real.

2. **It forces the boundary of cosmology to be drawn explicitly.** What part of the physical state is "the universe doing physics" versus "boundary / initial conditions the universe happened to draw"? Multiverse thinking makes the question unavoidable.

3. **It exposes inter-world structure as a research object.** If there are many worlds, they may stand in mathematical or physical relations to each other — a structure that is invisible from inside any one of them. The predicated-multiverse research subtheory in this library is one attempt to study that structure formally.

## Why a cosmologist might be skeptical

Equally important to record honestly. The strongest objection: most multiverse proposals are constructed so that their predicted worlds are causally disconnected from ours, hence in principle unobservable. A theory that places its content beyond observation is — at minimum — outside the standard scientific norm of falsifiability. Ellis & Silk (*Nature* 2014) argued vigorously that taking multiverse cosmology as established science erodes that norm.

The defense: the multiverse hypothesis is not posited directly; it is *inferred* from theories (inflation, string theory, quantum mechanics) that *are* testable. If those theories are confirmed and they predict a multiverse, the multiverse is a derived prediction whose individual instances may be unobservable but whose existence is empirically supported. Whether this defense succeeds is contested.
