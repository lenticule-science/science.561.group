The horizon problem is the puzzle that the [cosmic microwave background](../../structure/terms/cosmic-microwave-background.md) is uniform in temperature to one part in $10^5$ across the entire observable sky, even though regions of the sky separated by more than about one degree could not have been in causal contact at the moment of [recombination](./recombination.md).

The argument is direct. The CMB photons reaching us from opposite sides of the sky were emitted at the same cosmic time, $t \approx 380{,}000$ years after the [Big Bang](./big-bang.md), but from regions that were already separated by many times the [particle horizon](../../observation/terms/particle-horizon.md) at that time. Without an early phase of accelerated expansion, no signal could have traveled between those regions to equilibrate them. Yet they have the same temperature, the same spectral shape, and the same statistical properties. Why?

The standard cosmological model, before [cosmic inflation](./cosmic-inflation.md), had no answer. The uniformity had to be put in by hand — assumed as an initial condition, with no dynamical justification. This was unsatisfying for two reasons: it required a finely tuned initial condition (unconnected regions all happened to be at the same temperature), and it had no causal mechanism (the equilibration could not have happened by any process consistent with light-speed propagation).

Inflation resolves the horizon problem by postulating an early phase, before cosmic time $\sim 10^{-32}$ s, in which the universe expanded by a factor of at least $\sim 10^{26}$ in a tiny fraction of a second. Before inflation, all the regions we now see in the CMB were within a single causally connected patch — small enough to equilibrate by ordinary thermal contact. Inflation then stretched that patch to enormous size, distributing its uniform temperature across what later became the entire observable universe. The horizon problem becomes a non-problem: the regions that look unconnected today were connected before inflation.

The horizon problem is one of the strongest observational motivations for inflation, alongside the [flatness problem](./flatness-problem.md) and the absence of magnetic monopoles. It is not a logical proof — alternative explanations are possible — but it is the empirical phenomenon that inflation was designed to address, and it remains the cleanest statement of why an inflationary phase, or something with similar causal-stretching properties, is needed.
