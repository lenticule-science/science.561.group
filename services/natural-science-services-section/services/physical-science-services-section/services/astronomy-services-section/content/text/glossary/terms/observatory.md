An **observatory** is a facility that hosts astronomical instruments. Modern major observatories are large engineering and operational organizations: a flagship ground-based observatory (Mauna Kea, Cerro Paranal, La Palma, the Atacama high desert) houses multiple telescopes plus support infrastructure (instruments, control rooms, data pipelines, accommodation, fibre links to data centres). A space-based observatory (Hubble, JWST, Chandra, Athena) is a single satellite, instrumented with one telescope and several focal-plane cameras and spectrographs, operated remotely from a mission control centre.

Site selection for ground-based observatories is dominated by atmospheric quality: low water vapour (transparent infrared windows), low atmospheric turbulence (good seeing, supporting adaptive optics), high fraction of clear nights, low light pollution, and dry climate. The world's best optical sites — Mauna Kea, the Atacama, La Palma, the South Pole — share these properties. Radio observatories add a further constraint: low radio-frequency interference, motivating remote sites and protected radio quiet zones (Green Bank, the SKA core sites in South Africa and Western Australia).

Observatories are also social and political institutions. Telescope time at a flagship facility is a scarce, peer-reviewed, internationally allocated resource; large surveys are multi-decade collaborations involving hundreds to thousands of astronomers; the construction of a new flagship (TMT, GMT, ELT) involves international funding agreements, host-country negotiations, and increasingly, negotiations with Indigenous communities whose land and cultural heritage are bound up with the proposed sites (the Mauna Kea TMT controversy is the paradigmatic example).
