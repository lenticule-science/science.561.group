**Spectroscopy** is the measurement of the distribution of an object's light over wavelength. Where [photometry](photometry.md) integrates flux within a filter to a single number, spectroscopy resolves the spectrum into many wavelength bins, exposing emission and absorption lines, the continuum shape, and Doppler shifts.

Astronomical spectra carry an enormous amount of structural information. Absorption-line patterns identify chemical species (Fraunhofer's solar lines, the Balmer series in stars, the Lyman-alpha forest in quasar absorption); line strengths constrain temperature, pressure, and abundance; line widths constrain rotational and turbulent velocities; line profiles constrain stellar atmospheres and accretion geometries. The Doppler shift of a known line is the principal extragalactic distance and dynamics tool: galaxy [redshifts](../../../../services/cosmology-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/redshift.md), galactic [rotation curves](galactic-rotation-curve.md), and the [radial-velocity method](radial-velocity-method.md) for exoplanet detection are all spectroscopic.

Spectroscopy is performed at varying **resolving power** $R = \lambda/\Delta\lambda$. Low-resolution spectra ($R \sim 100$) classify galaxies and supernovae; medium-resolution ($R \sim 5{,}000$) measures stellar parameters and galaxy redshifts; high-resolution ($R \sim 50{,}000$) measures detailed stellar abundances, exoplanet radial velocities, and intergalactic absorption. Integral-field spectroscopy combines spatial and spectral information, producing a spectrum at every point of an extended object.
