The **astronomical unit** (au) is a unit of length defined by the IAU (2012) as exactly 149,597,870,700 metres. Historically it was the average distance from the Earth to the Sun, but the modern definition fixes it to a specific number of metres independent of any orbital measurement — this matters because the Earth–Sun distance is itself slowly variable.

The astronomical unit is the natural unit for distances inside the solar system: planetary semi-major axes are conveniently expressed in au (Mercury at 0.39 au, Jupiter at 5.20 au, Neptune at 30.07 au), the Kuiper belt is roughly 30–50 au, and the heliopause sits near 120 au. Beyond the solar system, distances are typically reported in **parsecs** (1 pc = 206,264.8 au, the distance at which 1 au subtends one arcsecond) or **light-years** (1 ly = 63,241 au).

The au's choice as the parsec's defining baseline is a historical-instrumental decision: trigonometric [parallax](parallax.md) measurements use Earth's orbit as the baseline, so the natural unit at which 1 au yields 1 arcsecond of parallax is the parsec. This is why nearby-star distances are reported in parsecs, not light-years, in the astronomical literature.
