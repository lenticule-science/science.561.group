A hypothesized totality of [worlds](world.md) (universes, branches, or causally disconnected regions) of which the observable universe is one element.
The term covers a family of distinct proposals that do not all mean the same thing; the [Tegmark classification](tegmark-classification.md) is the standard first-pass taxonomy.

A multiverse claim asserts at minimum:

1. There exist entities that count as *universes* — internally complete, self-consistent regions in which physics runs.
2. There is more than one such entity.
3. They stand in some structural relation: shared origin, common substrate, or mutual definition by a common generating mechanism.

Each of these is independently controversial.
(1) requires a definition of *universe* that does not collapse into "everything that exists" (because then there is exactly one by definition).
(2) requires either a generative mechanism (eternal inflation, the [many-worlds branching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Many-worlds_interpretation) of quantum mechanics, the string-theory landscape) or an a-priori commitment (mathematical universe hypothesis).
(3) is what distinguishes a multiverse — a structured population — from a mere disjoint pluralism.

## Empirical status

No direct observational evidence for any other universe currently exists.
Indirect evidence is debated: candidate signals (e.g. a "bruise" in the cosmic microwave background from a collision with a neighboring bubble universe) have been searched for and not confirmed.
Most multiverse hypotheses are constructed so that the absence of direct evidence is compatible with — sometimes a consequence of — the hypothesis itself.

This makes multiverse cosmology unusual among cosmological subjects: it is sometimes argued to lie outside science proper (Ellis & Silk, *Nature* 2014), sometimes argued to be a necessary consequence of accepted physics (Carroll on Everettian quantum mechanics; Linde on eternal inflation).
Both positions take the empirical situation seriously.
