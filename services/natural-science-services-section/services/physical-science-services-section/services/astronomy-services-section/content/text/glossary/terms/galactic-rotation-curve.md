A **galactic rotation curve** is the orbital velocity of stars and gas in a galaxy as a function of distance from the galactic centre. It is measured by Doppler shifts of spectral lines: in our own [Milky Way](milky-way.md) primarily from HI 21 cm and CO emission of cold gas; in external galaxies primarily from optical emission lines (H$\alpha$) of star-forming regions and HI radio measurements at large radii.

The Newtonian prediction, given the visible mass distribution, is that the rotation curve should rise in the inner regions (where mass is enclosed roughly $\propto r^3$) and fall in the outer regions (where enclosed mass is approximately constant), following Keplerian $v \propto r^{-1/2}$. The empirical observation, established systematically by Vera Rubin and Kent Ford for spiral galaxies in the 1970s and confirmed across thousands of galaxies since, is that rotation curves remain flat or rising out to the largest radii at which they can be measured. This requires that the enclosed mass continues to grow $\propto r$ well beyond where visible matter is found.

Flat rotation curves are one of the foundational lines of evidence for [dark matter](dark-matter.md): roughly 80–90% of the gravitational mass of a typical spiral galaxy is in a halo of non-luminous matter extending well beyond the stellar disk. Alternative explanations (modified gravity laws like MOND) reproduce the rotation curves themselves but face increasing difficulty with cluster-scale and cosmological observations.
