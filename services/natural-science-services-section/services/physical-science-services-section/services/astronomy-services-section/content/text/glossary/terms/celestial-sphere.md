title: "Celestial sphere"
type: term

The **celestial sphere** is the imaginary sphere of arbitrarily large radius, concentric with the observer, onto which all astronomical objects appear projected. It is a frame of reference for direction, not distance: a star and a quasar billions of times farther away appear at the same point if they share a line of sight from the observer.

Coordinates on the celestial sphere are typically given in **right ascension** (analogous to longitude, measured eastward along the celestial equator from the vernal equinox in hours, minutes, seconds) and **declination** (analogous to latitude, measured north or south of the celestial equator in degrees). Alternative coordinate systems — galactic, ecliptic, horizontal — share the same underlying spherical geometry with different reference planes and zero points.

The celestial sphere is a useful fiction. Its utility is that the geometry of observation — the angular separation between objects, the apparent motion of the sky as Earth rotates, the precession of the equinoxes, the parallax of nearby stars — admits a simple spherical-trigonometric treatment that does not require knowledge of distances. Once distances enter (parallax, standard candles, redshift), the celestial sphere is no longer the right frame; objects acquire three-dimensional positions and the analysis moves into [cosmography](../../cosmology/theories/observation/_index.md) or galactic coordinates.
