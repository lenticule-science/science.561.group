A **telescope** is an instrument for collecting and focusing electromagnetic radiation from astronomical sources. The principal design split is between **refracting telescopes** (which focus light with a lens, the original Galilean and Keplerian designs) and **reflecting telescopes** (which focus light with a mirror, dominant in modern professional astronomy because mirrors avoid chromatic aberration and can be made arbitrarily large with active support).

Two parameters dominate a telescope's capability: **aperture** (the diameter of the primary collecting surface, which sets light-gathering power $\propto D^2$ and diffraction-limited angular resolution $\propto \lambda/D$) and **field of view** (the angular extent of sky imaged in a single pointing, set by the focal-plane geometry and corrector optics). Survey telescopes (e.g., the Vera Rubin Observatory's Simonyi Survey Telescope) prioritize wide field; flagship telescopes (Hubble, JWST, the upcoming ELT and TMT) prioritize aperture.

Telescopes operate across the electromagnetic spectrum, with very different physical realizations at each wavelength: radio dishes can be hundreds of metres across (FAST, Arecibo before its 2020 collapse) or arrays kilometres across (VLA, ALMA, SKA); optical/infrared telescopes use precision-figured glass or beryllium mirrors with adaptive optics correcting atmospheric turbulence; X-ray telescopes use grazing-incidence "Wolter" optics because X-rays cannot be reflected at normal incidence; gamma-ray telescopes give up imaging entirely and use coincidence detection in scintillator stacks.
