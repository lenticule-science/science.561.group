A galaxy is a gravitationally bound system of [stars](star.md), gas, dust, and [dark matter](dark-matter.md), ranging from dwarf galaxies containing as few as $10^7$ stars to giant ellipticals containing $10^{13}$.

Galaxies come in several morphological types.
Spiral galaxies (like the Milky Way) have a flat rotating disk with spiral arms, a central bulge, and a surrounding halo of dark matter and old stars.
Elliptical galaxies are roughly spheroidal collections of old stars with little gas or dust.
Irregular galaxies lack coherent structure, often because of gravitational interactions with neighbors.

The mass of a galaxy is dominated by its dark matter halo, which extends far beyond the visible stars and gas.
Vera Rubin's measurements of galaxy rotation curves in the 1970s provided early evidence for dark matter: stars in the outer regions of spiral galaxies orbit faster than Newtonian gravity predicts from visible mass alone.
The discrepancy implies a large invisible mass component.

Galaxies are not distributed randomly.
They cluster into [galaxy clusters](galaxy-cluster.md) and are organized along the filaments of the [cosmic web](cosmic-web.md).
