An **exoplanet** is a planet orbiting a star other than the Sun. The first confirmed detection around a main-sequence star was Mayor and Queloz (1995, 51 Pegasi b, by radial velocity); detections around pulsars (Wolszczan and Frail, PSR B1257+12, 1992) preceded that by three years. As of 2026, more than 5,800 exoplanets have been confirmed, almost all by two methods: the [transit method](transit-method.md) (Kepler, K2, TESS, CHEOPS, PLATO) and the [radial-velocity method](radial-velocity-method.md) (HARPS, HIRES, ESPRESSO).

The detected population is heavily shaped by selection effects. Both transit and radial-velocity methods are most sensitive to large planets in short-period orbits, which is why hot Jupiters and short-period sub-Neptunes dominate the catalogue. Statistical inversion to recover the true planetary population — particularly the abundance of Earth-analog planets in habitable zones — is an active inference problem that the [predicated-astronomy](../../predicated-astronomy/_index.md) framing addresses naturally as a question about [selection effects as fiber restriction](../../../../services/cosmology-services-section/content/text/glossary/terms/selection-effect-as-fiber-restriction.md).

The major open questions are demographic (what is the true distribution of planets per star, by mass, period, and host type?), formational (does the diversity of architectures fit a single planet-formation theory?), and habitability-related (which observed exoplanets could host life, and what would constitute a biosignature detection?).
