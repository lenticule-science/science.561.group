The **Milky Way** is the spiral galaxy hosting the solar system. Its principal components are: a thin disk (~300 pc scale height, containing the Sun, most stars, and most molecular gas), a thick disk (~1 kpc scale height, older and more metal-poor), a central bulge and bar (older still, dominated by populations $\geq$ 10 Gyr), a stellar halo (sparse, very old, very metal-poor stars including globular clusters), and a dark-matter halo extending well beyond the visible structure with a virial radius of order 200 kpc.

Mapping the Milky Way is uniquely difficult precisely because we are inside it: extinction by interstellar dust obscures the Galactic plane in optical light, and our position 8 kpc from the Galactic centre means we lack the external view available for any other galaxy. Compensating measurements come from radio (HI 21 cm and CO surveys, which trace gas through dust), infrared (2MASS, WISE, JWST), and astrometry — most decisively from the Gaia mission, whose multi-billion-star parallax and proper-motion catalogue has revealed the disk's warps, flares, ridges, streams, and the kinematic substructure of past minor mergers (Gaia-Enceladus, Sequoia, et al.).

The Milky Way's [rotation curve](galactic-rotation-curve.md), measured from HI gas at large radii, was one of the original lines of evidence for [dark matter](dark-matter.md). It hosts a central supermassive [black hole](black-hole.md) (Sgr A\*, $4.1 \times 10^6 M_\odot$, mass measured directly from stellar orbits by Genzel and Ghez, Nobel 2020).
