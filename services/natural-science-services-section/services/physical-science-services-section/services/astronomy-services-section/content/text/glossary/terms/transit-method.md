The **transit method** detects [exoplanets](exoplanet.md) by monitoring a host star's brightness for the small, periodic dimming that occurs when a planet crosses the line of sight between the star and the observer. The dip is small (a Jupiter-sized planet across a Sun-sized star produces a ~1% dip; an Earth-sized planet across a Sun-sized star, ~0.01%) but extraordinarily diagnostic: the dip's depth gives the planet's radius (relative to the star), its duration constrains the orbital geometry, and the period between consecutive transits gives the orbital period and hence — via Kepler's third law and the host star's mass — the orbital semi-major axis.

Transit detection requires the orbital plane to be nearly edge-on as seen from Earth, a geometric selection cutting the detectable population by a factor of ~$R_\star/a$. It also requires precise photometric stability over long durations, which is why the most productive transit surveys are space-based: Kepler (2009–2018) discovered most of the known small planets, K2 extended its lifetime, TESS (2018–) is conducting an all-sky survey, and CHEOPS, JWST, and the upcoming PLATO and Roman missions extend coverage to fainter, longer-period, and atmospherically characterizable targets.

Transit spectroscopy — measuring the host star's spectrum during transit, when starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere — is the leading current method for constraining exoplanet atmospheric composition.

See also [radial-velocity method](radial-velocity-method.md).
